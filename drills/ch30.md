# Chapter 30 drills — The Nurse in Public Health, Home Health, Palliative Care, and Hospice

Authored from `sources/Ch30_Fact_Bank.md` (Stanhope & Lancaster, 6th ed.,
printed pp. 533–551). Blueprint weight for this chapter is 2 questions, so
coverage here is intentionally narrow and concentrated on the two most
testable ideas: the palliative-vs-hospice distinction and the two home
health documentation/terminology systems (OASIS and the Omaha System).

A gap the fact bank explicitly flags is skipped: the blueprint asks why a
home health assessment might take longer than a hospital assessment, but
the source never states a reason for that comparison, so no question
asserts one.

Topic: palliative care vs hospice
Subtopic: core distinction

Q: Which statement correctly distinguishes palliative care from hospice care?
A) Palliative care is the broad umbrella that can accompany curative treatment; hospice is a formalized, funded subset requiring a prognosis of 6 months or fewer
B) Hospice is the broad umbrella that can accompany curative treatment; palliative care requires a prognosis of 6 months or fewer
C) The two terms are interchangeable ways of describing end-of-life care
D) Palliative care is only available after hospice eligibility has been established
Answer: A
Rationale: Palliative care is the broad approach occurring over a longer period, offering a wider range of services not limited to the days or weeks before death, and it can run alongside curative treatment. Hospice is a formalized, funded program for patients with a physician-certified terminal prognosis of 6 months or fewer who choose comfort care over life-extending treatment.
Trap: The classic trap reverses the two terms or claims palliative care requires a terminal prognosis; palliative care is not time-limited.

Q: For a patient to be eligible for hospice care, the physician must certify which prognosis?
A) 3 months or fewer
B) 6 months or fewer
C) 12 months or fewer
D) No specific timeframe is required
Answer: B
Rationale: Hospice eligibility requires the physician to indicate the patient has 6 months or fewer to live, and the patient must acknowledge the terminal prognosis and select comfort-focused rather than life-extending care.
Trap: Any option naming a different timeframe, or claiming hospice has no timeframe requirement, is the distractor.

Q: A patient with advanced heart failure is still pursuing aggressive, curative-intent treatment but also wants help managing pain and shortness of breath, six months before any expectation of the disease being terminal. Which type of care fits this scenario?
A) Hospice care, because comfort needs have emerged
B) Palliative care, because it is not limited to end of life and can run alongside curative treatment
C) Neither, because comfort measures cannot be offered until curative treatment stops
D) Hospice care, because the patient has expressed a preference for comfort
Answer: B
Rationale: Palliative care occurs over a longer period, offers a wider range of services, is not limited to the days or weeks before death, and can be provided alongside curative treatment. This patient has not chosen to forgo life-extending treatment, so hospice does not fit.
Trap: The presence of comfort needs alone does not make a scenario hospice; hospice additionally requires a terminal prognosis of 6 months or fewer and a choice to forgo life-extending care.

Q: A patient chooses to stop life-extending treatment after a physician certifies a terminal prognosis of 6 months or fewer. Which type of care does this scenario describe?
A) Palliative care only
B) Hospice care
C) Transitional care
D) Skilled nursing care
Answer: B
Rationale: Hospice care is the formalized, funded program for patients with a physician-certified prognosis of 6 months or fewer who select comfort care over life-extending treatment.

Q: Which statement about the relationship between palliative care and hospice is accurate?
A) Hospice is a subset of palliative care with a shorter, defined timeframe
B) Palliative care is a subset of hospice reserved for patients who decline the hospice program
C) The two are unrelated services offered by different professions
D) Palliative care replaced hospice as the preferred model of end-of-life care
Answer: A
Rationale: Hospice is a subset of palliative care with a short timeframe; palliative is the broad umbrella and hospice is the narrower circle inside it, near the end of life.

Subtopic: barriers to hospice referral

Q: Why does hospice care often begin late in the disease process, according to the chapter?
A) Medicare regulations delay certification until the final weeks of life
B) Barriers and stigma persist because patients, families, and physicians may equate hospice with hopelessness and giving up
C) Hospice agencies require a second physician's confirmation before enrollment
D) Palliative care must be completed in full before hospice can begin
Answer: B
Rationale: Hospice often begins late because of barriers and stigmas — some patients, families, and physicians equate hospice with hopelessness and giving up. Many who resist hospice will accept palliative care.
Trap: The tested point is that late referral is a known barrier caused by stigma, not that hospice is intentionally meant to be brief.

Q: A family resists a hospice referral for a terminally ill relative but agrees to palliative care services. What does the chapter identify as the most likely explanation for this pattern?
A) Palliative care is less expensive than hospice
B) The family equates hospice with hopelessness and giving up, a stigma that does not attach to palliative care in the same way
C) Palliative care does not require a physician's order
D) Hospice is only available to patients without family caregivers
Answer: B
Rationale: The chapter states that stigma and the association of hospice with hopelessness and giving up are barriers to hospice referral, and that many who resist hospice will accept palliative care.

Topic: home health documentation and terminology
Subtopic: OASIS vs the Omaha System

Q: Which home health terminology system has three components — the Problem Classification Scheme, the Intervention Scheme, and the Problem Rating Scale for Outcomes?
A) OASIS
B) The Omaha System
C) OBQI
D) The CMS Conditions of Participation
Answer: B
Rationale: The Omaha System has three components designed to be used together: the Problem Classification Scheme, the Intervention Scheme, and the Problem Rating Scale for Outcomes.
Trap: Attributing the three components to OASIS is the classic trap. OASIS is the outcome-and-assessment data set, not the three-part terminology.

Q: What is the Omaha System, and who developed it?
A) A CMS-mandated billing form developed by federal regulators
B) The only ANA-recognized terminology developed inductively by and for nurses who practice in the community
C) A physician-developed hospital discharge protocol
D) A hospital-based acuity scoring tool
Answer: B
Rationale: The Omaha System is the only ANA-recognized terminology developed inductively by and for community nurses; it operationalizes the nursing process and is primarily used by home health agencies.
Trap: An option calling it physician-developed or hospital-based misattributes both its origin and its setting.

Q: What is OASIS, and who typically completes it?
A) A three-part terminology system used only by physicians
B) An outcome-and-assessment data set completed by the nurse or therapist while providing home care, forming the basis for outcome measurement
C) A billing code set maintained by the Omaha System
D) A patient-completed satisfaction survey
Answer: B
Rationale: OASIS (Outcome and Assessment Information Set) is a complex and important component of home health practice, completed by the nurse or therapist (physical, occupational, or speech-language) while providing home care, and is the basis for outcome measurement and quality improvement.

Q: A home health nurse wants to document a patient's problem, select a matching intervention, and rate the outcome using a single, unified, hierarchical framework. Which system is designed for this purpose?
A) OASIS
B) OBQI
C) The Omaha System
D) The CMS Conditions of Participation
Answer: C
Rationale: The Omaha System's three components — the Problem Classification Scheme, the Intervention Scheme, and the Problem Rating Scale for Outcomes — are designed to be used together and are comprehensive, hierarchical, multidimensional, and computer compatible.

Q: Which statements accurately describe OASIS-C2? Select all that apply.
A) It is used by interprofessional clinicians in Medicare-certified home health agencies
B) It is the outcome measurement tool mandated by the CMS Conditions of Participation
C) It has three components: the Problem Classification Scheme, the Intervention Scheme, and the Problem Rating Scale for Outcomes
D) It is used at designated intervals during the course of home health care
Answer: A, B, D
Rationale: OASIS-C2 is used by interprofessional clinicians in Medicare-certified home health agencies at designated intervals and is the outcome measurement tool mandated by the CMS Conditions of Participation.
Trap: The three-component structure belongs to the Omaha System, not OASIS.

Topic: home health care fundamentals
Subtopic: standard precautions and caregivers

Q: What do standard precautions mean in the home health setting?
A) Only patients with a known infectious diagnosis require precautions
B) All blood and body fluids are treated as potentially infectious
C) Precautions are only necessary during wound care procedures
D) Family caregivers are exempt from precaution requirements
Answer: B
Rationale: Standard precautions in the home mean that all blood and body fluids are treated as potentially infectious, the same principle applied in any setting. Infection prevention is a priority in home health and hospice.

Q: Which statement best reflects the chapter's description of family caregivers in home health?
A) Family caregivers are generally well prepared for the role by the time care transitions home
B) Family caregivers now provide care once delivered in hospitals and are often underprepared, which can be stressful and burdensome
C) Family caregivers are rarely needed once home health nursing begins
D) Family caregiver involvement is optional and does not affect the efficiency of home-based services
Answer: B
Rationale: Family caregivers provide many aspects of home care previously provided in hospitals; they are often not well prepared for the role, which can be stressful and burdensome. Partnership with the patient and family is essential for home-based services to be efficient.
Trap: A stem framing caregivers as fully equipped by default misreads the chapter's emphasis on caregiver support and partnership.

Topic: the initial home visit
Subtopic: guest role and visit sequence

Q: What is the governing principle for a nurse entering a patient's home for the first time?
A) The nurse directs the visit as the clinical authority in the home
B) The nurse is a guest whose services can be accepted or rejected, and must earn the family's trust to establish a partnership
C) The nurse may proceed with assessment and treatment regardless of the family's response, since a physician referral authorizes the visit
D) The nurse's primary task is to complete as many billable interventions as possible in the first visit
Answer: B
Rationale: When a nurse enters a patient's home, he or she is a guest and must recognize that the services offered can be accepted or rejected. The nurse needs to earn the trust of the family and establish a partnership with the patient and family.
Trap: A stem where the nurse asserts control over the home, or proceeds without agreement, is the distractor.

Q: According to Box 30.3, what should a home health nurse do before approaching the home for the first visit, after already reviewing the referral data and gathering supplies?
A) Arrive unannounced to observe the family's typical routine
B) Contact the patient or family by phone to obtain agreement for the visit and schedule the day and time
C) Notify the primary care physician of the exact arrival time
D) Skip neighborhood observation to avoid appearing intrusive
Answer: B
Rationale: Box 30.3's sequence is: review patient data, contact the patient or family by phone to obtain agreement for the visit and schedule the day and time, gather supplies and forms, observe the neighborhood for resources and safety when approaching the home, greet the patient/family, obtain signatures, provide care, discuss the plan of care, and document.
Trap: Arriving unannounced contradicts the requirement to schedule the visit by phone in advance.

Q: The initial home assessment described in Box 30.3 targets which three areas?
A) The patient, the family caregiver's income, and the home's square footage
B) The patient, the support system, and the environment
C) The patient's medications, insurance status, and transportation access
D) The patient's diagnosis, prognosis, and code status
Answer: B
Rationale: The first visit sets the stage for success or failure; the initial assessment of the patient, the support system, and the environment is critical.
Trap: An option naming only the patient's physical status is incomplete — the support system and environment are equally part of the initial assessment.

Q: Which activity is specifically described as occurring while the nurse approaches the home, before greeting the patient or family?
A) Obtaining signatures on consent forms
B) Observing the neighborhood for resources and safety
C) Documenting the visit in the electronic health record
D) Discussing the plan of care
Answer: B
Rationale: Box 30.3 places observing the neighborhood for resources and safety during the approach to the home, before the greeting, signature, and care-provision steps that follow.

Topic: transitional care and interprofessional models
Subtopic: definitions and the Triple Aim

Q: How does the chapter define transitional care?
A) Care provided exclusively during the final transition from curative treatment to hospice
B) A set of actions designed to ensure the coordination and continuity of health care as patients transfer between different locations and different levels of care
C) The period between hospital discharge and the first home health visit only
D) Care coordinated solely by family caregivers without professional involvement
Answer: B
Rationale: Transitional care is defined as "a set of actions designed to ensure the coordination and continuity of health care as patients transfer between different locations and different levels of care." Nurse-Family Partnership and transitional care are the two additional nurse-led models highlighted.

Q: The Triple Aim model that underlies community-based home health, palliative, and hospice services includes which three goals? Select all that apply.
A) Improve population health
B) Improve the care experience
C) Reduce per-capita cost
D) Maximize the number of billable home visits
Answer: A, B, C
Rationale: The Triple Aim model (Berwick, Nolan, Whittington, 2008) has long been the foundation of these community-based services: improve population health, improve the care experience, and reduce per-capita cost.

Topic: levels of prevention
Subtopic: applying the three levels in home health

Q: A home health nurse administers the seasonal flu vaccine to a patient. This is an example of which level of prevention?
A) Primary
B) Secondary
C) Tertiary
D) It cannot be classified in the home setting
Answer: A
Rationale: The Levels of Prevention box for home health lists administering seasonal or newer-strain flu vaccine, or providing case management so patients can obtain vaccines conveniently, as primary prevention.

Q: A home health nurse monitors a patient closely for early signs of a new medication side effect so treatment can begin promptly. This is an example of which level of prevention?
A) Primary
B) Secondary
C) Tertiary
D) Quaternary
Answer: B
Rationale: Secondary prevention in home health involves monitoring patients at home for early signs of new health problems, such as medication side effects, to initiate prompt treatment.

Q: A home health nurse teaches a patient newly diagnosed with diabetes how to modify the diet and administer insulin injections. This is an example of which level of prevention?
A) Primary
B) Secondary
C) Tertiary
D) Primary, because the teaching prevents future complications
Answer: C
Rationale: Tertiary prevention in home health includes instruction about dietary modifications and insulin injections for patients with a new diabetes diagnosis, which the patient and family then implement.
Trap: The teaching is aimed at an already-diagnosed condition, which places it at the tertiary level even though it also aims to prevent future complications.
