Topic: culture, race, and ethnicity
Subtopic: the three-way distinction

Q: A nurse cares for two African American patients, one of whose family has lived in the rural South for generations and one who immigrated recently from Haiti. Both patients are the same race. What does the chapter's discussion of this example illustrate?
A) Race and ethnicity are always identical for members of the same racial group
B) Individuals may be of the same race but of different cultures
C) Culture is a purely biological concept
D) Ethnicity determines race
Answer: B
Rationale: The chapter states explicitly that individuals may be of the same race but of different cultures, using African Americans — who may trace heritage to Africa, the Caribbean, North America, or elsewhere — as a heterogeneous group that should not be viewed as culturally and racially homogeneous.

Q: Which definition matches "culture" as the chapter defines it?
A) A biological variation within population groups based on physical markers derived from genetic ancestry
B) A set of beliefs, values, and assumptions about life that is widely held among a group of people and transmitted across generations
C) The shared feeling of peoplehood among a group of individuals
D) The degree of variation represented among populations based on lifestyle, ethnicity, and race
Answer: B
Rationale: Culture is a set of beliefs, values, and assumptions about life widely held among a group and transmitted across generations (Leininger). Option A is race, C is ethnicity, and D is cultural diversity.
Trap: All four terms — culture, race, ethnicity, cultural diversity — are commonly confused. Race is biological and not learned; ethnicity is about shared identity/peoplehood; culture is the learned, transmitted set of beliefs and values.

Q: Which basis distinguishes race from both culture and ethnicity?
A) Race is learned and transmitted across generations
B) Race is a biological variation based on physical markers from genetic ancestry, not learned
C) Race reflects a shared feeling of peoplehood
D) Race can change slowly over a person's lifetime through acculturation
Answer: B
Rationale: Race is biological variation based on physical markers derived from genetic ancestry — skin color, physical features, hair texture — and is not learned, unlike culture (learned and shared) or ethnicity (identity and membership).

Q: A sign in a break room reads "Thank you for not smoking" instead of "No smoking is permitted." According to the chapter's example, this reflects a culture that values which communication style?
A) Directness
B) Indirectness
C) High context, low affect
D) Nonverbal dominance
Answer: B
Rationale: The chapter uses this exact pair as its example of implicit culture: "No smoking is permitted" reflects a culture that values directness, while "Thank you for not smoking" reflects one that values indirectness.

Topic: six cultural phenomena
Subtopic: communication

Q: A nurse instructs Asian American patients on taking antituberculin medication. The patients smilingly respond "yes, yes," and the nurse assumes they understand and will comply. A week later the medications have not been taken. What is the best explanation and correction, per the chapter's worked scenario?
A) The patients were non-adherent due to low health literacy; the nurse should simplify the vocabulary used
B) Acceptance and avoidance of confrontation with authority figures are valued in Asian American culture; the nurse should have the patients repeat the instructions back and discuss the cultural meaning of TB
C) The patients did not speak English well enough to understand; the nurse should have used a translated pamphlet only
D) The patients were testing the nurse's authority; the nurse should escalate to a supervisor
Answer: B
Rationale: The chapter's classic scenario shows that acceptance by and avoidance of confrontation or disagreement with authority figures are important behaviors in Asian American culture. The corrected approach repeats instructions, allows questions, has the patient repeat instructions back, and discusses the cultural meaning and treatment of TB.
Trap: Nodding "yes" is not evidence of understanding. The same "have the patient repeat it back" correction applies to the simpatía pattern in Chapter 24 — treat nodding-without-confirmation as a universal red flag across chapters.

Subtopic: space, social organization, time perception

Q: A nurse notices that a patient from a certain cultural group appears uncomfortable when the nurse stands close during an exam, while a patient from a different cultural group appears offended when the nurse maintains distance. What does the chapter say this illustrates?
A) Most cultural groups have spatial preferences, and the nurse may offend the patient by using a distance that conflicts with the patient's cultural comfort zone
B) Personal space preferences are identical across cultures and any discomfort reflects individual anxiety
C) Spatial preference is dictated entirely by the patient's socioeconomic status
D) Nurses should always maintain at least a 9-inch distance regardless of culture
Answer: A
Rationale: Personal space is the physical area individuals need to feel comfortable, and most cultural groups have spatial preferences — one community may be comfortable at a 9-inch distance while another finds that same distance threatening and overly aggressive.

Q: When assessing social organization, the chapter advises nurses to find out all of the following EXCEPT:
A) Who is considered to be in the family
B) Who the key decision makers are
C) Whether the needs of the family supersede those of the individual
D) The family's political party affiliation
Answer: D
Rationale: The chapter directs nurses to find out who is considered family, who the key decision makers are, and whether family needs supersede individual needs — not political affiliation. It also notes nurses should advocate for the individual while recognizing family members may need to be included in decisions.

Q: A patient from a present-oriented culture is being counseled about smoking cessation. Which teaching approach best fits the chapter's guidance?
A) Emphasize the long-term consequences of continued smoking on future cardiovascular health
B) Emphasize the immediate benefits of quitting rather than future outcomes
C) Schedule teaching around the patient's future retirement goals
D) Withhold teaching until the patient raises the topic, since time is not a priority for this orientation
Answer: B
Rationale: For present-oriented patients, the chapter specifically advises focusing on immediate benefits rather than emphasizing future outcomes, since "the future is unknown, but the present is known."
Trap: "Explain the long-term consequences" sounds like sound patient teaching in general, but it is the named trap answer for present-oriented time perception.

Q: Which culture does the chapter name as an example of a past time orientation?
A) Vietnamese culture, which looks to ancestors' wishes and memories for direction
B) American middle-class culture, which delays gratification for future goals
C) Haitian culture, which values immediate benefit over long-term planning
D) Nigerian culture, which views birth as a natural process
Answer: A
Rationale: The chapter names Vietnamese culture as its example of past orientation — focus on wishes and memories of ancestors, looking to them for direction, with time viewed as more flexible.

Subtopic: environmental control and biological variations

Q: A patient believes that nothing she does will change the outcome of her cancer diagnosis because it is her destiny, and as a result she is less likely to follow a prevention protocol. Which environmental control worldview does this reflect?
A) Mastery over nature
B) Dominated by nature
C) Harmony with nature
D) Cultural blindness
Answer: B
Rationale: The "dominated by nature" worldview, named for African Americans and Hispanics or Latinos in the chapter, holds that the person has little or no control over what happens to them, making them less likely to engage in illness prevention activities.

Q: A patient views illness as disharmony with other forces and seeks naturalistic solutions such as herbs, acupuncture, and hot/cold treatments because "healing comes from within." Which environmental control worldview does this describe?
A) Mastery over nature
B) Dominated by nature
C) Harmony with nature
D) Ethnocentrism
Answer: C
Rationale: The "harmony with nature" worldview — named for African Americans, Asian Americans, and Native Americans/Alaskan Natives — perceives illness as disharmony with other forces; medicine can relieve symptoms but cannot cure, so patients seek treatment addressing mind, body, and spirit.

Q: A school nurse observes a bluish discoloration on the thigh of a child with brown skin, assumes it is a bruise, and reports it to child protective services, leading the mother to be accused of abuse. What does the chapter's scenario identify as the discoloration?
A) A hematoma from unreported trauma
B) A Mongolian spot, a normal bluish skin variation that may be mistaken for a bruise
C) A vitamin K deficiency bruise
D) A sign of untreated jaundice
Answer: B
Rationale: Mongolian spots are bluish discolorations sometimes present on the skin of African American, Asian American, Hispanic or Latino, and Native American/Alaskan Native babies, and the chapter warns explicitly that they may be mistaken for bruises.
Trap: This scenario is built specifically to illustrate the harm of unrecognized biological variation; a nurse's failure to recognize a Mongolian spot led to a false child-abuse allegation. Chapter 27's child-abuse indicators content makes this an easy pairing for a cross-chapter distractor.

Subtopic: nutrition

Q: A Buddhist patient declines a meat-based meal. Which of the Five Moral Precepts does the chapter cite as being in conflict with eating meat?
A) Only the precept against stealing
B) Only the precept against lying
C) Both the precept against harming living things and the precept against consuming intoxicants, since meat is viewed as an intoxicant
D) Only the precept against sexual misconduct
Answer: C
Rationale: Many Buddhists are vegetarian; eating meat is said to conflict with both the first precept (harming living things) and the fifth (consuming intoxicants), because meat is seen as an intoxicant.

Topic: immigrant issues
Subtopic: the four types of foreign-born

Q: A patient recently admitted to the US under the Refugee Act, referred by the UN High Commissioner for Refugees, asks whether she can enroll in Medicaid right away. What should the nurse know?
A) She must wait 5 years like other legal immigrants before Medicaid eligibility
B) She is immediately eligible for TANF, SSI, and Medicaid, unlike documented immigrants who face a waiting period
C) She is not eligible for any federal benefits as a refugee
D) She must first apply for lawful permanent resident status before any benefits apply
Answer: B
Rationale: Refugees are immediately eligible for TANF, SSI, and Medicaid — the only foreign-born category with immediate eligibility. Documented immigrants (lawful permanent residents) typically face a 5-year waiting period before Medicaid/CHIP eligibility.
Trap: This immediate-refugee vs. 5-year-documented-immigrant contrast is flagged as the single most testable point in the immigration section — do not let "refugee" and "documented immigrant" eligibility rules get swapped.

Q: A lawful permanent resident who has held "qualified" immigration status for 3 years applies for Medicaid. What should the nurse expect?
A) Immediate eligibility, the same as a refugee
B) Denial, because she must complete a 5-year waiting period from receiving qualified status before Medicaid/CHIP eligibility
C) Eligibility only for CHIP, not Medicaid
D) Automatic eligibility because she has lived in the US for over 2 years
Answer: B
Rationale: Documented immigrants (lawful permanent residents) usually face a 5-year waiting period living in the US after receiving "qualified" immigration status before becoming eligible for Medicaid and CHIP.

Q: A patient is in the US on a student visa, has a permanent residence in another country, and plans to return home after completing her degree. How should the nurse categorize her immigration status?
A) Documented immigrant
B) Refugee
C) Non-immigrant
D) Undocumented immigrant
Answer: C
Rationale: Non-immigrants are admitted for a limited duration and specified purpose and have a permanent residence outside the United States; the category includes students, tourists, temporary workers, business executives, diplomats, artists, entertainers, reporters, and those seeking medical treatment.

Q: Which services are undocumented immigrants eligible to receive, according to the chapter? Select all that apply.
A) Emergency medical services
B) Immunizations
C) Medicaid
D) Treatment for the symptoms of communicable diseases
E) Access to school lunches
Answer: A, B, D, E
Rationale: Undocumented immigrants are eligible for emergency medical services, immunizations, treatment for symptoms of communicable diseases, and access to school lunches — but not Medicaid, CHIP, the ACA, or marketplace coverage.
Trap: Medicaid is the item that gets slipped into this list; it belongs to the "not eligible" side.

Q: Which statement correctly describes the tax paradox the chapter describes for immigrant populations?
A) Undocumented immigrants pay no taxes and receive no benefits
B) Immigrants typically pay federal taxes, but the services they receive are paid for by states and localities, and they are not entitled to tax credits
C) Documented immigrants are exempt from federal taxes until naturalization
D) Only documented immigrants pay any form of tax
Answer: B
Rationale: The chapter names this dilemma explicitly: immigrants typically pay federal taxes, yet the services they receive are paid for by states and localities, and they are not entitled to tax credits. Both documented and undocumented immigrants pay taxes and contribute substantially to GDP, but undocumented immigrants are not entitled to benefits like Medicare and Social Security.

Q: A 15-year-old serves as the only available interpreter for her non-English-speaking grandmother's outpatient visit. What is the chapter's guidance?
A) This is acceptable as long as the child understands medical vocabulary
B) Children should be avoided as interpreters, particularly when the patient is an adult
C) Children are preferred interpreters because they are more familiar with American health care
D) This is acceptable only for pediatric visits, not for adult patients
Answer: B
Rationale: The HOW TO guidelines explicitly state to avoid using children as interpreters, particularly when the patient is an adult. Using family members, friends, or untrained staff can create errors in understanding and communicating, posing grave risks to the patient and liability to the institution.

Q: Which statement correctly distinguishes an interpreter from a translator?
A) A translator handles verbal communication; an interpreter handles written documents
B) An interpreter is associated with verbal communication and expressing equivalent meaning rather than word-to-word equivalence; a translator is associated with written documents
C) The two terms are interchangeable in the chapter
D) An interpreter only works with sign language, while a translator works with spoken language
Answer: B
Rationale: Translators work with written documents such as medical records and legal documents; interpreters handle verbal communication, focusing on accurate expression of equivalent meanings rather than word-to-word equivalence.

Q: Which practices are recommended in the chapter's guidelines for selecting and using an interpreter? Select all that apply.
A) Identify the patient's country of origin and language or dialect before selecting the interpreter
B) Ask the interpreter to translate the patient's own words, and ask the patient to repeat the information communicated
C) Allow the interpreter to summarize what was said rather than interpreting everything
D) Review the material with both the patient and the interpreter at the end of the interview
E) Consider the gender and age of the interpreter, since patients may prefer an interpreter of a particular gender or a more mature interpreter
Answer: A, B, D, E
Rationale: The guidelines call for identifying country of origin/language before selecting an interpreter, having the interpreter translate the patient's own words with the patient repeating information back, reviewing material with both patient and interpreter at the end, and considering gender/age preferences.
Trap: The interpreter must interpret everything said by all parties in the interaction, not summarize — summarizing risks omitting content and misrepresenting the spirit of what was said.

Subtopic: acculturation and somatization

Q: A nurse notices that in an immigrant family, children have adapted to American culture more quickly than their parents, and this has led to increasing family conflict. What does the chapter identify as a consequence of this dynamic?
A) A shift in the balance of power between adults and children, which can contribute to family conflict and at times violence
B) An automatic improvement in the parents' health literacy
C) A guaranteed increase in the family's Medicaid eligibility
D) A decrease in the children's risk for mental illness
Answer: A
Rationale: Children and adolescents often adjust to a new culture more easily than adults, which can shift the balance of power between adults and children and contribute to family conflict and at times violence. Inability of elders to acculturate may contribute to poor adherence to health care guidelines.

Q: A patient from a culture where mental illness carries high stigma reports chronic stomach pain and fatigue rather than describing feelings of depression. What term does the chapter use for this pattern?
A) Cultural blindness
B) Somatization
C) Cultural repatterning
D) Ethnocentrism
Answer: B
Rationale: Somatization occurs in cultures where body and mind are seen as one entity, or where mental illness carries high stigma; psychological distress is experienced and expressed as physical illness.

Topic: developing cultural competence
Subtopic: Orlandi's three stages

Q: Using Orlandi's framework, a nurse who is aware of cultural differences but still lacks some of the skills needed to act on that awareness would be classified at which stage, in the affective and skills dimensions respectively?
A) Oblivious and unskilled
B) Sympathetic and lacking some skills
C) Committed to change and highly skilled
D) Apathetic and highly skilled
Answer: B
Rationale: In Orlandi's model, the culturally sensitive stage pairs an affective response of "sympathetic" with skills described as "lacking some skills." The culturally incompetent stage is oblivious/apathetic/unskilled, and the culturally competent stage is knowledgeable/committed to change/highly skilled.

Subtopic: Campinha-Bacote's five constructs

Q: Which of Campinha-Bacote's five constructs is defined as self-examination and in-depth exploration of one's own biases, stereotypes, and prejudices?
A) Cultural knowledge
B) Cultural awareness
C) Cultural skill
D) Cultural encounter
Answer: B
Rationale: Cultural awareness is self-examination and in-depth exploration of one's own biases, stereotypes, and prejudices that influence behavior.

Q: A nursing instructor is designing a required course to build several of Campinha-Bacote's constructs. Which construct can NOT be taught directly in a classroom setting?
A) Cultural knowledge
B) Cultural skill
C) Cultural desire
D) Cultural awareness
Answer: C
Rationale: Cultural desire — the nurse's intrinsic motivation to provide culturally competent care because they want to rather than because they are directed to — cannot be directly taught in the classroom, unlike knowledge, skill, awareness, and encounter.
Trap: A stem that asks "which construct is developed through education" is testing this same fact from the other direction; cultural desire is always the wrong answer to that framing.

Q: A community health nurse deliberately seeks out cross-cultural interactions with patients from a group she has limited experience with, in order to update her existing beliefs and avoid stereotyping. This best illustrates which construct?
A) Cultural knowledge
B) Cultural encounter
C) Cultural desire
D) Cultural skill
Answer: B
Rationale: Cultural encounter is the process that permits nurses to seek opportunities to engage in cross-cultural interactions with diverse patients, to modify existing beliefs and possibly avoid stereotyping.

Topic: culturally competent nursing interventions
Subtopic: the four action modes

Q: A grandmother asks the delivery room nurse not to discard a piece of the amniotic sac on her grandbaby's face, believing it is a sign of something special in the baby's future life. The nurse, though unfamiliar with the belief, gives her the piece of the sac as requested. Which action mode does this illustrate, and why?
A) Cultural preservation, because the practice is scientifically supported
B) Cultural accommodation, because the practice is not harmful
C) Cultural repatterning, because the practice is harmful
D) Cultural brokering, because it mediates a conflict between belief systems
Answer: B
Rationale: Cultural accommodation applies when a practice is not harmful; the nurse supports and facilitates the patient's (or family's) use of the practice. The amniotic sac request is the chapter's worked example of accommodation.

Q: A nurse providing prenatal teaching to Haitian women learns that many are drinking an herbal tea from a local herbalist intended to help them have a "strong baby." After consulting a pharmacist, she discovers one of the herbs raises blood pressure — a problem many of the women already have. She explains why they should stop drinking that tea and enlists the herbalist's help. Which action mode is this, and why?
A) Cultural preservation, because herbal remedies are always scientifically supported
B) Cultural accommodation, because the practice is not harmful
C) Cultural repatterning, because the practice is harmful
D) Cultural brokering, because the nurse is mediating between two providers
Answer: C
Rationale: Cultural repatterning applies when a practice is harmful; the nurse works with the patient to reorder, change, or modify it. The Haitian herbal tea example is the chapter's worked example of repatterning — the tea's blood-pressure-raising herb made it harmful.

Q: A nurse supports and facilitates an older Chinese American patient's use of acupressure and acupuncture alongside prescribed biomedical treatment for hypertension. Which action mode does this represent, and what is the trigger condition?
A) Cultural preservation; the practice is scientifically supported
B) Cultural accommodation; the practice is not harmful
C) Cultural repatterning; the practice is harmful
D) Cultural brokering; there is a conflict between care cultures
Answer: A
Rationale: Cultural preservation applies when a practice is scientifically supported; the nurse supports and facilitates its use alongside biomedical care. Acupressure and acupuncture are the chapter's example, specifically because they are "accepted by increasing numbers of Western practitioners as a legitimate method."
Trap: Preservation, accommodation, and repatterning turn entirely on the practice's harm status — supported, neutral, or harmful, respectively. Read the stem for which of the three the practice is described as before selecting an answer.

Q: A nurse teaches migrant farmworkers about pesticide safety, environmental sanitation, and nutrition during what may be her only encounter with them, and separately contacts migrant health services for follow-up. Which action mode does this best illustrate?
A) Cultural preservation
B) Cultural accommodation
C) Cultural repatterning
D) Cultural brokering
Answer: D
Rationale: Cultural brokering is advocating, mediating, negotiating, and intervening between the patient's culture and the biomedical health care culture. The migrant worker teaching and health services referral is the chapter's worked example of brokering.

Topic: inhibitors to cultural competence
Subtopic: the seven inhibitors

Q: A nurse says, "I treat all my patients exactly the same, regardless of their cultural background." Which inhibitor does this statement demonstrate?
A) Cultural relativism
B) Cultural blindness
C) Ethnocentrism
D) Cultural conflict
Answer: B
Rationale: Cultural blindness is the tendency to ignore all differences among cultures and act as though they do not exist. The chapter states directly that nurses who say they treat all patients the same, regardless of cultural orientation, are demonstrating cultural blindness.
Trap: This statement sounds like fairness and equal treatment, but the chapter names it explicitly as the wrong answer disguised as virtue. Any answer option where a nurse claims to "treat everyone the same" should be treated as cultural blindness, not a correct or neutral practice.

Q: A nurse believes her own cultural standards are the correct measure by which all other groups' behavior should be judged, but she has not acted on this belief in patient care. Which term applies?
A) Cultural imposition
B) Ethnocentrism
C) Racism
D) Stereotyping
Answer: B
Rationale: Ethnocentrism is a type of cultural prejudice at the population level — the belief that one's own group determines the standards for behavior by which all other groups are to be judged. It remains a belief until acted upon.
Trap: If the nurse instead overrode the patient's values and forced her own standards into the plan of care, that added ACT would make it cultural imposition, not ethnocentrism. Belief alone = ethnocentrism; belief plus the act of imposing = cultural imposition.

Q: A nurse dismisses a patient's request for acupuncture and instead insists the patient follow only Western biomedical treatment, overriding the patient's stated preference for herbal therapy. Which inhibitor best fits this behavior?
A) Ethnocentrism
B) Cultural imposition
C) Prejudice
D) Cultural blindness
Answer: B
Rationale: Cultural imposition is the belief in one's own superiority (ethnocentrism) plus the act of imposing one's values on others — here, forcefully promoting Western medical traditions while ignoring the patient's value of herbal therapy, the chapter's own example.

Q: A nurse remarks, "All Asian people are hardworking." Which inhibitor does this best illustrate, and why?
A) Prejudice, because it expresses a negative reaction toward the group
B) Stereotyping, because it attributes a group trait to an individual without regard for individual differences, even though the statement sounds favorable
C) Racism, because it implies inferiority
D) Ethnocentrism, because it applies a population-level standard
Answer: B
Rationale: Stereotyping is attributing certain beliefs and behaviors about a group to an individual without giving adequate attention to individual differences. The chapter's own example — "All Asian people are hardworking" — is deliberately favorable-sounding, showing that a positive generalization is still stereotyping.
Trap: Do not assume stereotyping must be negative. A favorable-sounding generalization applied to an individual is still stereotyping; prejudice specifically requires a deeply held, often negative reaction.

Q: Which statement correctly distinguishes prejudice from racism as the chapter defines them?
A) Prejudice and racism are synonyms with no meaningful distinction
B) Prejudice is a deeply held, often negative reaction about a group or person with no regard for individual worth; racism is a specific form of prejudice holding that a group is inferior in intelligence, morals, beauty, or self-worth
C) Prejudice occurs at the population level, while racism occurs only at the individual level
D) Racism is a feeling, while prejudice always involves denial of opportunity
Answer: B
Rationale: Prejudice is a deeply held reaction, often negative, about another group or person, with no regard for individual worth. Racism is a form of prejudice specifically involving the belief that a group is inferior in intelligence, morals, beauty, or self-worth, and can result in denial of opportunity.

Q: Which of the three forms of racism described in the chapter is illustrated by discriminatory policies and resource allocation that disadvantage certain groups?
A) Individual racism
B) Institutional racism
C) Cultural racism
D) Structural ethnocentrism
Answer: B
Rationale: Institutional racism refers to discriminatory policies, priorities, and resource allocation pertaining to certain groups. Individual racism is based on characteristics of the group (skin color, hair texture, facial features), and cultural racism views a culture in derogatory or stereotypical ways because of, for example, dress or language.

Q: A nurse and a patient each hold different, unspoken expectations about how a home visit should proceed, and neither realizes the other's expectations differ, leading to mutual discomfort. What does the chapter call this, and how does it describe managing it?
A) Cultural blindness; it should be eliminated entirely through staff training
B) Cultural conflict; it is unavoidable, but nurses should know how to manage it
C) Racism; it requires formal reporting
D) Cultural relativism; it is the goal to strive toward
Answer: B
Rationale: Cultural conflict is a perceived threat that may arise from a misunderstanding of expectations between patients and nurses when either group is unaware of cultural differences. The chapter states plainly that although cultural conflict is unavoidable, it is important to know how to manage it.

Q: What does the chapter name as the goal that serves as the antidote to cultural imposition?
A) Cultural relativism — judging each culture on its own merit rather than the nurse's personal beliefs
B) Cultural blindness — treating all patients identically
C) Cultural shock — recognizing one's own disorientation
D) Cultural encounter — seeking cross-cultural interactions
Answer: A
Rationale: Cultural relativism is named as the goal: recognizing that patients have different approaches to health and that each culture should be judged on its own merit, not the nurse's personal beliefs.

Topic: cultural nursing assessment
Subtopic: brief vs. in-depth assessment and the negotiation sequence

Q: A nurse meeting a patient for the first time gathers an overview of ethnic background, language, religious affiliation, dietary practices, and the patient's perception of the health issue. When should the in-depth cultural assessment, as opposed to this brief one, take place?
A) During this same initial contact, immediately following the brief assessment
B) Data for an in-depth assessment should be gathered over a period of time and not restricted to the first encounter with the patient
C) Only after the patient has been discharged from care
D) Only if the patient specifically requests it
Answer: B
Rationale: The chapter states that data for an in-depth cultural assessment should be gathered over a period of time and not restricted to the first encounter, giving both nurse and patient time to build a helping relationship. The brief/general assessment is what happens at initial contact.
Trap: This is flagged as a likely correct answer for "when should the nurse complete the cultural assessment?" — the tempting wrong answer is doing it all at once during intake.

Q: Place the negotiation sequence used in cultural nursing assessment in the correct order.
A) Negotiate, listen, explain, recommend, acknowledge
B) Listen to the patient's perceptions, explain the nurse's perceptions, acknowledge/discuss similarities and differences, develop recommendations, negotiate care actions
C) Explain the nurse's perceptions first, then listen to the patient, then negotiate immediately
D) Acknowledge differences, negotiate, then listen and explain afterward
Answer: B
Rationale: The negotiation sequence is: nurses listen to patients' perceptions of their problems; nurses explain their own perceptions; both acknowledge and discuss similarities and differences; develop recommendations for managing problems; and negotiate with patients on nursing care actions.

Q: According to the chapter, what is the key to a successful cultural assessment?
A) Memorizing the customs of every culture the nurse might encounter
B) Nurses being aware of their own culture
C) Relying exclusively on a trained interpreter for all cultural information
D) Completing the assessment within the first five minutes of contact
Answer: B
Rationale: The chapter states plainly that the key to a successful cultural assessment lies in nurses being aware of their own culture.

Q: When eliciting cultural information from a family, the chapter notes that in some cultures the appropriate confidante for bridging the gap may not be the patient herself. Who does the chapter specifically name as a possible key informant?
A) The patient's employer
B) The woman's husband or a close male family friend
C) A hospital chaplain of the same faith
D) The eldest child in the household
Answer: B
Rationale: The chapter advises identifying a confidante who will help bridge the gap between cultures, noting that in some cultures the woman's husband or a close male family friend may be the person from whom the nurse needs to obtain cultural information.
