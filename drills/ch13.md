# Chapter 13 drills — Community Assessment and Evaluation

Authored from `sources/Ch13_Fact_Bank.md` (Stanhope & Lancaster, 6th ed.,
printed pp. 237–250). Ch 13 is a narrow, 2-question blueprint chapter built
around two named terms — windshield survey and key informant — surrounded by
the primary/secondary data framework and the community-as-client/partner
model. Items are anchored to that file; traps flagged there are called out
explicitly below.

---

Topic: community assessment
Subtopic: windshield survey

Q: A public health nurse drives slowly through an assigned neighborhood, noting housing conditions, businesses, open space, and signs of decay or vitality before planning a fuller assessment. What is this method called?
A) Windshield survey
B) Key informant interview
C) Focus group
D) Photovoice
Answer: A
Rationale: A windshield survey is a method of simple observation that gives a quick overview of a community — described as the "community equivalent of a head-to-toe assessment" — done by driving or walking through.
Trap: The stem is the classic test cue: a nurse driving or walking through to get a general first impression of housing and conditions. It is observational and preliminary, not a substitute for a fuller assessment if interventions are planned.

Q: What is the recommended way to conduct a windshield survey?
A) By a single nurse working alone to avoid bias
B) With more than one person, since one can drive/watch the road while another observes and takes notes
C) Only after secondary data has been fully analyzed
D) Only during a community-sponsored event such as a fair or festival
Answer: B
Rationale: A windshield survey is best conducted with more than one person — one drives or watches the road while another observes and takes notes, and the shared experience adds insight. A second tour may be needed to fill in blanks.
Trap: Option D describes participant observation, not a windshield survey.

Q: Which statements about the windshield survey are accurate? Select all that apply.
A) It is a form of simple observation
B) It is called the "community equivalent of a head-to-toe assessment"
C) It is a secondary data method because it summarizes existing records
D) It may require a second tour to fill in blanks
E) It provides context for a community's health status, strengths, risks, and vulnerabilities
Answer: A, B, D, E
Rationale: The windshield survey is a method of simple observation, nicknamed the "community equivalent of a head-to-toe assessment," may need a second tour to fill in blanks, and gives context for health status, strengths, risks, and vulnerabilities.
Trap: It is a primary data method — the nurse collects it firsthand — not a secondary one.

Subtopic: key informants

Q: A community health nursing student is told that county health department nurses and church leaders are often good sources of insight into local concerns, even though they may hold no formal title. What term describes these sources?
A) Gatekeepers
B) Stakeholders
C) Key informants
D) Community diagnosticians
Answer: C
Rationale: Key informants are community members who provide insight into local issues, strengths, and concerns, and often hold an informal role rather than a formal title. County health department nurses and church leaders are the examples given.

Q: A nurse assumes that a true key informant must be an elected official or an agency head. Why is this reasoning flawed?
A) Key informants must have clinical training
B) Key informants are chosen for their knowledge of and connection to the community, not their official rank
C) Key informants are always appointed by the health department
D) Key informants must be lifelong residents of the community
Answer: B
Rationale: A key informant is chosen for knowledge of and connection to the community, not official rank; the classic distractor is assuming a formal title is required, but it doesn't have to be.

Q: In addition to sharing their own knowledge of the community, what else can key informants do?
A) Certify the community assessment for accuracy
B) Identify other key informants
C) Independently authorize a community diagnosis
D) Replace the need for a windshield survey
Answer: B
Rationale: Key informants know many community members and can identify other key informants — a snowball referral process.

Subtopic: primary vs. secondary data

Q: Which of the following are primary data collection methods for a community assessment? Select all that apply.
A) Windshield survey
B) Key informant interviews
C) Focus groups
D) Census data
E) Photovoice
Answer: A, B, C, E
Rationale: Primary data is collected directly by the assessment team — windshield survey, key informant interviews, focus groups, participant observation, and photovoice are all primary methods. Census data is secondary.
Trap: Census data is a common distractor planted among primary-method lists; it is pre-existing numeric data from a recognized agency, which makes it secondary.

Q: A student mistakenly labels the windshield survey as a secondary data source because it produces a written summary the team can file and reuse later. What is the error?
A) There is no error; reused documents are always secondary data
B) The windshield survey is primary data because the nurse collects it firsthand through direct observation
C) The windshield survey is neither primary nor secondary
D) The windshield survey only becomes primary data if photographs are taken
Answer: B
Rationale: Primary data is defined by who collects it and how — directly, by the nurse or assessment team — not by whether the output gets filed for later use. The windshield survey is a primary data method because it involves the nurse observing firsthand.

Q: Which of the following are examples of secondary data used in community assessment? Select all that apply.
A) US Census
B) Vital statistics
C) BRFSS (Behavioral Risk Factor Surveillance System)
D) Focus groups
E) CDC WONDER
Answer: A, B, C, E
Rationale: Secondary data is pre-existing numeric data from a recognized agency — the Census, vital statistics, morbidity/mortality reports, BRFSS, and CDC WONDER are all secondary sources.
Trap: Focus groups involve direct interaction with community members and are a primary data method, not secondary.

Q: A comprehensive community assessment is best built using which combination of data?
A) Primary data alone, since it reflects firsthand community knowledge
B) Secondary data alone, since it is already validated by recognized agencies
C) Both primary and secondary data
D) Neither; community diagnosis relies only on clinical judgment
Answer: C
Rationale: A comprehensive community assessment uses both primary and secondary data — firsthand methods like windshield surveys and key informant interviews, and existing numeric sources like census and vital statistics.

Subtopic: community as client and partner

Q: A PHN designs an assessment in which the community's collective health and common good are the direct recipient of nursing care. This reflects which model?
A) Community as partner
B) Community as client
C) Community diagnosis
D) Participant observation
Answer: B
Rationale: Community as client frames the community itself as the unit and recipient of nursing care, with a focus on the collective or common good.

Q: How does the "community as partner" model differ from "community as client"?
A) It removes the community from the assessment process entirely
B) It emphasizes the community's active collaboration in its own assessment and care, rather than being only the recipient of care
C) It applies only to secondary data collection
D) It replaces the nursing process with an epidemiologic model
Answer: B
Rationale: Community as partner is a model in which the community actively collaborates in its own assessment and care, contrasted with community as client, where the community is framed as the recipient of care.

Subtopic: community diagnosis and framework

Q: A community diagnosis, the nursing diagnosis written for a community rather than an individual, may be formatted using which of the following? Select all that apply.
A) NANDA
B) The Omaha system
C) The Federal Poverty Guidelines
D) BRFSS categories
Answer: A, B
Rationale: Community diagnosis may use the NANDA taxonomy or the Omaha system.

Q: Which three factors define a community according to the chapter?
A) Income, education, and access to care
B) People, place, and function
C) Stakeholders, gatekeepers, and key informants
D) Assessment, diagnosis, and evaluation
Answer: B
Rationale: A community is defined by people (residents), place (geographic or shared space), and function (the community's aims and activities).

Q: A focus group works best under which condition?
A) When the topic is highly sensitive, encouraging catharsis
B) When the topic is not sensitive and participants feel comfortable speaking openly
C) When participants are unfamiliar with one another
D) When the group is used only to verify secondary data
Answer: B
Rationale: Focus groups use open-ended questioning of a small group and work best when the topic is not sensitive and participants feel comfortable speaking.

Q: A nurse notes that a certain resident controls who can enter and use a community center, effectively controlling access to that part of the community. This person is best described as a:
A) Key informant
B) Stakeholder
C) Gatekeeper
D) Community diagnostician
Answer: C
Rationale: Gatekeepers are people who control access to the community or its members — distinct from stakeholders, who are anyone with a personal or occupational interest in the community's life, and key informants, who are chosen for their insight and connections.
Trap: Key informant and gatekeeper both describe influential community members, but only the gatekeeper controls access; a key informant is valued for knowledge and connection, not control.
