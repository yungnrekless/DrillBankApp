# Chapter 1 drills — Public Health Nursing and Population Health

Authored from `sources/Ch1_Fact_Bank.md` (Stanhope & Lancaster, 6th ed.,
printed pp. 1–19). Every item is anchored to that file; where the fact bank
flags a trap, the trap line says so explicitly.

Topics follow the chapter's own highest-yield material: the three core
functions and ten essential services, the three levels of prevention with
the book's PHN examples, population/aggregate/community concepts, the
community-oriented vs. community-based distinction, and the eight
principles of public health nursing.

---

Topic: core functions and essential services
Subtopic: definitions and mission

Q: According to the IOM (1988), how is public health defined?
A) The branch of medicine concerned with preventing disease in individual patients
B) What we, as a society, do collectively to assure the conditions in which people can be healthy
C) The organized delivery of primary care to underserved populations
D) A government program that provides health insurance to low-income populations
Answer: B
Rationale: The IOM's 1988 definition is "what we, as a society, do collectively to assure the conditions in which people can be healthy."
Trap: Option A describes clinical medicine's focus on the individual, which is precisely what public health is not — the unit of concern is the collective, not the single patient.

Q: How does the chapter define public health nursing?
A) Nursing practice delivered in outpatient clinics and community health centers
B) Population-focused, community-oriented practice that combines nursing and public health, with the population as the unit of care
C) A nursing specialty limited to immunization and communicable disease control
D) Nursing care delivered to individuals and families in home settings
Answer: B
Rationale: Public health nursing is population-focused, community-oriented practice that combines nursing and public health; the unit of care is the population.
Trap: Option D describes community-based nursing, the setting-specific practice that is deliberately contrasted with population-focused public health nursing later in the chapter.

Q: How is population health defined in the chapter?
A) The average health status of individuals within a single clinical practice
B) A set of analytical strategies and approaches, first used in public health, to describe, analyze, and mobilize efforts to improve health in community-based populations
C) The proportion of a population enrolled in a government insurance program
D) A synonym for the ten essential public health services
Answer: B
Rationale: Population health is a set of analytical strategies and approaches, first used in public health, to describe, analyze, and mobilize efforts to improve health in community-based populations, now also applied to clinical populations.

Subtopic: the three core functions

Q: The IOM (1988) identifies which three functions as the core functions of public health? Select all that apply.
A) Assessment
B) Policy development
C) Assurance
D) Direct reimbursement of hospital care
E) Accreditation of nursing schools
Answer: A, B, C
Rationale: The three core public health functions are assessment, policy development, and assurance. They apply at all levels of government and are the single most testable triad in the chapter.

Q: A health department systematically collects data on a community's health status and makes that information available to the public. This activity corresponds to which core function?
A) Assessment
B) Policy development
C) Assurance
D) Evaluation
Answer: A
Rationale: Assessment is systematically collecting data on the population, monitoring the population's health status, and making information about the community's health available.
Trap: "Evaluation" is one of the ten essential services, not one of the three core functions — the question is asking about the core function level, and data collection/monitoring maps to assessment.

Q: A local health official ensures that a competent public health and personal health care workforce is available, and arranges personal health services for people who would otherwise go without them. Which core function is this?
A) Assessment
B) Policy development
C) Assurance
D) Mobilization
Answer: C
Rationale: Assurance means ensuring essential community-oriented health services are available — which may include providing personal health services to those who would otherwise not receive them — and ensuring a competent public health and personal health care workforce.
Trap: "Mobilization" names essential service 4 (mobilize community partnerships), not a core function; the three core functions are only assessment, policy development, and assurance.

Q: Providing leadership in developing policies that support population health, and using the scientific knowledge base to make policy decisions, describes which core function?
A) Assessment
B) Policy development
C) Assurance
D) Enforcement
Answer: B
Rationale: Policy development is providing leadership in developing policies that support the health of the population, including using the scientific knowledge base in policy decisions.

Subtopic: the ten essential public health services

Q: Which of the ten essential public health services map onto the core function of assessment? Select all that apply.
A) Monitor health status to identify community health problems
B) Diagnose and investigate health problems and health hazards
C) Research for new insights and innovative solutions to health problems
D) Mobilize community partnerships to identify and solve health problems
E) Enforce laws and regulations that protect health and ensure safety
Answer: A, B, C
Rationale: Assessment maps to essential services 1 (monitor), 2 (diagnose and investigate), and 10 (research). Mobilizing partnerships (service 4) maps to policy development, and enforcing laws (service 6) maps to assurance.
Trap: Services 4 and 6 sound like they could belong to assessment because they involve identifying and solving problems, but the chapter's mapping places them under policy development and assurance respectively.

Q: Which of the ten essential public health services map onto the core function of assurance? Select all that apply.
A) Enforce laws and regulations that protect health and ensure safety
B) Link people to needed personal health services and assure care that is otherwise unavailable
C) Assure a competent public health and personal health care workforce
D) Evaluate effectiveness, accessibility, and quality of health services
E) Inform, educate, and empower people about health issues
F) Develop policies and plans that support individual and community health efforts
Answer: A, B, C, D
Rationale: Assurance maps to essential services 6 (enforce laws), 7 (link people to services), 8 (assure workforce), and 9 (evaluate). Informing/educating (service 3) and developing policies (service 5) map to policy development instead.

Q: Which are named among the Ten Great Public Health Achievements of the 20th Century (CDC, 1999)? Select all that apply.
A) Immunizations
B) Motor-vehicle safety
C) Drinking-water fluoridation
D) Eradication of tobacco use
E) Declines in death from heart disease and stroke
Answer: A, B, C, E
Rationale: The ten achievements include immunizations, motor-vehicle safety, workplace safety, control of infectious diseases, safer/healthier foods, healthier mothers and babies, family planning, drinking-water fluoridation, tobacco recognized as a health hazard, and declines in death from heart disease and stroke.
Trap: The chapter credits tobacco being recognized as a health hazard, not the eradication of tobacco use — tobacco use was never eliminated, only its risk was formally acknowledged.

Topic: levels of prevention
Subtopic: definitions and the book's PHN examples

Q: A PHN develops a health-education program for school-age children about the effects of smoking. This is the book's example of which level of prevention, and what effect does that level have?
A) Primary prevention; reduces incidence
B) Secondary prevention; reduces prevalence
C) Tertiary prevention; reduces disability
D) Primary prevention; reduces chronicity
Answer: A
Rationale: Primary prevention includes general and specific measures to promote health and prevent the development of disease, reducing incidence. The book's example is exactly this school smoking-education program.

Q: A PHN develops toxin screenings for migrant workers exposed to pesticides and refers those who screen positive for treatment. Which level of prevention is this, and why?
A) Primary prevention, because it targets a population before disease develops
B) Secondary prevention, because it involves early detection and treatment
C) Tertiary prevention, because it addresses an occupational hazard
D) Primary prevention, because screening prevents pesticide exposure
Answer: B
Rationale: Secondary prevention stops the progress of disease through early detection and treatment, reducing prevalence and chronicity. The migrant-worker toxin-screening example is the book's illustration of secondary prevention.
Trap: Screening happening across a whole population (migrant workers) makes this feel primary or population-level "prevention," but the level is set by what the nurse is doing — early detection — not by the setting. Screening is the classic secondary cue even at the population level.

Q: A PHN mobilizes a community coalition to develop a Health Maintenance and Promotion Center in a neighborhood with high chronic illness. Which level of prevention does this illustrate?
A) Primary prevention
B) Secondary prevention
C) Tertiary prevention
D) Cannot be classified without knowing individual patient status
Answer: C
Rationale: Tertiary prevention stops deterioration, relapse, disability, or dependency through anticipatory nursing and medical care (rehabilitation). The book's example is the PHN mobilizing a coalition to build a Health Maintenance and Promotion Center in a neighborhood with high chronic illness.

Q: Which statement correctly matches a level of prevention to the outcome it is meant to reduce?
A) Primary prevention reduces prevalence and chronicity
B) Secondary prevention reduces incidence
C) Primary prevention reduces incidence; secondary prevention reduces prevalence and chronicity
D) Tertiary prevention reduces incidence
Answer: C
Rationale: Primary prevention promotes health and prevents disease development, reducing incidence. Secondary prevention stops disease progress through early detection and treatment, reducing prevalence and chronicity.
Trap: Swapping "incidence" and "prevalence/chronicity" between primary and secondary is the easiest way this pairing gets inverted on an exam.

Q: A nursing student states, "Because the migrant-worker toxin-screening example takes place across an entire population, it must be primary prevention." What is the flaw in this reasoning?
A) There is no flaw; population-level interventions are always primary prevention
B) The level of prevention is determined by what the nurse is doing (early detection and treatment), not by whether the intervention occurs at the population level
C) Toxin screening is actually tertiary prevention because it involves referral for treatment
D) The example is secondary only because pesticide exposure is not a disease
Answer: B
Rationale: The fact bank explicitly warns that the migrant-worker screening example is secondary even though it happens in a population — the level is set by what the nurse is doing (early detection), not the setting.
Trap: This is the chapter's own flagged trap. Screening at the population level is still secondary prevention; only education, immunization, or promotion delivered before disease onset counts as primary.

Q: A PHN's activities are described as anticipatory nursing and medical care aimed at stopping deterioration, relapse, disability, or dependency, including rehabilitation. Which level of prevention is being described?
A) Primary prevention
B) Secondary prevention
C) Tertiary prevention
D) Quaternary prevention
Answer: C
Rationale: Tertiary prevention is defined as stopping deterioration, relapse, disability, or dependency through anticipatory nursing and medical care (rehabilitation).
Trap: "Quaternary prevention" is not one of the three levels named in this chapter; the chapter uses only primary, secondary, and tertiary.

Topic: population health and community
Subtopic: population, aggregate, and subpopulation

Q: How is a population defined in the chapter?
A) A group of individuals enrolled in the same health insurance plan
B) A collection of individuals who share one or more personal or environmental characteristics
C) The total number of patients served by a single public health department
D) A subgroup identified by a single risk factor for disease
Answer: B
Rationale: A population is a collection of individuals who share one or more personal or environmental characteristics.

Q: How does the chapter define an aggregate, and how does it relate to subpopulations?
A) An aggregate is a subgroup or subpopulation that has some common characteristic; a whole community is made up of multiple, often overlapping subpopulations
B) An aggregate is the sum total of all subpopulations in a community with no overlap between them
C) An aggregate is a synonym for the entire population of a nation
D) An aggregate refers only to individuals receiving direct clinical care
Answer: A
Rationale: An aggregate is a subgroup or subpopulation with some common characteristic, and subpopulations are groups within a population; a whole community is made up of multiple, often overlapping subpopulations.
Trap: Presenting subpopulations as non-overlapping is the reversal to watch for — the chapter's own example is adolescents at risk for unplanned pregnancy overlapping with females 15–24.

Q: What does population-focused practice mean as the chapter uses the term?
A) Practice concerned with the health of whole populations and subpopulations rather than individual patients
B) Practice delivered exclusively in community settings rather than hospitals
C) Practice that prioritizes the individual patient's preferences within a population context
D) Practice limited to primary prevention activities
Answer: A
Rationale: Population-focused practice is concerned with the health of whole populations and subpopulations rather than individual patients.

Subtopic: population health gains and social determinants

Q: Between 1900 and 2017, average US life span rose from approximately what figure to what figure, and to what is this increase primarily attributed?
A) 47 to 78.6 years, driven mainly by advances in medical care
B) 47 to 78.6 years, driven mainly by public health efforts such as sanitation, safe water, and food safety
C) 60 to 78.6 years, driven mainly by childhood immunization alone
D) 47 to 65 years, driven mainly by improved surgical techniques
Answer: B
Rationale: Average US life span rose from 47 (1900) to 78.6 (2017), roughly a 60% increase, driven by public health efforts — sanitation, clean water, safer workplaces, food/drug safety, childhood immunization, and better nutrition/hygiene/housing — not medical care.
Trap: Attributing the gain to medical care is the reversal the chapter is explicit about: historically the largest gains in population health came from public health efforts, not medical care.

Q: According to the IOM's "shorter lives, poorer health" report, the US health disadvantage was attributed to which factors? Select all that apply.
A) Inadequate health care
B) Unhealthy behaviors
C) Adverse economic and social conditions
D) Environmental factors
E) A shortage of primary care physicians
Answer: A, B, C, D
Rationale: The IOM report attributed the US health disadvantage to inadequate health care, unhealthy behaviors, adverse economic and social conditions, environmental factors, and public policies/social values.
Trap: A physician shortage is a plausible-sounding health-policy concern but is not one of the factors this report names; the named factors are broader social determinants, not workforce supply.

Topic: community-oriented vs. community-based nursing
Subtopic: the confusable pair

Q: Which statement correctly describes the unit of focus and goal of community-oriented nursing practice?
A) Individuals and families where they live, work, and attend school; acute and chronic care
B) The community/population; preventing disease and disability and creating conditions for health via community diagnosis, surveillance, and monitoring
C) Individual patients enrolled in a managed care plan; comprehensive coordinated services
D) The community/population; providing acute and chronic care in community settings
Answer: B
Rationale: Community-oriented nursing practice focuses on the community/population, with the goal of preventing disease/disability, promoting and protecting health, and creating conditions for health through community diagnosis, surveillance, and monitoring.
Trap: Option D mixes the correct unit of focus (community/population) with the wrong goal (acute/chronic care), which belongs to community-based practice — the two columns of the book's table must not be crossed.

Q: A student nurse says, "Community-based and community-oriented nursing both mean the same thing — nursing that happens somewhere in the community." How should this be corrected?
A) The terms are in fact interchangeable and the distinction is not meaningful
B) "Based" refers to a setting where individual/family care happens; "oriented" refers to the whole population as the unit of care
C) "Oriented" refers to a setting where individual/family care happens; "based" refers to the whole population as the unit of care
D) "Based" refers to acute care only, while "oriented" refers to chronic care only
Answer: B
Rationale: The chapter's tested distinction is that "based" is about where — a setting in the community where individual/family care happens — while "oriented" is about whom — the population as the unit of care.
Trap: Option C is the exact inversion of the correct mapping and is the version most likely to appear as a tempting distractor.

Q: Which features describe community-based nursing practice, as distinct from community-oriented practice? Select all that apply.
A) Setting-specific practice for patients and families where they live, work, and attend school
B) Emphasis on acute and chronic care and on comprehensive, coordinated, continuous services
C) The unit of care is the whole community or population
D) Delivered by generalists or specialists such as maternal/infant, pediatric, adult, or psych/mental health nurses
E) Goal is community diagnosis, surveillance, and monitoring
Answer: A, B, D
Rationale: Community-based nursing is setting-specific practice for patients and families where they live, work, and attend school, emphasizing acute and chronic care and comprehensive, coordinated, continuous services, delivered by generalist or specialist nurses.
Trap: Options C and E describe community-oriented practice (population as unit of care; community diagnosis/surveillance/monitoring as the goal) and belong to the other column of the table.

Topic: eight principles of public health nursing
Subtopic: the tested principles

Q: According to the eight principles of public health nursing, what is the unit of care?
A) The individual patient
B) The family as a unit
C) The population
D) The aggregate at highest clinical risk
Answer: C
Rationale: The first principle states that the unit of care is the population, not the individual.

Q: The eight principles describe the PHN's primary obligation using which framing?
A) To maximize revenue for the health department
B) To achieve the greatest good for the greatest number
C) To prioritize the sickest individuals first
D) To defer entirely to physician-directed care plans
Answer: B
Rationale: The primary obligation, per the eight principles, is to achieve the greatest good for the greatest number — a utilitarian framing.

Q: A nursing instructor asks, "What is the priority intervention for a public health nurse planning activities for a community?" Absent a scenario describing an existing disease, what does the chapter say the default answer should be?
A) Tertiary prevention, because it addresses the most severe cases
B) Secondary prevention, because early detection has the highest yield
C) Primary prevention, because selecting activities should prioritize it
D) Whichever level the community requests through its representatives
Answer: C
Rationale: One of the eight principles states that primary prevention is the priority in selecting activities. The chapter notes that if a stem asks for the priority intervention, the answer defaults to primary prevention unless the scenario clearly describes an existing disease.
Trap: Secondary prevention (early detection) sounds efficient and evidence-driven, but the eight principles explicitly rank primary prevention as the priority absent a scenario indicating disease is already present.

Q: Which of the following are among the eight principles of public health nursing? Select all that apply.
A) The primary obligation is to achieve the greatest good for the greatest number
B) PHNs work with the population as an equal partner
C) There is an obligation to actively reach out to all who might benefit
D) Collaboration with a variety of professions, organizations, and entities
E) Services should be limited to those who actively seek out the health department
Answer: A, B, C, D
Rationale: The eight principles include the greatest-good obligation, working with the population as an equal partner, an obligation to actively reach out to all who might benefit, and collaboration with a variety of professions, organizations, and entities.
Trap: Limiting services to those who seek them out directly contradicts principle 6 — the obligation to actively reach out to all who might benefit, not wait for people to come forward.
