Topic: incidence and prevalence
Subtopic: basic concepts — risk, proportion, rate

Q: Why do epidemiologists prefer rates and proportions over raw case counts when describing disease frequency?
A) Rates and proportions are easier to calculate from surveillance data
B) They account for the size of the population at risk, so 50 cases means something different in a population of 250 versus 250,000
C) Raw counts are not permitted in peer-reviewed publications
D) Proportions eliminate the need to track time
Answer: B
Rationale: Rates and proportions take the size of the population at risk into account. Fifty cases of influenza might be a serious epidemic in a population of 250 but a low rate in a population of 250,000. A count alone tells you nothing without the denominator and the time period.

Q: What distinguishes a rate from a proportion?
A) A rate's denominator includes the numerator; a proportion's does not
B) A proportion's denominator depends on both population size and time; a rate's does not
C) A rate's denominator is a function of both population size and time, unlike a proportion
D) There is no meaningful difference; the terms are interchangeable
Answer: C
Rationale: A proportion is a ratio in which the denominator includes the numerator, ranging from 0 to 1. A rate is a ratio that is not a proportion, because its denominator depends on both population size and time.
Trap: The proportion definition (numerator inside the denominator) is often flipped with the rate definition (denominator = population size x time) — the two definitions get swapped in distractors.

Subtopic: incidence vs. prevalence

Q: A community health department wants to know how many new cases of tuberculosis are occurring so it can evaluate a prevention program. Which measure should it use?
A) Prevalence proportion
B) Incidence
C) Case fatality rate
D) Proportionate mortality ratio
Answer: B
Rationale: Incidence is the measure of choice for studying etiology and risk factors and for evaluating prevention programs, because it is affected only by the development of new disease, not by survival or duration.

Q: A health planner needs to estimate how much existing disease is in the community in order to size services. Which measure is most appropriate?
A) Incidence rate
B) Incidence proportion
C) Prevalence proportion
D) Attack rate
Answer: C
Rationale: Prevalence measures existing disease in the population at a particular time and is best used for planning services, since it reflects the size of the population currently needing care.

Q: According to the formula relating incidence and prevalence, when prevalence is small (P < 0.1), which expression is correct?
A) I = P x D
B) P = I x D
C) D = P x I
D) P = I / D
Answer: B
Rationale: When prevalence is small, P = I x D — prevalence equals the incidence rate multiplied by the average duration of disease.

Q: Breast cancer and lung cancer in women have similar incidence, but breast cancer has substantially higher prevalence. What explains this?
A) Breast cancer is diagnosed earlier, inflating incidence
B) Lung cancer has a much longer average duration of illness
C) Breast cancer has a much longer average duration because of higher survival, so more existing cases accumulate at any given time
D) Prevalence is unrelated to survival duration
Answer: C
Rationale: With five-year survival around 85% for breast cancer versus roughly 15% for lung cancer in women, breast cancer patients live with the disease far longer. Since prevalence = incidence x duration, the longer duration produces a higher prevalence even at similar incidence.
Trap: A stem that attributes the prevalence difference to incidence rather than duration is testing whether you remember that duration (survival/recovery), not new-case risk, is what separates the two cancers here.

Topic: mortality rates
Subtopic: denominators (Table 10.2)

Q: What is the denominator of the crude mortality rate?
A) Total deaths in the period
B) Midyear total population
C) Number of persons diagnosed with the disease
D) Live births in the same year
Answer: B
Rationale: The crude mortality rate uses deaths from any cause in the period as the numerator and the midyear total population as the denominator.

Q: What distinguishes the case fatality rate (CFR) from the cause-specific mortality rate?
A) They are identical measures with different names
B) The CFR's denominator is the number of persons diagnosed with the disease; the cause-specific rate's denominator is the total population at risk
C) The CFR's denominator is total deaths, while the cause-specific rate's denominator is the diagnosed population
D) The CFR is calculated per 100,000 and the cause-specific rate is calculated per 1,000
Answer: B
Rationale: CFR = deaths from a specific disease / number of persons diagnosed with that disease. Cause-specific mortality rate = deaths from a specific cause / total population at risk. The denominators are what separate them.

Q: Why is the proportionate mortality ratio (PMR) NOT considered a true rate?
A) Its numerator counts only deaths under age 65
B) Its denominator is total deaths in the period, not the population at risk, so it does not estimate the risk of death
C) It can only be calculated retrospectively
D) It excludes infectious disease deaths
Answer: B
Rationale: PMR's denominator is total deaths, not the population at risk. Because of this, it does not estimate risk and its size can shift purely because deaths from other causes rise or fall, even when the cause of interest stays constant.
Trap: This is the classic "not a rate" trap. Any answer option calling the PMR a risk estimate or a true rate is the intended distractor.

Q: A researcher computes a CFR of 86% for a disease. What is the survival rate?
A) 86%
B) 14%
C) Cannot be determined without additional data
D) 100%
Answer: B
Rationale: Survival rate = 1 minus CFR. An 86% CFR yields a 14% survival rate.
Trap: The fact bank notes the textbook itself is internally inconsistent (one place uses 87%/13%, another 86%/14%). Don't memorize a specific number as "the" figure for any disease — just apply the rule survival = 1 - CFR to whatever CFR the stem gives you.

Q: What are the correct numerator and denominator for the infant mortality rate?
A) Infant deaths under 28 days / live births in the same year
B) Infant deaths before 1 year of age / live births in the same year
C) Infant deaths before 1 year of age / total midyear population
D) Infant deaths 28 days to 1 year / total births including stillbirths
Answer: B
Rationale: Infant mortality rate = infant deaths before 1 year of age / live births in the same year.

Q: Why is comparing crude mortality rates across two populations potentially misleading?
A) Crude rates cannot be expressed per 100,000
B) Crude rates are affected by the population's age distribution, since older populations have higher mortality; this is corrected by age adjustment
C) Crude rates only apply to infectious diseases
D) Crude rates require CFR data that is often unavailable
Answer: B
Rationale: The crude mortality rate is affected by the population's age distribution — an older population will show more deaths regardless of underlying health. Rate/age adjustment corrects for this by adjusting to a standard population so comparisons are fair.

Topic: causal models
Subtopic: triangle, web, ecological model

Q: The epidemiologic triangle consists of which three elements?
A) Person, place, time
B) Agent, host, environment
C) Sensitivity, specificity, prevalence
D) Primary, secondary, tertiary prevention
Answer: B
Rationale: The epidemiologic triangle is the agent-host-environment model: disease results from complex relationships among causal agents, susceptible persons, and environmental factors, and a change in any one element changes risk.

Q: A nurse is investigating a single-source outbreak of a specific bacterial pathogen. Which causal model best fits this scenario, and why?
A) The web of causality, because bacterial disease is always multifactorial
B) The epidemiologic triangle, because it fits a simple single-agent infectious model
C) The ecological model, because it addresses policy-level determinants
D) None of these models apply to infectious disease
Answer: B
Rationale: The epidemiologic triangle (agent-host-environment) fits a simple single-agent infectious disease model. The web of causality is the better fit for multifactorial chronic disease where many variables interact.

Q: A nurse is studying the multiple interacting contributors to type 2 diabetes (diet, genetics, socioeconomic status, access to care, activity level). Which model best fits this situation?
A) The epidemiologic triangle, because diabetes has a single identifiable agent
B) The web of causality, which recognizes complex interrelationships among many factors increasing or decreasing risk
C) Point epidemic analysis
D) The case-control design
Answer: B
Rationale: The web of causality recognizes the complex interrelationships of many factors interacting, sometimes subtly, to raise or lower disease risk — a better fit than the triangle when many variables interact, as in multifactorial chronic disease.

Q: How does the ecological model extend beyond the epidemiologic triangle and web of causality?
A) It applies only to infectious disease outbreaks
B) It treats multiple determinants of health as interrelated and synergistic across biologic, behavioral, social, and environmental levels, including policy, culture, and economics, with a life-span perspective
C) It replaces the need for agent, host, and environment entirely
D) It is used only for calculating incidence rates
Answer: B
Rationale: The ecological model (IOM, 2002) treats the multiple determinants of health as interrelated and acting synergistically or antagonistically rather than as discrete factors, spanning biologic, mental, behavioral, social, and environmental levels including policy, culture, and economics — captured by the phrase "healthy people in healthy communities."

Topic: levels of prevention
Subtopic: definitions and the cardiovascular example

Q: A patient has no discernible pathological process but is susceptible to disease. An intervention aimed at this patient to promote health and prevent disease onset is an example of which level of prevention?
A) Primary
B) Secondary
C) Tertiary
D) Quaternary
Answer: A
Rationale: Primary prevention promotes health and prevents the occurrence of disease, injury, or disability in people who are susceptible but have no discernible pathological process (prepathogenesis). Examples include immunizations, seat-belt teaching, folic acid at preconception, and water fluoridation.

Q: Screening is described as the core intervention of which level of prevention?
A) Primary
B) Secondary
C) Tertiary
D) Screening is not classified under any level
Answer: B
Rationale: Secondary prevention is designed to increase the probability that a person with disease will have it diagnosed early enough that treatment is likely to result in a cure, and screening is at the core of secondary prevention.

Q: A nurse provides cardiac rehabilitation, medication management, and coordinates surgery for a patient recovering from a myocardial infarction. This is an example of which level of prevention?
A) Primary
B) Secondary
C) Tertiary
D) Screening
Answer: C
Rationale: In the chapter's cardiovascular disease example, tertiary prevention is cardiac rehabilitation, medication, and surgery — interventions aimed at limiting disability and enhancing rehabilitation.

Q: In the chapter's cardiovascular disease example, discussing a low-fat diet and regular physical exercise with a patient represents which level of prevention, and implementing blood pressure and cholesterol screening with a treadmill stress test represents which level?
A) Both are primary prevention
B) Diet/exercise discussion is primary; BP/cholesterol screening and stress testing is secondary
C) Diet/exercise discussion is secondary; screening is primary
D) Both are tertiary prevention
Answer: B
Rationale: The cardiovascular disease example splits the levels cleanly: primary is discussing low-fat diet and exercise; secondary is blood pressure/cholesterol screening and treadmill stress testing; tertiary is cardiac rehabilitation, medication, and surgery.

Topic: screening, validity, and reliability
Subtopic: sensitivity and specificity

Q: A screening test correctly identifies 92% of people who truly have the disease as positive. What is this property called?
A) Specificity
B) Sensitivity
C) Positive predictive value
D) Reliability
Answer: B
Rationale: Sensitivity is the proportion of persons with the disease whom the test correctly identifies as positive (true positives).

Q: A test correctly identifies people who do NOT have the disease as negative. What is this property called, and it corresponds to which type of result?
A) Sensitivity; true positives
B) Specificity; true negatives
C) Sensitivity; true negatives
D) Predictive value; false negatives
Answer: B
Rationale: Specificity is the proportion of persons without the disease whom the test correctly identifies as negative (true negatives).
Trap: Swapping sensitivity and specificity, or pairing sensitivity with true negatives, is the most common inversion tested on this pair.

Q: A nurse is choosing a screening test for a disease where missing a case would be dangerous because early treatment substantially improves outcomes. Which test characteristic should be prioritized?
A) High specificity, to minimize false positives
B) High sensitivity, to minimize false negatives and catch as many true cases as possible
C) High positive predictive value regardless of sensitivity
D) High reliability regardless of validity
Answer: B
Rationale: High sensitivity should be prioritized when early treatment matters and missing a case is dangerous, because a sensitive test minimizes false negatives.

Q: For a screening program where rescreening is impractical and it is important to reduce false-positive results, which test characteristic should be prioritized?
A) Sensitivity
B) Specificity
C) Negative predictive value only
D) Attack rate
Answer: B
Rationale: Specificity should be prioritized when rescreening is impractical and reducing false positives is important.

Subtopic: predictive values

Q: What does positive predictive value (PPV) tell you?
A) The proportion of people with a negative test who are actually disease-free
B) The proportion of people with a positive test who actually have the disease
C) The proportion of people with the disease who test positive
D) The proportion of the population that is disease-free
Answer: B
Rationale: PPV is the proportion of persons with a positive test who actually have the disease.

Q: What does negative predictive value (NPV) tell you, and what does it depend on besides sensitivity and specificity?
A) The proportion of persons with a negative test who are actually disease-free; predictive values also depend on disease prevalence
B) The proportion of persons with a positive test who have the disease; predictive values are independent of prevalence
C) The same thing as specificity
D) The same thing as the case fatality rate
Answer: A
Rationale: NPV is the proportion of persons with a negative test who are actually disease-free. Predictive values depend on sensitivity and specificity and also on the prevalence of disease in the population being tested.

Subtopic: series vs. parallel testing

Q: In series (sequential) testing, a person is classified as positive only if all tests are positive. What is the effect on sensitivity and specificity?
A) Increases sensitivity, decreases specificity
B) Increases specificity, decreases sensitivity, producing fewer false positives
C) Increases both sensitivity and specificity equally
D) Has no effect on either measure
Answer: B
Rationale: Series testing requires all tests to be positive to call the result positive, which enhances specificity and reduces false positives, at the cost of lower sensitivity.

Q: In parallel (simultaneous) testing, a person is classified as positive if any one test is positive. What is the effect, and what is the classic example?
A) Increases specificity; example is confirmatory Western blot alone
B) Increases sensitivity and reduces false negatives, at the cost of lower specificity; example is a blood bank considering a sample positive on either ELISA or Western blot
C) Has no effect on sensitivity or specificity
D) Increases both sensitivity and specificity
Answer: B
Rationale: Parallel testing calls a result positive if any test is positive, which enhances sensitivity (fewer false negatives) but lowers specificity. The blood bank example treats a sample as positive if it is positive on either ELISA or Western blot.

Q: In a series testing protocol for HIV using ELISA followed by Western blot, which test should be performed first, and why?
A) The more specific test first, to rule out disease quickly
B) The more sensitive test first, to catch all possible cases, followed by a more specific test to eliminate false positives
C) It does not matter which test is performed first
D) Both tests must be run simultaneously, not sequentially
Answer: B
Rationale: In series testing, the sequence matters: a very sensitive test is used first to catch all cases, and then a very specific test is applied to eliminate false positives. A person is called positive only if both are positive.
Trap: Reversing the order (specific test first) is the classic distractor. The sensitive test always goes first in a series protocol.

Subtopic: validity and reliability

Q: A blood pressure cuff consistently reads 10 mm Hg higher than the true value on every patient. How should this be characterized?
A) Reliable and valid
B) Unreliable and invalid
C) Reliable but not valid — consistent, but wrong
D) Valid but not reliable
Answer: C
Rationale: Reliability is the consistency or repeatability of a measure; validity is whether the measure is truly measuring what it claims to measure. A cuff that reads consistently 10 mm Hg high is reliable (consistent) but not valid (inaccurate) — a measure can be consistent without being valid.

Topic: study designs
Subtopic: which design for which purpose

Q: A researcher wants to study a rare disease efficiently by comparing exposure histories of people who have the disease against those who do not. Which design is most appropriate?
A) Prospective cohort study
B) Case-control study
C) Cross-sectional study
D) Ecological study
Answer: B
Rationale: Case-control studies enroll subjects based on outcome status (cases vs. controls) and are the best design for rare diseases because they do not require following a large population forward to accumulate enough new cases.

Q: Which measure of association is produced by a case-control study, and why can it not directly calculate incidence or prevalence?
A) Relative risk; because subjects are chosen by exposure status
B) Odds ratio; because subjects are chosen by outcome (disease) status, not by exposure or by sampling the whole population
C) Attack rate; because it follows an outbreak population
D) Case fatality rate; because it tracks disease progression
Answer: B
Rationale: In a case-control study, subjects are enrolled because they are known to have the outcome (cases) or not (controls), so neither incidence nor prevalence can be calculated directly. The design uses the odds ratio instead.

Q: Which study design provides the best estimate of incidence and the clearest temporal sequence between exposure and outcome?
A) Cross-sectional study
B) Case-control study
C) Prospective cohort study
D) Ecological study
Answer: C
Rationale: A prospective cohort study follows a group sharing a characteristic of interest forward over time to observe new outcomes developing, giving the best estimate of incidence and risk and the clearest temporal sequence — though it is expensive, slow, and poorly suited to rare diseases.

Q: A cohort study finds that smokers develop lung cancer at a rate of 20 per 1,000 person-years, compared to 2 per 1,000 person-years among nonsmokers. What measure of association does this represent, and how is it calculated?
A) Odds ratio; ratio of odds of exposure between cases and controls
B) Relative risk; the ratio of cumulative incidence rates in the exposed versus unexposed groups
C) Proportionate mortality ratio; deaths from lung cancer divided by total deaths
D) Case fatality rate
Answer: B
Rationale: A cohort study allows calculation of incidence, and relative risk (risk ratio) is the ratio of cumulative incidence rates in exposed versus unexposed groups. Only a cohort design, which follows people forward, can directly estimate incidence and therefore relative risk.

Q: A researcher collects exposure and outcome data simultaneously in a single population survey. Which study design is this, what measure does it yield, and what is its major limitation?
A) Cohort study; yields incidence; limited by attrition
B) Cross-sectional study; yields prevalence, not incidence; temporal sequence between exposure and outcome is unclear
C) Case-control study; yields the odds ratio; limited by recall bias only
D) Ecological study; yields incidence; limited by the ecological fallacy
Answer: B
Rationale: A cross-sectional study collects exposure and outcome data simultaneously ("correlational"). It gives prevalence, not incidence, allows no calculation of risk, and the temporal sequence between exposure and outcome is unclear.

Q: A study uses group-level (aggregate) data, such as comparing national dietary fat consumption to national breast cancer rates, without individual-level data. Which design is this, and what is its key weakness?
A) Cohort study; weakness is attrition
B) Case-control study; weakness is recall bias
C) Ecological study; weakness is the ecological fallacy, where group-level associations may not hold true for individuals
D) Cross-sectional study; weakness is selective-survival bias
Answer: C
Rationale: An ecological study uses group-level (aggregate) data and serves as a bridge between descriptive and analytic epidemiology; it is quick, easy, and inexpensive as a first study, but risks the ecological fallacy — associations seen at the group level may not hold true for individuals.

Q: In interpreting a relative risk (RR) or odds ratio (OR), what does a value less than 1 indicate?
A) Increased risk associated with exposure
B) No association between exposure and outcome
C) A protective association between exposure and outcome
D) The study design was flawed
Answer: C
Rationale: RR or OR = 1 indicates no association, > 1 indicates increased risk, and < 1 indicates a protective association. The physical-activity/CHD example in the fact bank gives an RR of 0.4, which is protective.

Topic: endemic, outbreak, epidemic, pandemic
Subtopic: the escalation ladder

Q: What is the defining feature of an endemic condition?
A) It occurs at a steady, expected rate in the population, such as malaria in parts of Africa
B) It always requires a case count exceeding a fixed numeric threshold
C) It has spread globally
D) It refers only to conditions with no vaccine available
Answer: A
Rationale: Endemic describes a health condition occurring at a steady, expected rate among the population, with malaria in parts of Africa given as the example.

Q: What distinguishes an outbreak from an epidemic?
A) An outbreak occurs in excess of the endemic level; an epidemic exceeds the endemic level AND spreads over a larger geographic area
B) An outbreak spreads globally; an epidemic is confined to one region
C) There is no meaningful distinction between the two terms
D) An outbreak requires laboratory confirmation; an epidemic does not
Answer: A
Rationale: An outbreak is a condition occurring in excess of the endemic level (example: 2015 measles among unvaccinated theme-park visitors). An epidemic exceeds the endemic level and also spreads over a larger geographic area (example: 2014-2016 Ebola in West Africa).

Q: Is there a fixed numeric threshold of case counts that defines an epidemic?
A) Yes, an epidemic requires at least 100 cases in a population
B) Yes, an epidemic requires the incidence rate to double the endemic rate
C) No — there is no specific threshold; it is relative to the usual (endemic) level, so a single case of an eradicated disease like smallpox would count as an epidemic
D) Yes, but only for infectious, not chronic, disease
Answer: C
Rationale: No specific threshold of incidence defines an epidemic — it is relative to the usual endemic level. Because smallpox has been eradicated, even a single case would constitute an epidemic.
Trap: Any answer option implying a fixed case count or percentage defines an epidemic is incorrect by the book's own explicit statement.

Q: What is a pandemic, and which of the following is given as an example?
A) A localized outbreak with high mortality; example is Ebola in West Africa
B) An epidemic that spreads globally; examples include COVID-19, the 1918 Spanish influenza, and 2003 SARS
C) Any disease with a case fatality rate above 50%
D) A condition that occurs at a steady, expected rate
Answer: B
Rationale: A pandemic is an epidemic that spreads globally. Examples given include COVID-19, the 1918 Spanish influenza (which infected more than one-third of the world), and 2003 SARS.

Topic: causality
Subtopic: bias, confounding, and the seven guidelines

Q: A study finds an association between alcohol use and low birth weight, but the association disappears after controlling for smoking. What best explains this?
A) Selection bias
B) Confounding — smoking is related to both the exposure (alcohol) and the outcome (low birth weight), and accounts for the apparent association
C) Misclassification bias
D) The ecological fallacy
Answer: B
Rationale: Confounding is bias resulting from the relationship between the outcome and study factor and some third factor not accounted for. The classic example given is smoking confounding an alcohol/low-birth-weight association.

Q: Which three general categories of bias can produce a statistical association that is not truly causal?
A) Selection bias, misclassification/information bias, and confounding
B) Recall bias, publication bias, and the ecological fallacy
C) Sensitivity, specificity, and predictive value
D) Sampling bias, response bias, and attrition bias
Answer: A
Rationale: A statistical association can arise from chance, bias, or confounding. The three general categories of bias named are selection bias (how subjects enter the study), misclassification/information bias (how data are collected or classified), and confounding (a third factor tied to both exposure and outcome).

Q: According to Box 10.3's seven guidelines for causal inference, why is a statistical association alone considered insufficient to establish causation?
A) Because association is unnecessary for causation
B) Because association is necessary but not sufficient — it must be evaluated against additional guidelines such as strength, consistency, biologic plausibility, temporal sequence, dose-response, specificity, and experimental evidence
C) Because only randomized trials can ever demonstrate an association
D) Because associations are always due to confounding
Answer: B
Rationale: A statistical association is necessary but not adequate proof of causation. Causal inference is evaluated against the seven guidelines in Box 10.3: strength of association, consistency of findings, biologic plausibility, correct temporal sequence, dose-response relationship, specificity of the association, and experimental evidence.

Q: Which of the seven guidelines for causal inference is described as the strongest form of epidemiologic evidence, though often infeasible or unethical to obtain?
A) Biologic plausibility
B) Specificity of the association
C) Experimental evidence
D) Consistency of findings
Answer: C
Rationale: Experimental evidence is named as the strongest epidemiologic evidence among the seven guidelines, but it is often infeasible or unethical to obtain, for example when it would require deliberately exposing people to a suspected harmful agent.

Q: A risk factor must precede the disease outcome in time for a causal relationship to be considered valid. Which of the seven guidelines does this describe?
A) Dose-response relationship
B) Correct temporal sequence
C) Specificity of the association
D) Strength of association
Answer: B
Rationale: Correct temporal sequence requires that the risk factor precede the outcome — one of the seven guidelines for causal inference in Box 10.3.

Q: A study shows that as exposure to a chemical increases, the risk of a given cancer also increases proportionally. Which causal inference guideline does this illustrate?
A) Specificity of the association
B) Dose-response relationship — a graded exposure-risk relationship that supports causation
C) Consistency of findings
D) Biologic plausibility
Answer: B
Rationale: A dose-response relationship — more exposure leading to more risk — is one of the seven causal inference guidelines and supports causation.
