Topic: disaster classification
Subtopic: natural vs. human-made disasters (Box 16.1)

Q: According to Box 16.1, which of the following are classified as NATURAL disasters? Select all that apply.
A) Communicable disease epidemics
B) Lightning-induced forest fires
C) Critical infrastructure failures
D) Cyberattacks
E) Tsunamis
Answer: A, B, E
Rationale: Box 16.1 lists communicable disease epidemics, lightning-induced forest fires, and tsunamis as natural disasters. Critical infrastructure failures and cyberattacks are human-made.
Trap: Communicable disease epidemics and lightning-induced forest fires are the chapter's two named "easy inversions" — both feel human-caused but are classified as natural.

Q: According to Box 16.1, which of the following are classified as HUMAN-MADE disasters? Select all that apply.
A) Fires (generally)
B) Critical infrastructure failures
C) Lightning-induced forest fires
D) Civil unrest (riots)
E) Communicable disease epidemics
Answer: A, B, D
Rationale: Box 16.1 classifies fires generally, critical infrastructure failures, and civil unrest as human-made. Lightning-induced forest fires and communicable disease epidemics are natural.
Trap: This is the mirror image of the natural-disasters item. Fires and critical infrastructure failures are the pair most often miscategorized as natural because they sound like accidents rather than deliberate acts.

Q: A wildfire ignited by a lightning strike during a dry-season thunderstorm is best classified as which type of disaster under Box 16.1?
A) Human-made, because fire is inherently a human-made hazard
B) Natural, because the ignition source was a natural weather event
C) A complex humanitarian emergency
D) It cannot be classified without knowing property damage
Answer: B
Rationale: Box 16.1 specifically lists lightning-induced forest fires as a natural disaster, distinguishing them from fires generally, which are classified as human-made.

Q: What was the "incident triad" in the March 11, 2011, disaster in northeastern Japan?
A) Earthquake, tsunami, and a nuclear reactor crisis
B) Earthquake, hurricane, and civil unrest
C) Tsunami, flooding, and a chemical plant explosion
D) Earthquake, tsunami, and a pandemic
Answer: A
Rationale: The 9.0 magnitude earthquake and the tsunami that followed were the two natural components; the human-made component that completed the incident triad was the Fukushima nuclear reactor crisis, later found to result from a mix of human-made factors.

Q: Which statement best reflects the chapter's key framing sentence about natural and human-made factors in disasters?
A) Natural events and human-made factors are unrelated causes of separate disasters
B) Natural events often trigger disasters, and predictable, preventable human-made factors can increase the disaster's effects
C) Human-made factors are the primary trigger for most disasters classified as natural
D) Natural disasters cannot be worsened by human action once they begin
Answer: B
Rationale: The chapter states, "Although natural events such as earthquakes or hurricanes often trigger disasters, predictable and preventable human-made factors can increase the effects of the disaster."

Q: Per the chapter's definition, what distinguishes an incident that a community absorbs on its own from one that qualifies as a disaster?
A) The number of fatalities
B) Whether the incident was natural or human-made
C) Whether the incident requires external assistance
D) Whether local media cover the event
Answer: C
Rationale: A disaster is defined as any natural or human-made incident causing disruption, destruction, or devastation that requires external assistance. An event a community can absorb with its own resources is not a disaster by this definition.
Trap: Severity alone does not define a disaster in this chapter — the discriminator is the need for outside help.

Q: Which statement accurately reflects the chapter's discussion of the trend in worldwide disasters and their toll?
A) Both the number of disasters and the number of lives lost have increased
B) The number of disasters has grown, but the number of lives lost has decreased
C) The number of disasters has decreased, while lives lost has increased
D) Both the number of disasters and lives lost have declined
Answer: B
Rationale: "Although the number of disasters worldwide continues to grow, the number of lives lost has decreased," likely due to better disaster forecasting and early warning systems.
Trap: An option stating that both figures are rising together is the classic wrong pairing here.

Q: How does the chapter reconcile the statement that disaster costs are higher in developed countries with the statement that developing countries bear a disproportionate burden?
A) The two statements are contradictory, and only one can be correct
B) Dollar costs are higher in developed countries due to labor, materials, and infrastructure costs, while developing countries suffer disproportionate burden due to poverty and limited resources
C) Developed countries have higher costs and higher burden equally
D) Developing countries have higher costs due to less resilient infrastructure
Answer: B
Rationale: Costs are higher in more developed countries because of the costs of labor, materials, and complex infrastructure. Developing countries experience a disproportionate burden because they are poor and have limited resources to rebound.

Topic: disaster management cycle
Subtopic: the four-stage cycle

Q: How many stages does this textbook's disaster management cycle include, and what are they?
A) Five stages: prevention, mitigation, preparedness, response, and recovery
B) Four stages: prevention (including mitigation and protection), preparedness, response, and recovery
C) Three stages: preparedness, response, and recovery
D) Four stages: mitigation, protection, response, and reconstruction
Answer: B
Rationale: "Disaster management includes four stages: prevention (including mitigation and protection), preparedness, response, and recovery." Mitigation and prevention are used interchangeably in this chapter rather than treated as separate phases.
Trap: If an answer option lists five phases with mitigation and prevention as separate stages, it is wrong by this textbook, even though other sources make that distinction.

Q: In this chapter's disaster management cycle, mitigation is best understood as which of the following?
A) A distinct fifth phase that occurs between prevention and preparedness
B) The same phase as prevention — reducing risks to people and property before a disaster occurs
C) A phase that occurs only after response has concluded
D) A synonym for recovery
Answer: B
Rationale: Mitigation is "an emergency management term for reducing risks to people and property from natural hazards before they occur" and is used interchangeably with prevention in this chapter.

Q: Which nursing skills does the chapter identify as applying across all four phases of the disaster management cycle? Select all that apply.
A) Assessment and priority setting
B) Collaboration and health education
C) Disease screening and mass clinic expertise
D) Prescriptive authority for controlled substances
E) Triage and rapid needs assessment
Answer: A, B, C, E
Rationale: Nursing skills that apply across all four phases include assessment, priority setting, collaboration, health education, disease screening, mass clinic expertise, and additionally providing psychological first aid, triage, and rapid needs assessment. Prescriptive authority is not named.

Topic: federal disaster framework
Subtopic: FEMA's role and the escalation ladder

Q: Which statement best describes FEMA's role as named in the chapter?
A) FEMA directly manages all local emergency response operations during a disaster
B) FEMA coordinates comprehensive, all-hazard planning at the national level, providing a menu of exercises and plan templates for communities
C) FEMA's role is limited to disbursing recovery funds after a presidential disaster declaration
D) FEMA sets state-level mutual aid agreements
Answer: B
Rationale: "The Federal Emergency Management Agency (FEMA) coordinates comprehensive, all-hazard planning at the national level, assuring a menu of exercises and plan templates to address plausible incidents in any given community."

Q: Place the disaster response escalation ladder in the correct order, from where response first begins to the point at which the National Response Framework may take effect.
A) State → county/city → local → federal
B) Local → county/city → state → federal
C) Federal → state → county/city → local
D) County/city → local → federal → state
Answer: B
Rationale: The first level of response occurs at the local level (fire, law enforcement, public health, emergency services). If needs exceed local resources, the county or city emergency management agency coordinates through an emergency operations center. When state resources are overwhelmed, governors may request federal assistance under a presidential declaration, and if the event is an incident of national significance, the NRF may take effect.

Q: A hurricane has overwhelmed a coastal county's emergency operations center, and the state's own resources and capabilities are now also overwhelmed. What is the next appropriate step in the escalation ladder?
A) The county directly contacts FEMA to activate the National Response Framework
B) The governor may request federal assistance under a presidential disaster or emergency declaration
C) Local fire and law enforcement resume primary responsibility
D) The American Red Cross assumes command of the response
Answer: B
Rationale: When state resources and capabilities are overwhelmed, governors may request federal assistance under a presidential disaster or emergency declaration. Only after that, if the event is an incident of national significance, does the NRF take effect.
Trap: Counties do not skip the state level to contact FEMA directly — escalation proceeds local to county/city to state to federal.

Topic: triage
Subtopic: the three priority levels

Q: What is the definition of triage as used in this chapter?
A) Treating patients in the order in which they arrive
B) The process of separating casualties and allocating treatment on the basis of an individual's potential for survival
C) Allocating treatment based strictly on injury severity
D) Assigning care based on insurance or ability to pay
Answer: B
Rationale: Triage is "the process of separating casualties and allocating treatment on the basis of an individual's potential for survival."

Q: Under disaster triage, which criteria must BOTH be met for a victim to receive the highest priority?
A) A life-threatening injury alone, regardless of prognosis
B) A life-threatening injury and a high probability of survival once stabilized
C) Any injury requiring more than 60 minutes of care
D) Being the first casualty found at the scene
Answer: B
Rationale: Highest priority requires both a life-threatening injury and a high probability of survival once stabilized. This survival clause is what distinguishes disaster triage from ED triage.
Trap: The classic distractor is a patient with the most severe, catastrophic injuries but a low probability of survival — under this rule, that patient is not highest priority.

Q: A disaster nurse triaging a mass-casualty scene finds a patient with a survivable but severe leg fracture and no systemic complications, and a patient with catastrophic head trauma who is unlikely to survive even with immediate intervention. How should these two patients be triaged?
A) The head trauma patient is highest priority because the injury is most severe
B) The head trauma patient is highest priority because triage always favors the sickest patient
C) The head trauma patient is not highest priority, because a high probability of survival once stabilized is also required; the leg fracture patient is triaged according to whether the injury is systemic or local
D) Both patients receive equal priority because both have identifiable injuries
Answer: C
Rationale: Highest priority requires both a life-threatening injury and a high probability of survival once stabilized. A patient with catastrophic, non-survivable injuries is not highest priority under this rule.

Q: A victim has injuries with systemic complications that are not yet life-threatening. Approximately how long can this victim's treatment wait under disaster triage?
A) Immediate treatment is required
B) 45 to 60 minutes
C) Several hours
D) Treatment can be deferred indefinitely
Answer: B
Rationale: The second priority level — systemic complications that are not yet life-threatening — could wait 45 to 60 minutes.

Q: Which victims are assigned to the last triage priority, and how long can they typically wait?
A) Victims with life-threatening injuries and low survival probability; wait several hours
B) Victims with local injuries without immediate complications; can wait several hours
C) Victims with no visible injuries; wait indefinitely
D) Victims with systemic complications; wait 45 to 60 minutes
Answer: B
Rationale: The last priority level is victims with local injuries without immediate complications, who can wait several hours.

Q: What principle underlies the establishment of priorities in disaster triage, according to the chapter?
A) Treating the most severely injured patient first, regardless of resources
B) Establishing priorities that benefit the largest aggregate of affected individuals with the most correctable problems
C) Treating patients strictly in order of arrival
D) Prioritizing patients most likely to require prolonged hospitalization
Answer: B
Rationale: "Establishing a priority of needs that benefit the largest aggregate of affected individuals with the most correctable problems is consistent with the basic tenets of triage."

Topic: response
Subtopic: the four community stress phases

Q: Which two community stress phases are associated with the RESPONSE phase of the disaster management cycle?
A) Disillusionment and reconstruction
B) Heroic and honeymoon
C) Heroic and disillusionment
D) Honeymoon and reconstruction
Answer: B
Rationale: The heroic and honeymoon phases are associated with response, while disillusionment and reconstruction are associated with recovery.
Trap: The chapter discusses all four phases together within the Response section "for purposes of continuity," which makes it easy to mistakenly assign all four to response.

Q: Which two community stress phases are associated with the RECOVERY phase of the disaster management cycle?
A) Heroic and honeymoon
B) Disillusionment and reconstruction
C) Honeymoon and disillusionment
D) Heroic and reconstruction
Answer: B
Rationale: Disillusionment and reconstruction are associated with recovery; heroic and honeymoon are associated with response.

Q: First responders at a disaster scene work for hours on end without rest, refuse breaks, and fight needed sleep. Which community stress phase does this describe, and what is the associated danger?
A) Honeymoon phase; the danger is survivor complacency
B) Heroic phase; exhausted, overworked responders present a danger to themselves and the community served
C) Disillusionment phase; the danger is despair among survivors
D) Reconstruction phase; the danger is prolonged unemployment
Answer: B
Rationale: The heroic phase involves an overwhelming need to help others survive; responders work without thought of their own needs and refuse rest breaks. Exhausted, overworked responders present a danger to themselves and the community served, and those with oversight may need to order helpers to take breaks.

Q: Survivors gather to repeatedly share their disaster stories, expressing thankfulness that their lives and the lives of loved ones were spared. Which community stress phase is this?
A) Heroic
B) Honeymoon
C) Disillusionment
D) Reconstruction
Answer: B
Rationale: In the honeymoon phase, survivors may be rejoicing that their lives and the lives of loved ones have been spared. They gather to share experiences, and repeated telling creates bonds among survivors.

Q: Weeks after a disaster, a community begins to realize that additional help is not immediately forthcoming, and residents express despair as they recognize a return to the previous normal is unlikely. Which phase is this, and which broader disaster cycle stage does it belong to?
A) Heroic phase; response
B) Honeymoon phase; response
C) Disillusionment phase; recovery
D) Reconstruction phase; recovery
Answer: C
Rationale: The disillusionment phase occurs as people begin to notice that additional help may not be forthcoming, producing despair and exhaustion, and a recognition that returning to the previous normal is unlikely. It is associated with recovery.

Q: Which community stress phase is described as the longest, with the goal of returning to a new state of normalcy?
A) Heroic
B) Honeymoon
C) Disillusionment
D) Reconstruction
Answer: D
Rationale: Reconstruction is the longest phase, during which homes, schools, churches, and other community elements are rebuilt and reestablished, with the goal of returning to a new state of normalcy.

Topic: response
Subtopic: rapid needs assessment

Q: Which of the following are among the six elements of a rapid needs assessment? Select all that apply.
A) Determining the magnitude of the incident
B) Defining the specific health needs of the affected population
C) Establishing priorities and objectives for action
D) Identifying existing and potential public health problems
E) Filing insurance claims on behalf of affected residents
Answer: A, B, C, D
Rationale: The six elements are determining the magnitude of the incident; defining the specific health needs of the affected population; establishing priorities and objectives for action; identifying existing and potential public health problems; evaluating the capacity of the local response including resources and logistics; and determining the external resource needs for priority actions. Filing insurance claims is not one of them.

Q: A rapid needs assessment includes evaluating the capacity of the local response, including resources and logistics, and determining what other element?
A) The external resource needs for priority actions
B) The long-term psychological impact on responders
C) The projected cost of rebuilding infrastructure
D) The media narrative surrounding the disaster
Answer: A
Rationale: The sixth element of the rapid needs assessment is determining the external resource needs for priority actions.

Q: For a sudden-impact disaster such as a tornado or earthquake, what should the rapid needs assessment focus on?
A) Mortality rates, nutritional status, immunization status, and environmental health
B) Ongoing hazards, injuries and deaths, shelter requirements, and clean water
C) Long-term economic recovery projections
D) School attendance and educational disruption
Answer: B
Rationale: Sudden-impact disasters like tornadoes and earthquakes require assessment focused on ongoing hazards, injuries and deaths, shelter requirements, and clean water. Gradual-onset disasters like famines instead focus on mortality rates, nutritional status, immunization status, and environmental health.
Trap: The assessment focus for sudden-impact and gradual-onset disasters is easy to swap — sudden-impact is about immediate hazards and shelter, gradual-onset is about nutrition and immunization trends over time.

Topic: preparedness
Subtopic: personal and family disaster preparedness

Q: What are the three steps of "Be Red Cross Ready" as presented in the chapter?
A) Get a kit, make a plan, be informed
B) Get insured, make a plan, contact authorities
C) Get a kit, evacuate early, notify neighbors
D) Make a plan, stockpile supplies, register with FEMA
Answer: A
Rationale: The three Red Cross Ready steps are: get a kit, make a plan, and be informed.

Q: When making a family disaster plan, how many meeting places should a family choose, and what is required of the emergency contact person?
A) One meeting place inside the home; the contact person should be a trusted neighbor
B) Two meeting places — one right outside the home and one outside the neighborhood; the contact person should be out of the area
C) Three meeting places spread across the community; the contact person should be a local relative
D) One meeting place outside the neighborhood; no contact person is needed if cell phones are charged
Answer: B
Rationale: Families should choose two meeting places — one right outside the home for a sudden emergency such as a fire, and another outside the neighborhood in case they cannot return home or must evacuate. The contact person should be out of the area, because it may be easier to call long distance or text if local phone lines are overloaded.
Trap: "A trusted neighbor" is a plausible-sounding but incorrect substitute for an out-of-area contact — the rationale is specifically that local lines may be overloaded while long-distance lines still work.

Q: According to the chapter's maintenance schedule for family preparedness, how often should stored water and stored food be replaced?
A) Water every 6 months, food every 3 months
B) Water every 3 months, food every 6 months
C) Both every 3 months
D) Both every 12 months
Answer: B
Rationale: Stored water should be replaced every 3 months and stored food every 6 months.
Trap: The two intervals are easy to swap since they use the same two numbers in opposite order.

Q: How often should a family plan to review its disaster plan, according to the chapter's maintenance schedule?
A) Every 3 months
B) Every 6 months
C) Every 12 months
D) Only after an actual disaster occurs
Answer: B
Rationale: The plan should be reviewed every 6 months, alongside replacing stored water every 3 months, stored food every 6 months, testing smoke detectors monthly, and practicing evacuation twice a year.
Trap: Five different intervals appear in this one box (water, food, plan review, smoke detectors, evacuation drills) — they are easy to shuffle, so learn each one individually rather than assuming a single cadence applies to all.

Q: How often does the chapter recommend testing smoke detectors, and how often should evacuation be practiced?
A) Smoke detectors monthly; evacuation twice a year
B) Smoke detectors every 6 months; evacuation monthly
C) Smoke detectors annually; evacuation every 3 months
D) Smoke detectors monthly; evacuation annually
Answer: A
Rationale: Smoke detectors should be tested monthly, with batteries changed at least once a year. Evacuation should be practiced twice a year, driving the planned route and plotting alternative routes in case main roads are impassable.

Topic: response
Subtopic: the first goal of response and biological incidents

Q: What is the FIRST goal of any disaster response, according to the chapter?
A) Triaging and treating casualties as quickly as possible
B) Reestablishing sanitary barriers as quickly as possible
C) Restoring electrical power to the affected area
D) Establishing a public information officer for media relations
Answer: B
Rationale: "The first goal of any disaster response is to reestablish sanitary barriers as quickly as possible," addressing basic needs of water, food, waste removal, vector control, shelter, and safety.
Trap: Because triage dominates the surrounding text on response, it is easy to assume treating casualties is the first goal — the chapter states sanitation comes first.

Q: In a biological incident where an agent has been released without warning, who does the chapter identify as effectively "first on the scene," and why?
A) Fire and EMS personnel, because they arrive within minutes of any incident
B) Law enforcement, because they secure the area before health responders enter
C) Health care professionals, because symptoms may not appear for days or weeks after exposure, by which time victims have already sought a health evaluation
D) Public health surveillance systems, because they detect agents in real time
Answer: C
Rationale: An unannounced biological release may go unnoticed, and victims may have left the area of exposure long before the act is recognized. The first signs may not be apparent for days or weeks, until victims become ill and seek a health evaluation — in this case, health care professionals are considered the "first on the scene."
Trap: This inverts the usual assumption that fire or EMS are always first on scene; for biological incidents specifically, delayed symptom onset shifts that role to health care professionals encountering ill patients later.

Q: Why are biological agents considered uniquely dangerous compared to many other disaster threats?
A) They require large quantities to affect a population
B) Only a small amount is needed to affect thousands of people, and some agents are easy to conceal, transport, and disseminate
C) They are always immediately obvious upon release
D) They primarily affect infrastructure rather than people
Answer: B
Rationale: Only a small amount of a biological agent is needed to affect thousands of people, and some agents are easy to conceal, transport, and disseminate.

Q: Why can the results of a biological release be hard to recognize, according to the chapter?
A) Biological agent symptoms often mimic influenza or other viral syndromes
B) Biological agents rarely cause any symptoms
C) Laboratory testing for biological agents is not available in the United States
D) Biological releases are always announced in advance by the perpetrators
Answer: A
Rationale: "Results of a biological release are hard to recognize because many biological agent symptoms mimic influenza or other viral syndromes."
