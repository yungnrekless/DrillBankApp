# Chapter 21 drills — Family Health Risks

Authored from `sources/Ch21_Fact_Bank.md` (Stanhope & Lancaster, 6th ed.,
printed pp. 359–379). Every item is anchored to that file; where the fact bank
flags a trap or an internal inconsistency, the trap line says so explicitly.

Home visits is the blueprint's named topic and the chapter's single most
developed procedural content (five phases, fundamental skills, safety
guidance, voluntary vs. required), so it carries the heaviest weight below.

---

Topic: family health risk concepts
Subtopic: risk, appraisal, and reduction

Q: How does the chapter define "risk"?
A) A factor guaranteeing an unhealthy outcome
B) A factor predisposing or increasing the likelihood of ill health
C) Any behavior a family considers unsafe
D) The presence of a diagnosed illness in the family
Answer: B
Rationale: Risk is defined as "a factor predisposing or increasing the likelihood of ill health."

Q: What is health risk appraisal?
A) Assigning a diagnosis based on presenting symptoms
B) The process of assessing for the presence of specific factors associated with an increased likelihood of an illness or unhealthy event
C) A one-time screening required before Medicaid eligibility
D) The process of ranking families by socioeconomic status
Answer: B
Rationale: Health risk appraisal is the process of assessing for the presence of specific factors in each risk category identified as associated with an increased likelihood of an illness (such as cancer) or an unhealthy event (such as an automobile accident).
Trap: The appraisal method produces an individual score, but that score is examined as a whole within the family — it also appraises risks likely to be experienced by other family members.

Q: Health risk reduction is based on which assumption?
A) Eliminating all risk factors is required before any benefit occurs
B) Decreasing the number or magnitude of risks will decrease the probability of an undesired event occurring
C) Behavioral risks cannot be modified through family intervention
D) Risk reduction is only effective at the individual level, not the family level
Answer: B
Rationale: Health risk reduction is based on the assumption that decreasing the number or the magnitude of risks will decrease the probability of an undesired event occurring.

Q: Health risks are classified into which three general categories (USDHHS, 2010)? Select all that apply.
A) Inherited biological risks, including age-related risks
B) Environmental risks (social, economic, physical)
C) Behavioral risks
D) Genetic risks distinct from biological risks
E) Institutional risks
Answer: A, B, C
Rationale: The three general categories are inherited biological risks (including age-related risks), environmental risks (composed of social, economic, and physical environments), and behavioral risks (personal health habits).
Trap: The chapter does not name a separate "genetic" or "institutional" category — genetics falls under biological risk.

Q: A family has a history of cardiovascular disease. Several members also smoke, and the family lives in a community that is unsupportive of exercise and low-fat diets. How does the chapter characterize the combined effect of these risks?
A) The risks are independent and do not interact
B) The combined effect of accumulated risks is often greater than the sum of the individual effects
C) Only the biological risk is clinically significant
D) Behavioral risks cancel out biological risks over time
Answer: B
Rationale: This is the chapter's worked cardiovascular example of the synergy principle: "Although single risk factors can influence outcomes, the combined effect of accumulated risks is often greater than the sum of the individual effects." Smoking is more likely if other family members also smoke, and diet/exercise are shaped by family and societal norms.
Trap: Treating accumulated risks as merely additive understates the chapter's point — the effect is synergistic, not simply summed.

Subtopic: life events and transitions

Q: A family experiences the anticipated birth of a grandchild. According to the chapter, how does this differ from an unexpected event such as sudden job loss?
A) There is no meaningful difference — both are equally stressful
B) The normative event allows anticipatory preparation, which can increase coping and decrease stress and negative outcomes; the non-normative event allows little or no time to prepare
C) Normative events are always more stressful because they involve more people
D) Non-normative events are always positive
Answer: B
Rationale: Normative (anticipated) events let families identify needed resources, make plans, learn new skills, and prepare — anticipatory preparation increases coping ability and decreases stress. Non-normative (unexpected) events leave little or no time to prepare and can result in increased stress, crisis, or dysfunction.

Q: Which of the following are examples of normative life events named in the chapter? Select all that apply.
A) Birth or adoption of a child
B) Death of a grandparent
C) A child moving out to go to school or take a job
D) An unexpected major illness
E) The marriage of a child
Answer: A, B, C, E
Rationale: Normative events are usually related to the addition or loss of a family member: birth or adoption of a child, death of a grandparent, a child moving out to school or a job, or the marriage of a child.
Trap: An unexpected major illness is named as a negative non-normative event, not a normative one.

Q: A family member receives a major job promotion that brings more responsibility, stress, and travel. How should this be classified?
A) A normative event, because promotions are expected career milestones
B) A non-normative event that is positive but still disruptive
C) A family crisis by definition
D) A behavioral risk
Answer: B
Rationale: Non-normative events can be positive but disruptive — the chapter names a job promotion and inheriting a substantial sum of money as examples that can change family dynamics even though they are positive.
Trap: It is tempting to assume any positive event must be low-risk. The chapter's point is the opposite: non-normative positive events still disrupt family functioning.

Subtopic: risk-tolerance principles (Murdaugh)

Q: Which statements reflect Murdaugh's principles of risk tolerance? Select all that apply.
A) Voluntarily assumed risks, such as overeating, are tolerated better than those imposed by others
B) Risks about which scientists debate and are uncertain are more feared than risks about which scientists agree
C) Risks of natural origin are often considered less threatening than those created by humans
D) Imposed risks are tolerated better than voluntarily assumed risks
E) Risks with scientific consensus are more feared than those with scientific disagreement
Answer: A, B, C
Rationale: Murdaugh's three principles: voluntary risks are tolerated better than imposed ones; scientifically uncertain risks are more feared than risks with agreement; natural risks are considered less threatening than human-made risks.
Trap: D and E each invert one of the three principles — all three principles run counter to naive intuition, which is exactly why they are testable in both directions.

Q: A patient objects strongly to living near a newly built chemical plant but shows little concern about her own long-standing overeating habit. Which principle explains this pattern?
A) Voluntarily assumed risks are tolerated better than those imposed by others
B) Natural risks are feared more than human-made risks
C) Scientific agreement increases fear of a risk
D) Behavioral risks are always perceived as more dangerous than environmental risks
Answer: A
Rationale: This illustrates Murdaugh's principle that voluntarily assumed risks (overeating) are tolerated better than risks imposed by others (a plant she did not choose).

Topic: family assessment tools
Subtopic: genogram and ecomap

Q: What does a genogram show, and what does it reveal?
A) The family's interactions with outside groups and organizations
B) A drawing of the family unit across several generations, using circles, squares, and connecting lines, allowing patterns such as recurring illness to be quickly assessed
C) A numeric score of the family's total accumulated health risk
D) A record of the family's insurance and financial eligibility status
Answer: B
Rationale: A genogram is a drawing that shows the family unit of immediate interest across several generations using circles, squares, and connecting lines. Patterns can be quickly assessed and guide the interviewer toward areas needing further exploration — the Graham family genogram revealed repeated hypertension, type 2 diabetes, cancer, and hypercholesterolemia.
Trap: Option A describes the ecomap, not the genogram — the genogram looks inward across generations, the ecomap looks outward to the community.

Q: In genogram conventions, which pairings are correct? Select all that apply.
A) Square = male
B) Circle = female
C) X through a square or circle = a death
D) Solid vertical line = marriage
E) Broken horizontal line = divorce or separation
Answer: A, B, C, E
Rationale: Square = male, circle = female, an X through either shape = a death, a solid horizontal line = marriage, a solid vertical line = offspring/children, and a broken horizontal line = divorce or separation.
Trap: Marriage is shown with a solid HORIZONTAL line, not vertical — the vertical line represents offspring. This is the easiest genogram pairing to invert.

Q: Completing an accurate genogram requires what, according to the chapter?
A) A single interview with the head of household only
B) Interviews with as many family members as possible, and developing a family chronology spanning three generations
C) Access to each family member's electronic health record
D) A genetic testing panel for each family member
Answer: B
Rationale: "Completing a genogram requires interviews with as many family members as possible. It is important to develop a family chronology, a timeline of family events over three generations, to extend the genogram."

Q: What does an ecomap represent, and what do its conventions show? Select all that apply.
A) A circle in the middle of the page represents the family
B) Other circles represent other groups and organizations
C) Lines between circles represent the flow of energy
D) An arrowhead shows the direction of energy flow into or out of the family
E) The darkness of a line shows the family's socioeconomic status
Answer: A, B, C, D
Rationale: An ecomap represents the family's interactions with other groups and organizations using circles and lines. The family is the circle in the middle; other circles are groups/organizations; lines show the flow of energy; an arrowhead shows the direction of flow (into or out of the family); and the darkness of the line shows the intensity of the energy — not socioeconomic status.
Trap: Darkness indicates intensity of energy, not income or status — a plausible-sounding but fabricated pairing.

Q: A nurse wants to assess a family's relationships with relatives, neighbors, church, school, work, and clubs, and whether energy flows positively or negatively between the family and these groups. Which tool should the nurse use, and why not the alternative?
A) A genogram, because it captures multi-generational patterns
B) An ecomap, because it depicts the family's interactions with outside groups and organizations rather than internal generational/biological patterns
C) Either tool works identically for this purpose
D) A behavioral health risk survey
Answer: B
Rationale: The ecomap depicts the family's interactions with other groups and organizations and the flow of energy, positive or negative. The genogram instead documents biological/generational patterns (illnesses, births, deaths, marriages) within the family itself.
Trap: Both tools use circles, which is exactly why they are confused — the genogram looks inward across time; the ecomap looks outward across the community.

Topic: home visits
Subtopic: purpose and fundamental skills

Q: What does a home visit offer that a clinic visit does not? Select all that apply.
A) A more accurate assessment of family structure and behavior in the home environment
B) Opportunities to identify barriers and supports for reaching family health goals
C) The ability to modify interventions directly to match the family's resources
D) A guarantee of lower total cost of care than clinic-based services
E) Contribution to the family's sense of control and active participation in its own health needs
Answer: A, B, C, E
Rationale: Home visits give a more accurate assessment of family structure, environment, and behavior; identify barriers and supports; let the nurse modify interventions to match resources; and contribute to the family's sense of control and active participation. Cost is actually the major disadvantage, not a guaranteed savings.
Trap: The chapter is explicit that it is not always the least costly service that is the most effective — cost and effectiveness are separate considerations.

Q: What are the five fundamental skills for effective home visits? Select all that apply.
A) Observing
B) Listening
C) Questioning
D) Probing
E) Prompting
Answer: A, B, C, D, E
Rationale: The five fundamental skills are observing, listening, questioning, probing, and prompting. Building a trusting relationship with the family is the cornerstone of successful home visits.

Subtopic: the five phases of a home visit

Q: During the initiation phase of a home visit, the nurse should focus on which activities?
A) Introducing self, interacting socially to establish rapport, and implementing the nursing process
B) Clarifying the source of referral, clarifying the purpose of the visit, and sharing that reason and purpose with the family
C) Reviewing the visit with the family and planning for future visits
D) Recording the visit and planning for the next visit
Answer: B
Rationale: The initiation phase involves clarifying the source of referral, clarifying the purpose for the home visit, and sharing information on the reason and purpose with the family.
Trap: Option A describes the in-home phase; option C describes termination; option D describes the postvisit phase — all five phases have distinct, non-interchangeable activities.

Q: Which activities belong to the previsit phase?
A) Introducing self and professional identity, then implementing the nursing process
B) Initiating contact, establishing a shared perception of purpose, determining the family's willingness, scheduling the visit, and reviewing the referral/record
C) Recording the visit
D) Reviewing what has been accomplished and planning future visits
Answer: B
Rationale: The previsit phase includes initiating contact with the family, establishing a shared perception of purpose, determining the family's willingness for a home visit, scheduling the visit, and reviewing the referral and/or family record.

Q: During the in-home phase, what is the purpose of the brief social period that occurs early in the visit?
A) It allows the nurse to assess the family's home for safety hazards
B) It allows the patient to assess the nurse and establish rapport
C) It is used to complete required documentation before the visit continues
D) It replaces the need for formal introductions
Answer: B
Rationale: "Next, a brief social period allows the patient to assess the nurse and establish rapport."
Trap: It is the patient assessing the nurse during this period, not the reverse — an easy detail to invert.

Q: What is the major focus of the termination phase of a home visit?
A) Introducing self and professional identity
B) Reviewing with the family what has occurred and what has been accomplished, which provides a basis for planning further visits
C) Recording the interaction for legal purposes
D) Scheduling the next telephone contact
Answer: B
Rationale: The termination phase's major focus is reviewing with the family what has occurred and what has been accomplished, which provides a basis for planning further home visits. Ideally, termination of the visit — and ultimately of service — begins at the first contact with the establishment of a goal or purpose.

Q: When is the nurse's responsibility for a home visit actually complete?
A) As soon as the nurse leaves the patient's home
B) Once the family verbally confirms satisfaction with the visit
C) Only once the interaction has been recorded
D) After the next scheduled visit occurs
Answer: C
Rationale: "Even though the nurse has concluded the home visit and left the patient's home, responsibility for the visit is not complete until the interaction has been recorded." This is the postvisit phase.

Subtopic: initiation phase specifics — numbers and procedure

Q: What is the maximum recommended length for the first telephone contact with a family before a home visit?
A) 5 minutes
B) 15 minutes
C) 30 minutes
D) 60 minutes
Answer: B
Rationale: "A first telephone contact should be a maximum of 15 min."
Trap: This is easy to confuse with the 30–60 minute figure, which describes the usual length of the home visit itself, not the initial phone call.

Q: What is the usual length of an established home visit?
A) 10–15 minutes
B) 15–30 minutes
C) 30–60 minutes
D) 60–90 minutes
Answer: C
Rationale: "Although the length of the visit may vary, approximately 30–60 min is usual."

Q: During the initiation phase, if a referral was received for the visit, what should the nurse specifically try to learn?
A) The family's insurance carrier
B) Whether the family is aware of the referral
C) Whether the family has previously refused services
D) The referring provider's specialty
Answer: B
Rationale: "If a referral was received, it is important and useful to learn if the family is aware of the referral."

Q: A family does not have a telephone. How should the nurse proceed to arrange the initial contact?
A) The visit cannot be arranged without a phone and should be canceled
B) A note can be dropped off at the home or sent by mail, informing the family of when and why the visit will occur and how to contact the nurse
C) The nurse should simply arrive unannounced
D) The visit must be rescheduled through the family's primary care provider
Answer: B
Rationale: If the family does not have a telephone, a note can be dropped off at the family home or sent by mail informing them of when and why the visit will occur and providing a way to contact the nurse.

Subtopic: when a family refuses a visit, and personal safety

Q: A family declines a scheduled home visit. What is the nurse's best initial response?
A) Interpret the refusal as a personal rejection and document noncompliance
B) Do not take it personally; explore the reasons for the refusal, since there may be a misunderstanding about its purpose or a lack of information about services and payment
C) Immediately close the case since services were declined
D) Report the family to social services for refusing care
Answer: B
Rationale: "Be aware that the family may refuse a home visit. Do not immediately interpret this as a personal rejection." The nurse should explore the reasons — there may be a misunderstanding about the visit's reason or a lack of information about services, including payment.

Q: A family with a member recently diagnosed with active tuberculosis refuses a follow-up home visit. What should the nurse do?
A) Respect the refusal permanently and close the referral
B) Leave open the possibility of future contact only, with no further action required
C) Persist in requesting the visit, because the nurse may be legally mandated to follow up on certain communicable and infectious diseases
D) Refer the case to law enforcement immediately
Answer: C
Rationale: "In some instances, the nurse will be mandated to persist in requesting a home visit because of legal obligations, such as follow-up of certain communicable and infectious diseases." TB follow-up is the chapter's own example of a required visit.
Trap: Ordinarily the nurse leaves the door open for future contact rather than pressing the issue — the exception is legally mandated communicable-disease follow-up, which this scenario is.

Q: According to the chapter's guidance on personal safety, what should the nurse do if a reasonable question exists about the safety of making a visit?
A) Make the visit but request an escort
B) Make the visit only during daylight hours
C) The nurse should not make the visit
D) Proceed as planned since home visits are generally safe
Answer: C
Rationale: "If a reasonable question exists about the safety of making a visit, the nurse should not make the visit." Home visits are generally safe, but the possibility of violence exists, so the nurse must use caution and good judgment.
Trap: Precautions like escorts, pairs, uniforms, and sign-out logs are used for known high-risk situations — but they do not override the flat rule that a genuine safety question means the visit is not made.

Subtopic: voluntary versus required visits

Q: A new mother requests that the nurse come to her home to assist with effective breastfeeding. How would this visit typically be characterized?
A) Required, nurse-controlled, and formal/investigatory
B) Voluntary, with easier entry for the nurse, patient-controlled interaction, and a more informal tone
C) Required, because breastfeeding support involves a health teaching mandate
D) Neither voluntary nor required — home visits are always neutral in tone
Answer: B
Rationale: Voluntary visits (requested by the patient) allow easier entry for the nurse, are patient-controlled, have a more informal tone, and typically involve mutual discussion of future visit frequency. The breastfeeding-assistance request is the chapter's own example.

Q: A family member has been diagnosed with tuberculosis, and the nurse must verify the patient is taking medication regularly. How would this visit typically be characterized?
A) Voluntary, patient-controlled, and informal
B) Required, with more difficult entry, nurse-controlled interaction, a more formal/investigatory tone, and possibly distorted nurse–patient communication
C) Voluntary, because the patient's health is at stake
D) Required, but otherwise identical in tone and control to a voluntary visit
Answer: B
Rationale: Required visits (often legally mandated) involve more difficult entry, nurse-controlled interaction, a more formal and investigatory tone with distorted communication, and there may be no mutual discussion of future visit frequency. TB medication verification is the chapter's own example, alongside COVID-19 quarantine follow-up.
Trap: Assuming all home visits share the same tone ignores the chapter's explicit contrast — required visits are structurally different from voluntary ones, not just more serious versions of the same thing.

Topic: contracting with families
Subtopic: premise, contract types, and process

Q: What is the stated premise of contracting with families?
A) Contracting shifts all responsibility to the nurse to ensure compliance
B) Family control — when the family has legitimate control, its ability to make healthful choices is increased
C) Contracting is a legally binding document required for all home visits
D) Contracting eliminates the need for ongoing nursing assessment
Answer: B
Rationale: "The premise of contracting is family control. It is assumed that when the family has legitimate control, its ability to make healthful choices is increased." Contracting involves a shift in responsibility and control toward a shared effort by patient and professional.

Q: Which statement correctly distinguishes a contingency contract from a noncontingency contract?
A) A contingency contract states a specific reward after completion of the patient's portion; a noncontingency contract does not specify rewards, relying on the implied reward of reaching the goals
B) A contingency contract is always written; a noncontingency contract is always verbal
C) A noncontingency contract offers a larger reward than a contingency contract
D) Contingency and noncontingency contracts are interchangeable terms for the same agreement
Answer: A
Rationale: A contingency contract states a specific reward for the patient after completion of the patient's portion of the contract. A noncontingency contract does not specify rewards — the implied rewards are the positive consequences of reaching the goals specified in the contract.

Q: For family health risk reduction, who should be involved in the contract?
A) Only the family member with the identified health problem
B) The head of household only, since that person can make decisions for the family
C) All responsible and appropriate members of the family, because the goal requires a total family system effort and change
D) Only the nurse and the primary care provider, with the family notified afterward
Answer: C
Rationale: "For family health risk reduction, it is essential that the contract be made with all responsible and appropriate members of the family. Involving only one individual is not sufficient if the goal is family health risk reduction, which requires a total family system effort and change."
Trap: If meeting with the entire family at once is not possible, each family member can still review the contract, give input, and sign it — the requirement is inclusion, not necessarily a single joint meeting.

Q: What are the three phases of contracting, and what do they have in common?
A) Assessment, diagnosis, and evaluation — each led by the nurse
B) Beginning, working, and termination — every activity in each phase is described as mutual
C) Initiation, previsit, and postvisit — mirroring the home visit phases exactly
D) Planning, implementing, and billing
Answer: B
Rationale: The three phases of contracting are beginning (mutual data collection, goal-setting, plan development), working (mutual division of responsibilities, time limits, implementation, evaluation/renegotiation), and termination (mutual termination of the contract). Every activity in the table begins with "mutual" — that is the point.

Q: Which patient groups may not choose to contract, according to the chapter? Select all that apply.
A) Persons with minimal cognitive skills
B) Those involved in an emergency situation
C) Persons unwilling to be more active in their own care
D) Those who do not see control or authority for health concerns as being within their domain
E) Persons with a documented chronic illness
Answer: A, B, C, D
Rationale: The four named groups are persons with minimal cognitive skills, those in an emergency situation, persons unwilling to be more active in their own care, and those who do not see control or authority for health concerns as within their domain. Some of these patients may learn to contract; others never will.
Trap: A chronic illness diagnosis alone is not one of the four named barriers to contracting.

Q: Before assuming that contracting will be an effective approach with a family, what should the nurse determine first?
A) The family's insurance coverage
B) The family's value system, since contracting may not be consistent with cultures that look to health care providers for more direct guidance
C) Whether the family has previously accepted a home visit
D) The number of adults living in the household
Answer: B
Rationale: "This approach... may not be consistent with other cultures that look to health care providers for more direct guidance; therefore, it is important to determine the family's value system before assuming that contracting will work."
