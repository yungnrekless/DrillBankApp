Topic: government and law
Subtopic: three branches of government

Q: A state health department issues regulations implementing a new infection-control statute. Which branch of government is exercising its health-policy role here?
A) Legislative
B) Executive
C) Judicial
D) Administrative-judicial hybrid
Answer: B
Rationale: The executive branch — the president/governor/mayor and administrative and regulatory departments and agencies such as USDHHS — suggests, administers, and regulates policy.
Trap: EAQ-style questions pin each distractor to a different branch. Administers/enforces/regulates always maps to executive, even when the underlying statute came from the legislature.

Q: Congress debates, amends, and ultimately passes a bill expanding a maternal health program. Which branch's health-policy role does this describe?
A) Executive
B) Legislative
C) Judicial
D) Regulatory
Answer: B
Rationale: The legislative branch (Congress at the federal level) identifies problems and proposes, debates, passes, and modifies laws.

Q: The Supreme Court rules on whether a state's reproductive health access law is constitutional. Which branch's role is this?
A) Executive
B) Legislative
C) Judicial
D) Executive, because the ruling affects how the law is enforced
Answer: C
Rationale: The judicial branch — federal, state, and local courts guided by the Supreme Court — interprets laws and their meaning, including questions like states' rights and reproductive health access.

Q: A state's nurse practice act was passed by the legislature, but the Board of Nursing licenses RNs and revokes licenses for violations. Which branch does the Board of Nursing belong to?
A) Legislative, because it was created by a legislative statute
B) Executive, because it is an administrative/regulatory agency
C) Judicial, because it adjudicates license revocations
D) A fourth, quasi-legislative branch unique to health professions
Answer: B
Rationale: A Board of Nursing is part of the executive branch — an administrative/regulatory agency — even though it is created by a legislative statute (the nurse practice act).
Trap: This is the chapter's single most testable trap. Test-writers rely on students assuming "created by the legislature" means "part of the legislative branch." Creation and operation are different branches here.

Q: Congress's authority to legislate on health care rests mainly on which constitutional provision?
A) The Tenth Amendment
B) Article I, Section 8
C) The Fourteenth Amendment's Equal Protection Clause
D) The Commerce Clause of Article III
Answer: B
Rationale: Congress's health-care authority rests mainly on Article I, Section 8 — providing for the general welfare, regulating interstate commerce, raising funds for the military, and the spending power.

Q: In Stewart Machine Co. v. Davis (1937), the Supreme Court upheld federal authority to provide unemployment and old-age benefits on what basis?
A) The police power of the states
B) Promoting the general welfare
C) The right to privacy
D) Preemption of state insurance law
Answer: B
Rationale: Stewart Machine Co. v. Davis (1937) upheld federal authority for unemployment/old-age benefits as promoting the general welfare, a landmark case under Article I, Section 8 authority.

Subtopic: three types of law

Q: A state orders mandatory isolation of a patient with active, contagious tuberculosis. What is the legal basis for this order?
A) Judicial law, based on precedent
B) Legislative/statute law
C) Constitutional law, through the state's police power
D) Common law duty to warn
Answer: C
Rationale: The legal basis for quarantine/isolation is constitutional law, specifically the state's police power — the state's authority to protect the health, safety, and welfare of its citizens, even overriding individual rights when the public is genuinely threatened, provided the action is necessary and reasonable.

Q: Which statement correctly defines police power?
A) A state's authority, requiring a compelling interest, to act to protect the health, safety, and welfare of its citizens
B) A federal agency's authority to enforce criminal law
C) A hospital's authority to set internal infection-control policy
D) A court's authority to compel testimony in a public health case
Answer: A
Rationale: Police power is the state's power to act to protect the health, safety, and welfare of its citizens. It must be used fairly and requires a compelling interest, especially when it infringes on individual rights.

Q: A state's nurse practice act is an example of which type of law?
A) Constitutional law
B) Legislation/statute law
C) Judicial/common law
D) Regulatory law
Answer: B
Rationale: Legislation/statute law comes from the legislative branch and is coded into statutes; a nurse practice act is statute law.

Q: Common law is built primarily on which principle?
A) Precedent — judges are bound by prior decisions unless distinguished
B) Codified statutes passed by a legislature
C) Constitutional guidance applied case by case
D) Administrative rule-making
Answer: A
Rationale: Common law rests on precedent — judges are bound by prior court decisions unless the current case can be distinguished from them. The opinions of courts are called case law.

Q: When a genuine threat to the public's health exists, how does the law weigh individual rights against community rights, according to the police-power principle?
A) Individual rights always take precedence over community rights
B) The community's rights outweigh the individual's rights, provided the action is necessary and reasonable
C) Individual and community rights are weighed equally in every case
D) Community rights outweigh individual rights only if a court has pre-approved the action
Answer: B
Rationale: When there is a genuine threat to the public's health, the community's rights outweigh the individual's rights, provided the action taken is necessary and done reasonably. This is the basis for quarantine/isolation and connects to communicable disease reporting.

Subtopic: nurse practice acts and scope of practice

Q: Which functions does every state's nurse practice act accomplish, at minimum? Select all that apply.
A) Defines the practice of professional nursing
B) Identifies the scope of nursing practice
C) Sets educational qualifications and other requirements for licensure
D) Determines the legal titles nurses may use
E) Sets mandatory minimum nurse-to-patient staffing ratios
Answer: A, B, C, D
Rationale: Every nurse practice act accomplishes at least four functions: defining the practice of professional nursing, identifying the scope of nursing practice, setting educational qualifications and other requirements for licensure, and determining the legal titles nurses may use.
Trap: Staffing ratios are not one of the four statutory functions named in the chapter; do not add facts beyond what the nurse practice act is stated to accomplish.

Q: Defining scope of practice forces clarification of which categories of nursing function?
A) Emergency, routine, and elective functions
B) Independent, interdependent, and dependent functions
C) Acute, chronic, and preventive functions
D) Licensed, certified, and delegated functions
Answer: B
Rationale: Defining scope of practice forces clarification of independent, interdependent, and dependent nursing functions. This is especially important for community nurses, who traditionally practice with high autonomy.

Q: A nurse licensed in State A is practicing temporarily in State B. Whose laws govern that nurse's practice?
A) State A's, because that is where the license was issued
B) State B's, because practitioners are subject to the laws of the state in which they practice
C) Federal law preempts both states' nurse practice acts
D) Whichever state's laws are more protective of the patient
Answer: B
Rationale: Practitioners are subject to the laws of the state in which they practice, and nurses can practice only with a license.

Subtopic: Boards of Nursing

Q: Which responsibilities belong to a state Board of Nursing? Select all that apply.
A) Licensing and examination of RNs and LPNs
B) Licensing and/or certification of advanced practice nurses
C) Approval of schools of nursing in the state
D) Revocation, suspension, or denial of licenses
E) Passing the nurse practice act that creates the Board
Answer: A, B, C, D
Rationale: Boards of Nursing generally license and examine RNs and LPNs, license/certify advanced practice nurses, approve schools of nursing, revoke/suspend/deny licenses, and write regulations about nursing practice and education.
Trap: The Board does not pass the nurse practice act — the legislature does. The Board operationalizes and enforces the act the legislature already passed.

Q: A state Board of Nursing is made up of which members?
A) Physicians and hospital administrators
B) Nurses and consumers
C) State legislators and attorneys
D) Judges and nursing school deans
Answer: B
Rationale: Every state has a Board of Nursing, created by the state's nurse practice act, made up of nurses and consumers.

Q: In branch terms, what is the correct division of labor between the legislature and the Board of Nursing regarding the nurse practice act?
A) The legislature writes the regulations; the Board passes the act
B) The legislature passes the act; the Board writes the regulations and enforces it
C) The Board passes the act; the legislature enforces it
D) Both the legislature and the Board jointly write and enforce the act
Answer: B
Rationale: The legislature passes the nurse practice act; the Board of Nursing (an executive/administrative body) operationalizes, implements, and enforces that statutory law by writing explicit regulations.

Subtopic: professional negligence and malpractice

Q: Which four elements must a patient prove, all together, to recover damages for professional negligence (malpractice)? Select all that apply.
A) Duty
B) Breach
C) Causation
D) Damages
E) Intent to harm
Answer: A, B, C, D
Rationale: To recover damages the patient must prove all four elements: duty (the nurse owed a duty to the patient), breach (the nurse failed to act as a reasonable, prudent nurse would), causation (that failure led to the injury), and damages (the injury supports a monetary claim). Missing any one element defeats the claim.
Trap: Malpractice is negligence, not an intentional tort — intent to harm is not one of the four required elements.

Q: A nurse fails to reassess a patient's pain per protocol (a clear deviation from the reasonable, prudent nurse standard), but the patient's outcome was identical to what it would have been with proper reassessment, and the patient suffered no injury or financial loss. Can the patient successfully sue for malpractice?
A) Yes, because duty and breach are both clearly established
B) No, because causation and damages are missing even though duty and breach are present
C) Yes, automatically, once any breach of the standard of care is shown
D) No, because there was no duty owed in this scenario
Answer: B
Rationale: All four elements — duty, breach, causation, and damages — must be present. Here duty and breach are established, but there is no injury (damages) and nothing the breach can be shown to have caused (causation), so the claim fails.
Trap: A clear breach feels like enough on its own, but breach without resulting, compensable harm defeats the claim — this is the scenario version of "missing any one of the four D's."

Q: What is the memory hook the fact bank offers for the four elements of malpractice?
A) The four C's: Care, Competence, Conduct, Consequence
B) The four D's: Duty, Dereliction, Direct cause, Damages
C) The four P's: Patient, Provider, Procedure, Proof
D) The four S's: Standard, Scope, Severity, Settlement
Answer: B
Rationale: The memory hook is the four D's — Duty, Dereliction (breach), Direct cause (causation), and Damages.

Q: A hospital-employed nurse commits a negligent act while working within the scope of her employment. Under respondeat superior, who may be held responsible?
A) Only the nurse individually
B) Only the employer, since the nurse was following employer direction
C) The employer, along with the individual nurse
D) Neither party, since the employer did not act directly
Answer: C
Rationale: Under respondeat superior, when a nurse is employed and acting within the scope of employment, the employer is responsible for the nurse's negligent actions along with the individual nurse, because the employer directed the function and is usually better able to pay.

Q: According to the fact bank, how common are reported negligence cases involving population-centered (community/public health) nurses?
A) They are the most common category of nursing malpractice claim
B) They are rare
C) They occur at the same rate as acute-care nursing claims
D) They have risen sharply in the last decade
Answer: B
Rationale: Reported negligence cases involving population-centered nurses are rare.

Subtopic: nursing advocacy and the policy process

Q: A nurse testifies before a state legislative committee to support a bill funding school-based health clinics. This is an example of which type of policy action?
A) Regulatory action
B) Legislative action
C) Judicial action
D) Administrative action
Answer: B
Rationale: Nurses influence policy through legislative action — communicating with policymakers, testifying, and lobbying.

Q: After a health-related bill is signed into law, a nurse participates in the agency rule-making process to shape how it will be implemented. This is an example of which type of policy action?
A) Legislative action
B) Regulatory action
C) Judicial action
D) Constitutional action
Answer: B
Rationale: Nurses also influence policy through regulatory action — participating in the rule-making process after a law has passed.

Q: What is presented as the leading example of national health-policy guidance in the chapter's discussion of nursing advocacy?
A) The Affordable Care Act
B) Healthy People 2030
C) The Nurse Practice Act Model Language
D) The USDHHS Strategic Plan
Answer: B
Rationale: Healthy People 2030 is presented as the leading example of national health-policy guidance, within the chapter's framing of the nurse as an agent of change and advocate for population health.
