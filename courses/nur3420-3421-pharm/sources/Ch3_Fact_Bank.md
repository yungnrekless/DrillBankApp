## Key terms

All 6 terms from the chapter's KEY TERMS box, quoted as given (p. 33). This is the smallest key-terms box in the course; the vocabulary load here is in the numbers, not the words.

| Term | p. | Definition as given |
|---|---|---|
| **Active transport** | 33 | "The **active (energy-requiring) movement** of a substance between different tissues **via pumping mechanisms contained within cell membranes.**" |
| **Diffusion** | 33 | "The **passive movement** of a substance (e.g., a drug) between different tissues **from areas of higher concentration to areas of lower concentration.** (Compare with active transport.)" |
| **Neonate** | 33 | "Pertaining to a person **younger than 1 month of age;** newborn infant." |
| **Older adult** | 33 | "Pertaining to a person who is **65 years of age or older.** (Note: **Some sources consider 'older adult' to be 55 years of age or older.**)" |
| **Pediatric** | 33 | "Pertaining to a person who is **12 years of age or younger.**" |
| **Polypharmacy** | 33 | "The use of **many different drugs concurrently** in treating a patient, who often has **several health problems.**" |

> **Trap (confusable pair).** **Active transport vs. diffusion.** The box defines them against each other, which is the tell that they will be swapped. Cue: ***diffusion is passive, needs no energy, and runs high-to-low concentration; active transport requires energy and uses a pump.*** The chapter attaches this to the placenta specifically (p. 33): "Transfer of both drugs and nutrients to the fetus occurs **primarily by diffusion** across the placenta, although **not all drugs cross the placenta.**"

> **Trap (level/stage swap).** **Both age definitions carry a footnote that contradicts the headline number.** *Older adult = **65** or older, "**but some sources consider... 55 or older**." Pediatric = **12** or younger, but Table 3.2's note says "**often the maximum age for a pediatric patient may be identified as 16 years of age**" and that the meaning "**may vary with the individual drug and clinical situation.**"* The chapter's own instruction is to "**consult the manufacturer's guidelines for specific dosing information.**" An item that hinges on a hard cutoff is testing the headline number; an item that hinges on judgment is testing the footnote.

## §1 Drug therapy during pregnancy

**The governing sentence** (p. 33): "A fetus is exposed to **many of the same substances as the mother, including any drugs that she takes — prescription, nonprescription, or street drugs.**"

**The three factor categories** (p. 33): "The factors that contribute to the safety or potential harm of drug therapy during pregnancy can be broadly broken down into three areas: **drug properties, fetal gestational age, and maternal factors.**"

| Factor category | Contents as given |
|---|---|
| **Drug properties** | "the drug's **chemistry, the dosage, and concurrently administered drugs.**" Relevant chemical properties: "**molecular weight, protein binding, lipid solubility, and chemical structure.**" Dosage variables: "**dose and duration of therapy**" |
| **Fetal gestational age** | determines "the potential for harmful drug effects" and also "**when a drug can most easily cross the placenta**" |
| **Maternal factors** | "**Any change in the mother's physiology can affect the amount of drug to which the fetus may be exposed.** Maternal **kidney and liver function** affect drug metabolism and excretion... Maternal **genotype** may also affect how certain drugs are metabolized (**pharmacogenomics**)" |

**The two-trimester rule, which is the chapter's most swappable pair** (pp. 33–34):

| Trimester | What is true of it |
|---|---|
| **First** | "the period of **greatest danger of drug-induced developmental defects**"; "The fetus is at **greatest risk for drug-induced developmental defects during the first trimester**"; during this period "the fetus undergoes **rapid cell proliferation. Skeleton, muscles, limbs, and visceral organs are developing at their most rapid rate**" |
| **Last (third)** | "During the **last trimester, the greatest percentage of maternally absorbed drug gets to the fetus**"; "**drug transfer to the fetus is more likely during the last trimester.** This is the result of **enhanced blood flow to the fetus, increased fetal surface area, and increased amount of free drug in the mother's circulation**" |

The chapter states the contrast in one sentence (p. 34): "**Although exposure of the fetus to drugs is most detrimental during the first trimester, drug transfer to the fetus is more likely during the last trimester.**"

- **Self-treatment:** "strongly discouraged **at any time during pregnancy, but especially during the first trimester**" (p. 33).
- **When drugs are necessary anyway** (p. 34): "certain situations require their use. **Without drug therapy, maternal conditions such as hypertension, epilepsy, diabetes, and infection could seriously endanger both the mother and the fetus,** and the potential for harm far outweighs the risks of appropriate drug therapy."

**Table 3.1 Pregnancy, Lactation, and Reproduction** (p. 34), reproduced in full:

| Category | Description as given |
|---|---|
| **A** | "Studies indicate **no risk to the human fetus.**" |
| **B** | "Studies indicate **no risk to the animal fetus; information for humans is not available.**" |
| **C** | "**Adverse effects reported in the animal fetus;** information for humans is not available." |
| **D** | "**Possible fetal risk in humans has been reported;** however, in selected cases **consideration of the potential benefit versus risk may warrant use** of these drugs in pregnant women." |
| **X** | "**Fetal abnormalities have been reported,** and **positive evidence of fetal risk in humans** is available from animal and/or human studies. **These drugs are not to be used in pregnant women.**" |

**Why the letter system exists and why it is going away** (p. 34): "This system of drug classification is based **primarily on animal studies and limited human studies.** This is due in part to **ethical dilemmas surrounding the study of potential adverse effects on fetuses.**"

**The 2015 FDA rule** (p. 34), reproduced because the transition is still incomplete and the exam may use either system: "In **June 2015**, the FDA ruled... Drugs currently on the market are **being phased in to the new categories, but the transition is not complete.** This information will ultimately **replace the A to X categories.**" The new rule "requires the use of **three subsections** in the prescribing information":

| Subsection | Content as given |
|---|---|
| **"Pregnancy"** | "information on **dosing and potential risks to the developing fetus**" |
| **"Lactation"** | "information regarding breastfeeding, such as the **amount of drug in breast milk and the potential effect on the child**" |
| **"Females and Males of Reproductive Potential"** | "information about **contraception, pregnancy testing, and infertility**" |

- **The textbook's own position:** "**this book will continue to use the letter categories** and the reader is referred to individual drug package inserts for the newest information."
- **The chapter's warning to the student:** "**The student will likely encounter both the old categories (A to X) and the new rules throughout his or her career.**"

> **Trap (four-options-three-levels).** **The pregnancy categories are a five-level concept and each level needs its own cue.** Cue: ***A = no risk in HUMANS (the only category with human evidence of safety); B = no risk in ANIMALS, humans unknown; C = adverse effects IN ANIMALS, humans unknown; D = possible risk in HUMANS reported, but benefit may still justify use; X = fetal abnormalities confirmed, NEVER use.*** The discriminator between A and B is **which species was studied**; between C and D is **which species showed harm**; between D and X is **whether benefit can ever justify it.**

> **Trap (confusable pair).** **B and C are the pair most often swapped, because both say "information for humans is not available."** Cue: ***B is the good news about animals (no risk); C is the bad news about animals (adverse effects reported).*** Note how many drugs in this course sit at C or D: Ch 44's NSAIDs are **C in the first two trimesters and D in the third**; Ch 10's opioids are mostly **D**; Ch 38's tetracyclines are **D**. A category letter alone does not mean "do not give."

> **Trap (level/stage swap).** **First trimester = greatest DANGER; last trimester = greatest TRANSFER.** The two superlatives point at different trimesters and the chapter puts them in one sentence precisely because they are counterintuitive together. Cue: *early = **organs are being built**, so damage is structural; late = **blood flow and surface area are maximal**, so more drug actually arrives.* An option saying the third trimester is when defects are most likely has fused the two.

> **Trap (plausible-but-adjacent).** **Review question 5 (p. 44)** gives a patient at 32 weeks asking about an OTC drug rated **pregnancy category A**, and the options paraphrase categories A through D. Held against Table 3.1, category A means "**studies indicate no risk to the human fetus.**" The distractors describe **C** ("has not caused problems in animals, but no testing has been done in humans" — which is actually a garbled **B**), **D**, and **X**. Official key not in the pull; see GAPS item 7.

## §2 Drug therapy during breastfeeding

**The risk** (p. 34): "Breastfed infants are at risk for exposure to drugs consumed by the mother. **A wide variety of drugs easily cross from the mother's circulation into the breast milk** and subsequently to the breastfeeding infant."

**The three properties that increase transfer** (p. 34): "The primary drug characteristics that increase the likelihood of drug transfer via breastfeeding include **fat solubility, low molecular weight, and high concentration.**"

**The reassuring counterweight** (p. 34): "Fortunately, **breast milk is not the primary route for maternal drug excretion. Drug levels in breast milk are usually lower than those in the maternal circulation.** The actual amount of exposure **depends largely on the volume of milk consumed.**"

**The decision rule** (p. 34): "The ultimate decision as to whether a breastfeeding mother takes a particular drug **depends on the risk/benefit ratio**... [and] "must be considered on a **case-by-case basis.**"

> **Trap (confusable pair).** **Fat solubility increases transfer into breast milk; the same property increases distribution into fat stores in older adults (§4) and is what makes a drug hard for the kidneys to clear (Ch 2).** Cue: *lipophilic = **goes everywhere and stays**, whether that is breast milk, adipose tissue, or the brain.* One property, three consequences across three chapters.

> **Trap (plausible-but-adjacent).** **Breast milk is not the primary route of maternal drug excretion.** An option implying that breastfeeding meaningfully clears drug from the mother has the physiology backwards; the concern runs one direction only, toward the infant.

## §3 The pediatric patient

**Table 3.2 Classification of Young Patients** (p. 35), reproduced:

| Age range | Classification |
|---|---|
| "**Younger than 38 weeks' gestation**" | **Premature or preterm infant** |
| "**Younger than 1 month**" | **Neonate or newborn infant** |
| "**1 month up to 1 year**" | **Infant** |
| "**1 year up to 12 years**" | **Child** |

Table note: "The meaning of the term **pediatric may vary with the individual drug and clinical situation.** Often the maximum age for a pediatric patient may be identified as **16 years of age.** Consult the manufacturer's guidelines."

**Why pediatric patients differ** (p. 35): "Pediatric patients handle drugs much differently than adult patients do, based primarily on the **immaturity of vital organs.** In both neonates and older pediatric patients, **anatomic structures and physiologic systems and functions are still in the process of developing.**"

**Pediatric pharmacokinetic changes** (p. 35, Lifespan Considerations box), reproduced by ADME phase:

| Phase | Changes as given |
|---|---|
| **Absorption** | "**Gastric pH is less acidic** because acid-producing cells in the stomach are **immature until approximately 1 to 2 years of age.**" "**Gastric emptying is slowed** because of a slow or irregular peristalsis." "**First-pass elimination by the liver is reduced** because of the immaturity of the liver and reduced levels of microsomal enzymes." "**Intramuscular absorption is faster and irregular.**" |
| **Distribution** | "**Total body water is 70% to 80% in full-term infants, 85% in premature newborns, and 64% in children 1 to 12 years of age.**" "**Fat content is lower** in young patients because of greater total body water." "**Protein binding is decreased** because of decreased production of protein by the immature liver." "**More drugs enter the brain** because of an **immature blood-brain barrier.**" |
| **Metabolism** | "**Levels of microsomal enzymes are decreased** because the immature liver has not yet started producing enough." "**Older children may have increased metabolism and require higher dosages** once hepatic enzymes are produced." "Many variables affect metabolism... including the status of liver enzyme production, **genetic differences,** and **substances to which the mother was exposed during pregnancy.**" |
| **Excretion** | "**Glomerular filtration rate and tubular secretion and resorption are all decreased** in young patients because of **kidney immaturity.**" "**Perfusion to the kidneys may be decreased,** which results in reduced renal function, concentrating ability, and excretion of drugs." |

**Pharmacodynamic differences** (p. 35): "**The sensitivity of receptor sites may vary with age;** thus **higher or lower dosages may be required** depending on the drug. In addition, **rapidly developing tissues may be more sensitive to certain drugs,** and therefore **smaller dosages may be required.**"

**The three drugs contraindicated during the growth years** (p. 35), which is the pharmacology payload of this section:

- "**tetracycline may permanently discolor a young person's teeth**"
- "**corticosteroids may suppress growth when given systemically (but not when delivered via asthma inhalers, for example)**"
- "**quinolone antibiotics may damage cartilage**"

**Characteristics affecting dosage** (p. 36), reproduced as the list it is:

- "**Skin is thinner and more permeable.**"
- "**Stomach lacks acid to kill bacteria.**"
- "**Lungs have weaker mucous barriers.**"
- "**Body temperature is less well regulated, and dehydration occurs easily.**"
- "**Liver and kidneys are immature,** and therefore drug metabolism and excretion are impaired."

**Dosage calculation** (p. 36): "**Calculating the dosage according to the body weight is the most commonly used method today. Most drug references recommend dosages based on milligrams per kilogram of body weight.**"

**The four pieces of information needed:**

1. "**Drug order**"
2. "**Pediatric patient's weight in kilograms (1 kg = 2.2 lb)** (e.g., a **10-lb baby weighs 4.5 kg;** divide the number of pounds by 2.2)"
3. "**Pediatric dosage as per manufacturer or drug formulary guidelines**"
4. "**Information regarding available dosage forms**"

**The five-step procedure** (p. 36), reproduced:

1. "**Determine the pediatric patient's weight in kilograms.**"
2. "Use a current drug reference to determine the **usual dosage range per 24 hours in milligrams per kilogram.** It must be noted that **some drugs are stated as mg/kg per dose.**"
3. "**Determine the dose parameters** by multiplying the weight by the **minimum and maximum daily doses** of the drug (**the safe range**)."
4. "Determine the **total amount of the drug to administer per dose and per day.**"
5. "**Compare the drug dosage prescribed with the calculated safe range.**"

**The stop rule** (p. 36): "**If the drug dosage raises any concerns or varies from the safe range, contact the health care provider or prescriber immediately and do not give the drug!**"

Two Clinical Pearls (p. 36):

- "**Pediatric doses use kilograms, not pounds. Take the weight in pounds and divide by 2.2 to get kilograms.**"
- "**Most pediatric doses are in mg/kg/day; however, some are in mg/kg/dose.**"

**The named error** (p. 36): "**A common source of medication error and potential toxicity is confusing pounds with kilograms.** Unless otherwise noted, the child's weight is to be given in **kilograms, not pounds.**"

**What else must be considered** (p. 36): "In calculating pediatric dosages, the factor of **organ maturity** always must be considered **along with age and weight.**"

**Developmental-stage administration guidance** (p. 36, summarized rather than reproduced; see the curation note). The box gives age-banded approaches from infancy to adolescence, and the discriminating idea per band is:

| Stage | The chapter's central approach |
|---|---|
| **Infants** | secure positioning, procedure performed "**swiftly and safely**"; allow **self-comforting** (pacifier, fingers in mouth) |
| **Toddlers** | "**brief, concrete explanation**... but with **realistic expectations of the child's actual understanding**"; hold securely; accept aggressive behavior "as a **healthy response, but only within reasonable limits**"; comfort **immediately after** |
| **Preschoolers** | "**brief, concrete explanation** at the patient's level **with the parent or caregiver present**"; "make use of **magical thinking** (e.g., using ointments or 'special medicines' to make discomfort go away)" |
| **School-age** | "**Explain the procedure, allowing for some control** over body and situation"; therapeutic play and art; "**set age-appropriate behavior limits** (e.g., okay to cry or scream, but not to bite)" |
| **Adolescents** | "**Prepare the patient in advance**... but **without scare tactics**"; "allow for expression in a way that **does not cause losing face**"; "**encourage self-expression, individuality, and self-care**" and "**participation in procedures**" |

> **Trap (level/stage swap).** **The four age bands are contiguous and the boundaries are exact.** Cue: ***premature = under 38 weeks' gestation; neonate = under 1 month; infant = 1 month up to 1 year; child = 1 year up to 12 years.*** The word **"neonate"** covers only the first month, which is far narrower than casual usage. Compare Ch 38's **sulfonamides contraindicated in infants younger than 2 months** and Ch 10's pediatric dosing cautions, both of which sit inside the neonate/infant boundary.

> **Trap (confusable pair).** **mg/kg/DAY vs. mg/kg/DOSE.** The chapter gives this its own Clinical Pearl because the error is a factor of however many doses per day. Cue: *most are **per day** and get divided; some are **per dose** and do not.* Reading the reference wrong in this one respect multiplies or divides the dose by three or four.

> **Trap (priority question).** **Divide pounds by 2.2, and never assume the recorded weight is in kilograms.** The chapter names pound/kilogram confusion as "a common source of medication error and potential toxicity." A stem giving a weight in pounds and a dose in mg/kg is testing the conversion; a stem where the weight's units are ambiguous is testing whether you verify before calculating.

> **Trap (priority question).** **If the calculated dose falls outside the safe range, do not give the drug.** The chapter says it with an exclamation mark: "contact the health care provider or prescriber immediately **and do not give the drug!**" Options that administer and then notify, or that adjust the dose independently, both fail.

> **Trap (SATA, five options mapped to categories).** **Review question 1 (p. 44)** asks which factors put the neonate at risk, offering **less acidic gastric pH, increased peristalsis in the GI tract, immature renal function, reduced first-pass elimination in the liver, and decreased protein binding.** Held against the p. 35 box: less acidic gastric pH, immature renal function, reduced first-pass elimination, and decreased protein binding are all listed. **Increased peristalsis** is the reversal — the box says "gastric emptying is **slowed** because of a **slow or irregular** peristalsis." Official key not in the pull; see GAPS item 7.

> **Trap (confusable pair).** **Review question 2 (p. 44)** asks what distinguishes infants and offers increased protein in circulation, fat composition lower than 0.001%, more muscular body composition, and **water composition of approximately 75%.** The box gives total body water as "**70% to 80% in full-term infants**," and says protein binding is **decreased** and fat content is **lower** — but "lower than 0.001%" is an absurd figure, not the chapter's. Three distractors, three different kinds of wrong: a **reversal**, an **impossible number**, and an **unsupported claim**.

## §4 The older adult patient

**The definition and the trend** (p. 37): "An older adult patient is defined as a person who is **65 years of age or older**... In this textbook, the word **older adult** is used instead of the word **geriatric or elderly;** however, these terms are synonymous."

**Population figures** (p. 37, box and body):

| Year | Percentage older than 65 |
|---|---|
| **1900** | **4%** |
| **2000** | **12%** |
| **2040** | **21.7%** |

- Body text: "Patients older than 65 years of age constituted **15.2%** of the population in **2016** but that number is expected to increase to **21.7% by 2040.** The **85 and over** population is projected to **more than double from 6.4 million (2016) to 14.6 million in 2040.**"
- "Life expectancy is currently approximately **86.6 years for women and 84.3 years for men.**"
- The historical contrast: "At the beginning of the twentieth century, older adults constituted a mere 4%... At that time, **more people died of infections than of chronic illnesses** such as heart disease, cancer, and diabetes."

**Drug consumption** (p. 37): "The older adult population **consumes a larger proportion of all medications** than other population groups." A survey of people aged **62 to 85** showed "**at least one prescription medication was used by 87%,** while **36%... used five or more medications** and **38% used over-the-counter medications.**"

- **Commonly prescribed for older adults:** "**antihypertensives, beta blockers, diuretics, insulin, and potassium supplements.**"
- **Most commonly used OTC drugs:** "**analgesics, laxatives, and nonsteroidal antiinflammatory drugs (NSAIDs).**"

**Polypharmacy** (p. 37): "The simultaneous use of multiple medications... **As the number of medications a person takes increases, so does the risk for drug interaction and ADRs.**"

- **Why it happens:** "the more frequent occurrence of chronic diseases and the multiple drug options available for treatment."
- **What compounds it:** "**reduced visual acuity and manual dexterity**"; **pill splitting**, which "occurs commonly for financial reasons, because lower- and higher-strength tablets often have similar costs" and which "**some insurance companies require**"; "**lack of adequate patient education**"; and "**use of multiple prescribers and multiple pharmacies.**"
- **The single-pharmacy recommendation:** "it is very important for the patient to **use only one pharmacy** so that **monitoring for drug interactions and duplicate therapy can occur.**"

**The prescribing cascade** (p. 37): "Some drugs may be given specifically to **counteract the adverse effects of other drugs** (e.g., a **potassium supplement to counteract the potassium loss caused by certain diuretic medications**), which is one example of what is known as the **prescribing cascade.**"

- **Why it is hazardous:** "Sometimes it is **difficult to distinguish adverse drug effects from disease symptoms.** Although such prescribing is sometimes appropriate, it also **increases the potential for more adverse drug events** (including drug interactions, hospitalization or prolonged hospital stays, **hip fractures secondary to drug-induced falls,** addiction risk, anorexia, confusion, urinary retention, and fatigue)."

**The dosing rule** (p. 37): "Appropriate drug doses for older adults may sometimes be **one-half to two-thirds of the standard adult dose.** As a general rule, dosing for the older adult should follow the admonition '**Start low and go slow,**' which means to start with the **lowest possible dose** (often less than an average adult dose) and **increase the dose slowly, based on patient response.**"

**Nonadherence** (p. 37): "reported to occur in roughly **55% of older adult patients** and is associated with **increased rates of hospitalization.** Some patients want to adhere... but **truly cannot afford the medicine.** Patients in this situation need to be referred to a **health care social worker or their prescriber.**"

**Table 3.3 Physiologic Changes in the Older Adult Patient** (p. 38), reproduced:

| System | Physiologic change and its consequence |
|---|---|
| **Cardiovascular** | "↓ **Cardiac output** = ↓ absorption and distribution"; "↓ **Blood flow** = ↓ absorption and distribution" |
| **Gastrointestinal** | "↑ **pH (alkaline gastric secretions)** = altered absorption"; "↓ **Peristalsis** = delayed gastric emptying" |
| **Hepatic** | "↓ **Enzyme production** = ↓ metabolism"; "↓ **Blood flow** = ↓ metabolism" |
| **Renal** | "↓ **Blood flow** = ↓ excretion"; "↓ **Function** = ↓ excretion"; "↓ **Glomerular filtration rate** = ↓ excretion" |

**Older adult pharmacokinetic changes** (p. 38, Lifespan Considerations box), reproduced by ADME phase with the chapter's numbers:

| Phase | Changes as given |
|---|---|
| **Absorption** | "**Gastric pH is less acidic** because of a gradual reduction in the production of hydrochloric acid." "**Gastric emptying is slowed** because of a decline in smooth muscle tone and motor activity." "Movement throughout the GI tract is slower." "**Blood flow to the GI tract is reduced by 40% to 50%** because of decreased cardiac output and decreased perfusion." "**The absorptive surface area is decreased** because the aging process **blunts and flattens villi.**" |
| **Distribution** | "In adults **40 to 60** years of age, total body water is **55% in males and 47% in females;** in those **older than 60,** total body water is **52% in males and 46% in females.**" "Decrease in total body water leads to **decreased distribution of some drugs, such as antibiotics, leading to risk for toxicity because of greater concentrations of drug in the blood stream.**" "**Fat content is increased** because of decreased lean body mass." "**Protein (albumin) binding sites are reduced**... leading to **greater amounts of free drug.**" |
| **Metabolism** | "**Levels of microsomal enzymes are decreased.**" "**Liver blood flow is reduced by approximately 1.5% per year after 25 years of age.**" "**Decreased metabolism leads to potential for drug toxicity.**" |
| **Excretion** | "**Glomerular filtration rate is decreased by 40% to 50%,** primarily because of decreased blood flow." "The **number of intact nephrons is decreased.**" "**Creatinine clearance is an important indicator of renal functioning,** and, therefore, if abnormal, **drug dosages may need to be adjusted.**" |

**Additional body-composition figures from the body text** (p. 39): "In both men and women, there is an approximately **20% reduction in muscle mass between 25 and 65 years of age** and a corresponding **20% increase in body fat.**"

- **Absorption detail** (p. 39): "By **65 years of age, there is an approximately 50% reduction in blood flow to the gastrointestinal tract.**"
- **The laxative loop** (p. 39): reduced GI muscle tone "often results in **constipation, for which older adults frequently take laxatives. This use of laxatives may accelerate GI motility enough to actually reduce the absorption of drugs.**"
- **Renal decline** (p. 39): "Renal function declines in roughly **two-thirds of older adults.**" This "is especially true for drugs with a **low therapeutic index such as digoxin.**"
- **Blood-brain barrier** (p. 38): "older adults commonly have **increased sensitivity to central nervous system depressant medications** (e.g., anxiolytics, tricyclic antidepressants) because of **reduced integrity of the blood-brain barrier.**"

**The creatinine trap, which the chapter gives its own Clinical Pearl** (p. 38): "**A low serum creatinine does not mean good renal function in the elderly. Creatinine is a by-product of muscle mass; thus the patient with a creatinine of 0.3 mg/dL may have a creatinine clearance that is indicative of renal dysfunction.**"

- **The reasoning:** "**Because muscle mass declines with age, serum creatinine level may provide a misleading index of renal function.** For example, a frail older woman may have a reported serum creatinine value that is **lower than normal, and this may lead one to falsely think that her renal function is normal.** In actuality, because this patient has **limited muscle mass, she cannot produce creatinine.**"
- **The accurate method:** "The **most accurate way to determine creatinine clearance is by collecting a patient's urine for 24 hours. This test is quite cumbersome, however, and is not used very often.**"

**Monitoring** (p. 38): "Laboratory assessments need to be conducted **at least annually**... [and] "may be indicated more frequently (e.g., **every 1, 3, or 6 months**) in those patients requiring **higher-risk drug regimens.**" Liver function is assessed by "**aspartate aminotransferase (AST) and alanine aminotransferase (ALT)**"; kidney function by "**serum creatinine and blood urea nitrogen levels.**"

**The Beers Criteria** (p. 40, EBP box): "Since the 1990s, a very effective tool, the **Beers Criteria,** has been used to **identify drugs that may be inappropriately prescribed, ineffective, or cause adverse drug reactions in older adult patients.**"

- **Stewardship and cycle:** "The **American Geriatric Society (AGS)** has been the steward of these criteria and produce updates on a **3-year cycle,** with the most recent update in **2019.**"
- **Scope:** "intended for use in **adults 65 years and older in all ambulatory, acute, and institutionalized settings of care, except for the hospice and palliative care settings.**"
- **The 2019 findings, reproduced:** "**30 individual medications or medication classes to avoid in most older people**"; "**16 criteria specific to more than 40 medications or medication classes to use with caution or avoid** when someone lives with certain diseases or conditions"; "**Four new medications or medication classes added to the list of drugs to be used with caution, such as dextromethorphan/quinidine, trimethoprim-sulfamethoxazole (TMP-SMX), rivaroxaban, and aspirin.**"
- **Usage statistic:** "**more than 90% of the older population using at least one prescription and 66% using three or more every month.**"

> **Trap (confusable pair).** **Total body water goes in OPPOSITE directions at the two ends of life, and this is the chapter's best distractor source.** Cue: ***premature newborn 85% → full-term infant 70–80% → child 1–12 years 64% → adult male 55%/female 47% → over 60, male 52%/female 46%.*** It falls monotonically across the whole lifespan. Fat content moves the other way: **lower in the young** ("because of greater total body water"), **increased in the older adult** ("because of decreased lean body mass"). So a **water-soluble** drug is over-diluted in an infant and over-concentrated in an older adult, and a **fat-soluble** drug does the reverse.

> **Trap (confusable pair).** **Several changes are IDENTICAL at both ends of the lifespan, which is what makes the age-band questions hard.** Both neonates and older adults have: **less acidic gastric pH, slowed gastric emptying, decreased protein binding, decreased microsomal enzyme levels, and reduced renal function.** The reasons differ (**immaturity** vs. **decline**) but the directions match. Cue: *if a stem describes a change that could fit either end, the discriminator is **total body water or fat content**, not the GI or hepatic findings.*

> **Trap (level/stage swap).** **The two 40% to 50% figures are different things.** *GI **blood flow** is reduced by 40% to 50%; **glomerular filtration rate** is reduced by 40% to 50%.* Same number, different organ, different pharmacokinetic phase (absorption vs. excretion). A third figure, **1.5% per year after age 25**, belongs to **liver blood flow** and drives metabolism.

> **Trap (priority question).** **A low serum creatinine in a frail older adult is not reassurance.** The chapter gives this its own Clinical Pearl and a worked example at 0.3 mg/dL. Cue: *creatinine comes from **muscle**; less muscle means less creatinine regardless of kidney function.* A stem in which a clinician clears a drug on the strength of a low creatinine is testing exactly this.

> **Trap (confusable pair).** **Polypharmacy vs. the prescribing cascade.** Cue: ***polypharmacy = many drugs at once*** (a count); ***prescribing cascade = a drug added specifically to treat another drug's adverse effect*** (a causal chain). The chapter's example is a **potassium supplement for diuretic-induced potassium loss** — which is worth holding alongside Ch 28's diuretic classes and Ch 29's electrolytes. The cascade is one route into polypharmacy, not a synonym for it.

> **Trap (setting vs. focus).** **"Start low and go slow" is about the older adult; the pediatric analogue is weight-based calculation.** Both ends need reduced doses, but for different reasons and by different methods: older adults by **fraction of the adult dose (one-half to two-thirds)** and cautious titration; children by **mg/kg** against a calculated safe range.

> **Trap (plausible-but-adjacent).** **Review question 4 (p. 44)** describes an 88-year-old with dizziness on standing, near-fainting in the afternoons, and a **15-point systolic drop on standing**, offering NSAIDs, cardiac glycosides, anticoagulants, and **antihypertensives**. The chapter names antihypertensives, beta blockers, and diuretics among the drugs "commonly prescribed for older adults," and names "**hip fractures secondary to drug-induced falls**" among polypharmacy's adverse outcomes. Official key not in the pull; see GAPS item 7.

## §5 The two ends of the lifespan, side by side

Assembled from the two pharmacokinetic boxes (pp. 35, 38) because the exam's comparison items are built on the differences, not on either column alone.

| | **Neonate / pediatric** | **Older adult** |
|---|---|---|
| **Gastric pH** | **Less acidic** — acid-producing cells immature until **1 to 2 years** | **Less acidic** — gradual reduction in hydrochloric acid production |
| **Gastric emptying** | **Slowed** — slow or irregular peristalsis | **Slowed** — decline in smooth muscle tone and motor activity |
| **GI blood flow** | not specified | **Reduced 40% to 50%**; approximately **50% reduction by age 65** |
| **Absorptive surface** | not specified | **Decreased** — aging "blunts and flattens villi" |
| **First-pass effect** | **Reduced** — immature liver, low microsomal enzymes | reduced via decreased liver blood flow |
| **IM absorption** | **Faster and irregular** | not specified |
| **Total body water** | **85%** premature, **70–80%** full-term, **64%** age 1–12 | **52%** male / **46%** female over 60 |
| **Fat content** | **Lower** (because body water is greater) | **Increased** (because lean body mass decreases); **~20% increase** between 25 and 65 |
| **Muscle mass** | not specified | **~20% reduction** between 25 and 65 |
| **Protein binding** | **Decreased** — immature liver makes less protein | **Reduced** — aging liver makes less albumin, plus reduced intake |
| **Blood-brain barrier** | **Immature** — more drugs enter the brain | **Reduced integrity** — increased sensitivity to CNS depressants |
| **Microsomal enzymes** | **Decreased** (immature); **older children may need higher doses** once enzymes appear | **Decreased**; liver blood flow falls **~1.5% per year after 25** |
| **Renal function** | **GFR, tubular secretion, and reabsorption all decreased**; perfusion may be decreased | **GFR decreased 40% to 50%**; fewer intact nephrons; declines in **two-thirds** of older adults |
| **Dosing approach** | **mg/kg** against a calculated safe range; **kg not pounds** | **one-half to two-thirds** of adult dose; "**start low and go slow**" |

> **Trap (confusable pair).** **Protein binding is decreased at BOTH ends, and the consequence is the same: more free (active) drug.** Ch 2 supplies the principle — "**only drug molecules that are not bound to plasma proteins can freely distribute**," and bound drug is "**pharmacologically inactive**." So both a neonate and an older adult on a highly protein-bound drug (Ch 2 names **warfarin and phenytoin**) carry a higher toxicity risk at the same nominal dose.

> **Trap (level/stage swap).** **The one row where the two columns point in opposite directions within pediatrics itself is metabolism.** Neonates have **decreased** enzyme levels, but "**older children may have increased metabolism and require higher dosages** once hepatic enzymes are produced." So the pediatric column is not uniformly "less" — a 7-year-old may need a **proportionally larger** mg/kg dose than an adult, which is the opposite of the reflex assumption that children always get less.
