> **Trap (confusable pair).** **Additive vs. synergistic vs. antagonistic, and the chapter gives you the arithmetic.** Cue: ***additive = 1 + 1 = 2; synergistic = 1 + 1 > 2; antagonistic = 1 + 1 < 2.*** All three appear in the drug chapters: **SMX-TMP** is synergistic (Ch 38), an **aminoglycoside plus a beta-lactam** is synergistic (Ch 39), and **a tetracycline plus a bactericidal antibiotic** is antagonistic (Ch 38). Note that Ch 10's key term box defines *synergistic effects* with the same "1 + 1 is greater than 2" wording, so the definition is consistent across chapters.

> **Trap (confusable pair).** **The four "pharmaco-" studies, distinguished by direction and subject.** Cue: ***pharmaco**kinetics = what the **body does to the drug**; **pharmaco**dynamics = what the **drug does to the body**; **pharmaceutics** = what the **dosage form** does; **pharmacotherapeutics** = the **clinical use.*** Pharmacology is the umbrella over all of them. Fig. 2.2 orders the first three as sequential **phases**: pharmaceutical → pharmacokinetic → pharmacodynamic.

> **Trap (confusable pair).** **Peak effect vs. peak level; onset of action vs. duration of action.** Cue: ***effect** words describe the **therapeutic response**; **level** words describe the **blood concentration.*** The chapter states the split at p. 25: "The terms **onset, peak, duration, and trough** are used to describe **drug effects. Peak and trough** are also used to describe **drug concentrations,** which are usually measured from blood samples." So *peak* does double duty and the stem's other words decide which sense is meant.

> **Trap (confusable pair).** **Adverse drug event vs. adverse drug reaction vs. adverse effects vs. medication error.** Four overlapping entries. Cue: ***ADE is the broadest*** (any undesirable occurrence, including **failing to administer**); ***ADR*** is narrower and specifically at **therapeutic dosages, not overdose**; ***medication error*** is "any **preventable** ADE" and **may or may not cause harm**; ***allergic reaction*** is called "**a type of adverse drug event.**" A stem describing a dose that was omitted is still an **ADE**.

> **Trap (confusable pair).** **Parent drug vs. metabolite vs. prodrug.** Cue: *the **parent drug** is what you administer; a **prodrug** is a parent drug that is **inactive until metabolized**; an **active metabolite** has activity **even if the parent did not.*** The worked example elsewhere in the course is **cefuroxime axetil** (Ch 38), "a **prodrug**... hydrolyzed in the liver to its active cefuroxime form."

> **Trap (plausible-but-adjacent).** **An agonist "binds to and stimulates"; an antagonist "binds to and inhibits."** Both bind. The distinction is what happens after binding, not whether attachment occurs, and Fig. 2.7 makes the point visually: the antagonist occupies the site and produces a "blocked response." Ch 10 uses the same three-way scheme for opioids and adds **partial agonist / agonist-antagonist** as a fourth.

## §1 Drug names, classification, and the drug lifecycle

**The three names every drug acquires** (p. 15):

| Name | Definition as given | Example (Fig. 2.1) |
|---|---|---|
| **Chemical name** | "describes the drug's **chemical composition and molecular structure**" | "(±)-2-(p-isobutylphenyl) propionic acid" |
| **Generic name** (nonproprietary) | "often much shorter and simpler... used in **most official drug compendiums** to list drugs"; assigned by the **United States Adopted Names Council**; **not trademarked** | **ibuprofen** |
| **Trade name** (proprietary) | "the drug's **registered trademark**" indicating "its commercial use is **restricted to the owner of the patent**" | **Motrin,** others |

- **Why trade names are catchier:** "Trade names are generally created by the manufacturer **with marketability in mind.** For this reason, they are usually **shorter and easier to pronounce and remember** than generic drug names."

**Patents and generics** (p. 15):

- "The **patent life**... of a newly discovered drug molecule is normally **17 years.**"
- "The research process for new drug development normally requires about **10 years,** and the manufacturer generally has the **remaining 7 years for sales profits** before patent expiration."
- "**After the patent expires,** other manufacturers may legally begin to manufacture **generic drugs with the same active ingredient.** At this point, the **drug price usually decreases substantially.**"
- **Biosimilars:** "A new category of the generic drug market is called **biosimilars. Biosimilar, by definition, is a copy version of an already authorized biological product.**"
- **Therapeutic equivalence:** "many institutions have implemented programs in which **one drug in a class of several drugs is chosen as the preferred agent, even though the drugs do not have the same active ingredients.** This is called **therapeutic equivalence.** Before one drug can be therapeutically substituted for another, the drugs must have been **proven to have the same therapeutic effect** on the body."
- **Bioequivalence** (p. 18): "If two drug products have the **same bioavailability and same concentration of active ingredient,** they are said to be **bioequivalent** (e.g., a brand-name drug and the same generic drug)."

**Drug classification** (p. 15): "Drugs can be classified by their **structure** (e.g., beta-adrenergic blockers) or by their **therapeutic use** (e.g., antibiotics, antihypertensives, antidepressants). Within the broad classification, each class may have **subclasses;** for example, **penicillins are a subclass within the group of antibiotics.**"

- **Prototypical drugs:** "the **first drug in a class** of drugs and are noted as **key drugs** throughout this textbook."

**FDA regulation and off-label use** (p. 16): "The US Food and Drug Administration (FDA) regulates the approval and clinical use of all drugs in the United States, including the requirement of an **expiration date** on all drugs. **Only FDA-approved indications are permitted to be described in the manufacturer's written information, or labeling.** At times, prescribers may choose to use drugs for **non–FDA-approved indications. This is known as off-label prescribing.** Evolving over time in clinical practice, previously off-label indications often become FDA-approved indications."

**The four sources of drugs** (p. 30, pharmacognosy): "**plants, animals, minerals, and laboratory synthesis.**"

| Source | Examples as given |
|---|---|
| **Plants** | "many **weak acids and weak bases (alkaloids)**" |
| **Animals** | "**conjugated estrogens** are derived from the **urine of pregnant mares**, hence the drug trade name **Premarin. Equine** is the term used for any horse-derived drug. **Insulin** comes from **pigs (porcine)** and humans. **Heparin** is... derived from pigs (**porcine heparin**)" |
| **Minerals** | "**salicylic acid, aluminum hydroxide, and sodium chloride**" |
| **Synthesis** | "many drugs in current use are synthetically derived, [but] **most were first isolated in nature**" |

> **Trap (confusable pair).** **Generic vs. trade name, and which is trademarked.** Cue: *the **trade** name is **traded** (commercial, trademarked, capitalized, marketing-driven); the **generic** name is **generic** (public, lowercase, from the USAN Council).* This chapter's rule explains a hazard Ch 38 spends a whole safety box on: **Zosyn and Unasyn** are trade names for penicillin products, and "trade names **don't always end in 'cillin.'**"

> **Trap (level/stage swap).** **17 years total, 10 in research, 7 for profit.** Three numbers that must stay in that relationship. An option that gives 17 years of *marketing exclusivity* has confused patent life with the post-approval remainder.

> **Trap (confusable pair).** **Bioequivalence vs. therapeutic equivalence.** Cue: ***bio**equivalent = **same active ingredient,** same bioavailability and concentration (a brand and its generic); **therapeutic** equivalence = **different** active ingredients that produce the **same therapeutic effect,** used for formulary substitution.* An option treating them as synonyms misses that therapeutic equivalence explicitly covers drugs that "**do not have the same active ingredients.**"

> **Trap (plausible-but-adjacent).** **Off-label prescribing is legal.** The chapter frames it neutrally and notes that off-label uses "often become FDA-approved indications." What is restricted is the **manufacturer's labeling,** not the prescriber's judgment.

## §2 Pharmaceutics: dosage form determines absorption

"**Pharmaceutics is the study of how various dosage forms influence the way in which the drug affects the body**" (p. 16). "**Dosage form determines the rate at which drug dissolution... occurs**" (p. 16).

**Table 2.1 Drug Absorption of Various Oral Preparations** (p. 16), reproduced **in the source's own order, fastest to slowest**:

| Rank | Preparation |
|---|---|
| **Fastest** | **Oral disintegration, buccal tablets, and oral soluble wafers** |
| | Liquids, elixirs, and syrups |
| | Suspension solutions |
| | Powders |
| | Capsules |
| | Tablets |
| | Coated tablets |
| **Slowest** | **Enteric-coated tablets** |

- **Why liquids beat solids** (p. 16): "Oral drugs that are liquids (e.g., elixirs, syrups) are **already dissolved** and are usually absorbed more quickly than solid dosage forms."
- **Why enteric-coated is slowest** (p. 16): "**Enteric-coated tablets... have a coating that prevents them from being broken down in the acidic pH environment of the stomach and are not absorbed until they reach the higher (more alkaline) pH of the intestines.** This pharmaceutical property results in **slower dissolution and therefore slower absorption.**"
- **Particle size matters** (p. 16): "Particle size within a tablet or capsule can make different dosage forms of the **same drug** dissolve at different rates... An example is the difference between **micronized glyburide and nonmicronized glyburide. Micronized glyburide reaches a maximum concentration peak faster.**"

|---|---|

| **Parenteral** | "Injectable forms, solutions, suspensions, emulsions, **powders for reconstitution**" |
| **Topical** | "Aerosols, ointments, creams, pastes, powders, solutions, foams, gels, **transdermal patches, inhalers,** rectal and vaginal suppositories" |

**Extended-release nomenclature** (p. 17): "Extended-release dosage forms are normally easily identified by various capital letter abbreviations attached to their names."

| Abbreviation | Meaning as given |
|---|---|
| **SR** | slow release or sustained release |
| **SA** | sustained action |
| **CR** | controlled release |
| **XL** | extended length |
| **XT** | extended time |

Clinical Pearl (p. 17): "**Do not crush drugs that have SR, SA, CR, XL, or XT as part of their name — they are extended-release formulations.**"

- **Why not** (p. 17): "**Extended-release oral dosage forms must not be crushed, because this could cause accelerated release of drug from the dosage form and possible toxicity. Enteric-coated tablets also are not recommended for crushing.** This would cause disruption of the tablet coating designed to **protect the stomach lining** from the local effects of the drug and/or **protect the drug from being prematurely disrupted by stomach acid.**"
- **What you can do instead:** "Capsules, powder, or liquid contents can often be added to **soft foods such as applesauce or pudding** or dissolved in a beverage. **Granules contained in capsules are usually for extended drug release and normally should not be crushed or chewed... However, they can often be swallowed when sprinkled on one of the soft foods. Consultation with a pharmacist** or use of other suitable source is necessary if any question exists."
- **The benefit that justifies the risk:** "Extended-release tablets and capsules release drug molecules in the patient's GI tract **over a prolonged period.** This ultimately **prolongs drug absorption as well as duration of action**... **Convenience of administration correlates strongly with patient adherence** because these forms often require **fewer daily doses.**"

**Combination dosage forms** (p. 16): "contain **multiple drugs in one dose** — for example, the cholesterol and antihypertensive medications **atorvastatin/amlodipine tablets called Caduet.**"

**Parenteral form requirements** (p. 17): "The arteries and veins that carry drugs throughout the body **can easily be damaged if the drug is too concentrated or corrosive. The pH of injections must be very similar to that of the blood.** Parenteral dosage forms that are injected intravenously are **immediately placed into solution in the bloodstream and do not have to be dissolved in the body. Therefore 100% absorption is assumed to occur immediately upon intravenous injection.**"

> **Trap (level/stage swap).** **Table 2.1 is an ordering item waiting to happen.** Cue: *the ranking runs **dissolve-in-the-mouth → liquids → suspensions → powders → capsules → tablets → coated → enteric-coated.*** The two anchors are what matter: **oral disintegrating/buccal is fastest** because no dissolution is needed, and **enteric-coated is slowest** because dissolution is deliberately postponed to the intestine.

> **Trap (priority question).** **Never crush SR, SA, CR, XL, XT, or enteric-coated.** Cue: *crushing an extended-release form **dumps the whole dose at once → toxicity**; crushing an enteric coat **exposes the stomach** (or exposes the drug to acid).* Two different harms, one action. This is the same rule Ch 10 attaches to **MS Contin, OxyContin, and Kadian** ("cannot be crushed," with **death** named as the consequence) and Ch 38 attaches to **extended-release clarithromycin.**

> **Trap (confusable pair).** **Enteric coating protects two different things depending on the drug.** The chapter names both purposes in one sentence: the coating protects "**the stomach lining from the local effects of the drug**" and/or protects "**the drug from being prematurely disrupted by stomach acid.**" A stem asking why a tablet is enteric-coated may want either.

> **Trap (plausible-but-adjacent).** **Review question 5 (p. 32)** teaches an enteric-coated stool softener and offers, among others, taking it with **2 to 3 ounces of orange juice**, drinking **6 to 8 ounces of water**, avoiding all other medications with any enteric-coated tablet, **crushing** it for swallowing difficulty, and swallowing it **whole without chewing.** Held against this chapter: crushing is contraindicated; the chapter's general instruction is that medications on an empty stomach are taken "**with at least 6 ounces of water**" (Box 2.1); and an **acidic** vehicle works against an enteric coating, which is designed to survive acid and dissolve in the alkaline intestine. Official key not in the pull; see GAPS item 8.

## §3 Absorption, bioavailability, and the first-pass effect

**Absorption** (p. 17): "the movement of a drug from its **site of administration into the bloodstream** for distribution to the tissues."

**Bioavailability** (p. 17): "the term used to express the **extent of drug absorption.**"

**The first-pass effect** (p. 17): "A drug that is absorbed from the intestine **must first pass through the liver before it reaches the systemic circulation. If a large proportion of a drug is chemically changed into inactive metabolites in the liver, then a much smaller amount of drug will pass into the circulation** (i.e., will be bioavailable). Such a drug is said to have a **high first-pass effect. First-pass effect reduces the bioavailability of the drug to less than 100%.**"

- **The consequence:** "Many drugs administered by mouth have a **bioavailability of less than 100%,** whereas **drugs administered by the intravenous route are 100% bioavailable.**"

| **Rectal** (footnoted: "**Leads to both first-pass and non–first-pass effects**") | |

**Rectal's split personality** (p. 19): "**All topical routes of drug administration avoid first-pass effects of the liver, with the exception of rectal administration.** Because the **rectum is part of the GI tract,** some drug will be absorbed into the capillaries that feed the portal vein to the liver. However, **some drugs also will be absorbed locally into perirectal tissues.**"

**Factors that alter absorption** (p. 18):

- **Route:** "How a drug is administered, or its route of administration, affects the rate and extent of absorption."
- **Stomach acidity,** which varies with "the **time of day;** the **age** of the patient; and the presence and types of **medications, foods, or beverages.**"
- **Food:** "Taking an **enteric-coated medication with a large amount of food** may cause it to be **dissolved by acidic stomach contents** and thus reduce intestinal drug absorption and **negate the coating's stomach-protective properties.**" But "**the presence of food may enhance the absorption of some fat-soluble drugs** or drugs that are more easily broken down in an acidic environment."
- **GI transit time:** "**Anticholinergic drugs slow GI transit time**... This may **reduce the amount of drug absorption for acid-susceptible drugs.**"
- **Surgical anatomy:** "**short bowel syndrome**" from small-intestine resection, and **bariatric weight-loss surgery,** where "stomach contents are delivered to the intestines more rapidly than usual. This is called **gastric dumping.**"
- **Blood flow:** "The stomach and small intestine are **highly vascularized. When blood flow to this area is decreased, absorption also may be decreased. Sepsis and exercise** are examples... blood tends to be **routed to the heart and other vital organs,** [and in exercise] "**to the skeletal muscles.**"

**Box 2.1 Drugs to Be Taken on an Empty Stomach and Drugs to Be Taken With Food** (p. 18), reproduced:

| | Drugs as given |
|---|---|
| **Empty stomach** (with at least **6 ounces of water**) | "**alendronate sodium and risedronate sodium**" |
| **With food** | "**carbamazepine, iron and iron-containing products, hydralazine, lithium, propranolol, spironolactone, nonsteroidal antiinflammatory drugs, and theophylline**" |

**Route speed ranking** (p. 18): "**The parenteral route is the fastest route by which a drug can be absorbed, followed by the enteral and topical routes.**"

**The routes in detail:**

| Route | Key facts as given (pp. 18–19) |
|---|---|
| **Enteral** | "absorbed into the systemic circulation through the **mucosa of the stomach and/or small or large intestine.** Orally administered drugs are absorbed from the intestinal lumen into the blood system and **transported to the liver.**" |
| **Sublingual and buccal** | "absorbed into the **highly vascularized tissue under the tongue**... absorbed **rapidly** because the area has a **large blood supply. These drugs bypass the liver and yet are systemically bioavailable.**" Buccal is "the oral mucosa between the **cheek and the gum.**" Example: **sublingual nitroglycerin** |
| **Parenteral** | "a general term meaning **any route of administration other than the GI tract.** It most commonly refers to injection." Sub-routes: "**intradermally, subcutaneously, intraarterially, intramuscularly, intrathecally, intraarticularly, or intravenously. Intraarterial, intrathecal, or intraarticular injections are usually given by physicians.**" IM and subcutaneous are "absorbed **more slowly** than those given intravenously... usually absorbed over a period of **several hours**"; **depot drugs** are "designed for **slow absorption over a period of several days to a few months or longer.**" |
| **Topical** | "application of medications to various body surfaces... **skin, eyes, ears, nose, lungs, rectum, or vagina.** Delivers a **uniform amount of drug over a longer period,** but effects are "**slower in their onset and more prolonged in their duration.**" |

- **The topical hazard** (p. 19): "This can be a problem if the patient **begins to experience adverse effects** from the drug and **a considerable amount of drug has already been absorbed.**"

> **Trap (confusable pair).** **First-pass vs. non–first-pass, and rectal is the exception on both lists.** Cue: *anything that reaches the **portal circulation** gets first-passed, so **oral and rectal** do; **sublingual, buccal, IV, IM, subcutaneous, and transdermal** do not.* Rectal is footnoted as **both.** This is the concept behind Ch 10's equianalgesic table: **oral morphine 30 mg = parenteral morphine 10 mg** precisely because the oral route loses two-thirds to first pass.

> **Trap (confusable pair).** **Sublingual and buccal are enteral by dosage form but non–first-pass by circulation.** Table 2.2 lists "sublingual or buccal tablets" under **Enteral**; Box 2.2 lists both under **non–first-pass.** Both are correct, and the apparent contradiction is the trap: the route is classified by **where the drug is placed,** the first-pass status by **which vein drains it.**

> **Trap (level/stage swap).** **The route speed ranking is parenteral → enteral → topical.** Topical is **slowest to onset and longest in duration**, which is why the chapter flags it as hazardous once adverse effects appear. Compare Ch 10's **transdermal fentanyl**, which takes 6 to 12 hours to work and 17 hours to halve after removal.

> **Trap (setting vs. focus).** **Critical thinking exercise 1 (p. 31)** gives a trauma patient in shock with decreased cardiac output and urine output under 30 mL/hr, and asks the best route. The chapter's relevant facts: parenteral is fastest; **IV is 100% bioavailable**; IM and subcutaneous depend on absorption "over a period of several hours"; and **decreased blood flow decreases absorption.** Review question 4 (p. 32) asks the same idea from the heart-failure side, offering altered biliary function, increased glomerular filtration, reduced liver metabolism, and **diminished circulation.** Official keys not in the pull; see GAPS item 8.

> **Trap (plausible-but-adjacent).** **Box 2.1's third category is the interesting one.** Macrolides and oral opioids are *specified* for an empty stomach but *given* with food to reduce GI irritation. Ch 38 says exactly this about **erythromycin**: absorption "is enhanced if it is taken on an empty stomach, but because of the high incidence of stomach irritation... many of these drugs are taken with a meal or snack." The absorption rule and the tolerance rule point in opposite directions and the book chooses tolerance.

## §4 Distribution

"**Distribution** is... transport of the drug through the bloodstream to its site of action" (p. 21).

**Speed of distribution by tissue** (p. 21):

| Speed | Tissues as given |
|---|---|
| **Rapid** | "**heart, liver, kidneys, and brain**" |
| **Slower** | "**muscle, skin, and fat**" |

**Protein binding, which is the testable core** (p. 22):

- "**Only drug molecules that are not bound to plasma proteins can freely distribute to extravascular tissue** (outside the blood vessels) **to reach their site of action.** If a drug is bound to plasma proteins, the **drug-protein complex is generally too large to pass through the walls of blood capillaries** into tissues."
- **Albumin:** "the **most common blood protein** and carries the **majority of protein-bound drug molecules.**"
- **The active fraction:** "This **unbound portion is pharmacologically active** and is considered '**free**' drug, whereas '**bound**' drug is **pharmacologically inactive.**"
- **Low albumin states:** "Certain conditions that cause **low albumin levels, such as extensive burns and malnourished states, result in a larger fraction of free (unbound and active) drug. This can raise the risk for drug toxicity.**"
- **Competition between drugs:** "When an individual is taking **two medications that are highly protein bound, the medications may compete for binding sites** on the albumin molecule. Because of this competition, **there is more free or unbound drug.** Protein binding may lead to an **unpredictable drug response called a drug-drug interaction.**"

**Volume of distribution** (p. 22): "A **theoretical volume**... used to describe the various areas in which drugs may be distributed. These areas, or **compartments,** may be the **blood (intravascular space), total body water, body fat, or other body tissues and organs.**"

| Drug type | Volume of distribution | Blood concentration |
|---|---|---|
| "highly **water-soluble (hydrophilic)**" | "**smaller** volume of distribution" | "**high** blood concentrations" |
| "**fat-soluble (lipophilic)**" | "**larger** volume of distribution" | "**low** blood concentrations" |

**Hard-to-reach sites** (p. 22): "There are some sites in the body into which it may be very difficult to distribute a drug. These sites typically either have a **poor blood supply (e.g., bone)** or have **physiologic barriers** that make it difficult for drugs to pass through (**e.g., the brain due to the blood-brain barrier**)."

> **Trap (confusable pair).** **Bound is inactive; free is active.** Counterintuitive if you assume "bound to a carrier" means "being delivered." Cue: *bound = **stuck in the bloodstream, too big to leave, doing nothing**; free = **out in the tissue, working.*** Everything downstream follows: low albumin → more free drug → **toxicity**; two protein-bound drugs together → each displaces the other → **more free drug from both.**

> **Trap (confusable pair).** **Protein-binding displacement is a distribution-phase interaction, not a metabolic one.** Table 2.10's distribution row gives **warfarin with amiodarone**: "Both drugs compete for protein-binding sites... **Higher levels of free (unbound) warfarin and amiodarone, which increases actions of both drugs (harmful).**" Compare the metabolism row, where the mechanism is enzyme competition instead. Ch 38 uses the same concept for **macrolides**, which are "**highly protein bound**" and metabolized in the liver, giving them two interaction mechanisms at once.

> **Trap (level/stage swap).** **Hydrophilic drugs have a SMALL volume of distribution and HIGH blood levels.** The relationship is inverse and easy to flip. Cue: *water-soluble drugs **stay in the water compartment** (the blood), so the volume is small and the concentration is high; fat-soluble drugs **disperse into fat everywhere,** so the apparent volume is large and blood levels are low.*

> **Trap (plausible-but-adjacent).** **Bone is hard to reach because of poor blood supply, not because of a barrier.** The chapter names two separate reasons and gives one example of each: **bone = poor perfusion; brain = blood-brain barrier.** Ch 38 uses this: some antibiotics "do not penetrate into the site of infection, such as the **lung or bone or abscesses,** which can lead to treatment failures," and **ceftriaxone** is prized because it "**passes easily through the blood-brain barrier.**"

## §5 Metabolism

"Metabolism is also referred to as **biotransformation.** It involves the biochemical alteration of a drug into **an inactive metabolite, a more soluble compound, a more potent active metabolite** (as in the conversion of an inactive prodrug to its active form), **or a less active metabolite**" (p. 22).

- **The organ:** "**The organ most responsible for the metabolism of drugs is the liver.** Other metabolic tissues include **skeletal muscle, kidneys, lungs, plasma, and intestinal mucosa.**"

**Table 2.4 Mechanisms of Biotransformation** (p. 23), reproduced:

| Type | Mechanism | Result |
|---|---|---|
| **Oxidation, Reduction, Hydrolysis** | "Chemical reactions" | "**Increase polarity** of chemical, making it **more water-soluble and more easily excreted.** This often results in a **loss of pharmacologic activity.**" |
| **Conjugation** (glucuronidation, glycination, sulfation, methylation, alkylation) | "Combination with another substance (e.g., glucuronide, glycine, sulfate, methyl groups, alkyl groups)" | "Forms a **less toxic product** with **less activity.**" |

**Cytochrome P-450** (p. 22): "Hepatic metabolism involves the activity of a **very large class of enzymes known as cytochrome P-450 enzymes** (or simply P-450 enzymes), also known as **microsomal enzymes.**"

- **What they target:** "**lipid-soluble drugs** (also known as **lipophilic** ['fat loving']) that are typically **very difficult to eliminate. The P-450 enzymes are responsible for the metabolism of the majority of medications.**"
- **The alternative for water-soluble drugs:** "Medications with **water-soluble (hydrophilic)** molecules may be **more easily metabolized by simpler chemical reactions such as hydrolysis.**"
- **Substrates:** "Drug molecules that are the metabolic targets of specific enzymes are said to be **substrates** for those enzymes."
- **Why it matters:** "**The P-450 system is one of the most important systems that influences drug-drug interactions.**"

| **2E1** | acetaminophen, ethanol |

**P-glycoprotein** (p. 23): "Another common drug interaction involves a plasma membrane protein, **P-glycoprotein, which acts as a drug transport mechanism, transporting drugs out of the cell.** Many drugs can be affected at the P-glycoprotein level."

**Enzyme inducers and inhibitors** (p. 23):

| | Definition as given | Result |

| | Obstructive jaundice | **Decreased** |

| **Drugs** | Barbiturates | **Increased** |

> **Trap (confusable pair).** **Enzyme inducer vs. inhibitor, and the effect is opposite to what the word sounds like.** Cue: ***inducer → speeds metabolism → LESS drug → therapeutic failure; inhibitor → slows metabolism → MORE drug → toxicity.*** The trap is that "inhibitor" sounds like it should reduce drug effect. It reduces **enzyme** activity, which **raises** drug levels. Ch 38 names **erythromycin** as "a **strong inhibitor** of cytochrome P450 enzymes" and therefore "associated with many drug interactions," while **azithromycin** has "**minimal effects**" on P-450 and few interactions.

> **Trap (confusable pair).** **Fast vs. slow acetylator.** Cue: ***fast = increased metabolism; slow = decreased metabolism → drug accumulates → toxicity.*** Ch 38 defines **slow acetylation** as "a common genetic host factor in which the rate of metabolism of certain drugs is **reduced**" and pairs it with **G6PD deficiency** as the two genetic host factors. The two are not interchangeable: *G6PD = red cells burst; slow acetylation = drug piles up.*

> **Trap (SATA, five options mapped to categories).** **Table 2.6 sorts into increased and decreased, and only three entries increase metabolism:** **fast acetylator, barbiturates, rifampin, and phenytoin.** Everything else in the table — cardiovascular dysfunction, renal insufficiency, starvation, obstructive jaundice, slow acetylator, ketoconazole — **decreases** it. A select-all asking which conditions slow metabolism has six keys.

> **Trap (plausible-but-adjacent).** **CYP2D6 metabolizes codeine, hydrocodone, and oxycodone.** This is the mechanism behind Ch 10's statement (p. 141) that "**genetic differences in cytochrome P-450 enzymes can cause different patients... to respond more or less effectively to a given drug,**" and therefore that patients "**must not automatically be viewed with suspicion if they complain that a given drug does not work for them.**" A pharmacogenomic fact with an ethical consequence.

> **Trap (confusable pair).** **CYP3A4's substrate list includes macrolides and sulfamethoxazole,** which is why Ch 38's macrolide interactions (carbamazepine, cyclosporine, theophylline, warfarin) and sulfonamide interactions (phenytoin, warfarin, sulfonylureas) look so similar. Same enzyme bottleneck, two antibiotic classes.

## §6 Excretion and half-life

**Excretion** (p. 23): "the **elimination of drugs from the body.** All drugs, whether they are **parent compounds or active or inactive metabolites,** must eventually be removed."

- **The organs:** "**The primary organ responsible for this elimination is the kidney.** Two other organs that play a role... are the **liver and the bowel.**"
- **Why metabolism helps excretion:** "Drugs that have been metabolized by the liver become **more polar and water-soluble.** This makes their **elimination by the kidneys much easier because the urinary tract is water-based.**"
- "The kidneys themselves are also capable of metabolizing various drugs, **although usually to a lesser extent than the liver.**"

**The three renal processes** (p. 24, Fig. 2.5): "renal excretion is accomplished through **glomerular filtration, active tubular reabsorption, and active tubular secretion.**"

| Process | What it does as given |
|---|---|
| **Glomerular filtration** | "**Free (unbound) water-soluble drugs and metabolites** go through **passive** glomerular filtration" |
| **Active tubular reabsorption** | "Many substances present in the nephrons go through active reabsorption and are **taken back up into the systemic circulation and transported away from the kidney.** This process is an **attempt by the body to retain needed substances**" |
| **Active tubular secretion** | "Some substances may also be **secreted into the nephron from the vasculature surrounding it**" |

Fig. 2.5 labels the glomerular filtration rate as "**GFR = 125 mL/min.**"

**Biliary excretion and enterohepatic recirculation** (p. 24): "The excretion of drugs by the intestines... is referred to as **biliary excretion.** Drugs that are eliminated by this route are **taken up by the liver, released into the bile, and eliminated in the feces.** Once certain drugs, such as **fat-soluble drugs,** are in the bile, they may be **reabsorbed into the bloodstream, returned to the liver, and again secreted into the bile. This process is called enterohepatic recirculation. Enterohepatically recirculated drugs persist in the body for much longer periods.**"

- **Less common routes:** "the **lungs and the sweat, salivary, and mammary glands.**"

**Half-life** (p. 24): "the time required for **one-half (50%) of a given drug to be removed from the body.** It is a measure of the **rate at which the drug is eliminated.**"

- **The worked example:** "if the peak level of a drug is **100 mg/L** and the measured drug level in **8 hours is 50 mg/L,** then the estimated half-life of that drug is **8 hours.**"

**Table 2.7 Example of Drug Half-Life Viewed From Different Perspectives** (p. 25), reproduced:

| Hours after peak | 0 | 8 | 16 | 24 | 32 | 40 |
|---|---|---|---|---|---|---|
| **Drug concentration (mg/L)** | **100 (peak)** | 50 | 25 | 12.5 | 6.25 | **3.125 (trough)** |
| **Number of half-lives** | 0 | 1 | 2 | 3 | 4 | **5** |
| **Percentage of drug removed** | 0 | **50** | **75** | **88** | **94** | **97** |

**The five half-life rule** (p. 24): "**After about five half-lives, most drugs are considered to be effectively removed from the body.** At that time approximately **97% of the drug has been eliminated** and what little amount remains is usually **too small to have either therapeutic or toxic effects.**"

**Steady state** (p. 25): "the physiologic state in which the amount of drug removed via elimination is **equal to** the amount of drug absorbed with each dose. **This physiologic plateau phenomenon typically occurs after four to five half-lives** of administered drug. Therefore, **if a drug has an extremely long half-life, it will take much longer for the drug to reach steady-state blood levels.** Once steady-state blood levels have been reached, there are **consistent levels of drug in the body that correlate with maximum therapeutic benefits.**"

**Onset, peak, and duration** (p. 25):

| Term | Definition as given |
|---|---|
| **Onset of action** | "the time required for the drug to **elicit a therapeutic response**" |
| **Peak effect** | "the time required for a drug to reach its **maximum therapeutic response.** Physiologically this corresponds to **increasing drug concentrations at the site of action**" |
| **Duration of action** | "the length of time that the drug concentration is sufficient (**without more doses**) to elicit a therapeutic response" |

**Peak and trough levels** (p. 25): "**If the peak blood level is too high, then drug toxicity may occur.** The toxicity may be mild, such as intensification of the effects of the given drug (e.g., **excessive sedation** resulting from overdose of a drug with sedative properties). However, it also can be severe (e.g., **damage to vital organs**). **If the trough blood level is too low, then the drug may not be at therapeutic levels to produce a response.**"

- "In therapeutic drug monitoring, **peak (highest) and trough (lowest) values are measured to verify adequate drug exposure, maximize therapeutic effects, and minimize drug toxicity.** This monitoring is **often carried out by a clinical pharmacist.**"

> **Trap (level/stage swap).** **Five half-lives to clear (97%); four to five half-lives to reach steady state.** Two different milestones that both use the same unit. Cue: *clearing **out** takes about **5** half-lives; building **up** to steady state takes **4 to 5.*** The percentages in Table 2.7 are the other half of the item: **50, 75, 88, 94, 97** at one through five half-lives.

> **Trap (confusable pair).** **Long half-life means slow to steady state, not just slow to clear.** The chapter draws the consequence explicitly. Ch 38's **azithromycin** (half-life 60 to 70 hours) and Ch 10's **methadone** (half-life 25 hours, and "**longer than its duration of action**") are the worked examples: methadone's accumulation deaths happen precisely because steady state arrives long after the patient stops feeling the dose.

> **Trap (confusable pair).** **Reabsorption returns drug to the body; secretion removes it.** Cue: *re**absorption** = the body **taking it back** ("an attempt by the body to retain needed substances"); **secretion** = pushing more **into** the nephron for elimination.* This is the mechanism **probenecid** exploits: it blocks the tubular transporter, and Ch 44 spells out that this keeps uric acid in the urine while Ch 38 notes it keeps **penicillin in the blood.** One transporter, two therapeutic uses, opposite intents.

> **Trap (plausible-but-adjacent).** **Enterohepatic recirculation prolongs drug action.** It is a distinct mechanism from a long half-life or protein binding, and it applies particularly to **fat-soluble** drugs. A stem describing a drug that persists far longer than its measured elimination suggests may be describing recirculation.

> **Trap (confusable pair).** **Peak too high = toxicity; trough too low = no effect.** The two failure modes sit at opposite ends of the same curve, which is why therapeutic drug monitoring measures both. Ch 39 applies this concretely: **aminoglycoside troughs above 2 mcg/mL** risk oto- and nephrotoxicity, while **vancomycin below 5 mcg/mL** is subtherapeutic. Note that the same word "trough" carries opposite concern in the two drugs: an aminoglycoside trough is watched for being **too high,** a vancomycin trough for being **too low.**

## §7 Pharmacodynamics: how drugs act

"**Pharmacodynamics relates to the mechanisms of drug action in living tissues**" (p. 25). "A **positive change in a faulty physiologic system is called a therapeutic effect** of a drug. Such an effect is the **goal of drug therapy.**"

**The fundamental limit** (p. 25): "Once the drug is at the site of action, it can **modify (increase or decrease) the rate** at which that cell or tissue functions or it can **modify the strength of function.** **A drug cannot, however, cause a cell or tissue to perform a function that is not part of its natural physiology.**"

**The three mechanisms** (p. 25): "Drugs can exert their actions in three basic ways: through **receptors, enzymes, and nonselective interactions.**"

- "Not all mechanisms of action have been identified for all drugs. Thus a drug may be said to have an **unknown mechanism of action, even though it has observable therapeutic effects.**"

### Receptor interactions (p. 25)

- "A **receptor** can be defined as a **reactive site on the surface or inside of a cell**... Most commonly, this site is a **protein structure within the cell membrane.**"
- **Affinity:** "The degree to which a drug **attaches to and binds with a receptor** is called its **affinity. The drug with the best 'fit' and strongest affinity for the receptor will elicit the greatest response.**"
- Fig. 2.7's caption states the lock-and-key model: "Drugs act by **forming a chemical bond with specific receptor sites, similar to a key and lock. The better the 'fit,' the better the response.**"

**Table 2.8 Drug-Receptor Interactions** (p. 26), reproduced:

| Drug type | Action as given |
|---|---|
| **Agonist** | "Drug binds to the receptor; **there is a response.**" |
| **Partial agonist (agonist-antagonist)** | "Drug binds to the receptor; **the response is diminished** compared with that elicited by an agonist." |
| **Antagonist** | "Drug binds to the receptor; **there is no response.** Drug **prevents binding of agonists.**" |
| **Competitive antagonist** | "Drug **competes with the agonist** for binding to the receptor. **If it binds, there is no response.**" |
| **Noncompetitive antagonist** | "Drug **combines with different parts of the receptor and inactivates it;** agonist then has no effect." |

### Enzyme interactions (p. 26)

"**Enzymes are the substances that catalyze nearly every biochemical reaction in a cell.** For a drug to alter a physiologic response in this way, it may either **inhibit (more common) or enhance (less common)** the action of a specific enzyme. This process is called **selective interaction.**"

### Nonselective interactions (p. 26)

"Drugs with nonspecific mechanisms of action **do not interact with receptors or enzymes.** Instead, their main targets are **cell membranes and various cellular processes such as metabolic activities.**"

- **How they work:** "By incorporating themselves into the normal metabolic process, they **cause a defect in the final product or state.** This defect may be an **improperly formed cell wall that results in cell death through cell lysis,** or it may be the **lack of a necessary energy substrate, which leads to cell starvation and death.**"
- **Which drugs:** "**Some cancer drugs and antibiotics** have this mechanism of action."

> **Trap (confusable pair).** **Competitive vs. noncompetitive antagonist.** Cue: ***competitive** fights the agonist **for the same site** (so more agonist can outcompete it); **noncompetitive** binds a **different part** of the receptor and **inactivates it** (so more agonist does not help).* Ch 10 names **naloxone and naltrexone** as "**competitive antagonists with a strong affinity**" for the opioid receptors, which is why naloxone can be out-lasted by a long-acting opioid and must be re-dosed.

> **Trap (plausible-but-adjacent).** **A drug cannot make a cell do something it cannot already do.** This is the chapter's own limiting statement and it rules out a whole family of distractor phrasings. Drugs **modify rate or strength** of existing function; they do not add new function.

> **Trap (confusable pair).** **The nonselective mechanism is the one behind cell-wall antibiotics.** "An improperly formed cell wall that results in **cell death through cell lysis**" is exactly Ch 38's description of the **beta-lactams**: they "cause the formation of defective cell walls that are unstable and easily broken down... Bacterial death usually results from **lysis (rupture)** of the bacterial cells." The general principle here, the specific drug class there.

> **Trap (setting vs. focus).** **Affinity determines magnitude of response, not whether the drug is an agonist.** An antagonist can have very high affinity and produce **no** response at all. A stem equating "binds strongly" with "works well" has conflated affinity with intrinsic activity.

## §8 Pharmacotherapeutics: the seven types of therapy

"**Before drug therapy is initiated, an end point or expected outcome of therapy needs to be established.** This desired therapeutic outcome is **patient-specific, established in collaboration with the patient**" and the health care team. "Outcomes need to be **clearly defined and must be either measurable or observable** by monitoring. **Outcome goals must be realistic and prioritized**" (p. 26).

- **Example goals:** "**curing a disease, eliminating or reducing a preexisting symptom, arresting or slowing a disease process, preventing a disease** or other unwanted condition, or otherwise **improving quality of life.**"
- "**These goals and outcomes are not the same as nursing goals and outcomes**" (see Ch. 1).

**Patient therapy assessment** (p. 26): "the process by which a practitioner **integrates his or her knowledge of medical and drug-related facts with information about a specific patient's medical and social history.**" Items to consider: "**drugs currently used (prescription, over-the-counter, herbal, and illicit or street drugs), pregnancy and breastfeeding status, and concurrent illnesses that could contraindicate initiation** of a given medication."

**The seven types of therapy** (pp. 26–27), reproduced with the chapter's own examples:

| Type | Definition as given | Examples as given |
|---|---|---|
| **Acute** | "often involves **more intensive drug treatment** and is implemented in the **acutely ill** (those with rapid onset of illness) or the **critically ill.** It is often needed to **sustain life or treat disease**" | "**vasopressors** to maintain blood pressure, **volume expanders** for a patient in shock, and **intensive chemotherapy** for newly diagnosed cancer" |
| **Maintenance** | "**does not eradicate preexisting problems** the patient may have, but will **prevent progression** of a disease or condition. Used for **chronic illnesses**" | "**hypertension**... maintenance therapy maintains the patient's blood pressure within given limits, which **prevents certain end-organ damage**"; "**oral contraceptives** for birth control" |
| **Supplemental (replacement)** | "**supplies the body with a substance needed to maintain normal function.** This substance may be needed either because it **cannot be made by the body or because it is produced in insufficient quantity**" | "**insulin** to diabetic patients and **iron** to patients with iron-deficiency anemia" |
| **Palliative** | "to make the patient as **comfortable as possible.** Focuses on providing **relief from the symptoms, pain, and stress of a serious illness.** The goal is to **improve quality of life for both the patient and the family**" | "**high-dose opioid analgesics to relieve pain in the final stages of cancer**" |
| **Supportive** | "**maintains the integrity of body functions while the patient is recovering** from illness or trauma" | "**fluids and electrolytes** to prevent dehydration in a patient who is vomiting and has diarrhea; **fluids, volume expanders, or blood products** to a patient who has lost blood during surgery" |
| **Prophylactic** | "drug therapy provided to **prevent illness or other undesirable outcome during planned events**" | "**preoperative antibiotic therapy** for surgical procedures. The antibiotic is given **before the incision is made**"; "**disease-specific vaccines** to individuals traveling to geographic areas where a given disease is **endemic**" |
| **Empirical** | "based on **clinical probabilities.** It involves drug administration when a certain pathologic condition has a **high likelihood of occurrence based on the patient's initial presenting symptoms**" | "**antibiotics active against the organism most commonly associated with a specific infection before the results of the culture and sensitivity reports are available**" |

**Timing note on palliative care** (p. 27): "It is **typically used in the end stages of an illness when attempts at curative therapy have failed; however, it can be provided along with curative treatment.**"

**Monitoring** (p. 27): "Evaluating the clinical response requires familiarity with **both the drug's intended therapeutic action (beneficial effects) and its unintended possible adverse effects.**"

- Examples: "observing for the therapeutic effect of **reduced blood pressure** following antihypertensive drugs and observing for the toxic effect of **leukopenia** after administering antineoplastic drugs. Another example is **performing a pain assessment after giving pain medication.**"
- **A caution about this textbook:** "this textbook **highlights only the most common adverse effects** of a given drug; however, the drug may have **many other less commonly reported adverse effects. Consult comprehensive references or a pharmacist** when there is uncertainty."

**Accumulation** (p. 27): "**A drug can accumulate when it is absorbed more quickly than it is eliminated or when it is administered before the previous dose has been metabolized or cleared** from the body."

**Therapeutic index** (p. 27): "**The ratio of a drug's toxic level to the level that provides therapeutic benefits.** The **safety** of a particular drug therapy is determined by this index."

| Index | Meaning as given | Examples |
|---|---|---|
| **Low therapeutic index** | "the **difference between a therapeutically active dose and a toxic dose is small.** A drug with a low therapeutic index has a **greater likelihood... of causing an adverse reaction, and therefore requires closer monitoring**" | "**warfarin and digoxin**" |
| **High therapeutic index** | "**rarely associated with overdose events**" | "**amoxicillin**" |

**Drug concentration** (p. 27): "**Toxic drug levels are typically seen when the body's normal mechanisms for metabolizing and excreting drugs are compromised.** This commonly occurs when **liver and kidney functions are impaired.**"

**Patient's condition** (p. 28): "A patient's response to a drug may vary greatly, depending on **physiologic and psychological demands. Disease of any kind, infection, cardiovascular function, and GI function** can alter a patient's therapeutic response. **Stress, depression, and anxiety** also can be important psychological factors."

**Tolerance and dependence** (p. 28):

| Term | Definition as given |
|---|---|
| **Tolerance** | "a **decreasing response to repeated drug doses**" |
| **Dependence** | "a **physiologic or psychological need** for a drug" |
| **Physical dependence** | "the **physiologic need for a drug to avoid physical withdrawal symptoms** (e.g., tachycardia in an opioid-addicted patient)" |
| **Psychological dependence** | "also known as **addiction** and is the **obsessive desire for the euphoric effects** of a drug. Addiction typically involves the **recreational use** of various drugs such as **benzodiazepines, opioids, and amphetamines**" |

> **Trap (confusable pair).** **Prophylactic vs. empirical therapy.** Both happen before you know the organism, which is what makes them swappable. Cue: ***prophylactic = no illness yet, a planned event** (surgery, travel); **empirical = illness is present,** you are treating the **most likely** cause while awaiting cultures.* Ch 38 runs the identical distinction as its own three-way scheme (**empiric / definitive / prophylactic**) and builds a review question on it. Note a subtle difference in wording between the chapters: Ch 38 defines empiric therapy by the **practitioner's judgment of likely pathogens**, this chapter by **clinical probabilities based on presenting symptoms.** Same concept, two phrasings.

> **Trap (four-options-three-levels).** **The seven therapy types are a ready-made option set, and three of them are close together.** Cue: ***maintenance** = chronic disease, **prevent progression** (a daily beta blocker for hypertension); **supplemental** = **replace a missing substance** (insulin, iron); **supportive** = **hold body functions steady during recovery** (fluids, blood products).* Review question 3 (p. 32) asks which term describes a daily beta blocker for hypertension and offers palliative, maintenance, supportive, and supplemental. The chapter uses **hypertension as its own example of maintenance therapy.** Official key not in the pull; see GAPS item 8.

> **Trap (confusable pair).** **Palliative is about comfort, not about giving up.** The chapter says it "**can be provided along with curative treatment,**" which contradicts the common assumption that palliative means curative care has stopped. Its example, **high-dose opioids in end-stage cancer,** connects directly to Ch 10's statement that in cancer pain "the main consideration... is **patient comfort and not trying to prevent drug addiction.**"

> **Trap (level/stage swap).** **Low therapeutic index = dangerous; high = safe.** The direction is easy to invert because "low" sounds like low risk. Cue: *the index is a **ratio of toxic to therapeutic**, so a **small** ratio means the two doses are **close together** = **dangerous** (warfarin, digoxin); a **large** ratio means a wide safety margin (amoxicillin).* Ch 39's aminoglycosides and vancomycin are low-therapeutic-index drugs, which is exactly why they get therapeutic drug monitoring.

> **Trap (confusable pair).** **Tolerance vs. physical dependence vs. psychological dependence, again.** This chapter and Ch 10 define the same trio and they do **not** agree exactly. Ch 10's key terms say **opioid tolerance** is "**same as physical dependence**" and **tolerance** is "same as physical dependence." This chapter keeps them separate: **tolerance is a decreasing response; dependence is a need.** When a stem turns on the distinction, this chapter's version is the more precise one; see GAPS item 6.

## §9 Drug interactions and adverse drug events

**The setup** (p. 28): "**The more drugs a patient receives, the more likely that a drug interaction will occur. This is especially true in older adults,** who typically have an **increased sensitivity to drug effects** and are receiving several medications. In addition, **over-the-counter medications and herbal therapies and food** can interact significantly with prescribed medications."

- **Definition:** "Alteration of the action of one drug by another... A drug interaction can **either increase or decrease** the actions of one or both of the involved drugs. **Drug interactions can be either beneficial or harmful.**"
- **The most common mechanism:** "**Most commonly, drug interactions occur when there is competition between two drugs for metabolizing enzymes, such as the cytochrome P-450 enzymes.**"

**Table 2.9 Common Food and Drug Interactions** (p. 28), reproduced. Three of its four rows are load-bearing in chapters already written:

| Food | Drug (category) | Result |
|---|---|---|
| **Leafy green vegetables** | **warfarin** (anticoagulant) | "**Decreased anticoagulant effect** from warfarin" |
| **Dairy products** | **tetracycline, levofloxacin, ciprofloxacin, moxifloxacin** (antibiotics) | "**Chemical binding of the drug** leading to **decreased effect and treatment failures**" |
| **Grapefruit juice** | amiodarone, buspirone, carbamazepine, cyclosporine, tacrolimus, felodipine, nifedipine, nimodipine, nisoldipine, **simvastatin, atorvastatin** | "**Decreased metabolism of drugs and increased effects**" |
| **Aged cheese, wine** | **Monoamine oxidase inhibitors** | "**Hypertensive crisis**" |

**Table 2.10 Examples of Drug Interactions and Their Effects on Pharmacokinetics** (p. 28), reproduced. One example per phase:

| Phase | Drug combination | Mechanism | Result |
|---|---|---|---|
| **Absorption** | **antacid with levofloxacin** | "Antacids **bind** to the levofloxacin, preventing adequate absorption" | "**Decreased effectiveness** of levofloxacin (harmful)" |
| **Distribution** | **warfarin with amiodarone** | "Both drugs **compete for protein-binding sites**" | "**Higher levels of free (unbound)** warfarin and amiodarone, which **increases actions of both** (harmful)" |
| **Metabolism** | **erythromycin with cyclosporine** | "Both drugs **compete for the same hepatic enzymes**" | "**Decreased metabolism of cyclosporine,** possibly resulting in **toxic levels** (harmful)" |
| **Excretion** | **amoxicillin with probenecid** | "**Inhibits the secretion of amoxicillin into the kidneys**" | "**Elevation and prolongation of plasma levels of amoxicillin (can be beneficial)**" |

**Incompatibility** (p. 28): "a term most commonly used to describe **parenteral** drugs. Drug incompatibility occurs when **two parenteral drugs or solutions are mixed together, and the result is a chemical deterioration of one or both of the drugs or the formation of a physical precipitate.**"

- **What you see:** "usually produces a **precipitate, haziness, or color change** in the solution."
- **The nursing action:** "**Before administering any intravenous medication, the nurse must always inspect the bag for precipitate. If the solution appears cloudy or if visible flecks are seen, the bag must be discarded.**"

**Adverse drug events** (p. 29): "**Adverse drug event (ADE) is a broad term for any undesirable occurrence involving medications.** A similarly broad term also seen in the literature is **drug misadventure.**"

- **Outcome range:** "from **no effects to mild discomfort to life-threatening complications, permanent disability, disfigurement, or death.**"
- "ADEs can be **preventable or nonpreventable.** Fortunately, **many ADEs result in no measurable patient harm.** ADEs can be both **external and internal.** The most common causes of ADEs **external to the patient are errors by caregivers.**"

**The four categories of adverse drug reaction** (pp. 29–30):

| Category | Definition as given |
|---|---|
| **Pharmacologic reaction** | "an **extension of the drug's normal effects** in the body. For example, a drug that is used to lower blood pressure... causes a pharmacologic ADR when it **lowers the blood pressure to the point at which the patient becomes unconscious.**" These are "**predictable, well known,** and result in **minor or no changes in patient management.** They are **related to dose** and **usually resolve upon discontinuation**" |
| **Allergic (hypersensitivity) reaction** | "**involves the patient's immune system.** Immune system proteins known as **immunoglobulins**" (see Ch. 47, 48) |
| **Idiosyncratic reaction** | "An **abnormal and unexpected** response to a medication, **other than an allergic reaction,** that is **peculiar to an individual patient**" (key term, p. 14) |
| **Drug interaction** | see above |

**Teratogenic, mutagenic, and carcinogenic effects** (p. 30):

| Effect | Definition as given | Agent name |
|---|---|---|
| **Teratogenic** | "result in **structural defects in the fetus.**" "**Drugs that are capable of crossing the placenta** can cause drug-induced teratogenesis" | **teratogens** |
| **Mutagenic** | "**permanent changes in the genetic composition** of living organisms... **alterations in chromosome structure, the number of chromosomes, or the genetic code of the DNA molecule**" | **mutagens** |
| **Carcinogenic** | "the **cancer-causing effects** of drugs, other chemicals, radiation, and viruses" | **carcinogens** |

- **The teratogenic window:** "The period during which the fetus is **most vulnerable to teratogenic effects begins with the third week of development and usually ends after the third month.**"
- **When mutagens act:** "Drugs that affect genetic processes are **active primarily during cell reproduction (mitosis).**"
- **Mutagenic agents named:** "**Radiation, viruses, chemicals (e.g., industrial chemicals such as benzene), and drugs.**"

**Box 2.3 Exogenous Causes of Cancer** (p. 30), reproduced: "**Dietary customs, drug abuse, carcinogenic drugs, workplace chemicals, radiation, environmental pollution, food-processing procedures, food-production procedures, oncogenic viruses, smoking.**"

**G6PD deficiency** (p. 30, cultural implications box): "an enzyme found in abundant amounts in the tissues of most individuals. This enzyme **reduces the risk for hemolysis in red blood cells when they are exposed to oxidizing drugs, such as aspirin, nitrate derivatives, and sulfonamides.**"

- **Inheritance:** "The deficit is **sex-linked**... and is **carried on the X chromosome.** It is transmitted from **mother, a healthy carrier, to a son or daughter.** The daughter then also would be a **healthy carrier.**"
- **Populations:** "most commonly occurs in specific parts of **Africa, Asia, the Mediterranean, and the Middle East.** Additionally, it is estimated that **14% of Sardinians and more than 50% of the Kurdish Jewish population** also have G6PD deficiencies."
- **The reaction:** "patients with this deficiency may suffer **life-threatening hemolysis of the red blood cells, or hemolytic anemia. Red blood cells are destroyed quicker than they can be replaced.** Manifestations may include **pallor, jaundice, dark urine, shortness of breath, and tachycardia.**"
- "**Individuals with adequate quantities of the enzyme have no problems when taking these drugs.**"

> **Trap (confusable pair).** **Dairy blocks tetracyclines and fluoroquinolones by chemical binding.** Table 2.9 is the general principle; Ch 38 gives the mechanism (**chelation** of divalent and trivalent metal ions) with a **2-hours-before / 3-hours-after** rule, and Ch 39 gives the fluoroquinolone version with a **1-hour** rule. Cue: *the interaction is the same, the **spacing interval differs by class.***

> **Trap (confusable pair).** **Grapefruit juice DECREASES metabolism, which INCREASES drug effect.** The direction confuses people because "decreased" sits next to "juice." Cue: *grapefruit **inhibits** the enzyme → drug accumulates → **stronger effect and toxicity risk.*** Contrast leafy greens with warfarin, where the food supplies **vitamin K** and **decreases** the drug's effect.

> **Trap (confusable pair).** **Probenecid plus amoxicillin is the chapter's one "beneficial" interaction.** Table 2.10 marks the other three examples "(harmful)" and this one "(**can be beneficial**)." It is also the same fact Ch 38 lists in its penicillin interaction table ("**Prolongs the effects of penicillins**") and that Ch 44 gives from probenecid's side. Three chapters, one transporter.

> **Trap (SATA, five options mapped to categories).** **Table 2.10 assigns one interaction to each pharmacokinetic phase,** which makes it a natural matching item. Cue: ***absorption = binding in the gut** (antacid + levofloxacin); **distribution = protein-binding competition** (warfarin + amiodarone); **metabolism = enzyme competition** (erythromycin + cyclosporine); **excretion = blocked renal secretion** (amoxicillin + probenecid).*

> **Trap (confusable pair).** **Pharmacologic ADR vs. allergic vs. idiosyncratic.** Cue: ***pharmacologic = too much of the drug's own effect*** (predictable, dose-related, resolves on discontinuation); ***allergic = the immune system*** (immunoglobulins); ***idiosyncratic = weird, individual, and explicitly NOT allergic.*** Review question 1 (p. 31) describes a woman who took a sleep medicine and "felt restless all night and did not sleep at all," offering allergic reaction, idiosyncratic reaction, mutagenic effect, and synergistic effect. An **opposite-of-intended** response in one individual matches the idiosyncratic definition. Official key not in the pull.

> **Trap (level/stage swap).** **The teratogenic window is week 3 through month 3.** Not conception onward, and not the whole pregnancy. Compare Ch 44, where NSAIDs are pregnancy category **C in the first two trimesters and D in the third** — a *late* pregnancy risk, which is a different mechanism (maternal bleeding, decreased amniotic fluid, neonatal toxicity) than teratogenesis.

> **Trap (plausible-but-adjacent).** **G6PD deficiency is sex-linked and carried on the X chromosome, and daughters of carrier mothers become carriers.** The chapter's genetics are specific. Its drug list — **aspirin, nitrate derivatives, sulfonamides** — connects to Ch 38's warning that **sulfonamides, nitrofurantoin, and dapsone** cause hemolysis in G6PD deficiency, and to Ch 10's listing of G6PD deficiency as an **acetaminophen contraindication.**

> **Trap (priority question).** **Inspect every IV bag for precipitate before administering.** "If the solution appears cloudy or if visible flecks are seen, **the bag must be discarded.**" Ch 39 supplies the concrete case: **quinupristin/dalfopristin must be reconstituted in D5W only** and "cannot be mixed with saline or heparin."

## §10 Toxicology and antidotes

"**Toxicology** is the study of poisons and unwanted responses to both drugs and other chemicals... **the science of the adverse effects of chemicals on living organisms**" (p. 31).

- "**Clinical toxicology** deals specifically with the **care of the poisoned patient.**"
- "Poisoning can result from a variety of causes, ranging from **drug overdose to ingestion of household cleaning agents to snakebite.**"
- "**Poison control centers** are health care institutions equipped with sufficient personnel and information resources to recommend appropriate treatment."
- **Toxicity relates to therapeutic action** (p. 16): "**Toxic effects are often an extension of a drug's therapeutic action.**"

**The two priorities in treating the poisoned patient** (p. 31):

1. "**preserve the patient's vital functions by maintaining the airway, ventilation, and circulation.**"
2. "**prevent absorption of the toxic substance and/or speed its elimination** from the body."

**Table 2.11 Common Poisons and Their Antidotes** (p. 31), reproduced in full:

| Substance | Antidote |
|---|---|
| **acetaminophen** | **acetylcysteine** |
| **Organophosphates** (e.g., insecticides) | **atropine** |
| **Tricyclic antidepressants, quinidine** | **sodium bicarbonate** |
| **Calcium channel blockers** | **Intravenous calcium** |
| **Iron salts** | **deferoxamine** |
| **digoxin** and other cardiac glycosides | **digoxin antibodies** |
| **ethylene glycol** (automotive antifreeze), **methanol** | **Ethanol** (same as alcohol used for drinking), given intravenously |
| **Benzodiazepines** | **flumazenil** |
| **Beta blockers** | **glucagon** |
| **Opiates, opioid drugs** | **naloxone** |
| **Carbon monoxide** (by inhalation) | **Oxygen** (at high concentration) |

> **Trap (SATA, five options mapped to categories).** **Table 2.11 is the most matchable table in the chapter,** and two of its rows are already carried by other chapters: **acetaminophen → acetylcysteine** and **opioids → naloxone** are both developed at length in Ch 10. Cue for the counterintuitive ones: ***calcium channel blocker overdose is treated with calcium; beta blocker overdose with glucagon; methanol and antifreeze with ethanol.***

> **Trap (plausible-but-adjacent).** **Airway, ventilation, and circulation come before the antidote.** The chapter numbers its priorities, and preserving vital functions is **first**; preventing absorption and speeding elimination is **second**. A stem offering an antidote as the first action in an unstable poisoned patient has skipped priority one. This matches Ch 10's escalation ladder for respiratory depression: **stimulate → ventilate → then naloxone.**

> **Trap (confusable pair).** **"Toxic effects are often an extension of a drug's therapeutic action."** This is the same idea as the **pharmacologic ADR** category in §9, and it explains why toxicity is usually predictable. A distractor framing all toxicity as idiosyncratic contradicts both statements.
