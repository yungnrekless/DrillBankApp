| **Gastric glands** | 788 | "Secretory glands in the stomach containing the following cell types: **parietal, chief, mucous, endocrine, and enterochromaffin.**" |

| **Pepsin** | 788 | "An enzyme in the stomach that **breaks down proteins.**" |

Three more definitions sit in the body text rather than the box, and all are testable:

- **Pepsinogen** (p. 789): "a **proenzyme (enzyme precursor)** that becomes pepsin **when activated by exposure to acid.**"
- **Peptic ulcer disease** (p. 790): "a general term for **gastric or duodenal ulcers** that involve **digestion of the GI mucosa by the enzyme pepsin.**"
- **Stress-related mucosal damage** (p. 790): "an important issue for **critically ill patients,**" with stress ulcer prophylaxis defined as "therapy to prevent severe GI damage."

> **Trap (SATA, five options mapped to categories).** **The three cells and what each secretes.** Cue: ***par**ietal = **acid** (HCl); **chief** = **pepsinogen**; **mucous** = **mucus**.* Parietal is the one every drug in the chapter aims at. Note the two extra cell types the **gastric glands** definition lists but the chapter never discusses again: **endocrine** and **enterochromaffin.** A five-option SATA that includes those two is testing whether you read the gland definition or only the three-cell paragraph.

> **Trap (level/stage swap).** **Pepsinogen vs. pepsin.** Cue: *pepsinogen is the **inactive proenzyme** the **chief cells** release; pepsin is the **active proteolytic enzyme** it becomes **after exposure to acid.*** That order explains why suppressing acid also suppresses proteolysis. An option with chief cells secreting *pepsin* directly, or pepsinogen digesting protein, has the stage wrong.

> **Trap (confusable pair).** **Mucous cells vs. surface epithelial cells** are the same cell under two names, and the chapter uses both (p. 789). An option naming the unfamiliar one is not thereby wrong.

> **Trap (confusable pair).** **Gastric hyperacidity vs. GERD vs. peptic ulcer disease.** Cue: *hyperacidity = **too much acid produced**; GERD = acidic contents **backing up into the esophagus**; PUD = **pepsin digesting the mucosa.*** The chapter also ranks them: "The **most harmful** of these involve hypersecretion of acid and include **peptic ulcer disease and esophageal cancer.** However, the **most common** condition is **mild to moderate hyperacidity**" (p. 789). *Most harmful* and *most common* are different answers to different stems.

> **Trap (plausible-but-adjacent).** **The lay terms are all one thing.** "Many lay terms (e.g., **indigestion, sour stomach, heartburn, acid stomach**) have been used to describe this condition of overproduction of hydrochloric acid by the parietal cells" (p. 789). Four names, one condition, not four disorders.

## §1 The parietal cell, which is the map for the whole chapter (pp. 789–790)

**What the stomach secretes** (pp. 788–789): **hydrochloric acid,** "an acid that aids digestion and also serves as **a barrier to infection**"; **bicarbonate,** "a base that is a natural mechanism to prevent hyperacidity"; **pepsinogen,** "an enzymatic precursor to pepsin"; **intrinsic factor,** "a **glycoprotein** that facilitates gastric absorption of **vitamin B12**"; **mucus,** which "protects the stomach lining from both hydrochloric acid and digestive enzymes"; and **prostaglandins,** which "have a variety of **antiinflammatory and protective functions** (see Chapter 44)."

**Three zones, three glands** (Fig. 50.1, p. 789): "The three primary types of glands in the stomach are the **cardiac, pyloric, and gastric glands.** These glands are named for their positions in the stomach." The **gastric glands** in the fundus are the ones that matter — "highly specialized secretory glands composed of several different types of cells: parietal, chief, mucous, endocrine, and enterochromaffin."

**The three receptors and their second messengers** (pp. 789–790) — the single highest-yield paragraph in the chapter:

| Receptor (first messenger) | Second messenger | Blocked by |
|---|---|---|
| **Histamine** | "occupation results in the production of **adenylate cyclase.** Adenylate cyclase converts ATP to **cyclic adenosine monophosphate (cAMP),** which provides **energy for the proton pump**" | **H2 receptor antagonists** (Fig. 50.2: "H2 blocker") |
| **Acetylcholine (ACh)** | "the second messenger that drives the proton pump is **not cAMP** but is instead **calcium ions**" | **Anticholinergics** such as **atropine** — but "these drugs are **no longer used for this purpose** and have been superseded by other drug classes discussed in this chapter" |
| **Gastrin** | calcium ions, same as ACh | **Nothing.** "There is **currently no drug** to block the binding of the hormone gastrin to its corresponding receptor" |

**The pump.** "The proton pump — or, more precisely, the **hydrogen–potassium–adenosine triphosphatase (ATPase) pump** — is a pump for the transport of **hydrogen ions** and is located in the parietal cells. **The pump requires energy to work**" (pp. 789–790). Its action "is the **final step** in the acid-secretory process of the parietal cell" (p. 794), and "because hydrogen ions are **protons** ... this ion pump is also called the **proton pump.**"

> **Trap (confusable pair).** **Histamine runs on cAMP; ACh and gastrin run on calcium.** Stated as an explicit contrast: for ACh and gastrin "the second messenger ... is **not cAMP but is instead calcium ions.**" Cue: *only the **histamine** arm goes through **adenylate cyclase → cAMP.*** This is also why prostaglandins are said to work by preventing **histamine** binding specifically: "**Without adenylate cyclase, no cAMP can be formed and no second messenger is available to activate the proton pump**" (p. 791).

> **Trap (plausible-but-adjacent).** **Three receptors, one current drug target.** Cue: *histamine = **blocked, in current use**; ACh = **blockable but obsolete** for this purpose; gastrin = **no blocker exists at all.*** An option offering a "gastrin receptor antagonist" names a drug class the chapter says does not exist; an option offering anticholinergics for hyperacidity names real pharmacology the chapter has explicitly retired.

> **Trap (level/stage swap).** **Where each class acts, in order along the pathway.** Cue: *H2RA = at the **receptor,** upstream; PPI = at the **pump,** the **final step**; antacid = **after** secretion, in the **lumen.*** Only the antacid works after the acid exists. Only the PPI blocks all three receptor arms at once, because all three converge on the same pump — which is why PPIs stop "more than 90% of acid secretion over 24 hours" while H2RAs "reduce but do not completely abolish acid secretion."

## §2 Acid-related pathophysiology (pp. 789–791)

**GERD** (p. 789): "the tendency of **excessive and acidic stomach contents to back up, or reflux, into the lower (and even upper) esophagus.** Over time, this condition can lead to more serious disorders such as **erosive esophagitis** and **Barrett esophagus, a precancerous condition.**"

**What stimulates HCl** (p. 789): "**food, caffeine, chocolate, and alcohol.** In moderation, any of these is usually not problematic. However, **excessive consumption of large, fatty meals or alcohol, as well as emotional stress,** may result in hyperproduction." Also "**the sight, smell, and taste of food**" (p. 790).

**Acid-peptic disorders** (p. 790): "Because the process of ulceration is driven by the **proteolytic ... actions of pepsin together with the caustic effects of hydrochloric acid,** peptic ulcer disease and related problems are also referred to by the more general term **acid-peptic disorders.**"

### *Helicobacter pylori* (p. 790)

| Fact | As given |
|---|---|
| Original name | "In **1983** a **gram-negative spiral bacterium, *Campylobacter pylori*,** was isolated from several patients with **gastritis**" |
| Renaming | "changed to ***Helicobacter pylori*** because it was thought to have more characteristics of the *Helicobacter* genus" |
| Prevalence by age | "approximately **40% to 60%** for patients **older than 60** ... but only **10%** for those **younger than 30**" |
| Prevalence by ulcer type | "**90% of patients with duodenal ulcers** and **70% of those with gastric ulcers**" |
| Not sufficient | "also found in many patients who do **not** have peptic ulcer disease, and its presence is **not associated with acute, perforating ulcers.** ... **more than one factor is involved in ulceration**" |
| Guidelines | "The **American College of Gastroenterologists** ... **last updated in 2017**" |

**First-line therapy, as printed:** "a **10- to 14-day** course of a **proton pump inhibitor (PPI)** and the antibiotics **clarithromycin and either amoxicillin or metronidazole** (see Chapters 38 and 39) **or** a combination of a **PPI, bismuth subsalicylate** (see Chapter 51), and the antibiotics **tetracycline and metronidazole.** Many different combinations are used, but **all incorporate the aforementioned key drugs.**"

### Stress-related mucosal damage (pp. 790–791)

- Prophylaxis "is undertaken in **almost every critically ill patient in an intensive care unit (ICU)** and for many patients on general medical surgical units."
- "GI lesions are a common finding in ICU patients, **especially within the first 24 hours after admission.**"
- Cause is "**multifactorial and is not fully understood.** Factors include **decreased blood flow, mucosal ischemia, hypoperfusion, and reperfusion injury.**" Predisposing procedures: "**passing nasogastric (NG) tubes, placing patients on ventilators.**"
- High-risk indicators: "**Coagulopathy,** a **history of peptic ulcer or GI bleed, sepsis, use of steroids, ICU stay of longer than 1 week,** and **occult bleeding.**"
- "Guidelines suggest that all such patients receive **either a histamine receptor–blocking drug or a PPI.**"
- The stop rule: "**data do not support the continued use of stress ulcer prophylaxis once the patient is moved out of the ICU.**"

> **Trap (confusable pair).** ***Campylobacter pylori* vs. *Helicobacter pylori*.** Same organism, renamed. Cue: ***Campylobacter* = the **1983 name**; *Helicobacter* = current.* An option using the old name is historically correct, not wrong. What never changed is the descriptor: **gram-negative spiral bacterium.**

> **Trap (level/stage swap).** **The four *H. pylori* numbers are two different pairs.** Cue: *by **age,** 40–60% over 60 vs. 10% under 30; by **ulcer type,** **90% duodenal** vs. **70% gastric.*** Duodenal is the higher one, which is counterintuitive if you expect a stomach organism to track with stomach ulcers. Swapping 90 and 70, or applying the age figures to ulcer types, is the cheapest distractor here.

> **Trap (plausible-but-adjacent).** ***H. pylori* is necessary for the regimen, not sufficient for the ulcer.** The chapter says the organism is found "in many patients who do **not** have peptic ulcer disease" and that "**more than one factor is involved in ulceration.**" An option stating that *H. pylori* causes PUD, full stop, is stronger than the chapter's own claim.

> **Trap (setting vs. focus).** **Stress ulcer prophylaxis is defined by the *setting,* and it ends when the setting does.** Cue: *ICU = **prophylaxis indicated**; stepped down = **data do not support continuing it.*** This is the rare item where the right answer is to **stop** a working drug, and it is stated as a data conclusion. A stem about a transferred patient still on a PPI is asking about deprescribing, not efficacy.

> **Trap (SATA, five options mapped to categories).** **The high-risk list is six named items:** coagulopathy, history of peptic ulcer or GI bleed, sepsis, steroid use, ICU stay longer than 1 week, occult bleeding. Plausible non-members from adjacent sentences: **ventilator** and **NG tube,** which are named as **predisposing procedures,** and "**within the first 24 hours after admission,**" which is a **timing** observation. Category membership is the whole item.

## §3 Antacids (pp. 791–793) — the densest testable block

### Mechanism, stated as a negation (p. 791)

"Antacids work primarily by **neutralizing gastric acidity.** They **do not prevent the overproduction of acid** but instead help **neutralize acid secretions.**" Secondarily, and only at low dose: antacids "**promote gastric mucosal defensive mechanisms, especially at lower dosages** ... by **stimulating the secretion of mucus, prostaglandins, and bicarbonate** from the cells inside the gastric glands."

**Drug effect:** "the **reduction of the symptoms** ... such as pain and reflux ('heartburn')," attributed to "**base-mediated inhibition of the protein-digesting ability of pepsin,** increase in the **resistance of the stomach lining to irritation,** and increase in the **tone of the cardiac sphincter,** which reduces reflux."

**Why combination products exist:** many aluminum- and calcium-based formulations "also include **magnesium,** which not only contributes to the acid-neutralizing capacity but also **counteracts the constipating effects of aluminum and calcium.** However, **antacids containing magnesium must be avoided in patients with renal failure.**" Many also contain **simethicone,** "which reduces gas and bloating."

**Indications:** "the **acute relief of symptoms** associated with **peptic ulcer, gastritis, gastric hyperacidity, and heartburn.**"

**Contraindications:** "The only usual contraindication ... is **known allergy.** Other contraindications may include **severe renal failure or electrolyte disturbances** ... and **GI obstruction;** for example, **magnesium antacids may be contraindicated for patients with small bowel obstructions, because of the laxative effect.**"

### Adverse effects, mapped to salt (p. 791 unless noted)

| Salt | Adverse effects as given |
|---|---|
| **Magnesium** | "especially **milk of magnesia, can cause diarrhea**"; commonly have "a **laxative effect,** and frequent administration of these antacids alone **often cannot be tolerated**" (p. 793); **avoid in renal failure** |
| **Aluminum** | "**constipation**"; Box 50.1 adds it "**binds to phosphate**" and may produce "**hypophosphatemia,** which is characterized by **loss of appetite, malaise, muscle weakness, and/or bone pain**" |
| **Calcium** | "**constipation**"; "can also cause **kidney stones**"; **acid rebound** is "more common with the calcium-containing products"; chronic high-dose use or use in renal failure "can cause a syndrome known as **milk-alkali syndrome**" |
| **Sodium** | "Excessive use of **any** antacid can theoretically result in **systemic alkalosis.** This is **more common with sodium bicarbonate**"; Box 50.1: may lead to "**metabolic alkalosis** if the drug is abused or used over the long term" |

- **Milk-alkali syndrome:** "characterized by **headache, nausea, alkalosis, and hypercalcemia.**"
- **Alkalosis manifestations** (Box 50.1): "**irritability, muscle twitching, numbness and tingling, cyanosis, slow and shallow respirations, headache, thirst, and nausea.**"
- **Acid rebound:** "**rebound hyperacidity ... in which the patient experiences hyperacidity when antacid use is discontinued.**" Mechanism (Box 50.1): it "occurs with the discontinuation of antacids that have **high acid-neutralizing capacity** and with **overuse or misuse.** If acid neutralization is **sudden and high,** the result is an immediate elevation in pH to alkalinity and **just as rapid a decline in pH to a more acidic state.**"
- **Hypercalcemia, early vs. late** (Box 50.1): early = "**constipation, headache, increased thirst, dry mouth, decreased appetite, irritability, and a metallic taste in the mouth**"; later = "**confusion, drowsiness, increase in blood pressure, irregular heart rate, nausea, vomiting, and increased urination.**"
- **The masking warning:** "**Long-term self-medication with antacids may mask symptoms of serious underlying disease such as bleeding ulcer or malignancy.**"

### Drug profile: antacids, general (pp. 792–793)

- "**Pharmacokinetic parameters are not normally listed for antacids,** but these drugs are generally **excreted quickly through the GI tract and/or the electrolyte homeostatic mechanisms of the kidneys.**"
- Pregnancy: "considered **safe for use during pregnancy if prolonged administration and high dosages are avoided.**"
- **Renal compromise:** "**Aluminum- and sodium-based antacids are often recommended for patients with renal compromise** because they are **more easily excreted.**" But: "Both **calcium- and magnesium-based** antacids are **more likely to accumulate to toxic levels in patients with renal disease.**"
- "**Calcium carbonate neutralization will produce gas and possibly belching.** For this reason, it may be combined with an antiflatulent drug such as **simethicone.**"

> **Trap (confusable pair).** **Magnesium causes diarrhea; aluminum and calcium cause constipation.** The flagship confusable, and it appears four times (adverse effects, drug profile, human need statements, planning outcomes). Cue: ***M**agnesium = **M**ilk of **M**agnesia = laxative = **diarrhea**; **A**luminum and **C**alcium = **c**onstipation.* The combination product exists to cancel the pair: "The **net effect** ... is a **balancing out of both adverse effects**" (p. 797). An option with magnesium constipating, or one recommending a magnesium-only product to a patient with loose stools, has the pair reversed.

> **Trap (priority question).** **Which antacid for the patient with renal disease.** Review question 4 (p. 800) is built on this, offering "cannot use antacids," aluminum-, calcium-, and magnesium-based. The governing sentence: "**Aluminum- and sodium-based antacids are often recommended for patients with renal compromise because they are more easily excreted**" (p. 793). Cue: *renal compromise → **aluminum**; avoid **magnesium** (must be avoided in renal failure) and **calcium** (accumulates, milk-alkali).* The override is that sodium, despite sharing aluminum's excretion advantage, is separately disqualified for cardiac and hypertensive patients — so aluminum is the safer of the two.

> **Trap (plausible-but-adjacent).** **Sodium bicarbonate is on both sides of the renal question.** Page 793 puts it on the "easily excreted in renal compromise" list; p. 797 says it "is **generally not recommended** as an antacid because of the high risk for systemic electrolyte disturbances and alkalosis. The **sodium content** ... is very problematic for patients who have **hypertension, heart failure, or renal insufficiency.**" **Renal insufficiency appears on both sides.** The chapter does not reconcile it; see GAPS item 4.

> **Trap (confusable pair).** **Milk-alkali syndrome vs. metabolic alkalosis vs. acid rebound.** Three antacid complications that all sound like "too much base." Cue: *milk-alkali = **calcium,** a four-part syndrome including **hypercalcemia**; **metabolic/systemic alkalosis** = **sodium bicarbonate,** from abuse or long-term use, **no** hypercalcemia; **acid rebound** = pH **swings back acidic when you stop,** more common with **calcium.*** Hypercalcemia pins milk-alkali; **discontinuation** pins acid rebound.

> **Trap (level/stage swap).** **Early vs. late hypercalcemia.** Box 50.1 splits them, so a stem can too. Cue: *early = constipation, headache, thirst, dry mouth, decreased appetite, irritability, **metallic taste**; later = **confusion, drowsiness, elevated BP, irregular heart rate,** nausea, vomiting, increased urination.* **Neurologic and cardiac** findings belong only to the later stage; metallic taste is the most distinctive early cue.

> **Trap (plausible-but-adjacent).** **Antacids relieve symptoms; they do not reduce acid production and they do not coat anything.** The chapter states the first half as a negation ("**do not prevent the overproduction of acid**") and Critical Thinking Exercise 2 (p. 799) is built on the second: a patient says antacids "**coat my stomach and protect my ulcer.**" Cue: *coating is **sucralfate's** mechanism ("a **liquid bandage,**" p. 796).* What antacids do to the mucosa is **indirect and dose-dependent** — they stimulate the stomach's own mucus, prostaglandins, and bicarbonate, "especially at lower dosages."

> **Trap (priority question).** **Long-term antacid self-medication is a red flag, not a success story.** The case study (p. 798, a patient who "self-treated for heartburn for years by drinking large amounts of antacids," told to stop) and the teaching bullet ("**A health care provider should be contacted after 2 weeks of self-prescribed antacid use,**" p. 799) both point at the masking warning. Cue: *years of relief with escalating use → the priority is **diagnostic evaluation,** not a better antacid.*

### The four interaction mechanisms (p. 792)

The chapter's most explicitly enumerated list, and it reads as a purpose-built SATA:

1. "**Adsorption** of other drugs to antacids, which **reduces the ability of the other drug to be absorbed**"
2. "**Chelation,** which is the **chemical inactivation** of other drugs that produces **insoluble complexes**"
3. "**Increased stomach pH,** which **increases the absorption of basic drugs** and **decreases the absorption of acidic drugs**"
4. "**Increased urinary pH,** which **increases the excretion of acidic drugs** and **decreases the excretion of basic drugs**"

Governing principle: "**Most drugs are either weak acids or weak bases.** Therefore pH conditions in **both the GI and urinary tracts** will affect the extent to which drug molecules are absorbed."

**Table 50.1, rebuilt from the prose on the same page** (the table's columns bleed):

| Result | Drugs as listed |
|---|---|
| **Increased effects** (pH effects on the interacting drug) | benzodiazepines, sulfonylureas (effects "also may be **reduced,** depending on the drugs involved"), sympathomimetics, valproic acid |
| **Reduced effects** (decreased GI absorption) | allopurinol, tetracycline, thyroid hormones, captopril, corticosteroids, digoxin, histamine antagonists, phenytoin, isoniazid, ketoconazole, methotrexate, nitrofurantoin, phenothiazines, salicylates, quinolone antibiotics |

**General spacing rule:** "**Advise patients to dose any interacting drugs at least 1 to 2 hours before or after antacids are taken.**"

**The quinolone interaction, called out separately:** "**Significant patient harm may ensue** when the **quinolone antibiotics (ciprofloxacin, levofloxacin, moxifloxacin)** are given with antacids ... **Antacids can reduce their absorption by more than 50%.** Thus antacids must be given **either 2 hours before or 2 hours after the dose of a quinolone antibiotic.**" Implementation adds the consequence: "**Serious infections may then go unsuccessfully treated because of altered absorption**" (p. 798).

> **Trap (SATA, five options mapped to categories).** **The four mechanisms are a closed list and each has a direction.** Cue: *adsorption and chelation are **physical/chemical binding in the gut** (both reduce absorption); the two pH mechanisms are **directional and opposite** — **stomach** pH up favors **basic** drugs and hurts **acidic** ones; **urinary** pH up excretes **acidic** drugs and retains **basic** ones.* The pH statements are written to mirror each other, so reversing one produces something that looks exactly like the other. Anchor: *in the **stomach** pH governs **absorption**; in the **urine** pH governs **excretion.***

> **Trap (confusable pair).** **The generic 1-to-2-hour rule vs. the quinolone 2-hour rule.** Cue: *any interacting drug → **at least 1 to 2 hours** before or after the antacid; a **quinolone** → **2 hours** before or after, no shorter, because the loss is **more than 50%** of an antibiotic treating a serious infection.* The chapter uses "at least 1 to 2 hours" as the default and breaks the quinolones out because the consequence is worst there.

> **Trap (confusable pair).** **This chapter's tetracycline number is not Chapter 38's.** Review question 5 (p. 800) puts a patient on **oral tetracycline** who requests an antacid; the option matching **this** chapter is "Give the tetracycline, but delay the antacid for **1 to 2 hours,**" because tetracycline sits on this chapter's 1-to-2-hour list. **Chapter 38** teaches the tighter tetracycline rule (**2 hours before, 3 hours after**). Cue: *answer from the chapter the item was written for; a stem quoting 2-before/3-after is a **Chapter 38** item.* See GAPS item 5.

> **Trap (plausible-but-adjacent).** **"Explain that the antacid cannot be given while the patient is taking the tetracycline" is the over-correction distractor.** Nothing in this chapter withholds an antacid for an interaction; the whole section is about **spacing,** and implementation says so: "**You may safely implement this dosing schedule without interrupting the safe dosing of other medications**" (p. 798). Cue: *the antacid interaction answer is almost always **separate the doses,** not **stop the drug.***

> **Trap (plausible-but-adjacent).** **Antacids destroy enteric coatings** — a fifth interaction mechanism in effect, and it is **not** on the numbered list of four. It lives only in the patient teaching box: antacids "may promote **premature dissolution of the enteric coating** ... and if the coating is destroyed early in the stomach, **gastric upset may occur**" (p. 799). A SATA including it is testing whether you read the teaching box.

## §4 H2 receptor antagonists (pp. 793–794)

- **Names:** "commonly abbreviated as **H2RAs** and also called **H2 receptor blockers,** are the **prototypical acid-secretion antagonists.**"
- **The limit:** "These drugs **reduce but do not completely abolish acid secretion.**"
- **Why they took over:** "the **most popular drugs** for the treatment of many acid-related disorders ... attributed to their **efficacy, patient acceptance, and excellent safety profile.**"
- **Roster:** "**cimetidine, famotidine, and nizatidine. Ranitidine (Zantac) was pulled from the market in 2020.** There is little difference among the **three** available H2 receptor antagonists from the standpoint of efficacy. Drugs in this class are available **over the counter.**"
- **Mechanism:** "**competitively block the H2 receptor** of acid-producing parietal cells. This makes the parietal cell **less responsive not only to histamine but also to the stimulation of ACh and gastrin. Up to 90% inhibition of vagal- and gastrin-stimulated acid secretion occurs when histamine is blocked. However, complete inhibition has not been shown.**"
- **Indications:** "**GERD, peptic ulcer disease, and erosive esophagitis;** adjunct therapy in the control of **upper GI tract bleeding;** and ... **pathologic gastric hypersecretory conditions such as Zollinger-Ellison syndrome.** The latter is one form of **hyperchlorhydria, or excessive gastric acidity.**" Also "**stress ulcer prophylaxis in critically ill patients.**"
- **Contraindications:** "**known drug allergy. Liver and/or kidney dysfunction are relative contraindications that may warrant dosage adjustment.**"

### Adverse effects (pp. 793–794)

Overall "**remarkably low incidence ... (less than 3% of cases).**" CNS effects "occur in **less than 1%** of patients ... but are **sometimes seen in older adults** ... include **confusion and disorientation. Be alert for mental status changes ... especially if they are new to the patient.**"

**cimetidine:** "may induce **impotence and gynecomastia** ... the result of **cimetidine's inhibition of estradiol metabolism and displacement of dihydrotestosterone from peripheral androgen-binding sites.**" Teaching adds: "**Gynecomastia and impotence may occur with cimetidine and are reversible**" (p. 799). The class "may increase the secretion of **prolactin** from the anterior pituitary gland." **famotidine:** "**Thrombocytopenia** has been reported."

**Table 50.2 — adverse effects by body system** (p. 794): **Cardiovascular** — "Hypotension (**monitor for this effect with intravenous administration**)"; **Central nervous** — "Headache, lethargy, confusion, depression, hallucinations, slurred speech, agitation"; **Endocrine** — "Increased prolactin secretion, gynecomastia (with cimetidine)"; **Gastrointestinal** — "Diarrhea, nausea, abdominal cramps"; **Genitourinary** — "Impotence, increased blood urea nitrogen, increased creatinine levels"; **Hepatobiliary** — "Elevated liver enzyme levels, jaundice"; **Hematologic** — "Agranulocytosis, thrombocytopenia, neutropenia, aplastic anemia"; **Integumentary** — "Urticaria, rash, alopecia, sweating, flushing, exfoliative dermatitis."

### Interactions (p. 793)

- "**Cimetidine carries a higher risk for drug interactions** than the other three drugs, **especially in older adults.**" It "**binds enzymes of the hepatic cytochrome P-450 microsomal oxidase system,**" so by inhibiting metabolism it "may **raise the blood concentrations** of certain drugs. **Nizatidine and famotidine have essentially no effect.**"
- "This interaction has **little clinical significance for most drugs;** however, significant interactions are more likely to arise with medications having a **narrow therapeutic range,** such as **theophylline, warfarin, lidocaine, and phenytoin.**"
- "All H2 receptor antagonists and B vitamins may **inhibit the absorption of certain drugs, such as ketoconazole, that require an acidic GI environment**." *(Printed as given; "and B vitamins" is very likely a scan artifact — GAPS item 2.)*
- "**Smoking** has also been shown to **decrease the effectiveness** of H2 antagonists."
- "For optimal results, **H2 receptor antagonists are taken 1 to 2 hours before antacids.**"

### Dosages and profiles (p. 794)

Dosage ranges are printed in the chapter's own table and are **lookup values, not memorizable structures**; read them there. The doses that carry meaning are quoted in the profiles and traps below.

**cimetidine (Tagamet):** "In **1977** ... became the **first drug in this class** to be released on the market. It is the **prototypical** H2 receptor antagonist and was the **first major prescription drug to be made available over the counter.** Because of its potential to cause drug interactions, **its use has been largely replaced by famotidine.** Cimetidine is **still used to treat certain allergic reactions.**" PK, PO: onset **15–60 min**, peak **1–2 hr**, half-life **2 hr**, duration **4–5 hr**.

**famotidine (Pepcid):** "the **last H2 receptor antagonist introduced** and has **very few drug interaction concerns.** It is available in **oral and injectable forms. The dosing is the same for both forms.**" PK, PO: onset **1.4 hr**, peak **3 hr**, half-life **2.6–4 hr**, duration **9–12 hr**; IV: onset **immediate**, peak **less than 15 min**, half-life **2.6–4 hr**, duration **9–12 hr**.

> **Trap (confusable pair).** **cimetidine vs. famotidine vs. nizatidine.** All three are equally effective; the discriminator is interactions and adverse effects. Cue: ***cimetidine** = **first** (1977), the prototype, the **P-450 inhibitor,** the one with **gynecomastia and impotence,** the one **replaced**; **famotidine** = **last** introduced, **very few interactions,** the replacement, and the one with **thrombocytopenia**; **nizatidine** = named, "essentially no effect" on P-450, but **no profile and no dosage row.*** An option attributing gynecomastia to famotidine has the pair reversed.

> **Trap (confusable pair).** **Which cimetidine interactions actually matter.** The chapter says the P-450 interaction has "**little clinical significance for most drugs**" and names the exceptions by shared property: **narrow therapeutic range** — **theophylline, warfarin, lidocaine, phenytoin.** Review question 2 (p. 800) offers codeine, penicillin, **phenytoin,** and acetaminophen; only phenytoin is on the list, and the other three appear nowhere in the H2RA interaction paragraph. (Official key not in the pull; GAPS item 5.)

> **Trap (level/stage swap).** **"Up to 90%" is the H2RAs; "more than 90% over 24 hours" is the PPIs, and only one is complete.** Cue: *H2RA = **up to 90% of vagal- and gastrin-stimulated** secretion, and "**complete inhibition has not been shown**"; PPI = "more than 90% ... over 24 hours" and "**blocks all gastric acid secretion.***" Identical-looking numbers, different meanings: one is a ceiling on a **partial** effect, the other describes a **total blockade** producing **achlorhydria.**

> **Trap (confusable pair).** **Competitive/reversible (H2RA) vs. irreversible (PPI).** Cue: *H2RAs "**competitively block**" a receptor, so the effect is surmountable and ends with the drug; PPIs "**irreversibly inhibit**" the pump, so recovery requires that "**the parietal cell must synthesize new hydrogen-potassium-ATPase.***" That sentence is why PPI duration outlasts its half-life by days.

> **Trap (priority question).** **New confusion in an older adult on an H2RA.** CNS effects are "less than 1%" overall, which makes them easy to dismiss, but the chapter attaches them to **older adults** twice, and assessment repeats it: "Older adult patients are known to react to these drugs with **more disorientation and confusion**" (p. 797). Cue: *the default is not "expected age-related change" but **assess mental status and suspect the drug,** "especially if they are new to the patient."*

> **Trap (plausible-but-adjacent).** **Ranitidine (Zantac) is gone** — "pulled from the market in **2020**" (p. 793). It is the most recognizable name in the class and still appears in older question banks. An option offering ranitidine is testing currency, not pharmacology.

> **Trap (setting vs. focus).** **IV H2RAs are a blood-pressure watch; oral ones are not.** Table 50.2 flags hypotension with "**monitor for this effect with intravenous administration,**" and implementation repeats it: "hypotension may occur with **rapid infusion**" (p. 798). Cue: *the adverse effect belongs to the **route and rate,** not the molecule.*

## §5 Proton pump inhibitors (pp. 794–796)

**Roster** (printed twice, pp. 794 and 795): "**lansoprazole (Prevacid), omeprazole (Prilosec), rabeprazole (AcipHex), pantoprazole (Protonix), esomeprazole (Nexium), and dexlansoprazole (Dexilant). Zegerid is a combination of omeprazole and sodium bicarbonate.**"

### Mechanism and consequences (pp. 794–795)

- "PPIs **bind directly to the hydrogen-potassium-ATPase pump mechanism and irreversibly inhibit** the action of this enzyme, which results in a **total blockage of hydrogen ion secretion** from the parietal cells." The inhibition "**blocks all gastric acid secretion.**"
- "The PPIs **stop more than 90% of acid secretion over 24 hours,** which makes most patients **temporarily achlorhydric (without acid).**"
- **Achlorhydria's complications:** "**bacterial overgrowth, intestinal metaplasia, and hip fracture. However, food absorption is not affected.**"
- **Recovery:** "the parietal cell must **synthesize new hydrogen-potassium-ATPase.**"
- **Selectivity:** hydrogen-potassium-ATPase "is **structurally and mechanically distinct** from other hydrogen-transporting enzymes and **appears to exist only in the parietal cells.** Thus the action of PPIs is **limited to their effects on gastric acid secretion.**"

### Indications (p. 795)

"**first-line therapy for erosive esophagitis,** symptomatic **GERD that is poorly responsive to other medical treatment such as therapy with H2 receptor antagonists,** short-term treatment of **active duodenal ulcers and active benign gastric ulcers, gastric hypersecretory conditions (e.g., Zollinger-Ellison syndrome), nonsteroidal antiinflammatory drug (NSAID)-induced ulcers, and stress ulcer prophylaxis.**" Long term: "**maintenance of healing of erosive esophagitis** and **pathologic hypersecretory conditions.**" And: "**All of the PPIs can be used in combination with antibiotics to treat patients with *H. pylori* infections.**"

### Tube and oral administration rules (p. 795)

- **esomeprazole:** capsules "may be **opened, the granules dissolved in 50 mL of water,** and the solution given through the tube."
- **lansoprazole and omeprazole:** "Similar tubal administration is also listed by the manufacturer for **lansoprazole capsules** and **omeprazole powder for oral suspension.**"
- **pantoprazole:** "granules are to be used with NG tubes that are **larger than 16 French,** and it **may clog the tube if used with smaller tubes**" — restated at p. 796 as "at least size 16 French."
- Standing instruction: "**Consult the drug packaging for drug-specific instructions.** Be aware of the **particle size** ... and the **tube size** being used."
- Timing: "Orally administered PPIs (**and H2 receptor antagonists**) often work **best when taken 30 to 60 minutes before meals.**"

### Contraindications, adverse effects, interactions (p. 795)

**Contraindication:** "**known drug allergy**" only. **Adverse effects:** "generally well tolerated ... **similar to that for placebo or H2 receptor antagonists.**" The long-term concerns:

| Concern | As given |
|---|---|
| Overprescribing / infection | may be "**overprescribed**" and may "**predispose patients to GI tract infections** because of the **reduction of the normal acid-mediated antimicrobial protection**" |
| Osteoporosis | "**New concerns** ... for long-term users of PPIs to develop **osteoporosis** ... it is speculated that PPIs **speed up bone mineral loss**" |
| **FDA 2010** | a warning "regarding **long-term use of high-dose PPIs,** which has been associated with ***Clostridioides* (formerly *Clostridium*) *difficile* infections; risk for wrist, hip, and spine fractures; and pneumonia**" |
| **FDA 2011** | "**depletion of magnesium** was added to the warning" |
| Unconfirmed | "**concern about a possible link** between PPIs and **dementia,** as well as the development of **systemic lupus erythematosus**" |

**Interactions:** "**Few drug interactions occur with the PPIs;** however, they may **increase serum levels of diazepam and phenytoin.**" Also "an **increased chance of bleeding** in patients who are taking **both a PPI and warfarin**"; "**interference with the absorption of ketoconazole, ampicillin, iron salts, and digoxin**"; "**Sucralfate may delay the absorption of PPIs**"; and "**Food may decrease the absorption of PPIs,** and it is recommended that they be taken **on an empty stomach.**"

**The clopidogrel interaction, with mechanism:** "When **omeprazole** is given with **clopidogrel,** there is some concern of a **decrease in clopidogrel's effectiveness** because clopidogrel is **dependent on its conversion to an active metabolite by the CYP-450 enzyme system, specifically CYP2C19. Omeprazole is a strong inhibitor of CYP2C19. Other PPIs are less strong CYP2C19 inhibitors and are generally considered better alternatives.**"

### Dosages and profiles (pp. 795–796)

Dosage ranges are printed in the chapter's own table and are **lookup values, not memorizable structures**; read them there. The doses that carry meaning are quoted in the profiles and traps below.

- **omeprazole (Prilosec):** "the **first drug in this breakthrough class** of antisecretory drugs." PK as printed: onset **2 hr**, peak **5 days**, half-life **0.5–1 hr**, duration **1–5 days** *(see GAPS item 7)*.
- **lansoprazole (Prevacid):** "available in a **delayed-release capsule, granules for oral suspension, and orally disintegrating tablets (Prevacid SoluTab).** The capsules can be **opened and mixed (not crushed) with apple juice** for administration via NG tube, or the **SoluTab can be dissolved in water.** Lansoprazole is also available as **combination products for the treatment of *H. pylori* infection.**" PK as printed: onset **1.7 hr**, peak **4 weeks**, half-life **1–2 hr**, duration **24 hr** *(GAPS item 7)*.
- **pantoprazole (Protonix):** "the **first PPI available for intravenous use.** It was also the **first drug to be used as a continuous infusion for the treatment of GI bleeding.**" PK: PO onset **2.5 hr**, peak **2–2.5 hr**, half-life **1 hr**, duration **7 days**; IV onset and peak **end of infusion**, half-life **1 hr**, duration **7 days**.

> **Trap (confusable pair).** **PPI vs. H2RA, on five axes at once.**

> **Trap (priority question).** **PPIs go on an empty stomach, whole.** Review question 6 (p. 800) is a SATA offering: empty stomach, with meals, do not crush or chew, open the capsule and chew the contents, and give only when the patient complains of heartburn. The chapter supports **empty stomach** and **not crushed, opened, or chewed** ("the capsule must be taken **whole and not crushed, opened, or chewed,**" p. 798). It contradicts *with meals*; it contradicts *chewing the contents* (lansoprazole granules may be **sprinkled** and "**swallowed immediately,**" never chewed); and it contradicts *prn dosing,* since every PPI indication in the chapter is a scheduled course. (Official key not in the pull; GAPS item 5.)

> **Trap (confusable pair).** **"Empty stomach," "30 to 60 minutes before meals," and "before meals" are one instruction stated three ways** (pp. 795, 798, 799). Any of the three is defensible. Contrast the drugs that go **with** food: **cimetidine** ("with meals," p. 798), **misoprostol** ("with food ... with meals and at bedtime"), and **simethicone** ("**after** meals and at bedtime").

> **Trap (level/stage swap).** **Two FDA dates, different contents.** Cue: ***2010** = *C. difficile,* **wrist/hip/spine fractures,** and **pneumonia,** tied to **long-term high-dose** use; **2011** = **magnesium depletion,** added to the same warning.* Separately, **osteoporosis, dementia, and systemic lupus erythematosus** are named as **concerns and speculation,** not FDA warnings. Putting magnesium depletion in 2010, or calling the dementia link an FDA warning, crosses the categories.

> **Trap (confusable pair).** **omeprazole vs. the other PPIs, on clopidogrel** — the only place the chapter distinguishes PPIs pharmacologically. Cue: ***omeprazole is a strong CYP2C19 inhibitor;** clopidogrel **needs** CYP2C19 to become active; so omeprazole may make clopidogrel **less effective,** and other PPIs are "generally considered better alternatives."* The direction is the trap: here the interaction **weakens** the other drug, unlike the cimetidine interactions, which **raise** the other drug's level.

> **Trap (SATA, five options mapped to categories).** **PPI tube administration is drug-specific, and the French size belongs to exactly one drug.** Cue: ***pantoprazole** = NG tube **larger than 16 French**; **esomeprazole** = granules in **50 mL of water**; **lansoprazole** = capsule opened into **apple juice** (not crushed), **SoluTab** dissolved in water, or granules on **a tablespoon of applesauce** swallowed **immediately**; **omeprazole** = **powder for oral suspension.*** Assigning 16 French to lansoprazole, or applesauce to pantoprazole, is the whole item.

> **Trap (plausible-but-adjacent).** **Achlorhydria's complications do not include malabsorption of food.** The chapter lists bacterial overgrowth, intestinal metaplasia, and hip fracture, then rules the obvious distractor out in the next sentence: "**However, food absorption is not affected.**"

> **Trap (setting vs. focus).** **pantoprazole answers route questions, not potency questions.** Cue: *pantoprazole = **first PPI available IV,** **first used as a continuous infusion for GI bleeding,** and the only PPI in the dosage table listing **stress ulcer prophylaxis.*** Nothing claims it suppresses acid better; the chapter treats the class as interchangeable on efficacy except for the CYP2C19 point.

## §6 Miscellaneous acid-controlling drugs (pp. 796–797)

These "are **unique in terms of their mechanisms** ... include **sucralfate, misoprostol, and simethicone.** ... Other drugs are **bismuth subsalicylate (Pepto-Bismol; see Chapter 51)** and **metoclopramide (see Chapter 52).**"

### sucralfate (Carafate), p. 796

| Aspect | As given |
|---|---|
| Role | "a **mucosal protectant** in the treatment of **active stress ulcerations** and in **long-term therapy for peptic ulcer disease**" |
| Site | "acts **locally, not systemically,** binding **directly to the surface of an ulcer**" |
| Chemistry | "has **sucrose as its basic structure.** Once ... in contact with the **acid of the stomach,** it begins to **dissociate into aluminum hydroxide (an antacid) and sulfate anions**" |
| Barrier | the "**sulfated sucrose molecules** ... **bind to positively charged tissue proteins at the bases of ulcers and erosions,** forming a **protective barrier that can be thought of as a liquid bandage**"; this also "**limits the access of pepsin**" |
| Other actions | the aluminum salt "**stimulates secretion of both mucus and bicarbonate base**"; sucralfate "**binds and concentrates epidermal growth factor** ... which **promotes ulcer healing**" and "**stimulates the gastric secretion of prostaglandin molecules,** which serve a **mucoprotective** function" |
| Why it fell out of use | "effects are **transient,** and **multiple daily dosing (up to four times daily) is therefore needed**" |
| Indications | "**stress ulcers, esophageal erosions, and peptic ulcer disease**" |
| Contraindication | "**known drug allergy**" |
| Adverse effects | "**uncommon** but include **nausea, constipation, and dry mouth**" |
| Pharmacokinetics | "**Only minimal systemic absorption occurs,** and the drug is **virtually inert** ... **no pharmacokinetic table is listed**" |
| Interactions | "**physical interference with the absorption of other drugs.** This can be alleviated by taking **other drugs at least 2 hours ahead of sucralfate**" |
| Timing | "best given **1 hour before meals and at bedtime**"; antacids "avoided for **30 minutes before or after**" (p. 798) |
| Pregnancy / dose | "**pregnancy category B** ... normally dosed at **1 g orally four times daily**" |

### misoprostol (Cytotec), p. 796

| Aspect | As given |
|---|---|
| Class | "a **prostaglandin E analogue**" *(printed without the subscript; Chapter 44 calls it "a synthetic prostaglandin E1 analogue")* |
| Indication | "shown to effectively **reduce the incidence of gastric ulcers in patients taking NSAIDs** (see Chapter 44)" |
| Mechanism | "Prostaglandins are thought to **inhibit gastric acid secretion.** They are also thought to **protect the gastric mucosa from injury (cytoprotective function),** possibly by **enhancing the local production of mucus or bicarbonate,** by **promoting local cell regeneration,** and by **helping to maintain mucosal blood flow**" |
| Contraindications | "**known drug allergy** and in **pregnant women**" |
| Adverse effects | "**headache, GI distress, and vaginal bleeding**" |
| Interactions | "**no major drug interactions,** although **antacids may reduce drug absorption**" |
| Not for ulcer healing | prostaglandin analogues "**promote the healing of duodenal ulcers**" but "must be used in **dosages that usually produce disturbing adverse effects, such as abdominal cramps and diarrhea.** Thus they are **not thought to be as effective as H2 receptor antagonists and PPIs for this indication**" |
| Pregnancy category | "also used for its **abortifacient properties,** as discussed in **Chapter 34.** For this reason, it is classified a **pregnancy category X** drug" |
| Dose | "**200 mcg four times daily with meals** for the **duration of NSAID therapy** in patients at **high risk for ulceration**" |
| Pharmacokinetics, PO | onset **30 min**; peak **12 min**; half-life **20–40 min**; duration **1–2 days** *(printed as given; peak precedes onset — GAPS item 7)* |

### simethicone (Mylicon), pp. 796–797

- Role: "reduce the discomforts of **gastric or intestinal gas (flatulence)** and **aid in its release by the mouth or rectum** ... classified as an **antiflatulent** drug."
- **Mechanism:** "works by **altering the elasticity of mucus-coated gas bubbles, which causes them to break into smaller ones.** This **reduces gas pain and facilitates the expulsion of gas.**"
- Source of gas: "**swallowing of air,** as well as **normal digestive processes.** Gas in the **upper** GI tract ... consists largely of **nitrogen** ... usually expelled ... by **belching.** However, the composition of **flatus** is determined largely by the **dietary intake of carbohydrates** and the **metabolic activity of the bacteria in the intestines.**"
- Gas-producing foods: "**legumes (beans)** and **cruciferous vegetables (e.g., cauliflower, broccoli).**" Associated disorders: "**diverticulitis, dyspepsia (heartburn), peptic ulcers, and spastic or irritable colon;** gaseous distention can also occur **postoperatively.**"
- Safety and dose: "**no listed adverse effects, drug interactions, or pharmacokinetic parameters.** It is available **only for oral use.** ... **one to two tablets four to six times daily as needed.**"

> **Trap (confusable pair).** **sucralfate vs. misoprostol.** Both prevent ulcers, both sit in the same section, and their pregnancy categories are at **opposite ends** of the letter scale — the most dangerous swap in the chapter. Cue: ***sucralfate** = physical barrier, local, **category B, 1 g four times daily**; **misoprostol** = prostaglandin analogue, inhibits secretion and protects mucosa, **category X** because it is an abortifacient, **200 mcg four times daily with meals.*** Both are dosed **four times daily,** so frequency does not discriminate; **units (g vs. mcg)** and **pregnancy category** do. The raw scan bleeds the two profiles together into "34. For this **sucralfate** reason, it is classified a pregnancy category X drug" — precisely the wrong pairing.

> **Trap (priority question).** **Pregnancy is the misoprostol contraindication, not a caution.** Review question 8 (p. 800) offers hypothyroidism, type 2 diabetes, **pregnancy,** and hypertension for a first dose of misoprostol. The chapter names exactly two contraindications — "**known drug allergy** and ... **pregnant women**" — with the reason, **abortifacient properties.** None of the other three options appears in the profile. (Official key not in the pull; GAPS item 5.)

> **Trap (plausible-but-adjacent).** **simethicone does not touch acid.** Review question 1 (p. 800) offers "neutralizes gastric pH," "buffers the effects of pepsin," "decreases gastric acid secretion," and "**causes mucus-coated gas bubbles to break into smaller ones.**" The first three are the mechanisms of **antacids** and of **H2RAs/PPIs,** transplanted onto the wrong drug. Cue: *simethicone changes **bubble elasticity;** it has **no acid effect, no adverse effects, no interactions, and no pharmacokinetics.***

> **Trap (confusable pair).** **Sucralfate contains an antacid but is not one.** It "dissociates into **aluminum hydroxide (an antacid)**," and its adverse effects carry aluminum's signature (**nausea, constipation, dry mouth**). But its **mechanism is the barrier,** not neutralization, and its interaction mechanism is "**physical interference with the absorption of other drugs.**" Calling sucralfate an antacid picks up a true component and mislabels the whole.

> **Trap (level/stage swap).** **The three sucralfate clocks are three different numbers.** Cue: ***other drugs → at least 2 hours ahead** of sucralfate; **antacids → avoid 30 minutes before or after**; **sucralfate itself → 1 hour before meals and at bedtime.*** Review question 3 (p. 800) exploits this, offering "**waiting 30 minutes** before administering **other** drugs" (the antacid window applied to the wrong category) beside "**giving other drugs 2 hours before**" (the actual rule) and "on an **empty stomach**" (supported at p. 799). "With meals" and "restrict fluids" appear nowhere in the profile. (Official key not in the pull; GAPS item 5.)

> **Trap (plausible-but-adjacent).** **Misoprostol prevents NSAID ulcers; it is not the drug for healing a duodenal ulcer.** The chapter concedes prostaglandin analogues "promote the healing of duodenal ulcers," then rules them out on tolerability. Cue: *its indication is **prophylaxis during NSAID therapy,** dosed "**for the duration of NSAID therapy,**" not a healing course.*

## §7 Nursing process (pp. 797–799)

### Assessment (p. 797)

- **General:** history "with special focus on **GI tract–related disorders** and signs and symptoms of **ulcer disease and GERD.** Assess **current bowel patterns** ... and **GI tract–related pain.**"
- **Baseline labs:** "**hepatic function (e.g., serum ALP, ALT, and AST levels)** and **renal function (serum creatinine and BUN levels).**"
- **Medication history:** acid-controlling drugs "have **many interactions,** so it is **critical to patient safety** to pay close attention to **all** medications the patient is taking," including "**prescription drugs, over-the-counter products, herbals, and supplements.**"
- **Cardiac history, and why:** attention to "**heart failure, hypertension,** other cardiac diseases ... **edema, fluid and electrolyte imbalances, and renal disease,**" because "the **high sodium content of various antacids** ... could lead to **exacerbation of cardiac problems, renal dysfunction, and fluid-electrolyte problems.**"

| Class | Assess |
|---|---|
| **Antacids (Al/Mg)** | identify all other medications; combination products "may have **fewer adverse effects** than either type ... by itself" |
| **Calcium antacids** | usable as a **source of calcium,** but carry risk of "**rebound hyperacidity, milk-alkali syndrome, and changes in systemic pH,** especially if the patient has **abnormal renal functioning**" |
| **H2RAs** | "**renal and liver function,** as well as **level of consciousness**"; older adults show "more **disorientation and confusion**" |
| **nizatidine** | baseline "**BUN, creatinine, bilirubin, ALP, AST, and ALT**" |
| **PPIs** | "**swallowing capacity** because of the **size of some of the oral capsules**"; history of **GI tract infections**; **osteoporosis**; interactions with "**diazepam, phenytoin, warfarin, ampicillin, and iron salts**" |
| **sucralfate, simethicone** | "**bowel patterns and bowel sounds** ... **abdominal distention and rigidity, which may indicate a medical emergency**" |

### Human need statements (p. 797)

They map one-to-one onto the antacid salt categories, which is what makes them exam-shaped:

1. "**Altered gastrointestinal elimination, constipation,** related to the adverse effects of **aluminum-containing antacids** and other drugs used to treat hyperacidity"
2. "**Altered gastrointestinal elimination, diarrhea,** related to the adverse effects of **magnesium-containing antacids** and other drugs used to treat hyperacidity"
3. "**Altered effective perception, deficient knowledge,** related to **lack of information about antacids, H2 receptor antagonists, or PPIs,** including their use and potential adverse effects"

Planning outcomes (pp. 797–798) mirror the same split: minimal to no **constipation** through "taking **aluminum/magnesium combination products**"; minimal to no **diarrhea** through "**avoidance of antacids containing only magnesium**"; and knowledge of "the **various adverse effects and when to seek further medical attention.**"

### Implementation (p. 798) — the timing table

### Evaluation (p. 798)

- **Therapeutic response:** "**relief of symptoms** associated with **peptic ulcer, gastritis, esophagitis, gastric hyperacidity, or hiatal hernia** (i.e., **decrease in epigastric pain, fullness, and abdominal swelling**)."
- **Adverse effects:** "range from **constipation or diarrhea** to **nausea, vomiting, abdominal pain, and hypotension.**"

### Patient teaching (p. 799)

Keep a "**daily journal of precipitating and/or alleviating factors.**" Antacids: not within **1 to 2 hours** of other medications; with **at least 8 oz of water**; may cause "**premature dissolution of the enteric coating**" of enteric-coated drugs; "**not to be used for prolonged periods without medical attention** ... contact a health care provider **after 2 weeks** of self-prescribed use." > **Trap (level/stage swap).** **The antacid-plus-H2RA interval is printed as three different numbers in four places** (1 to 2 hours before, p. 793; 1 hour apart, p. 797; 1 to 2 hours after, p. 798; 1 hour before or after, p. 799). The chapter never reconciles them, so a stem quoting **either 1 hour or 1 to 2 hours** can be defended from the text. What is **not** defensible anywhere is giving them **simultaneously.** Anchor on direction rather than number: *the **H2RA goes first,** the **antacid follows.***

> **Trap (confusable pair).** **Six drugs, five food rules.** Cue: *cimetidine **with** meals; misoprostol **with** food, with meals and at bedtime; simethicone **after** meals and at bedtime; PPIs **30 to 60 minutes before** meals, on an empty stomach; sucralfate **1 hour before** meals and at bedtime; famotidine **without regard to meals or food.*** Famotidine is the only one with no food rule at all.

> **Trap (confusable pair).** **Which oral forms may be altered and which may not.** Cue: *may be altered — **lansoprazole** capsules into applesauce or apple juice (**not crushed**), **SoluTab** in water, **esomeprazole** granules in 50 mL water, **sucralfate** tablets crushed or dissolved, **chewable antacids** thoroughly chewed; may **not** — **omeprazole** capsules ("whole and not crushed, opened, or chewed") and **pantoprazole** tablets ("without crushing or splitting").* Lansoprazole and omeprazole, same class, land on **opposite sides** of this line.

> **Trap (priority question).** **Black, tarry stools or hematemesis outranks everything else here.** They appear in three separate places and are labeled "**a sign of possible GI tract bleeding.**" Cue: *constipation and diarrhea are **call the prescriber**; **melena and hematemesis** are the bleeding cue in a chapter whose whole patient population is at risk.* Separately, **abdominal distention with rigidity** is the one finding the chapter calls "a **medical emergency**" (p. 797).

> **Trap (SATA, five options mapped to categories).** **Baseline labs split by organ and by drug.** Cue: *hepatic = **ALP, ALT, AST** (plus **bilirubin** for nizatidine); renal = **serum creatinine, BUN.*** **nizatidine** is the one drug given an explicit six-item list. No item in this chapter calls for a **platelet count,** despite famotidine's thrombocytopenia and the hematologic adverse-effect row — so a lab option is best checked against the **assessment** section, not the adverse-effect table.

> **Trap (setting vs. focus).** **The cardiac history is assessed because of the *antacid's sodium,* not because acid-controlling drugs are cardiotoxic.** Cue: *heart failure, hypertension, edema → the question is **which antacid,** and the answer excludes **sodium bicarbonate.*** Key points restates it: "**Cautious use of antacids is recommended in patients who have heart failure, hypertension, or other cardiac diseases or who require sodium restriction, especially if the antacid is high in sodium**" (p. 799).

**Answer keys are in two different places and neither is in the pull.** The **case study** (p. 798) and **critical thinking exercises** (p. 799) say "For answers, see http://evolve.elsevier.com/Lilley." The **review questions** (p. 800) say "For answers, see **Answers to Review Questions at the back of the book,**" outside this chapter's page range. **Case study: Proton Pump Inhibitors** (p. 798). A 50-year-old attorney "self-treated for heartburn for years by drinking large amounts of antacids"; her physician "instructed her to **stop taking the antacids**" and referred her to a gastroenterologist. Endoscopy found "**GERD and gastritis secondary to stress-induced hyperacidity,**" and she was prescribed **omeprazole (Prilosec) 20 mg once a day.** The four questions asked: why stop the antacids; what else will be tested for; the rationale for PPIs in GERD; and PPI teaching. Relevant chapter text: antacids "**may mask symptoms of serious underlying disease such as bleeding ulcer or malignancy**"; **acid rebound** follows abrupt discontinuation of high-neutralizing-capacity antacids; PPIs are "**first-line therapy for erosive esophagitis**" and for GERD "**poorly responsive**" to H2RAs; and the omeprazole 20 mg/day for 4 to 8 weeks row matches her prescription exactly.

**Critical thinking exercise 1** (p. 799): a patient with "a history of **decreased renal function**" reports great relief from an antacid that is "a combination of **aluminum hydroxide and magnesium hydroxide.**" Governing sentence: "**antacids containing magnesium must be avoided in patients with renal failure**" (p. 791); both calcium- and magnesium-based antacids "are **more likely to accumulate to toxic levels in patients with renal disease**" (p. 793).

**Critical thinking exercise 2** (p. 799): "I like taking antacids because they **coat my stomach and protect my ulcer.**" See the antacid mechanism trap in §3 — coating is sucralfate's action.

**Review question 7** (p. 800) is arithmetic, not judgment: pantoprazole "**40 mg in 100 mL normal saline IVPB** ... **Infuse over 30 minutes,**" pump programmable only in mL/hour. 100 mL ÷ 30 min × 60 min/hr = **200 mL/hr.** That is my computation, not the printed key.
