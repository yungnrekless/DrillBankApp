## 1. The organizing distinction: why there are two chapters

Chapter 45 covers cell cycle–**specific** drugs; this one covers cell cycle–**non**specific drugs. As given: Chapter 45 "describes the various antineoplastic drugs that are effective against cancer cells during **specific phases** in the cell growth cycle. In contrast, this chapter focuses on drugs that have antineoplastic activity **regardless of the phase of the cell cycle**" (p. 726). For the cell growth cycle itself, and for emetic potential, shared dose-limiting effects, and hyperuricemia, the chapter defers to **Chapter 45**.

**Blueprint split:** Chapter 45 = **2 questions**, Chapter 46 = **1**. Three of 121 items; one study unit, hinged on the phase distinction.

> **Trap (confusable pair).** **Specific vs. nonspecific.** Cue: ***specific = one phase; nonspecific = any phase.*** The word describes the **phase**, not the tumor type and not selectivity for cancer over normal cells. "Works on any type of cancer" and "spares normal cells" are the axis swaps.

> **Trap (plausible-but-adjacent).** **Two drugs here are cell cycle–SPECIFIC, flagged in parentheses:** "**bleomycin (which is actually a cell cycle–specific drug)**" (p. 729), and hydroxyurea, which works "in the **S and G₁ phases**" and is here only "because it is **included as a miscellaneous drug**" (p. 731). Chapter membership is not classification.

> **Trap (level/stage swap).** Right drug, wrong phase. Hydroxyurea's **S and G₁** is the only phase claim printed in this chapter; any option putting an alkylating drug or an anthracycline in a named phase is wrong on the chapter's own terms.

## 2. The class roster

"There are **two broad classes** of cell cycle–nonspecific cancer drugs: **alkylating drugs and cytotoxic antibiotics**" (p. 726). Two further groupings are not cell-cycle-based at all: **miscellaneous** and **hormonal**.

| Group | Subcategories as printed | Named drugs |
|---|---|---|
| **Alkylating** (p. 727) | Classic alkylators (**nitrogen mustards**) | chlorambucil, **cyclophosphamide**, ifosfamide, **mechlorethamine**, melphalan |
| | **Nitrosoureas** | **carmustine**, lomustine, streptozocin |
| | **Miscellaneous alkylators** | altretamine, **busulfan**, **carboplatin**, **cisplatin**, dacarbazine, oxaliplatin, procarbazine, temozolomide, thiotepa |
| **Cytotoxic antibiotics** (p. 729) | **Anthracyclines**; **anthracenedione**; other | daunorubicin, **doxorubicin**, epirubicin, idarubicin, valrubicin; **mitoxantrone**; **bleomycin**, dactinomycin, mitomycin, plicamycin |
| **Miscellaneous** (p. 731) | not subdivided | **bevacizumab** (angiogenesis inhibitor, anti-**VEGF**), **imatinib** (**bcr-abl tyrosine kinase** inhibitor, **CML**), **hydroxyurea**, **mitotane**, **octreotide** (carcinoid crisis) |
| **Hormonal** (p. 732) | aromatase inhibitors; SERMs; progestins; androgens; estrogen receptor antagonists; antiandrogens; GnRH agonists; antineoplastic hormone | anastrozole; tamoxifen; megestrol; fluoxymesterone; fulvestrant; bicalutamide; leuprolide; estramustine |

**Mechanisms.** Alkylating drugs "**alter the chemical structure of the cells' DNA**," so "abnormal chemical bonds form between the adjacent DNA strands," yielding defective nucleic acids and cell death (p. 727). Cytotoxic antibiotics work by **intercalation**, "inserted between the two strands of a DNA molecule, ultimately blocking DNA synthesis"; they also "inhibit the enzyme **topoisomerase II**" and many "generate **free radicals**" (p. 729).

> **Trap (SATA, five options mapped to categories).** Membership rule: **alkylating drugs and cytotoxic antibiotics** are the two nonspecific classes; **antimetabolites, mitotic inhibitors, and topoisomerase inhibitors** are Chapter 45's and appear as headings **nowhere** in Chapter 46 — those are the intended misses.

> **Trap (confusable pair).** **Bifunctional vs. polyfunctional.** Cue: ***bi = two sites on the DNA molecule; poly = several reactions per single drug molecule.*** **Mechlorethamine** and **cisplatin** are bifunctional; **cyclophosphamide** is "a **polyfunctional** alkylating drug and is a **prodrug requiring in vivo activation**" (pp. 728–729).

> **Trap (plausible-but-adjacent).** **Three labels that sound right and are not.** A cytotoxic **antibiotic** is not an anti-infective — these are "natural substances produced by the **mold *Streptomyces***" that kill cancer cells (p. 729). They inhibit topoisomerase II but are **not** "topoisomerase inhibitors," a Chapter 45 heading. **Imatinib** "is **not a monoclonal antibody** but rather ... a **tyrosine kinase inhibitor**" (p. 731).

## 3. The drug-to-toxicity pairings (the whole testable core)

From Table 46.2 (p. 728) and Table 46.5 (p. 730), quoted as printed:

| Drug | Class | Severe adverse effect(s) as given | p. |
|---|---|---|---|
| **cisplatin** | alkylating | "Nephrotoxicity, peripheral neuropathy, ototoxicity" | 728 |
| **carboplatin** | alkylating | "Nephrotoxicity, neurotoxicity, bone marrow suppression" | 728 |
| **cyclophosphamide** | alkylating | "Hemorrhagic cystitis" | 728 |
| **busulfan** | alkylating | "Pulmonary fibrosis" | 728 |
| **bleomycin** | cytotoxic antibiotic | "Pulmonary fibrosis, pneumonitis" | 730 |
| **doxorubicin, idarubicin** | anthracycline | "Liver and cardiovascular toxicities" | 730 |
| **dactinomycin, daunorubicin** | cytotoxic antibiotic | "Liver toxicity, tissue damage in the event of extravasation, heart failure" | 730 |
| **mitoxantrone** | anthracenedione | "Cardiovascular toxicity" | 730 |
| **mitomycin** | cytotoxic antibiotic | "Liver, kidney, and lung toxicities" | 730 |
| **plicamycin** | cytotoxic antibiotic | "Tissue damage in the event of extravasation" | 730 |

> **Trap (confusable pair).** **Doxorubicin = heart; bleomycin = lungs.** Cue: ***"doxo" hits the cardio; "bleo" blows out the lungs.*** Monitor **cardiac function** for doxorubicin ("severe cases of **cardiomyopathy** are associated with **large cumulative doses**," limited by dose caps and **dexrazoxane**) and **respiratory function** for bleomycin. The class rule hides it: cytotoxic antibiotics share bone marrow suppression, and "**the one exception is bleomycin**, which instead causes **pulmonary toxicity**" (p. 729). Nephro- or ototoxicity offered for doxorubicin is cisplatin's profile in disguise.

> **Trap (confusable pair).** **Cisplatin vs. carboplatin.** Cue, from Table 46.2's own footnote: ***carboplatin has LESS nephrotoxicity and neurotoxicity but MORE bone marrow suppression***, and **ototoxicity is listed for cisplatin only.** The chapter warns this pair has killed patients: "**medication errors, resulting in deaths, have occurred when cisplatin was confused for carboplatin**" (p. 728).

> **Trap (confusable pair).** **Cisplatin nephrotoxicity vs. cyclophosphamide hemorrhagic cystitis.** Both alkylating, both urinary, both managed with fluids. Cue: ***cisplatin injures the KIDNEY (creatinine, urine output); cyclophosphamide injures the BLADDER — "the sudden onset of hematuria with bladder pain and discomfort"*** (p. 735). Hydration is the stated prevention for both (pp. 727, 735). **Mesna is never named in this chapter — see Limits.**

> **Trap (confusable pair).** **The "rubicins."** Daunorubicin, doxorubicin, epirubicin, idarubicin, valrubicin — "even though these drugs are in the same class, their **use and drug effects are very different**" (p. 730). Cue: *never verify a drug as "a rubicin."* Table 46.5 splits them: **daunorubicin** = heart **failure** plus extravasation damage; **doxorubicin/idarubicin** = liver and cardiovascular toxicity.

> **Trap (priority question).** **Cisplatin patient needs an antibiotic for pneumonia — which class?** (Review question 1, p. 737.) Default rule as printed: "**avoid administering an alkylating drug with any other drug capable of causing similar toxicities**." The worked example *is* the item — cisplatin plus an **aminoglycoside** gives "**additive nephrotoxic effects**" and likely renal failure (p. 728). Penicillin, sulfa, and fluoroquinolone are the wrong options.

> **Trap (plausible-but-adjacent).** **Three supportive drugs, three separate problems.** **Amifostine** reduces "the **renal toxicity associated with cisplatin**"; **allopurinol** reduces **uric acid** (both p. 735); **dexrazoxane** protects against **doxorubicin cardiotoxicity** (p. 729).

> **Trap (plausible-but-adjacent).** **Bleomycin and doxorubicin raise serum digoxin.** They "cause **serum digoxin levels to increase**" (p. 730) — the mechanism behind critical thinking exercise 2, whose patient has a slow irregular rhythm, nausea, and "yellow hazy circles around the lights."

## 4. Extravasation

> **Trap (setting vs. focus).** **The catheter is the setting; the drug is the hazard.** Box 46.1 names the axis: "**infiltration may occur with any intravenous catheter; it is the specific drug and its characteristics** ... **that poses the concern**" (p. 728). Central access reduces but does not eliminate risk — extravasation still occurs with central and PICC lines. "A central line makes extravasation impossible" is the swap.

> **Trap (confusable pair).** **Irritant vs. vesicant.** Cue: ***an irritant irritates the vein; a vesicant kills the tissue.*** A vesicant causes "**cell death** with extravasation and **necrosis with ulcerative properties**," so it "may lead to **massive tissue injury**, whereas extravasation of an **irritant** results in **significantly less damage**" (p. 728). **Doxorubicin is the named strong vesicant**, and "**central venous access is needed for administration of vesicants**" (pp. 727–728).

> **Trap (priority question).** **First action on detecting extravasation.** Default: "**stop the infusion immediately and contact the prescriber, leaving the intravenous catheter in place**," then aspirate residual drug and blood, then consult policy on antidote, packs, and elevation (p. 728). Do **not** pull the catheter first — it is the route for the antidote. Antidotes (Table 46.3): **carmustine → sodium bicarbonate**; **mechlorethamine → sodium thiosulfate**; **cold compresses** for both.

**Handling.** Antineoplastics are handled with caution "because of their possible **carcinogenic, mutagenic, and teratogenic** properties" (p. 734): pharmacy mixes under a **laminar airflow hood** in mask, gown, and gloves; units **double-flush** secretions and keep **spill kits**; nurses giving them "must be **certified** to administer chemotherapy" (p. 733).
