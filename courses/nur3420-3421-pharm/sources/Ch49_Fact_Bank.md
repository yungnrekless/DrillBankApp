## Key terms (quoted as given, pp. 773–774)

| Term | p. | Definition as given |
|---|---|---|
| Active immunization | 773 | "A type of immunization that causes the development of a complete and long-lasting immunity to a certain infection through exposure of the body to the associated disease antigen; it can be natural active immunization (i.e., having the disease) or artificial active immunization (i.e., receiving a vaccine or toxoid)." |
| Active immunizing drugs | 773 | "Toxoids or vaccines that are administered to a host to stimulate host production of antibodies." |
| Antibodies | 773 | "Immunoglobulin molecules that have an antigen-specific amino acid sequence and are synthesized by the humoral immune system (B cells) in response to exposure to a specific antigen. Their purpose is to attack and destroy molecules of this antigen." |
| Antibody titer | 773 | "The amount of an antibody needed to react with and neutralize a given volume or amount of a specific antigen." |
| Antigens | 773 | "Substances, usually proteins and foreign to a host, which stimulate the production of antibodies and that react specifically with those antibodies. Examples of antigens include bacterial exotoxins and viruses. An allergen (e.g., dust, pollen, mold) is an antigen that can produce an immediate-type hypersensitivity reaction or allergy." |
| Antiserum | 773 | "A serum that contains antibodies. It is usually obtained from an animal that has been immunized against a specific antigen." |
| Antitoxin | 773 | "An antiserum against a toxin (or toxoid). It is most often a purified antiserum obtained from animals (usually horses) by the injection of a toxin or toxoid so that antibodies to the toxin (i.e., antitoxin) can be collected from the animals and used to provide artificial passive immunity to humans exposed to a given toxin (e.g., tetanus immunoglobulin)." |
| Antivenin | 773 | "An antiserum against a venom (poison produced by an animal) used to treat humans or other animals that have been envenomed (e.g., by snakebite, spider bite, or scorpion sting)." |
| Biologic antimicrobial drugs | 773 | "Substances of biologic origin used to prevent, treat, or cure infectious diseases (e.g., vaccines, toxoids, immunoglobulins). These drugs are often simply referred to as biologics. However, biologics also refers to drugs of bioterrorism (e.g., anthrax spores, smallpox virus), depending on the context." |
| Booster shot | 773 | "A repeat dose of an antigen, such as a vaccine or toxoid, which is usually administered in an amount smaller than that used in the original immunization. It is given to maintain the immune response of a previously immunized patient at, or return the response to, a clinically effective level." |
| Cell-mediated immune system | 773 | "The immune response that is mediated by T cells (as opposed to B cells, which produce antibodies). T cells mount their immune response through activities such as the release of cytokines (chemicals that stimulate other protective immune functions) as well as through direct cytotoxicity (e.g., phagocytosis of an antigen)." |
| Herd immunity | 773 | "Resistance to a disease on the part of an entire community or population because a large proportion of its members are immune to the disease." |
| Immune response | 773–774 | "A cascade of biochemical events that occurs in response to entry of an antigen (foreign substance) into the body; key processes of the immune response include phagocytosis ('eating of cells') of foreign microorganisms and synthesis of antibodies that react with specific antigens to inactivate them. Immune response centers on the blood but may also involve the lymphatic system and the reticuloendothelial system." |
| Immunization | 774 | "The induction of immunity by administration of a vaccine or toxoid (active immunization) or antiserum (passive immunization)." |
| Immunizing biologics | 774 | "Toxoids, vaccines, or immunoglobulins that are targeted against specific infectious microorganisms or toxins." |
| Immunoglobulins | 774 | "Glycoproteins synthesized and used by the humoral immune system (B cells) to attack and kill all substances foreign to the body. The term is synonymous with immune globulins." |
| Passive immunization | 774 | "A type of immunization in which immunity to infection occurs by injecting a person with antiserum or concentrated antibodies that directly give the host the means to fight off an invading microorganism (artificial passive immunization). The host's immune system therefore does not have to manufacture these antibodies. This process also occurs when antibodies pass from mother to infant during breastfeeding or through the placenta during pregnancy (natural passive immunization)." |
| Passive immunizing drugs | 774 | "Drugs containing antibodies or antitoxins that can kill or inactivate pathogens by binding to the associated antigens. These are directly injected into a person (host) and provide that person with the means to fend off infection, bypassing the host's own immune system." |
| Recombinant | 774 | "Relating to or containing a combination of genetic material from two or more organisms. Such genetic recombination is one of the key methods of biotechnology and is often used to manufacture immunizing drugs and various other medications." |
| Reticuloendothelial system | 774 | "Specialized cells located in the liver, spleen, lymphatics, and bone marrow that remove particles from the circulation, such as aging antibody molecules." |
| Toxin | 774 | "Any poison produced by a plant, animal, or microorganism that is highly toxic to other living organisms." |
| Toxoids | 774 | "Bacterial exotoxins that are modified or inactivated (by chemicals or heat) so that they are no longer toxic but can still bind to host B cells to stimulate the formation of antitoxin; toxoids are often used in the same manner as vaccines to promote artificial active immunity in humans. They are one type of active immunizing drug (e.g., tetanus toxoid)." |
| Vaccines | 774 | "Suspensions of live, attenuated, or killed microorganisms that can promote an artificially induced active immunity against a particular microorganism. They are another type of active immunizing drug (e.g., tetanus vaccine)." |
| Venom | 774 | "A poison that is secreted by an animal (e.g., snake, insect, or spider)." |

## 1. Active versus passive immunization

Table 49.1 (p. 775), de-bled, with speed and duration from pp. 776–777 and Key Points (p. 786).

| Axis | **Active** | **Passive** |
|---|---|---|
| Drug type (artificial) | **Toxoid or vaccine** | **Immunoglobulin, antitoxin, or antivenin** |
| Mechanism (artificial) | "an antigen-antibody response similar to that after antigen exposure in the natural disease process" | "direct administration of exogenous antibodies; antibody concentration will decrease over time" |
| Mechanism (natural) | "Production of one's own antibodies during actual infection" | "Transmission of antibodies from mother to infant through placenta or during breastfeeding" |
| Speed | Delayed; the host must respond first | "allows for **more rapid** prevention or treatment of disease" |
| Duration | "**long-lasting or permanent**" | "**transitory (short-lived)**"; lasts until the immunoglobulins are cleared by the **reticuloendothelial system** |
| Host antibody response? | Yes | **No** — "an antibody response is not stimulated in the host" |
| Use | people "who have **at least a partially functioning immune system**" | the **immunodeficient**, those for whom **active immunization is contraindicated**, and those **exposed to or anticipating exposure** |

> **Trap (confusable pair).** ***A**ctive = you make **A**ntibodies, slow but lasting; **P**assive = **P**re-made antibodies **P**assed to you, fast but fading.* Two swaps to expect: passive labeled long-lasting, and active offered to an immunocompromised patient — whom the chapter sends to "**passive** immunizing drugs such as immunoglobulins" (p. 777).

> **Trap (level/stage swap).** Artificial vs. natural crosses both columns: **four states, not two.** *Artificial active = **a shot**; natural active = **you had the disease**; artificial passive = **immunoglobulin or antivenin**; natural passive = **placenta or breast milk**.* The last is the one mis-tagged as active.

> **Trap (plausible-but-adjacent).** Toxoid vs. vaccine is **not** the active/passive pair — both are active. *Toxoid = the **bacterial exotoxin**, defanged; vaccine = the **organism**, defanged* (p. 775).

> **Trap (SATA, five options mapped to categories).** Passive drugs "are divided into **three groups: antitoxins, immunoglobulins, and snake and spider antivenins**" (p. 777). *Names ending in **-immunoglobulin**, **antivenin**, or **antitoxin** are passive; **vaccine** or **toxoid** are active.* Exception: the **RSV** drug "is not an immunizing drug per se but is a specialized **antiviral** drug" (p. 776).

> **Trap (setting vs. focus).** Individual protection vs. **herd immunity** — the axis is **who** is protected, not how. "The successful immunization of **95% or more** of a population confers protection on the **entire population**" (p. 777). Calling it a stronger individual response swaps population for person.

## 2. Live versus inactivated, and who cannot receive them

| | **Live / attenuated** | **Killed / inactivated** |
|---|---|---|
| Immunity | "**lifelong immunity** against that particular disease" | "only **partial immunity** is conferred" |
| Boosters | not described as needing them | "must be given **periodic booster shots**" |
| Named here | MMR, varicella (Varivax), **FluMist** (intranasal) | inactivated polio, hepatitis B, most influenza (IM) |

Contraindications, quoted (p. 777): "allergy to the immunization itself or allergy to any of its components, such as **eggs or yeast**"; defer "until after recovery from a **febrile illness or temporary immunocompromised state** (e.g., after cancer chemotherapy), if possible." Key Points (p. 786): withhold from "those with **active infections, febrile illnesses, or history of a previous reaction**... The use of these drugs in **pregnant women is usually contraindicated**," and "Patients who are **immunocompromised are at greater risk** for experiencing serious adverse effects." MMR is "contraindicated in... **pregnant women**" (p. 784); pregnancy "needs to be avoided for **3 months after** measles virus vaccination," on "the theoretic risk that the **live virus vaccine may cause a fetal infection**" (p. 780).

> **Trap (confusable pair).** ***Live = lifelong, but not for the pregnant or the immunocompromised; killed = partial, so it needs boosters.*** The properties travel in pairs, so "killed → lifelong" or "live → needs boosters" is wrong on both halves at once.

> **Trap (priority question).** Default for "who should not receive this?" is **live vaccine + pregnancy or immunocompromise.** Printed overrides: **hepatitis B** — "pregnancy is not considered a contraindication" (pp. 780, 782); **rabies** — give it anyway (p. 777); **egg allergy** — hives-only patients may be vaccinated (p. 777); **minor illness** — the febrile rule excludes "cold, mild infection, **ear infection**, or low-grade fever" (p. 783), which is exactly one of the options in review question 1 (p. 786).

> **Trap (confusable pair).** **DTaP vs. Tdap**, the only pair with its own printed safety box (p. 783). ***Capital letters = more of that antigen.*** DTaP: "higher amounts of antigens," "**pediatric patients 6 weeks to 6 years of age**," "indicated for **initial immunizations**." Tdap: "reduced diphtheria toxoid," "a **booster** vaccine for older children, adolescents, and adults."

## 3. Adverse effects

Table 49.2 (p. 778). **Minor** — CNS: "Fever, adenopathy"; integumentary: "Minor rash, soreness at injection site, urticaria, arthritis." **Severe** — CNS: "Fever higher than 103° F (39.4° C), encephalitis, convulsions, peripheral neuropathy, anaphylactic reaction, shock"; integumentary: rash; respiratory: dyspnea; other: cyanosis. "The **overwhelming majority**" are minor, treated "with **acetaminophen and rest**." **Serum sickness** follows "**repeated injections of equine (horse)-derived** immunizing drugs." Serious reactions are reported to **VAERS**, "cosponsored by the US Food and Drug Administration (FDA) and the CDC." Interactions are "**not generally a problem**," except **immunosuppressive drugs**, which "hinder the generation of the active immunity."

> **Trap (level/stage swap).** Fever sits on **both** lists; the number discriminates. *Plain fever = **minor**; "**higher than 103° F (39.4° C)**" = **severe**.* Review question 6 (p. 786) asks which of six findings to report immediately — sort each against Table 49.2, not against how alarming it sounds.

> **Trap (priority question).** Antipyretic timing reverses the intuitive answer: acetaminophen or ibuprofen "**should not be given until after the vaccine has been administered**" (p. 778). Premedicating is the distractor.

> **Trap (plausible-but-adjacent).** Passive immunity is not immunity you keep. Critical thinking exercise 1 (p. 786) supplies the misconception verbatim after a black widow antivenin: "Now I will be immune to black widow spiders after this shot, right?"
