> **Trap (confusable pair).** **Cell cycle–specific vs. nonspecific.** Cue: ***specific = one phase only; nonspecific = any phase.*** The book defeats the "which is stronger" distractor itself: drugs "may have actions that fall into both of these categories," and "**regardless** of the cell cycle characteristics of a drug, it is more effective on rapidly growing tumors" (p. 706). Nonspecific is not stronger, just phase-indifferent.

> **Trap (confusable pair).** **Growth fraction vs. mitotic index vs. doubling time.** Cue: ***growth fraction = a percentage; mitotic index = a count per 1000 cells; doubling time = a clock.*** "The actual **number** of cells that are in the M phase is called the mitotic index" (p. 705); "**tumors with shorter doubling times are often difficult to cure** because of rapid regrowth" (p. 704).

> **Trap (confusable pair).** **Differentiation vs. anaplasia.** Cue: ***differentiation = maturing into specialized cells; anaplasia = the absence of it.*** The tested half is the consequence: "more highly differentiated tumors generally have a **better** therapeutic response... Cancers with a larger proportion of **undifferentiated** cells are often **less** responsive to chemotherapy or radiation" (p. 705).

> **Trap (confusable pair).** **Analogue vs. antimetabolite.** Not synonyms; the chapter nests them: "Analogues may have **agonist or antagonist** activity. An **antagonist analogue** is also known as an **antimetabolite**" (p. 709). Cue: ***every antimetabolite is an analogue; not every analogue is one.***

> **Trap (confusable pair).** **Mutagen vs. carcinogen vs. oncogenic.** Cue: ***mutagen changes DNA; carcinogen produces cancer; oncogenic is the adjective, usually attached to viruses.*** The chapter keeps them distinct: mutagenicity "often (**but not always**) leads to carcinogenicity" (p. 704).

## §1 The organizing distinction, and why there is a Part 2

Quoted, because Chapters 45 and 46 both hang off it (p. 706):

> "Cancer chemotherapy drugs can be subdivided into two main groups **based on where in the cell cycle they have their effects.** Antineoplastic drugs that are **cytotoxic (cell killing) in any phase** of the cycle are called **cell cycle–nonspecific** drugs. Those drugs that are **cytotoxic during a specific cell cycle phase** are called **cell cycle–specific** drugs... **This chapter discusses the cell cycle–specific drugs. Chapter 46 focuses on cell cycle–nonspecific drugs,** as well as various miscellaneous antineoplastic drugs."

Table 45.5, the five phases (p. 705):

| Phase | What happens | Drug target |
|---|---|---|
| **G<sub>0</sub>: resting** | "Most normal human cells exist predominantly in this phase." "**Cancer cells in this phase are not susceptible to the toxic effects of cell cycle–specific drugs.**" | none |
| **G<sub>1</sub>: first gap / postmitotic** | "Enzymes necessary for DNA synthesis are produced." | hormonal drugs, antineoplastic enzymes (Fig. 45.3) |
| **S: DNA synthesis** | "DNA synthesis takes place, from DNA strand separation to replication of each strand." | antimetabolites, topoisomerase I inhibitors |
| **G<sub>2</sub>: second gap / premitotic** | "RNA and specialized proteins are made." | topoisomerase II inhibitors |
| **M: mitosis** | "Divided into four subphases: **prophase, metaphase, anaphase, and telophase;** cell divides into two daughter cells." | mitotic inhibitors |

"A complete cell cycle from one mitosis to the next is called the **generation time**"; "chemotherapy is most effective when used in a **rapidly dividing or highly proliferative tumor**" (p. 705).

> **Trap (level/stage swap).** **G<sub>0</sub> is the escape hatch, not just "the resting one."** G<sub>0</sub> cells are "**considered out of the cell cycle**" (p. 704) and are **immune to cell cycle–specific drugs**. That single fact is the rationale for combination regimens and for Chapter 46's existence. If a stem asks why chemotherapy fails to eradicate every cell, G<sub>0</sub> is the answer, not drug resistance.

> **Trap (level/stage swap).** **Prophase, metaphase, anaphase, telophase sit inside M; they are not cell cycle phases.** The five are **G<sub>0</sub>, G<sub>1</sub>, S, G<sub>2</sub>, M.** An option listing "prophase" alongside G<sub>1</sub> and S is the swap.

## §2 The five cell cycle–specific classes

Key points, p. 724, verbatim: "**Cell cycle–specific drug classes include antimetabolites, mitotic inhibitors, alkaloid topoisomerase II inhibitors, topoisomerase I inhibitors, and antineoplastic enzymes.**" One distinguishing fact each:

| Class | Phase | Distinguishing fact | Drugs |
|---|---|---|---|
| **Antimetabolites** | **S** | "Work **primarily in the S phase**... during which DNA synthesis is most active" (p. 709). Cell cycle–specific analogues antagonizing folic acid, purines, pyrimidines. | methotrexate, pemetrexed, pralatrexate (folate); cladribine, fludarabine, mercaptopurine, pentostatin, thioguanine (purine); capecitabine, cytarabine, floxuridine, fluorouracil, gemcitabine (pyrimidine) |
| **Mitotic inhibitors** | late S, G<sub>2</sub>, **M** | Plant-derived; "they all work **shortly before or during mitosis**" (p. 712). Vincas bind **tubulin** and "**prevent the assembly**" of microtubules, dissolving mitotic spindles; taxanes cause "the formation of **nonfunctional microtubules,** which **halts mitosis during metaphase.**" | vincristine, vinblastine, vinorelbine (vinca); paclitaxel, docetaxel, cabazitaxel (taxane) |
| **Alkaloid topoisomerase II inhibitors** | late S, **G<sub>2</sub>** | "Derivatives of **epipodophyllotoxin**... inhibiting the enzyme **topoisomerase II, which causes breaks in DNA strands**" (p. 712). | etoposide (VP-16), teniposide |
| **Topoisomerase I inhibitors** | **S** | "The **camptothecins** inhibit proper DNA function in the S phase by binding to the DNA–topoisomerase I complex," retarding **religation** and producing "a DNA strand break" (p. 714). | topotecan, irinotecan |
| **Antineoplastic enzymes** | G<sub>1</sub> (Fig. 45.3 only) | "Approved **exclusively for the treatment of acute lymphocytic leukemia**" (p. 715). Converts **asparagine** to aspartic acid and ammonia, so "**leukemic cells are then unable to synthesize the asparagine required.**" Unique toxicity: "**impaired pancreatic function**... hyperglycemia and severe or fatal **pancreatitis.**" | asparaginase, pegaspargase |

> **Trap (SATA, five options mapped to categories).** **Which classes are cell cycle–specific?** The key-points sentence names exactly five, and a five-option SATA maps onto it. **In:** antimetabolites, mitotic inhibitors, alkaloid topoisomerase II inhibitors, topoisomerase I inhibitors, antineoplastic enzymes. **Out** (Chapter 46's nonspecific territory): alkylating drugs, cytotoxic antibiotics, hormonal and antihormonal drugs, miscellaneous. All-or-nothing scoring, so learn the five as a set.

> **Trap (confusable pair).** **Topoisomerase I vs. II inhibitors.** Cue: ***I = camptothecins (topotecan, irinotecan), S phase, ovarian and colorectal; II = epipodophyllotoxins (etoposide, teniposide), late S and G<sub>2</sub>, testicular and small cell lung.*** The book files the topoisomerase II inhibitors under the mitotic-inhibitor heading (p. 712) but lists them separately in the key points, so an option calling etoposide a mitotic inhibitor is not thereby wrong.

> **Trap (confusable pair).** **Vinca alkaloids vs. taxanes.** Both plant-derived M-phase drugs acting on microtubules, which is why they swap. Cue: ***vincas PREVENT microtubule assembly ("vin-," periwinkle); taxanes build NONFUNCTIONAL microtubules ("-taxel," yew).*** Same endpoint, opposite mechanism.

> **Trap (setting vs. focus).** **Vincristine: the axis is ROUTE, not drug.** Intrathecal is a *legitimate* route in this same chapter for **cytarabine** ("may be given intravenously, subcutaneously, or intrathecally," p. 712) and for methotrexate. For vincristine it is lethal: "**several deaths have been reported**" from inadvertent intrathecal administration, and every product carries "**For Intravenous Use Only—Fatal If Given By Other Routes**" (p. 713). Safeguards: dilute in **25 to 50 mL**, **never dispense in a syringe**, and note that intrathecal drugs "are not normally dispensed in a minibag." Do not let a stem imply intrathecal is safe because another drug on the unit uses it.

> **Trap (plausible-but-adjacent).** **Vincristine is the neurotoxin, not the myelosuppressant.** It is "the **most significant neurotoxin** of the cytotoxic drug class, but it continues to be used in part because of its **relative lack of bone marrow suppression**" (p. 713). Reflexive "monitor the CBC" is the wrong assessment here; assess for **peripheral neuropathy** (p. 718).

> **Trap (plausible-but-adjacent).** **Cytarabine syndrome is not an allergy.** The chapter's own critical thinking exercise 2. It is "**fever, muscle and bone pain, maculopapular rash, conjunctivitis, and malaise,** usually **6 to 12 hours** after administration," treated or prevented with **corticosteroids** (p. 712). Rash plus fever reads as hypersensitivity; the 6-to-12-hour window and the bone pain say otherwise.

> **Trap (level/stage swap).** **Irinotecan has two diarrheas with two antidotes, separated by timing.** ***Early cholinergic diarrhea → atropine. Delayed diarrhea, 2 to 10 days after infusion → loperamide,*** aggressively, since it "can be severe and even life threatening" (p. 714). Also: **laxatives and diuretics are not given with irinotecan.**

> **Trap (plausible-but-adjacent).** **Filgrastim plus topotecan is a timing question.** Given together, "**myelosuppression has actually been shown to be worsened**"; give filgrastim **24 hours after** the topotecan infusion completes (p. 714). The distractor gives it concurrently or prophylactically.

> **Trap (confusable pair).** **Leucovorin vs. allopurinol/rasburicase: two rescues, two problems.** ***Leucovorin rescues from METHOTREXATE; allopurinol and rasburicase rescue from TUMOR LYSIS.*** High-dose methotrexate "is **always** given in conjunction with the 'rescue' drug leucovorin. **Leucovorin is an antidote for folic acid antagonists**" (p. 710). Leucovorin also appears *with* irinotecan and fluorouracil as part of a regimen (p. 714), which is the adjacent-fact decoy.

## §3 The four toxicities that carry the weight

### 3.1 Myelosuppression and nadir

"**Myelosuppression,** also known as **bone marrow suppression**... commonly results from drug- or radiation-induced destruction of rapidly dividing cells in the bone marrow, primarily the **cellular precursors of WBCs, RBCs, and platelets**... Myelosuppression, in turn, leads to **leukopenia, anemia, and thrombocytopenia**" (p. 707).

| Line down | Name | Assess for |
|---|---|---|
| WBC | **leukopenia** / neutropenia | Infection. "**Because fever is a principal early sign of infection,** take oral or axillary temperature at least every 4 hours" (p. 720); chills, tachycardia, purulent cough, lethargy, acute confusion |
| RBC | **anemia** | "Often leads to **hypoxia and fatigue**" (p. 707); pallor of skin, mucous membranes, conjunctiva; shortness of breath |
| Platelets | **thrombocytopenia** | "**Makes the patient more susceptible to bleeding**" (p. 707): petechiae, purpura, ecchymosis, gingival bleeding, prolonged bleeding from puncture sites |

**Nadir** (p. 707): "The lowest level of WBCs in the blood after chemotherapy (or radiation) treatment is called the nadir. **The nadir normally occurs approximately 10 to 28 days after dosing**... **Anticipation of this nadir can be used to guide the timing of prophylactic administration of antibiotics and blood stimulants known as hematopoietic growth factors** (see Chapter 47)."

Numbers: normal **ANC 1500 to 8000 cells/mm<sup>3</sup>**; **severe neutropenia is less than 500** (p. 716); an ANC of **500 or lower** indicates high infection risk (Box 45.2); chemotherapy is **held** below 500 (p. 708). Platelets **under 100,000/mm<sup>3</sup>** mean "high risk for uncontrolled bleeding and/or hemorrhage," though "some guidelines may use **50,000**" (p. 717).

> **Trap (confusable pair).** **Myelosuppression vs. nadir.** Cause and timepoint, not synonyms, and the key-terms box printed them side by side for that reason. Cue: ***myelosuppression is the CONDITION; nadir is the MOMENT (roughly days 10 to 28).*** "When is the patient at greatest risk for infection" wants **nadir**; "why are all three cell lines low" wants **myelosuppression**.

> **Trap (SATA, five options mapped to categories).** **Match the sign to the cytopenia.** Review questions 3, 6, and 8 all run this drill with five options sorted into three buckets. ***Thrombocytopenia → bleeding, bruising, petechiae. Anemia → hypoxia, fatigue, pallor. Neutropenia → fever, chills, sore throat.*** Two reliable decoys: **"elevated WBC count"** is not a sign of infection in a neutropenic patient (that count is the thing that is down), and **fatigue** belongs to anemia even though it feels universal.

> **Trap (priority question).** **Severe bone marrow suppression: risk for infection outranks fatigue, body image, and mobility.** Review question 4 offers all four. Default: **the physiologic threat to life wins,** and here that is infection, because the patient has lost the ability to mount a defense. The other three are all documented in this chapter, which is what makes them good distractors. Override only if the scenario supplies active bleeding or an airway/circulation problem.

> **Trap (plausible-but-adjacent).** **Neutropenic fever checks are oral or axillary, every 4 hours** (p. 720). The rectal route is avoided in these patients, so an option offering the "most accurate" route answers a different question than the chapter asks.

### 3.2 Emetic potential

"The likelihood that a given drug will produce vomiting is known as its **emetic potential.** Anticancer drugs cause nausea and vomiting by **stimulating the cells of the chemoreceptor trigger zone**" (Chapter 52 covers the antiemetics). "**Antiemetics are given before chemotherapy**" (p. 706). Box 45.1's three tiers, restricted to this chapter's drugs (p. 707):

| Tier (as printed) | Cell cycle–specific drugs from this chapter |
|---|---|
| **Low (Less Than 10%–30%)** | asparaginase, cladribine, **cytarabine (<1000 mg/m<sup>2</sup>)**, docetaxel, etoposide, floxuridine, fludarabine, **fluorouracil (<1000 mg/m<sup>2</sup>)**, gemcitabine, hydroxyurea, mercaptopurine, **methotrexate (<250 mg/m<sup>2</sup>)**, paclitaxel, pegaspargase, pentostatin, teniposide, thioguanine, topotecan, vinblastine, vincristine, vinorelbine |
| **Moderate (30%–60%)** | **irinotecan**, **methotrexate (250–1000 mg/m<sup>2</sup>)** |
| **High (60% to More Than 90%)** | **cytarabine (>1000 mg/m<sup>2</sup>)**, **methotrexate (>1000 mg/m<sup>2</sup>)** |

> **Trap (level/stage swap).** **Emetic potential is dose-tiered, not drug-tiered, for three of these.** Cytarabine, fluorouracil, and methotrexate each appear in more than one tier with a dose threshold attached. Cue: ***methotrexate crosses all three bands at 250 and 1000 mg/m<sup>2</sup>; cytarabine crosses low to high at 1000.*** An option assigning flat emetic risk to "methotrexate" with no dose is the swap. Note also that the cell cycle–specific drugs cluster in **low**; the high tier is dominated by Chapter 46's alkylating and platinum drugs, and the box says so: "Drugs in this list not covered in this chapter are described in Chapter 46."

> **Trap (priority question).** **Antiemetics are prophylactic, not PRN.** Review question 2's options are distraction with light activity, antiemetics **30 to 60 minutes before** chemotherapy, antiemetics **only on request**, and **holding fluids**. Default: **prevent, do not rescue.** "Only upon request" is the trap that sounds patient-centered; holding fluids is the trap that sounds preventive and instead risks dehydration.

### 3.3 Extravasation and vesicants

"**Extravasation** is the unintended leakage of a chemotherapy drug (**with vesicant potential**) into the surrounding tissues **outside of the IV line.** Extravasation is a **serious complication** with chemotherapy. Specific treatment of extravasations varies **depending on the drug**" (p. 707). The irritant/vesicant line (p. 718): these drugs "may be **irritants** (irritating the intravenous site and vein) or **vesicants** (causing **cell death with extravasation and necrosis with ulcerations**)."

Management of vesicant extravasation (p. 721): "the drug is usually **discontinued immediately; leave the intravenous cannula in place** (for possible use of **antidotes through cannula**)... Use of antidotes and other drugs, as well as use of **hot or cold packs,** is usually outlined in the protocol (see **Box 46.1**)." And: "**Follow all hospital or infusion protocols without exception** because treatment of extravasation is handled **differently depending on the specific drug.**"

Table 45.8, the only antidote table in this chapter (p. 712), covers **etoposide, teniposide, vinblastine, vincristine**: antidote **hyaluronidase (Wydase) 150 units/mL**, 1 to 6 mL by **multiple subcutaneous injections** into the site, repeated over the next few hours, plus **warm compresses.** Its footnote: "**Important: Administration of corticosteroids and topical cooling appear to worsen toxicity.**"

> **Trap (priority question).** **Extravasation: STOP the infusion first, and leave the cannula in.** Review question 5's options are cold compresses with elevation, subcutaneous epinephrine, stopping the infusion while leaving the catheter in place, and injecting the antidote through the catheter. Default: **stop the exposure before treating it.** The antidote option is the strong distractor because it is eventually correct, just not first, and it depends on the cannula still being there.

> **Trap (confusable pair).** **Irritant vs. vesicant.** Cue: ***irritant inflames the vein and site; vesicant KILLS tissue (necrosis, ulceration).*** The consequence is emergency status: only vesicant extravasation triggers the stop-and-antidote protocol. Topotecan sits at the mild end, where extravasation brings "a **mild local reaction such as erythema or bruising**" (p. 722).

> **Trap (plausible-but-adjacent).** **"Apply cold compresses" is right elsewhere and wrong here.** For the four drugs in Table 45.8, **warm** compresses are specified and **topical cooling appears to worsen toxicity** (p. 712). Cold packs are legitimate in *some* protocols (p. 721 lists "hot or cold packs"), which is why the option reads as safe. Anchor: compress temperature is **drug-specific**.

### 3.4 Tumor lysis syndrome

Key term (p. 701) plus the adverse-effects discussion (p. 710): "Metabolic toxicity also includes **tumor lysis syndrome, a common postchemotherapy condition.** This syndrome is often associated with **induction (initial) chemotherapy for rapidly growing hematologic malignancies.** It may include **hyperphosphatemia, hyperkalemia, and hypocalcemia.** These electrolyte abnormalities are often treated with **diuretics such as mannitol, intravenous calcium supplementation, oral or rectal potassium exchange resin, and oral aluminum hydroxide. Hyperuricemia can lead to nephropathy, and hemodialysis may be required** in severe cases." Where the uric acid comes from (Box 45.2, p. 716): "Killing of cells leads to **release of waste products, such as uric acid, into the blood, resulting in hyperuricemia.**"

Both prophylactic drugs are "indicated for the **hyperuricemia associated with tumor lysis syndrome** and usually given **in anticipation of this condition**" (p. 709). **allopurinol:** "Although allopurinol is **chemically similar to purines, it does not disrupt DNA synthesis.** Instead, it **inhibits xanthine oxidase,** which reduces serum and/or urinary levels of uric acid"; it is "also used commonly in oral form to **treat gout.**" **rasburicase:** "an **enzyme that degrades uric acid to more soluble end products.**"

> **Trap (confusable pair).** **Tumor lysis electrolytes: three up, one down.** ***UP: potassium, phosphate, uric acid. DOWN: calcium.*** The distractor flips calcium, because "everything is elevated" is the intuitive reading of cells bursting. The key term carries the discriminator: "...including uric acid, phosphate, and potassium, and by **reduced serum calcium levels**" (p. 701).

> **Trap (confusable pair).** **Allopurinol vs. rasburicase.** Same problem, opposite ends. Cue: ***allopurinol PREVENTS uric acid from being made (xanthine oxidase inhibitor); rasburicase DESTROYS uric acid already there.*** The chapter also pre-empts one specific distractor: allopurinol looks like a purine but "**does not disrupt DNA synthesis,**" so it is not an antimetabolite and has no direct antineoplastic action.

> **Trap (level/stage swap).** **Tumor lysis syndrome is an INDUCTION-phase, rapidly-growing-hematologic-malignancy event.** The stage cues are printed: "**induction (initial)** chemotherapy" for "**rapidly growing hematologic malignancies**" (p. 710). A stem describing maintenance therapy for a slow-growing solid tumor is the wrong stage however plausible the electrolytes look. And prophylaxis is given **in anticipation**, not in response.

## §4 Tumor vocabulary, compressed

Key points, p. 724: "**Tumors are generally classified by tissue of origin,** as follows: **epithelial (carcinoma), connective (sarcoma), lymphatic (lymphoma), and leukocytes (leukemia).**" Table 45.1, benign vs. malignant (p. 702):

| Characteristic | Benign | Malignant |
|---|---|---|
| Potential to metastasize | No | Yes |
| Encapsulated | Yes | No |
| Similar to tissue of origin | Yes | No |
| Rate of growth | Slow | Unpredictable and unrestrained |
| Recurrence after surgical removal | Rare | Common |

> **Trap (SATA, five options mapped to categories).** **Tissue of origin determines the name.** Five options map onto five buckets: ***epithelial → carcinoma; connective (bone, cartilage, fat, muscle, synovium, vessels, nerve) → sarcoma; lymph tissue → lymphoma; white blood cells → leukemia; bone marrow → multiple myeloma*** (Table 45.2, p. 702). Why it matters: tissue of origin "determines **the type of treatment, the likely response to therapy, and the prognosis**" (p. 703).

> **Trap (confusable pair).** **Benign is not the same as harmless.** The chapter plants its own correction: "**However, a benign tumor can be lethal if it grows large enough to mechanically interrupt the normal function of a critical tissue or organ**" (p. 702). The reliable discriminator stays **metastatic potential**, not size or symptoms.

> **Trap (confusable pair).** **Leukemia and lymphoma vs. solid tumor.** Cue: ***they do not form solid masses; they are "interspersed throughout the lymphatic or circulatory system"*** (p. 703). The book calls them **circulating** tumors or, "more precisely," **hematologic tumors / hematologic malignancies** (p. 701), so an unfamiliar term is not thereby wrong.

> **Trap (plausible-but-adjacent).** **Paraneoplastic syndrome is defined by what it is NOT:** symptom complexes that "**cannot be explained by local or distant spread of their tumors**" (p. 701). A distractor describing symptoms from a metastasis pressing on a structure is describing metastatic disease.
