> **Trap (confusable pair).** **Pain threshold vs. pain tolerance.** The single most swappable pair in the box, because both are "how much pain." Cue: ***thresh**old is **physio**logic, the **stimulus level** that first registers as pain, and it is "**similar for most individuals**"; **tolerance** is **psycho**logic, how much you can **endure without it interfering with function,** and it "**can vary from patient to patient**" (p. 138).* If a stem varies by culture, attitude, or environment, it is describing **tolerance**. Note the second-order trap: tolerance here means *pain* tolerance, which is **not** the same word as the *drug* tolerance defined two entries later.

> **Trap (confusable pair).** **Physical dependence vs. psychologic dependence (addiction) vs. tolerance.** Three entries, and the box quietly equates two pairs. Cue: ***tolerance = need more for the same effect*** (a receptor change); ***physical dependence = withdrawal if you stop*** (a body adaptation, and the box calls it "same as" tolerance); ***psychologic dependence = craving and use for effects other than pain relief***, which is the one the box equates with **addiction**. The discriminator in a stem is **why** the patient wants the drug: relief (not addiction) versus effects other than relief (addiction).

> **Trap (confusable pair).** **Opioid naive vs. opioid tolerant.** Cue: *naive = **first time,** not accustomed; tolerant = **1 week or longer** of opioid use, "legally or otherwise."* The "or otherwise" clause matters, because it means a street-opioid user is **opioid tolerant** and needs higher doses, not lower. This pair carries the fentanyl-patch contraindication (§11) and is therefore a safety item, not just vocabulary.

> **Trap (confusable pair).** **Agonist vs. antagonist vs. agonist-antagonist vs. partial agonist.** Four entries, and the box states that **agonist-antagonist and partial agonist are the same thing.** Cue: *agonist **causes** a response; antagonist **blocks** a response; agonist-antagonist / partial agonist causes a **weaker** response than a full agonist.* An option treating "partial agonist" and "agonist-antagonist" as different drug classes is wrong on the chapter's own equation.

> **Trap (plausible-but-adjacent).** **"Narcotic" is a legal term, not a pharmacologic one.** The chapter says the appropriate term in pharmacology is **opioid**, that "law enforcement professionals use the term narcotics" (p. 142), and that narcotic "is falling out of use." A stem using "narcotic" is not wrong, but an option asserting the two words are pharmacologically distinct drug classes is.

## §1 Classifying pain: onset, source, and cause

The chapter classifies pain three separate ways, and items are built by mixing the axes.

### By onset and duration

**Table 10.2 Acute Versus Chronic Pain** (p. 139), reproduced:

| | **Acute** | **Chronic** |
|---|---|---|
| **Onset** | "Sudden (**minutes to hours**); usually **sharp, localized**; physiologic response (**SNS: tachycardia, sweating, pallor, increased blood pressure**)" | "Slow (**days to months**); long duration; **dull, persistent aching**" |
| **Duration** | "**Limited (has an end)**" | "**Persistent or recurring (endless)**" |
| **Examples** | "**Myocardial infarction, appendicitis, dental procedures, kidney stones, surgical procedures**" | "**Arthritis, cancer, lower back pain, peripheral neuropathy**" |

The body text adds the numbers the table omits: acute pain "typically occurs over **less than a 6-week period**" (key term, p. 135); chronic pain is "persistent or recurring, lasting **3 to 6 months**" (p. 138), and the key term adds two alternative qualifiers, "**longer than 1 month after healing of an acute injury**" or "pain that accompanies a **nonhealing tissue injury**."

### By source

| Source | Definition as given (p. 139) | Responds best to |
|---|---|---|
| **Somatic** | "originates from **skeletal muscles, ligaments, and joints**" | "**nonopioid analgesics such as NSAIDs**" (including **bone pain**) |
| **Visceral** | "originates from **organs and smooth muscles**" | "**usually require opioids** for relief" |
| **Superficial** | "originates from the **skin and mucous membranes**" | "**usually require opioids** for relief" |
| **Deep** | "occurs in **tissues below skin level**" | (opposite of superficial) |

### By cause

- **Vascular:** "thought to originate from the vascular or perivascular tissues and is thought to account for a **large percentage of migraine headaches**" (p. 139).
- **Referred:** "occurs when **visceral nerve fibers synapse at a level in the spinal cord close to fibers that supply specific subcutaneous tissues.** An example is the pain associated with **cholecystitis, which is often referred to the back and scapular areas**" (p. 139).
- **Neuropathic:** "usually results from **damage to peripheral or CNS nerve fibers by disease or injury** but also may be **idiopathic** (unexplained)" (pp. 139–140).
- **Phantom:** "occurs in the area of a body part that has been removed, surgically or traumatically, and is often described as **burning, itching, tingling, or stabbing.** It can also occur in **paralyzed limbs after spinal cord injury**" (p. 140).
- **Cancer:** "can be **acute or chronic or both.** It most often results from **pressure of the tumor mass against nerves, organs, or tissues.** Other causes include **hypoxia from blockage of blood supply to an organ, metastases, pathologic fractures, muscle spasms, and adverse effects of radiation, surgery, and chemotherapy**" (p. 140).
- **Central:** "occurs with **tumors, trauma, inflammation, or disease (e.g., cancer, diabetes, stroke, multiple sclerosis) affecting CNS tissues**" (p. 140).

> **Trap (confusable pair).** **Somatic vs. visceral, and which drug class each needs.** This is the pair with a *therapeutic* consequence, which is what makes it a good item. Cue: ***soma** = body wall, **s**keletal muscle, ligaments, joints, and **bone** → **NSAIDs**; **viscera** = organs and smooth muscle → **opioids**.* An option treating bone metastasis pain with an opioid alone is not wrong clinically (the chapter does exactly that in its case study) but as a *pharmacology* item the chapter pins somatic and bone pain to nonopioids.

> **Trap (confusable pair).** **Superficial vs. deep** are defined only against each other ("opposite of"), so the only discriminator is **skin and mucous membranes** versus **tissues below skin level.** Note the asymmetry a distractor exploits: **superficial** pain groups with **visceral** for treatment (both "usually require opioids"), even though anatomically superficial and deep are the opposed pair.

> **Trap (confusable pair).** **Referred vs. phantom vs. central.** All three describe pain felt somewhere other than where you would expect. Cue: *referred = organ pain felt **elsewhere in an intact body** (gallbladder to scapula); phantom = pain in a part that is **gone**; central = pain from **CNS damage itself**.* A paralyzed limb after spinal cord injury is the chapter's own edge case and it files it under **phantom**, not central.

> **Trap (level/stage swap).** **The chronic-pain time boundary is stated three different ways in this chapter**: "lasting 3 to 6 months" (p. 138), "longer than 3 to 6 months" (key term), and acute as "less than a 6-week period" (key term). There is a gap between 6 weeks and 3 months that the chapter never assigns. If a stem gives a 2-month duration, no definition in this chapter cleanly covers it.

## §2 How pain is transmitted: nociception and the gate theory

**The four processes of nociception** (Fig. 10.1, p. 138), numbered as the figure numbers them:

1. **Transduction** — "Noxious stimuli propagate pain message"; injured tissue releases chemicals that act on peripheral nerve fiber receptors.
2. **Transmission** — "The pain impulse moves from the spinal cord to the brain."
3. **Perception of pain** — at the brain.
4. **Modulation** — "Neurons from brain stem release neurotransmitters that **block** the pain impulse."

**The chemicals released by injured cells** (p. 139): "**bradykinin, histamine, potassium, prostaglandins, and serotonin.**" The chapter attaches the drug classes directly to two of them: "**NSAIDs → prostaglandins; antidepressants → serotonin.**"

**Gate theory** (p. 139), "proposed by **Melzack and Wall in 1965**":

- Pain impulses "activate pain receptors in the **dorsal horn of the spinal cord. It is here that the so-called gates are located.**"
- "If impulses are **stopped by a gate** at this junction, **no impulses are transmitted** to the higher centers of the brain."
- "The cells that control the gate have a **threshold.** Impulses that reach these cells must **rise above this threshold** before an impulse is permitted to travel up to the brain."
- The brain modulates the gate: "the brain can control the gate, either **keeping the gate closed** or **allowing it to open** so that the brain is stimulated and pain is sensed."

**Table 10.3 A and C Nerve Fibers** (p. 140), reproduced:

| Fiber | Myelin sheath | Fiber size | Conduction speed | Type of pain |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| **A** | **Yes** | **Large** | **Fast** | "**Sharp and well localized**" |
| **C** | **No** | **Small** | **Slow** | "**Dull and nonlocalized**" |

**Which fiber does what to the gate** (p. 139): "**Closing** of the gate is affected by the activation of **A fibers.** This causes the inhibition of impulse transmission to the brain and **avoidance of pain sensation. Opening** of the gate is affected by the stimulation of **C fibers.** This allows impulses to be transmitted to the brain and **pain to be sensed.**"

- **The clinical payoff:** "massaging a painful area often reduces the pain. When an area is rubbed or liniment is applied, **large sensory A nerve fibers** from peripheral receptors carry pain-modulating impulses to the spinal cord... **the A fibers tend to close the gate**" (p. 140).

**Endogenous painkillers** (p. 140): "**enkephalins and endorphins**... produced within the body to fight pain and are considered the **body's painkillers.** Both are capable of **bonding with opioid receptors and inhibiting the transmission of pain impulses by closing the spinal cord gates.**" The term **endorphin** "is a condensed version of the term **endogenous morphine**," and these are "released whenever the body experiences pain or prolonged exertion. They are responsible for the phenomenon of '**runner's high.**'"

**Table 10.1 Conditions That Alter Pain Tolerance** (p. 138), reproduced:

| Pain threshold | Conditions |
|---|---|
| **Lowered** | "**Anger, anxiety, depression, discomfort, fear, isolation, chronic pain, sleeplessness, tiredness**" |
| **Raised** | "**Diversion, empathy, rest, sympathy, medications (analgesics, antianxiety drugs, antidepressants)**" |

**The mu opioid receptor gene** (p. 138): "Pain perception is closely linked to the **number of mu receptors.** This number is controlled by a **single gene, the mu opioid receptor gene. When the number of receptors is high, pain sensitivity is diminished.** Conversely, when the receptors are reduced or missing altogether, **relatively minor noxious stimuli may be perceived as painful.**"

> **Trap (confusable pair).** **A fibers vs. C fibers, and which one opens the gate.** Cue: ***A** = **A**ll the good things (myelinated, l**A**rge, f**A**st, sharp and localized) and it **closes** the gate; **C** = the opposite on every row, and it **opens** the gate so pain is felt.* The direction is the part distractors flip, and the massage example is the chapter's own check on it: rubbing works because it recruits **A** fibers.

> **Trap (confusable pair).** **Gate closed vs. gate open.** Counterintuitive by one step: **closed gate = no pain.** A stem describing effective analgesia is describing a **closed** gate. Endorphins and enkephalins **close** the gates; C-fiber stimulation opens them.

> **Trap (level/stage swap).** **The four nociception processes are easy to mis-order.** Cue: ***trans**duction happens at the **injury** (stimulus becomes an impulse); **trans**mission carries it **cord to brain**; **perception** is the brain feeling it; **modulation** is the brain **damping it back down.*** Only modulation runs downward. An option describing "the pain impulse moving from spinal cord to brain" as *modulation* has swapped the two directional steps.

> **Trap (plausible-but-adjacent).** **Table 10.1 is titled "Conditions That Alter Pain Tolerance" but its column header reads "Pain Threshold."** The chapter's own table conflates the two terms it spent two key-term entries separating. Flagged in the GAPS list; do not read the table as evidence that the two words are interchangeable.

## §3 Special pain situations

**PCA (patient-controlled analgesia)** (p. 140): "commonly used in the hospital setting... patients are able to **self-medicate by pressing a button** on a PCA infusion pump. This has been shown to be **very effective and reduces the total opioid dose used. Morphine and hydromorphone** are commonly given by PCA."

- **PCA by proxy:** "Potential hazards of PCA include **well-meaning family members pressing the dosing button rather than letting able patients do so on their own. Numerous deaths have occurred** when well-meaning family members have administered too much of the opioid drug. This is called **PCA by proxy. The Institute for Safe Medication Practices (ISMP) advises against PCA by proxy.**"
- **"For patients who are unable to self-medicate using the PCA pump, a different method of pain control must be used."**

**Cancer pain** (p. 140): "The main consideration in pain management for pain associated with cancer is **patient comfort and not trying to prevent drug addiction.**"

- Opioid tolerance "can occur in as little as **1 week.**"
- "Cancer patients usually require **increasingly higher opioid doses** and thus **do become physically dependent**... however, **actual psychologic dependence or addiction in such patients is unusual.**"
- Preferred routes for long-term control: "**oral, intravenous, subcutaneous, transdermal, and rectal** dosing routes are favored over **multiple intramuscular injections.**"

**Acute-on-chronic pain in opioid-tolerant patients** (pp. 140–141): "habitual street opioid users or patients with chronic pain are **opioid tolerant and generally require high dosages.** Effective management of acute-on-chronic pain requires patients to receive **equivalent amounts of their chronic pain medication in addition to an extra 20% to 40% more opioids** to treat the acute pain."

- **Why long-acting is preferred here:** "**Longer-acting opioids such as extended-release oxycodone are usually better choices** than shorter-acting immediate-release drug products for these patients. This is because the **shorter-acting drugs are more likely to produce a psychologic 'high' or euphoria, which only reinforces addictive tendencies.**"
- **The ethical statement, printed as a rule:** "The label of 'addict' can be used **unfairly** to justify refusal to prescribe pain medications, resulting in **undertreatment of pain**... This is now regarded as an **inappropriate and inhumane clinical practice.** In these situations, **control of the patient's pain takes ethical and clinical priority over concerns regarding drug addiction.**"
- **Genetics as an alternative explanation:** "Genetic differences in **cytochrome P-450 enzymes** can cause different patients, whether addicted or not, to respond more or less effectively to a given drug. For this reason, **patients must not automatically be viewed with suspicion if they complain that a given drug does not work for them.**"

**Breakthrough pain** (p. 141): "For patients receiving **long-acting** opioids, breakthrough pain often occurs **between doses**... because the analgesic effects wear off as the drug is metabolized and eliminated."

- **The regimen:** "Treatment with '**prn**' (as needed) doses of **immediate-release** dosage forms (e.g., oxycodone IR) given **between scheduled doses of extended-release** dosage forms (e.g., oxycodone ER) is often helpful."
- **The escalation rule:** "If the patient requires **larger doses for breakthrough pain,** the dose of the scheduled extended-release opioid **may need to be shortened** or a **more potent drug started.**"
- **The crushing warning:** "**Chewing or crushing of any extended-release opioid drug can cause oversedation, respiratory depression, and even death as a result of rapid drug absorption.**"

> **Trap (priority question).** **PCA is patient-controlled, and only the patient presses the button.** If a stem has a family member, a nurse, or a spouse pressing it "to help," that is **PCA by proxy**, which ISMP advises against and which the chapter says has caused **numerous deaths.** The correct alternative when a patient cannot self-medicate is not "have someone press it for them" but "**a different method of pain control must be used.**"

> **Trap (confusable pair).** **Physical dependence vs. addiction, in the cancer patient specifically.** The chapter says cancer patients on escalating opioids **do become physically dependent** and **will** show withdrawal if doses are stopped abruptly, but that "**actual psychologic dependence or addiction in such patients is unusual.**" A stem describing withdrawal symptoms in a cancer patient is describing **physical dependence,** not addiction, and is not a reason to reduce the opioid.

> **Trap (confusable pair).** **Immediate-release vs. extended-release, and which is scheduled.** Cue: ***ER is scheduled around the clock; IR is prn for breakthrough.*** The distractor reverses them, which produces a regimen that both undertreats baseline pain and oversedates. Second layer: **ER forms cannot be crushed or chewed** (MS Contin, OxyContin, Kadian all say so in the dosage table), and the consequence the chapter names is **death.**

> **Trap (plausible-but-adjacent).** **The 20% to 40% rule applies to acute-on-chronic pain,** not to opioid dosing generally. An opioid-tolerant patient with a new acute injury gets their **usual chronic dose plus 20% to 40% more**, not a standard opioid-naive starting dose. Starting such a patient at naive doses is the undertreatment error the chapter spends a paragraph condemning.

## §4 The WHO analgesic ladder and adjuvant drugs

**The three-step ladder** (p. 142), "often applied as the pain management standard":

| Step | Content as given |
|---|---|
| **1** | "the use of **nonopioids** (with or without adjuvant medications) once the pain has been identified and assessed" |
| **2** | "If pain persists and/or increases... the use of **opioids with or without nonopioids and with or without adjuvants**" |
| **3** | "If pain persists or increases... the use of **opioids indicated for moderate to severe pain,** administered with or without nonopioids or adjuvant medications" |

- The chapter's own caveat: "**Not all patients will be treated effectively using the ladder method,** and some may need to seek an experienced pain management physician."
- Applied to cancer: "The WHO's three-step analgesic ladder provides a **standard for pain management in cancer patients**" (p. 159).

**Adjuvant drugs** (p. 142): "Drugs from other chemical categories are often added to the opioid regimen as adjuvant drugs. These **assist the primary drugs** in relieving pain." Named classes: "**NSAIDs (Ch. 44), antidepressants (Ch. 16), antiepileptic drugs (Ch. 14), and corticosteroids (Ch. 33).**"

- **Why adjuvants are used:** "This approach allows the use of **smaller dosages of opioids** and **reduces some of the adverse effects** that are seen with higher dosages of opioids, such as **respiratory depression, constipation, and urinary retention.** It permits drugs with **different mechanisms of action to produce synergistic effects.**"
- Supporting drugs: "**Antiemetics (Ch. 52) and laxatives (Ch. 51)** also may be needed to prevent or relieve associated constipation, nausea, and vomiting."

**Adjuvants in neuropathic pain** (p. 142): "Adjuvant drugs are commonly used in the treatment of **neuropathic pain, in which opioids are not completely effective.**"

- Named adjuvants: "the antidepressant **amitriptyline** and the anticonvulsants **gabapentin and pregabalin.**"
- **Allodynia:** "hypersensitivity or **hyperalgesia to mild stimuli such as light touch or a pinprick or the bed sheets on a person's feet. This is also known as allodynia.**"
- Also manifests as "hyperalgesia to uncomfortable stimuli, such as **pressure from an inflated blood pressure cuff.**"
- Neuropathic pain descriptors: "**heat, cold, numbness and tingling, burning, or electrical sensations.**"

**The full adjuvant list from the nursing section** (p. 159), which is longer than the pharmacology section's and is the one to learn:

| Adjuvant | Reason given |
|---|---|
| **NSAIDs** | "analgesic, antiinflammatory effects" |
| **acetaminophen** | "analgesic effects" |
| **corticosteroids** | "mood elevation and antiinflammatory, antiemetic, and appetite stimulation effects" |
| **anticonvulsants** | "treatment of **neuropathic** pain" |
| **tricyclic antidepressants** | "treatment of **neuropathic** pain and for their innate analgesic properties and **opioid-potentiating** effects" |
| **neuroleptics** | "treatment of chronic pain syndromes" |

| **psychostimulants** | "reduction of **opioid-induced sedation** when opioid dosage adjustment is not effective" |

> **Trap (four-options-three-levels).** **The WHO ladder is a level-based concept, so expect one option per step.** Cue for each: *step 1 = **nonopioid** only; step 2 = **opioid** added, any combination; step 3 = opioids **indicated for moderate to severe pain.*** Note what does **not** discriminate: adjuvants are optional at **every** step, so an option that assigns adjuvants to one particular step is wrong regardless of which step it picks.

> **Trap (confusable pair).** **Neuropathic pain is the one opioids do not fully answer.** Cue: *if the stem says **burning, tingling, electrical, light touch hurts, or allodynia,** the answer is an **adjuvant** (gabapentin, pregabalin, amitriptyline), not a higher opioid dose.* This is the clearest "right drug class, wrong pain type" item the chapter sets up.

> **Trap (SATA, five options mapped to categories).** **Adjuvants are named by what they fix, not by being analgesics.** Three of the nine on the p. 159 list treat **neuropathic** pain (anticonvulsants, TCAs, local anesthetics); one treats **opioid-induced sedation** (psychostimulants); one is for **chronic pain syndromes** (neuroleptics). A select-all asking which adjuvants treat neuropathic pain has exactly three keys in this chapter.

## §5 Opioid pharmacology: classes, receptors, and actions

**Origin** (p. 143): "originated from the **opium poppy plant.** The word **opium** is a Greek word that means '**juice**.' More than **20 different alkaloids** are obtained from the unripe seed of the poppy... only **3 are clinically useful: morphine, codeine, and papaverine.** Of these, **only morphine and codeine are pain relievers; papaverine is a smooth muscle relaxant.**"

**Table 10.4 Chemical Classification of Opioids** (p. 143), reproduced:

| Chemical category | Opioid drugs |
|---|---|
| **Meperidine-like drugs** | **meperidine, fentanyl, remifentanil, sufentanil, alfentanil** |
| **Methadone-like drugs** | **methadone** |
| **Morphine-like drugs** | **morphine, heroin, hydromorphone, codeine, hydrocodone, oxycodone** |
| **Other** | **tramadol, tapentadol** |

- **Why the chemical class matters clinically** (p. 143): "Knowing the chemical structure of the different opioids can be important in determining the appropriate drug for patients who experience **significant allergic reactions** to a specific opioid. For example, if a patient experiences **anaphylaxis from morphine,** giving a drug with an **unrelated structure such as fentanyl** would be appropriate."

**Table 10.5 Opioid Receptors and Their Characteristics** (p. 143), reproduced:

| Receptor type | Prototypical agonist | Effects of opioid stimulation |
|---|---|---|
| **mu** | **morphine** | "**Supraspinal analgesia, respiratory depression, euphoria, sedation**" |
| **kappa** | **ketocyclazocine** | "**Spinal analgesia, sedation, miosis**" |
| **delta** | **Enkephalins** | "**Analgesia**" |

- "The **mu receptors in the dorsal horn** of the spinal cord appear to play the **most crucial role.** Less important but still involved are the **kappa and delta** receptors" (p. 138).
- "Pain receptors are located in **both the central nervous system (CNS) and various body tissues**" (p. 138).

**Mild vs. strong agonists** (p. 142):

| | Drugs as given |
|---|---|
| **Mild agonists** | "**codeine, hydrocodone**" |
| **Strong agonists** | "**morphine, hydromorphone, oxycodone, meperidine, fentanyl, and methadone**" |

**The three action types** (p. 143):

- **Agonist:** "binds to an opioid pain receptor in the brain and causes an **analgesic response.**"
- **Agonist-antagonist** (partial agonist, mixed agonist): "binds to a pain receptor and causes a **weaker pain response** than does a full agonist. Different drugs in this class exert their agonist and/or antagonist effects by binding in **different degrees to kappa and mu** opioid receptors." Not normally first-line, "but are sometimes useful in pain management in **obstetric patients** (because they avoid oversedation of the mother and/or fetus)."
- **Antagonist:** "binds to a pain receptor but **does not reduce pain signals.** It functions as a **competitive antagonist** because it competes with and **reverses the effects of agonist and agonist-antagonist drugs** at the receptor sites."

**The analgesic ceiling effect** (p. 142): "The opiate agonists-antagonists such as **pentazocine and nalbuphine** are associated with an **analgesic ceiling effect.** This means that the drug reaches a **maximum analgesic effect, so that analgesia does not improve even with higher dosages.** Such drugs are **useful only in patients who have not been previously exposed to opioids.**"

**Meperidine's problem, stated early and repeated** (p. 142): "Meperidine is **not recommended for long-term use** because of the accumulation of a **neurotoxic metabolite, normeperidine, which can cause seizures.**"

**Propoxyphene** (p. 142): "In **2010,** the mild agonist **propoxyphene (Darvocet) was withdrawn from the market** due to adverse effects."

**The route trend** (p. 142): "because of associated **bruising and bleeding risks,** as well as **injection discomfort,** there is now a **strong trend away from intramuscular injections** in favor of **intravenous, oral, and transdermal** routes."

> **Trap (confusable pair).** **mu vs. kappa.** Both produce analgesia and sedation, so the discriminators are the *other* two effects on each row. Cue: ***mu = supra**spinal analgesia, plus **respiratory depression and euphoria** (the two that make opioids dangerous and abusable); **kappa = spinal** analgesia, plus **miosis**.* An option pinning respiratory depression to kappa has the receptors crossed. Delta is the sparse row: **analgesia only,** with **enkephalins** (an endogenous substance, not a drug) as its prototype.

> **Trap (confusable pair).** **Which drugs abuse most.** Cue: *"Opioids that have an affinity for **mu** receptors and have **rapid onset of action** produce marked **euphoria** and are most likely to be abused" (p. 146).* Two conditions, both required. A slow-onset mu agonist is not the profile the chapter names.

> **Trap (confusable pair).** **The analgesic ceiling belongs to two different places in this chapter.** The **agonists-antagonists** (pentazocine, nalbuphine, butorphanol, buprenorphine) have it as a class property, **and codeine has it alone among the full agonists**: codeine "is the **only agonist to possess a ceiling effect**" (p. 148). An option saying no full agonist has a ceiling is wrong on codeine; an option saying all opioids have one is wrong on morphine.

> **Trap (plausible-but-adjacent).** **Chemical class predicts cross-allergy, not potency.** The reason to know Table 10.4 is that a morphine-anaphylaxis patient can receive **fentanyl** (meperidine-like, unrelated structure). It says nothing about which drug is stronger. Note the trap inside the trap: **codeine, hydrocodone, and oxycodone are all morphine-like,** so they are the wrong choices in a true morphine allergy despite being "weaker."

> **Trap (setting vs. focus).** **Agonists-antagonists are chosen for the setting, not the severity.** The chapter names two: **obstetric procedures** (avoiding oversedation of mother and fetus) and **patients with a history of opioid addiction** (lower misuse risk). They are "**normally not strong enough for management of longer-term chronic pain**" and must **not** be given with full agonists, because they "may both **reduce analgesic effects and cause withdrawal symptoms** in opioid-tolerant patients" (p. 150).

## §6 Equianalgesia: Box 10.2

The chapter attaches a death warning to this block, which is the clearest signal in the chapter that it will be tested.

**Box 10.2 Equianalgesic Doses** (p. 144), reproduced exactly:

| Drug | Oral dose (mg) | Parenteral dose (mg) | Oral-to-parenteral ratio | Dosing interval (hr) |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| **Morphine** | **30** | **10** | **3 : 1** | **12** (continuous release); **4** (immediate release) |
| **hydromorphone** | **7.5** | **1.5** | **5 : 1** | **4** (immediate release) |
| **Oxycodone** | **15** | N/A | N/A | **4** (immediate release) |
| **Hydrocodone** | **30** | N/A | N/A | **6 hr** |

**The basic conversion equation** as printed:

> (24-hour amount of current drug) ÷ *x* = (EA dose of current drug) ÷ (EA dose of desired drug)
>
> "where *x* = the amount of desired opioid in 24 hours and **EA** = the equianalgesic dose obtained from the table above."

**The chapter's worked example** (p. 144), reproduced because the arithmetic is the item:

> "A patient with colon cancer is currently taking **oral oxycodone 80 mg every 12 hours** and needs to be converted to **intravenous morphine** because of a bowel obstruction. What is the equivalent intravenous morphine dose?"
>
> - **Step 1.** 24-hour oxycodone: 80 mg × 2 doses = **160 mg per 24 hours**
> - **Step 2.** From the table: **15 mg oxycodone = 10 mg parenteral morphine**
> - **Step 3.** 160 ÷ *x* = 15 ÷ 10, so *x* = **107 mg** ("approximately **100 mg of injectable morphine per 24 hours**")

**The potency warning, stated three times in three places** (pp. 143, 148, 149):

- "**hydromorphone (Dilaudid) is seven times more potent than morphine.** Deaths have been reported in which a nurse gave the patient morphine and, **not realizing the equianalgesic equivalency,** gave the same patient hydromorphone a short time later" (p. 143).
- Clinical Pearl: "**Hydromorphone (Dilaudid) is seven times more potent than morphine**" (p. 149).
- "**One milligram of IV or IM hydromorphone is equivalent to 7 mg of morphine.** Many nurses are unfamiliar with the potency difference, and because it is given in **low doses (0.2 to 1 mg)** some inadvertently assume **low dose means low potency.** Many medication errors and deaths have occurred because of lack of knowledge of this potency difference" (p. 149).

**Fentanyl's equivalence** (p. 148): "Fentanyl in a dose of **0.1 mg intravenously** is roughly equivalent to **10 mg of morphine intravenously.**"

**Why it matters at the bedside** (p. 161): "Be familiar with equianalgesic doses of opioids, because **lack of knowledge may lead to inadequate analgesia or overdose.**"

> **Trap (confusable pair).** **Oral dose vs. parenteral dose, and the direction of the ratio.** Cue: *the **oral** number is always the **bigger** one, because oral drug is partly lost to first-pass metabolism.* Morphine is **30 PO : 10 parenteral (3:1)**; hydromorphone is **7.5 PO : 1.5 parenteral (5:1)**. A conversion that raises the dose when switching PO to IV has the ratio inverted, and it is the direction that kills.

> **Trap (confusable pair).** **The two ratios are different numbers, 3:1 and 5:1.** Applying morphine's 3:1 to hydromorphone overdoses the patient by two-thirds. There is **no** oral-to-parenteral ratio for oxycodone or hydrocodone in this table, because both are **oral only** ("N/A"); an option converting oxycodone to an IV dose of oxycodone is converting to a dosage form the chapter says does not exist ("**All available oxycodone dosage forms are orally administered**," p. 143).

> **Trap (level/stage swap).** **Low dose does not mean low potency.** The chapter names this exact inference as a killer: hydromorphone is dosed at **0.2 to 1 mg** precisely *because* it is **seven times** morphine's potency. A stem in which a nurse gives morphine and then hydromorphone "because the hydromorphone dose was so small" is the chapter's own reported fatality pattern.

> **Trap (plausible-but-adjacent).** **7.5 : 1.5 is also 5 : 1, but the potency ratio to morphine is 7 : 1.** Two different numbers about the same drug. Cue: *the **5:1** is hydromorphone **oral-to-parenteral**; the **7:1** is hydromorphone **versus morphine.*** Confusing them is the difference between a route conversion and a drug conversion.

## §7 Opioid indications and contraindications

**Main use** (p. 143): "to alleviate **moderate to severe pain.** The amount of pain control or unwanted adverse effects depends on the **specific drug, the receptors to which it binds, and its chemical structure.**"

**By setting:**

- **Surgery and anesthesia:** "Strong opioid analgesics such as **fentanyl, sufentanil, and alfentanil** are commonly used **in combination with anesthetics during surgery**" (p. 143).
- **Postoperative:** fentanyl injection "has become popular because of its **rapid onset and short duration**"; morphine and hydromorphone, "because they are available in **injectable forms,** are often **first-line analgesics in the immediate postoperative setting**" (p. 143).
- **Transdermal fentanyl is not a postoperative drug:** "comes in a patch formulation for use in **long-term** pain management and is **not to be used for postoperative or any other short-term pain control**" (p. 143).
- **Cough suppression** (p. 143): "Opioids also **suppress the medullary cough center.** The most commonly used opioid for this purpose is **codeine** (see Ch. 36). **Hydrocodone** is also used in many cough suppressants."
- **Diarrhea** (p. 143): constipation "is sometimes helpful in treating diarrhea. Some of the opioid-containing antidiarrheal preparations are **camphorated opium tincture (paregoric)** and **diphenoxylate/atropine (Lomotil)** tablets."

**Contraindications** (p. 144): "**known drug allergy and severe asthma.**"

**"Not absolute contraindications," but extreme caution** (p. 144), reproduced as the list it is:

- "**respiratory insufficiency,** especially when resuscitative equipment is not available"
- "conditions involving **elevated intracranial pressure** (e.g., severe head injury)"
- "**morbid obesity and/or sleep apnea**"
- "**myasthenia gravis**"
- "**paralytic ileus** (bowel paralysis)"
- "**pregnancy,** especially with long-term use or high dosages"

**The allergy-is-usually-not-allergy paragraph** (p. 144): "It is **not uncommon for patients to state they are allergic to codeine, when in the overwhelming majority of the nausea was the 'allergic' reaction.** Many patients will claim to be allergic to **morphine because it causes itching. Itching is a pharmacologic effect resulting from histamine release and not an allergic reaction.** Thus it is important to determine the **exact nature** of a patient's stated allergy."

- Restated at p. 146: "**true anaphylaxis is rare, even with intravenously administered opioids.** Some patients may complain of **flushing, itching, or wheal formation** at the injection site, but this is usually **local and histamine mediated and not a true allergy.**"

> **Trap (confusable pair).** **Allergy vs. adverse effect, opioid edition.** Cue: ***nausea from codeine = GI upset, not allergy. Itching from morphine = histamine release, not allergy.*** Both are named explicitly. A stem in which a patient says "I'm allergic to morphine, it makes me itch" is testing whether you document an allergy (wrong) or investigate the reaction (right). The chapter's contrast case is real anaphylaxis, which justifies switching to a **structurally unrelated** opioid.

> **Trap (SATA, five options mapped to categories).** **Review question 7 (p. 163) asks which disorders contraindicate an opioid,** offering renal insufficiency, severe asthma, sleep apnea, severe head injury, and liver disease. Held against p. 144: only **severe asthma** is listed as an actual contraindication; **sleep apnea** and **severe head injury (elevated ICP)** appear on the "extreme caution, not absolute" list; **renal insufficiency** and **liver disease** are not on either list, though renal impairment drives drug *selection* elsewhere in the chapter (§11). The official key is not in the pull; see GAPS item 10.

> **Trap (setting vs. focus).** **Transdermal fentanyl is a chronic-pain dosage form of an acute-pain drug.** Injectable fentanyl is favored postoperatively for its rapid onset and short duration; the **patch of the same drug** is explicitly barred from postoperative use. The route, not the molecule, decides.

## §8 Opioid adverse effects

**Table 10.6 Opioid-Induced Adverse Effects by Body System** (p. 146), reproduced:

| Body system | Adverse effects |
|---|---|
| **Cardiovascular** | **Hypotension, flushing, bradycardia** |
| **Central nervous** | **Sedation, disorientation, euphoria, lightheadedness, dysphoria** |
| **Gastrointestinal** | **Nausea, vomiting, constipation, biliary tract spasm** |
| **Genitourinary** | **Urinary retention** |
| **Integumentary** | **Itching, rash, wheal formation** |
| **Respiratory** | **Respiratory depression and possible aggravation of asthma** |

**Respiratory depression, the one that kills** (p. 146): "**The most serious adverse effect of opioid use is CNS depression, which may lead to respiratory depression. When death occurs from opioid overdose, it is almost always because of respiratory depression.**"

- "**Respiratory depression is strongly related to the degree of sedation**" (p. 146), and the safety box repeats it: "**Respiratory depression is strongly associated with excessive sedation**" (p. 142).
- Higher risk in "**asthma, chronic obstructive pulmonary disease, or sleep apnea**."
- Can be partly prevented "by using drugs with **very short duration of action and no active metabolites.**"
- **Tolerance develops to it:** "Long-term opioid use is generally associated with **tolerance to respiratory depression**" (p. 142).

**Histamine release** (pp. 146–147): "opioids cause **histamine release.** It is thought that this histamine release is responsible for many of the drugs' unwanted adverse effects, such as **itching or pruritus, rash, and hemodynamic changes.** Histamine release causes **peripheral arteries and veins to dilate,** which leads to **flushing and orthostatic hypotension.**"

- **The gradient, which is the testable part:** "The amount of histamine release is **related to its chemical class. The naturally occurring opiates (e.g., morphine) elicit the most histamine release; the synthetic opioids (e.g., meperidine) elicit the least.**"

**Constipation** (p. 142, 146): "Opioids **decrease gastrointestinal (GI) tract peristalsis** because of their CNS depression... **Stool becomes excessively dehydrated because it remains in the GI tract longer.**" Also "opioids **slow peristalsis and increase absorption of water** from intestinal contents. These two actions combine to produce constipation. This is **more pronounced in hospitalized patients who are nonambulatory.**"

- **Prevention** (p. 142): "increased intake of **fluids, stool softeners such as docusate sodium,** or **stimulants such as bisacodyl or senna.** Agents such as **lactulose, sorbitol, and polyethylene glycol (MiraLAX)** have been proven effective. **Bulk-forming laxatives, such as psyllium, may be used but do require an increase in fluid intake to avoid fecal impactions or bowel obstructions.**"
- **Drugs specifically for opioid-induced constipation** (p. 146): "**naloxegol (Movantik), methylnaltrexone (Relistor), naldemedine (Symproic), lubiprostone (Amitiza), and alvimopan (Entereg).** They are usually used in patients taking opioids **chronically.**"

**Nausea and vomiting** (p. 142): opioids "**stimulate the vomiting center in the CNS**" and irritate the GI tract, "stimulating the **chemoreceptor trigger zone**." Managed "with the use of antiemetics such as **phenothiazines.**"

**Sedation and mental clouding** (p. 142): "**Any change in mental status must always be evaluated to ensure that causes other than drug-related CNS depression are ruled out.**" Persistent sedation "may be managed with a **decrease in the dosage** of opioid or **change in drug used,**" and the prescriber "may also order various **CNS stimulants.**"

**Subacute overdose** (p. 142): "may be **more common than acute respiratory depression** and may progress **slowly (over hours to days), with somnolence and respiratory depression.** Before analgesic dosages are changed or reduced, **advancing disease must be considered, especially in the dying patient.**"

- **The diagnostic manoeuvre:** "**holding one or two doses** of an opioid analgesic is enough to judge if the mental and respiratory depression is associated with the opioid. **If there is improvement with this measure, the opioid dosage is often decreased by 25%.**"

**Other opioid adverse effects** (p. 142): "**Dry mouth, urinary retention, pruritus, dysphoria, euphoria, sleep disturbances, or sexual dysfunction** may occur but are **less common** than the previously mentioned adverse effects." Management: "sucking of **sugar-free hard candy** or use of artificial saliva drops or gum for dry mouth; use of **diphenhydramine** for pruritus."

**Urinary retention** (p. 146): "the **inability to void,** another unwanted adverse effect of opioids, caused by **increasing bladder tone.**"

**The FDA black box warnings** (pp. 143, 146, 147), three separate ones in this chapter:

| Year | Warning |
|---|---|
| **2013** | "the FDA approved a **risk evaluation and mitigation strategy (REMS)** for **long-acting** opioids... requires **prescriber and patient education**" |
| (undated) | "the FDA required **black box warnings on all opioids** related to the risk for **misuse and abuse that can lead to addiction and death**"; also "on all **immediate-release and long-acting** opioids" |
| **2016** | "the FDA issued a black box warning for **all opioids and all benzodiazepines regarding the risk of combined use.** The combination should be used **only if no other alternatives are available.** Risks include **extreme sleepiness, respiratory depression, coma, and death**" |

> **Trap (confusable pair).** **Sedation precedes respiratory depression, so sedation is the earlier warning sign.** Stated twice. Cue: *a stem describing a patient who is newly hard to rouse on an opioid is describing **impending respiratory depression,** not a benign side effect.* An option that reassures the family or continues the infusion is answering the wrong question.

> **Trap (confusable pair).** **Which opioids release the most histamine.** Cue: ***natural = most (morphine); synthetic = least (meperidine).*** So itching and flushing point toward morphine, and a patient who cannot tolerate the itching may do better on a synthetic. The distractor reverses the gradient, which sounds equally plausible if you have not read the sentence.

> **Trap (level/stage swap).** **Tolerance develops to respiratory depression but NOT to constipation.** The chapter says long-term use is "generally associated with tolerance to respiratory depression," while constipation is managed with ongoing bowel regimens indefinitely and has five dedicated drugs for chronic opioid users. An option promising that constipation will resolve as the patient adjusts is wrong; the same promise about sedation and respiratory depression is defensible.

> **Trap (SATA, five options mapped to categories).** **Review question 4 (p. 163) asks the common adverse effects of IV morphine,** offering diarrhea, constipation, pruritus, urinary frequency, and nausea. Table 10.6 pins **constipation, pruritus (integumentary: itching), and nausea** to opioids, and lists **urinary retention** rather than frequency and **constipation** rather than diarrhea. The two distractors are both **direction reversals** of a real opioid effect, which is the tidiest way to build a five-option SATA. Official key not in the pull; see GAPS item 10.

> **Trap (priority question).** **Subacute overdose is diagnosed by holding doses, not by giving naloxone.** For gradual somnolence over hours to days, the chapter's move is to **hold one or two doses** and see whether the patient improves, then **decrease the dose by 25%.** Naloxone is for **severe** respiratory depression. Reaching for naloxone in the subacute case reverses analgesia unnecessarily and, in an opioid-tolerant patient, precipitates withdrawal.

## §9 Toxicity, reversal, and withdrawal

**The two antagonists** (p. 146):

| | **naloxone (Narcan)** | **naltrexone** |
|---|---|---|
| Use as given | "used in the management of **opioid overdose**" | "used for **alcohol and opioid addiction**" |
| Route | "available **only in injectable** dosage forms" (p. 150); also "the intravenous form... with a **nasal adapter**" for first responders | "available **only orally**" |
| Both | "bind to and occupy **all of the receptor sites (mu, kappa, delta)**. They are **competitive antagonists with a strong affinity** for these binding sites" | |

- **Naloxone has no agonist activity:** "It has **no agonistic morphine-like properties**... the drug **does not produce analgesia or respiratory depression**" (p. 150).
- **It is a diagnostic test as well as a treatment:** "**Failure of the drug to significantly reverse the effects of the presumed opioid overdose indicates that the condition may not be related to opioid overdose**" (p. 150).
- **Naloxone also treats itching:** "can be used in **small doses to treat itching** associated with opioid use" (p. 146).
- **Over-the-counter since 2016:** "naloxone has become available **without a prescription** and is being used by **first responders**" (p. 150).
- **Adverse effects:** "**opioid withdrawal syndrome,** which can occur with **abrupt over-reversal in opioid-tolerant patients**... raised or lowered blood pressure, dysrhythmias, pulmonary edema, and withdrawal" (p. 150).

**Titrating the reversal** (p. 146): "**Careful titration of dose until the patient begins to breathe independently will prevent over-reversal. The effects of naloxone are short-lived and usually last about 1 hour. With long-acting opioids, respiratory depressant effects may reappear, and naloxone may need to be re-dosed periodically until symptoms resolve.**"

**The escalation ladder for respiratory depression** (p. 146), in the chapter's own order:

1. "**Stimulating the patient** may be adequate to reverse mild hypoventilation."
2. "If this is unsuccessful, **ventilatory assistance using a bag and mask or endotracheal intubation** may be needed."
3. "**Administration of opioid antagonists (e.g., naloxone)** also may be necessary to reverse **severe** respiratory depression."

**Withdrawal** (p. 146): "The extent of opioid tolerance is most visible when an opioid drug is **discontinued abruptly** or when an **opioid antagonist is administered.** This usually leads to symptoms of opioid withdrawal, also known as **abstinence syndrome.** This can occur after as little as **2 weeks of opioid therapy in opioid-naive patients. Gradual dosage reduction** after chronic opioid use helps minimize the risk and severity."

**Withdrawal timing follows half-life** (p. 146):

| Opioid group | Drugs named | Onset | Peak |
|---|---|---|---|
| **Short-acting** | "**codeine, hydrocodone, morphine, and hydromorphone**" | "**within 6 to 12 hours**" | "**24 to 72 hours**" |
| **Long half-life** | "**methadone, levorphanol, and transdermal fentanyl**" | "**may not appear for 24 hours or longer** after drug discontinuation" | "**and may be milder**" |

> **Trap (confusable pair).** **naloxone vs. naltrexone.** One letter and one syllable apart, and both are pure antagonists at all three receptors. Cue: ***nalox**one = the **acute** drug, **injectable**, for **overdose**; **naltrex**one = the **chronic** drug, **oral**, for **addiction** (alcohol and opioid).* The route is the fastest discriminator: if the stem needs it now, it is naloxone.

> **Trap (priority question).** **Naloxone's duration is shorter than the opioid it reverses.** "The effects of naloxone... last about **1 hour.**" With a long-acting opioid, "**respiratory depressant effects may reappear.**" A stem in which a patient improves after naloxone and is then left unmonitored is set up for the re-sedation item; the answer is **re-dose and keep monitoring,** not "the crisis is over."

> **Trap (level/stage swap).** **Titrate naloxone to breathing, not to consciousness.** The endpoint the chapter names is "**until the patient begins to breathe independently.**" Pushing past that is **over-reversal**, which reverses analgesia (the patient wakes in severe pain) and, in an opioid-tolerant patient, precipitates withdrawal. Table 10.8 lists naloxone among drugs **not recommended in cancer pain** for exactly this reason: it "**reverses analgesia as well as CNS depressant effects.**"

> **Trap (confusable pair).** **Withdrawal onset tracks half-life, and the direction is counterintuitive.** Cue: ***short-acting drugs → withdrawal comes SOONER (6 to 12 hr) and PEAKS harder (24 to 72 hr); long half-life drugs → LATER (24 hr or more) and MILDER.*** A distractor pairing methadone with rapid, severe withdrawal has it backwards on both axes.

## §10 Interactions and laboratory effects

**Drug interactions** (p. 146): "Co-administration of opioids with **alcohol, antihistamines, barbiturates, benzodiazepines, phenothiazine, and other CNS depressants** can result in **additive respiratory depressant effects.**"

- **MAOIs:** "The combined use of opioids, such as **meperidine, with monoamine oxidase inhibitors, such as selegiline,** can result in **respiratory depression, seizures, and hypotension.**"
- **Benzodiazepines carry their own 2016 black box** (see §8).

**Laboratory test interactions** (p. 147): opioids "can cause an **abnormal increase in the serum levels of amylase, alanine aminotransferase, alkaline phosphatase, bilirubin, lipase, creatinine kinase, and lactate dehydrogenase.** Other abnormal results include a **decrease in urinary 17-ketosteroid levels** and an **increase in the urinary alkaloid and glucose concentrations.**"

**Safety: Laboratory Values Related to Drug Therapy** (p. 147), the normal ranges as printed:

| Test | Normal range | Why it matters, as given |
|---|---|---|
| **Alkaline phosphatase (ALP)** | **30–120 units/L** | "highest concentrations in the **liver, biliary tract, and bone**"; raised in obstructive biliary disease and cirrhosis |
| **Alanine aminotransferase (ALT)**, formerly SGPT | **4–36 units/L** ("Older adults may have slightly higher levels") | "found mainly in the **liver**... **Most ALT elevations are from liver disease**"; if drugs are metabolized by the liver, "this metabolic process will be altered and possibly lead to **toxic levels of drugs**" |
| **Gamma-glutamyl transferase (GGT)** | **8–38 units/L** (male/female 45 years and older) | "present in liver tissue"; "**Individuals of African ancestry have normal values that are double** the values of those who are white" |
| **Aspartate aminotransferase (AST)**, formerly SGOT | **0–35 units/L** | "elevated with **hepatocellular** diseases"; elevation "is **directly related to the number of cells** affected" |
| **Lactic dehydrogenase (LDH)** | **100–190 units/L** | "found in cells of many body tissues... **the total LDH level is not a specific indicator of one disease**" |

> **Trap (plausible-but-adjacent).** **Opioids raise liver enzymes without necessarily damaging the liver.** The lab-interaction paragraph lists ALT, ALP, bilirubin, amylase, lipase, CK, and LDH as **drug-induced abnormal results.** An option interpreting a rise in these values during opioid therapy as proof of hepatotoxicity is over-reading a known assay interaction. Contrast with **acetaminophen,** where hepatotoxicity is real and is the drug's defining danger (§12).

> **Trap (confusable pair).** **Additive CNS depression vs. the MAOI reaction.** Cue: *alcohol, antihistamines, barbiturates, benzodiazepines, phenothiazines → **additive respiratory depression** (more of the same effect); **meperidine + MAOI** → **respiratory depression, seizures, and hypotension** (a distinct, named triad).* The seizure component is the tell, and it lines up with meperidine's normeperidine problem elsewhere in the chapter.

## §11 Opioid drug profiles

### codeine sulfate (p. 148)

- "a **natural opiate alkaloid (Schedule II)** obtained from opium... similar to morphine."
- **"About 10% of a codeine dose is metabolized to morphine in the body."** Clinical Pearl: "**Codeine is metabolized to morphine in the body.**"
- **"Codeine is less effective as an analgesic and is the only agonist to possess a ceiling effect** (meaning increasing the dose will not increase response)."
- "more commonly used as an **antitussive** drug."
- **The schedule changes with the combination:** "Codeine **combined with acetaminophen** (tablets or elixir) is classified as a **Schedule III** controlled substance... When codeine is **not combined** with other drugs, it is classified as a **Schedule II**."
- "Codeine causes **gastrointestinal tract upset,** and many patients will say they are allergic to codeine, **when in fact it just upsets their stomach.**"
- **"Codeine is contraindicated [in] pediatric patients and laboring or breast feeding mothers."**

### fentanyl (p. 148)

- "a **synthetic opioid (Schedule II)** used to treat **moderate to severe** pain... **high abuse potential.**"
- Dosage forms: "**parenteral injections, transdermal patches (Duragesic), buccal lozenges (Fentora), and buccal lozenges on a stick (Actiq).**"
- **Potency:** "**0.1 mg intravenously is roughly equivalent to 10 mg of morphine intravenously.**"
- Injectable used "in **perioperative** settings and in **intensive care unit** settings for **sedation during mechanical ventilation.**"

**The transdermal patch rules,** gathered from the profile (p. 148) and the safety box (p. 145). This is the densest safety cluster in the chapter:

| Rule | As given |
|---|---|
| **Who may receive it** | "**only by patients who are considered opioid tolerant**"; giving it to non-opioid-tolerant patients "**may result in severe respiratory depression**" |
| **What counts as opioid tolerant** | "taking, **for a week or longer, morphine 60 mg daily, oral oxycodone 30 mg daily, or a minimum of 8 mg of oral hydromorphone daily,** or an equianalgesic dose of another opioid" |
| **Not for** | "**not to be used in opioid-naive patients or for acute pain relief**"; "**not to be used for acute pain situations such as postoperative pain**" |
| **Onset** | "take **6 to 12 hours** to reach steady-state pain control after the first patch is applied, and **supplemental short-acting therapy may be required**" |
| **Duration** | "adequate pain control for **72 hours**"; "A new patch is to be applied **every 72 hours**" |
| **Offset** | "It takes about **17 hours** for the amount of fentanyl to reduce by **50%** once the patch is removed" |
| **Old patch** | "**It is important to remove the old patch when applying a new one**" |
| **Other opioids** | "**All other extended-released opioids must be discontinued or tapered before** the initiation"; "ALL other 'around-the-clock' opioids must be discontinued" |
| **Heat** | "heat, such as in the form of a **heating pad/pack, must never be applied** over a transdermal fentanyl patch. The increased circulation... **may result in increased absorption of medication, causing an overdose**" |
| **Cutting** | "**cutting the patch**" accelerates diffusion and is a named hazard |
| **Monitoring** | "monitor patients closely for respiratory depression, **especially within the initial 24 to 72 hours,** that is the timeframe for **peak effects** of serum concentrations" |
| **Titration philosophy** | "it is '**preferable' to underestimate** the patient's 24-hour fentanyl requirements and plan for the possible use of **rescue medication**" |
| **Disposal** | "the patch be **folded in half and disposed of by flushing down the toilet**"; a used patch "may **still contain a significant amount of medication**" |
| **Children** | "children have **pulled used fentanyl patches from the trash, which has resulted in death**"; also patches "becoming displaced and then becoming adhered to the skin of an infant, toddler, or child" |
| **Storage** | "**Do not store medications in warm, moist places** such as medicine cabinets in the bathroom" |
| **Street use** | "Fentanyl patches are often **cut into pieces and sold on the street as 'chicklets'**" |

Three Clinical Pearls make the same points in one line each (p. 148): "**Fentanyl patches take 6 to 12 hours to produce pain control after initial administration, and action can last up to 17 hours after removing.**" and "**Be sure to remove old fentanyl patch before applying a new patch.**"

### hydromorphone (Dilaudid) (p. 149)

- "a **very potent** opioid analgesic and is a **Schedule II** drug... approximately **seven times more potent than morphine.**"
- "It is often referred to as **Dilaudid, because hydromorphone can be mistaken for morphine.**"
- **Exalgo** is "the **osmotic extended-release** oral delivery system of hydromorphone, which is **difficult to crush or extract for injection,** to help reduce the abuse potential."
- Route options (Table 10.7, p. 159): "May be given **SUBQ, rectally, IV, PO, or IM.**"

### meperidine hydrochloride (Demerol) (p. 149)

- "a **synthetic** opioid analgesic (**Schedule II**)."
- **"Must be used with caution, if at all, in older adult patients and in patients who require long-term analgesia or who have kidney dysfunction."**
- **"An active metabolite, normeperidine, can accumulate to toxic levels and lead to seizures. For this reason, meperidine is now rarely used and is not recommended for long-term pain treatment."**
- **The two surviving uses:** "still used in **emergency department settings for acute migraine headaches** and in the **immediate postoperative period to reduce shivering.**"
- Table 10.8 (p. 160) bars it from cancer pain: "**Short (2–3 hr) duration of analgesia; administration may lead to CNS toxicity (tremor, confusion, or seizures).**"
- "**highly protein bound, so watch for interactions and toxicity**" (Table 10.7).

### methadone hydrochloride (Dolophine) (p. 149)

- "a **synthetic** opioid analgesic (**Schedule II**). It is the **opioid of choice for the detoxification treatment of opioid addicts in methadone maintenance programs.**"
- **The pharmacokinetic oddity:** "**It is unique in that its half-life is longer than its duration of action** because it is **bound into the tissues of the liver, kidneys, and brain.** With repeated doses, the drug **accumulates in these tissues and is slowly released,** thus allowing for **24-hour dosing.**"
- **"Methadone is eliminated through the liver, which makes it a safer choice than some other opioids for patients with renal impairment."**
- **The warnings:** "Recent FDA reports have cited the **prolonged half-life** of the drug as a cause of **unintentional overdoses and deaths.** There is also concern that methadone may cause **cardiac dysrhythmias.**"

### morphine sulfate (p. 150)

- "a **naturally occurring alkaloid** derived from the opium poppy, is the **drug prototype for all opioid drugs.** Schedule II... indicated for **severe pain** and has a **high abuse potential.**"
- Dosage forms: "**oral, rectal, epidural, and injectable** dosage forms, including **PCA cartridges.**" Extended-release: "**MS Contin and Kadian.**"
- **The toxic metabolite:** "Morphine also has a potentially toxic metabolite known as **morphine-6-glucuronide.** Accumulation of this metabolite is **more likely to occur in patients with renal impairment.** For this reason, other Schedule II opioids such as **hydromorphone (Dilaudid) and fentanyl may be safer analgesic choices for patients with renal insufficiency.**"
- **Embeda** (morphine and naltrexone) "is the newest morphine product."
- Table 10.7 (p. 159): "**Always monitor respiratory rate.**" Morphine sulfate (**Duramorph**) is the epidural infusion form.

### oxycodone hydrochloride (p. 150)

- "structurally related to morphine and has **comparable analgesic activity** (Schedule II)."
- Combinations: "**Percocet**" (with acetaminophen), "**Percodan**" (with aspirin).
- Forms: "immediate-release formulations (**Oxy IR**) and sustained-release formulations (**OxyContin**)."
- **Hydrocodone:** "a somewhat **weaker** but commonly used opioid... most commonly in combination with acetaminophen (**Vicodin, Norco**)." Long-acting abuse-deterrent products: "**Hysingla ER and Zyhydro ER.** In **2014, hydrocodone was rescheduled as a CII drug.**"

**The naming conventions the chapter spells out** (p. 143): "The product **OxyContin** is a sustained-release form of oxycodone designed to last up to **12 hours.** The '**Contin**' in the product name is a trademark... and refers to the '**continuous-release**' nature of the drug formulation... Similarly, **MS Contin** is a long-acting or sustained-release form of morphine. The '**MS**' stands for **morphine sulfate.**"

- "**A continuous- or extended-release dosage form... has a prolonged duration of action, most often 8 to 24 hours, and cannot be crushed.**"
- "The analgesic effects of **immediate-release** oral dosage forms of all three drugs typically last for about **4 hours.**"

### Opioid agonists-antagonists (p. 150)

- "often called agonists-antagonists (**Schedule IV**). They bind to the **mu receptor** and compete with other substances for these sites."
- **"They have a lower risk for misuse and addiction."**
- **"The antagonistic activity of this group can produce withdrawal symptoms in patients who are opioid-dependent."**
- **The four available:** "**buprenorphine (Buprenex), butorphanol (Stadol), nalbuphine (Nubain), and pentazocine (Talwin).**" A transdermal buprenorphine, **Butrans**, "is also available."
- **Uses:** "short-term pain control, such as **after obstetric procedures**"; "sometimes chosen for patients who have a **history of opioid addiction**... can both help prevent **overmedication** and reduce **posttreatment addictive cravings.**"
- **Buprenorphine + naloxone** combination products "offer physicians an **in-office treatment of addiction.**"
- **Limits:** "**normally not strong enough for management of longer-term chronic pain**"; "**not to be given concurrently with full opioid agonists,** because they may **both reduce analgesic effects and cause withdrawal symptoms** in opioid-tolerant patients."
- "Adverse reactions are similar to those with opioids but with a **lower incidence of respiratory depression.**"

> **Trap (confusable pair).** **morphine vs. hydromorphone in renal impairment, and the reason flips per drug.** Cue: ***morphine → morphine-6-glucuronide accumulates in renal impairment,** so hydromorphone or fentanyl is safer; **meperidine → normeperidine accumulates,** causing seizures, and it too is contraindicated in kidney dysfunction; **methadone → eliminated by the liver,** so it is the one that is *safer* in renal impairment.* Review question 8 (p. 163) asks exactly this for a **renal cancer** patient, offering fentanyl, hydromorphone, morphine, and methadone. Note that the chapter supports **three** of those four as reasonable and rules out only morphine, so read the stem for which property it is testing. Official key not in the pull; see GAPS item 10.

> **Trap (confusable pair).** **Codeine's two schedules.** Cue: ***alone = Schedule II** (high abuse potential); **combined with acetaminophen = Schedule III.*** A distractor that assigns one schedule to "codeine" without specifying the formulation is under-specified, and the chapter is explicit that the combination is the lower schedule.

> **Trap (level/stage swap).** **Methadone's half-life is longer than its duration of action.** This is the reverse of every other drug in the chapter and is why unintentional overdoses happen: the analgesia wears off before the drug does, so a patient dosing to comfort accumulates. Cue: *if a stem describes a patient taking more methadone because the pain returned, the danger is **accumulation,** not tolerance.*

> **Trap (confusable pair).** **The fentanyl patch's three time constants.** Cue: ***6 to 12 hours** to start working; **72 hours** per patch; **17 hours** to fall by half after removal.* All three are tested, and the 17-hour figure is the one that matters in an overdose stem: removing the patch does **not** end the exposure. Peak serum effect lands in the **first 24 to 72 hours,** which is when monitoring is tightest. **Caution on the first number: the chapter prints a conflicting onset of 12–24 hours and peak of 48–72 hours in its pharmacokinetics table.** The 6-to-12-hour figure above is the drug profile's and the Clinical Pearl's. Neither is reconcilable with the other; see GAPS item 7 and the trap on that row. The **17 hours** and **72 hours** figures are uncontested and are the safe ones to hold.

> **Trap (priority question).** **Heat over a fentanyl patch is an overdose mechanism.** Heating pad, sauna, hot bath, fever. The chapter gives the mechanism (increased circulation → increased absorption) and the outcome (overdose). If a stem has a patient with a patch applying heat for comfort, that is the teaching error, ahead of any dosing question.

> **Trap (setting vs. focus).** **Agonists-antagonists reduce respiratory depression risk but cannot be added to a full agonist.** Both facts, together, are the item: they look like a safer opioid to "add" for an opioid-tolerant patient, and doing so **precipitates withdrawal and reduces analgesia.** Table 10.8 (p. 160) bars pentazocine, butorphanol, and nalbuphine from cancer pain for this reason, plus the **analgesic ceiling** and "possible production of unpleasant psychologic adverse effects, including **dysphoria, delusions, and hallucinations.**"

## §12 Nonopioid and miscellaneous analgesics

**Acetaminophen (Tylenol)** is "the **most widely used nonopioid analgesic**" (p. 151).

- **The abbreviation problem:** "commonly abbreviated **APAP** in the United States; however, this abbreviation is **not recognized in Canada.** There is a current movement to **stop using APAP** as an abbreviation, because of the potential that patients will **not realize they are receiving a prescription with acetaminophen** and may take additional over-the-counter acetaminophen."

**Mechanism** (p. 151): "similar to that of the **salicylates.** It **blocks peripheral pain impulses by inhibition of prostaglandin synthesis.** Acetaminophen also **lowers febrile body temperatures by acting on the hypothalamus**... Heat is dissipated through **vasodilation and increased peripheral blood flow.**"

- **The defining negative:** "**In contrast to NSAIDs, acetaminophen lacks antiinflammatory effects.**"
- What it therefore avoids: "not usually associated with **cardiovascular effects (e.g., edema)** or **platelet effects (e.g., bleeding),** as are aspirin and other NSAIDs. It also does not cause the aspirin-related **GI tract irritation or bleeding,** nor any of the aspirin-related **acid-base changes.**"

**Indications** (p. 151): "**mild to moderate pain and fever.**"

- **"Acetaminophen is also the antipyretic (antifever) drug of choice in children and adolescents with flu syndromes, because the use of aspirin in these populations is associated with a condition known as Reye's syndrome."**

**Contraindications** (p. 151): "**known drug allergy, severe liver disease,** and the genetic disease known as **glucose-6-phosphate dehydrogenase (G6PD) deficiency.**"

**Maximum daily doses** (p. 151, Clinical Pearl), the highest-yield numbers in this section:

| Patient | Maximum |
|---|---|
| **Healthy patient** | **4000 mg/day** |
| **Older adult** | **3000 mg/day** |
| **Liver disease or chronic alcohol consumption** | **2000 mg/day** |

The body text adds nuance the pearl compresses: "Currently, the **FDA limits total daily doses to 4000 mg;** however, the **manufacturer of Tylenol suggests a limit of 3000 mg/day.**" The dosage table (p. 148) prints "not to exceed **3–4 g/day**" and "In alcoholics, not to exceed **2 g/day**," with a footnote reading "The maximum recommended daily dose... for a typical adult patient with **normal liver function is 3000 mg/24 hr.** For **hepatically compromised** patients, this dosage may be **2000 mg or even lower.**"

- **The combination-product trap, in the chapter's words:** "Acetaminophen is combined with **hydrocodone (Vicodin, Norco)** or **oxycodone (Percocet, Tylox),** and patients may **exceed the recommended limit of acetaminophen without knowing these products also contain acetaminophen.** In **2011,** the FDA announced that **combination products are to be limited to 325 mg of acetaminophen.**"

**Adverse effects** (p. 151): "generally well tolerated... Possible adverse effects include **skin disorders, nausea, and vomiting.** Much less common but more severe are **blood disorders or dyscrasias (e.g., anemias), nephrotoxicities, and hepatotoxicity. Hepatotoxicity is the most serious adverse effect** and "is associated with **excessive doses.**"

**Toxicity and its antidote** (pp. 151–152):

- "many people do not realize that acetaminophen, **despite its over-the-counter status, is a potentially lethal drug when taken in overdose.** Depressed patients (**especially adolescents**) may intentionally overdose... **as an attention-seeking gesture without realizing the grave danger involved.**"
- **The toxic dose:** "Acute ingestion of acetaminophen doses of **150 mg/kg (approximately 7 to 10 g) or more may result in hepatotoxicity.**"
- **Acute vs. chronic:** "**Acute hepatotoxicity can usually be reversed with acetylcysteine, whereas long-term toxicity is more likely to be permanent.**"
- **When to draw the level:** "a serum acetaminophen concentration is determined **no sooner than 4 hours after ingestion.** If a serum acetaminophen level **cannot be determined, it is assumed that the overdose is potentially toxic and treatment with acetylcysteine needs to be started.**"
- **The antidote:** "**Acetylcysteine is the recommended antidote**... works by **preventing the hepatotoxic metabolites of acetaminophen from forming.** It is **most effective when given within 10 hours of an overdose.**"
- **Administration reality:** "Oral acetylcysteine is **notoriously bad tasting with an odor of rotten eggs,** and vomiting of an oral dose is common. It is recommended that the **dose be repeated if vomiting occurs within 1 hour of dosing.** An intravenous formulation (**Acetadote**) is also available and **much better tolerated.**"

**Acetaminophen interactions** (p. 152): "**Alcohol is potentially the most dangerous.** Chronic heavy alcohol abusers may be at increased risk for **liver toxicity**... a maximum daily dose of **2000 mg** is generally recommended for such patients... **Ideally, alcohol consumption is not to exceed three drinks daily.** Other hepatotoxic drugs need to be avoided." Other interacting drugs: "**phenytoin, barbiturates, warfarin, isoniazid, rifampin, beta blockers, and anticholinergic drugs.**"

**The other nonopioids and miscellaneous drugs:**

| Drug | What the chapter says |
|---|---|
| **NSAIDs** (aspirin, ibuprofen, naproxen, **celecoxib [Celebrex]**) | "**all of the drugs in the NSAID class... are nonopioid analgesics.** They are discussed in **Chapter 44.**" Used "especially [for] pain associated with **inflammatory conditions such as arthritis,** because they have significant **antiinflammatory** effects in addition to their analgesic effects" (p. 151) |
| **tramadol (Ultram)** | "**nonopioid analgesic (with opioid-like activity).**" Oral only, including **Ultracet** (with acetaminophen), extended-release (**ConZip, Ryzolt, Ultram ER**), and an orally disintegrating tablet (**Rybix**) (p. 152) |
| **tapentadol (Nucynta)** | "structurally related to tramadol with a **dual mechanism of action. It is a mu agonist and a norepinephrine reuptake inhibitor.** It is a **Schedule II** narcotic" (p. 152) |
| **transdermal lidocaine (Lidoderm)** | "a **topical anesthetic** formulated in a patch and placed onto painful areas of the skin. It is indicated for the treatment of **postherpetic neuralgia**" (p. 152) |
| **capsaicin** | "a **topical product made from several different types of peppers.** It works by **decreasing or interfering with substance P,** a pain signal in the brain. Available **over the counter.** Used for **muscle pain, joint pain, and nerve pain** and **should be used on a scheduled basis, not as needed**" (p. 151) |
| **milnacipran (Savella)** | "a **selective serotonin and norepinephrine dual-uptake inhibitor.** Indicated for the treatment of **fibromyalgia**"; the rationale given is that fibromyalgia patients "have **reduced levels of norepinephrine** in their brains" (p. 151) |

> **Trap (confusable pair).** **Acetaminophen vs. NSAIDs.** They share **analgesic and antipyretic** effects, so the whole discriminator is the third property. Cue: ***acetaminophen has NO antiinflammatory effect;** NSAIDs do.* Everything else follows from it: acetaminophen is the wrong drug for arthritis and inflammatory pain, and the right drug when you must avoid bleeding, GI irritation, or edema.

> **Trap (level/stage swap).** **The three acetaminophen ceilings are 4000 / 3000 / 2000, and the chapter itself is inconsistent about which is default.** Cue: ***4000 = FDA limit for a healthy adult; 3000 = what the Tylenol manufacturer (and the dosage-table footnote) recommends; 2000 = liver disease or chronic alcohol.*** A stem naming an older adult or a drinker is asking for a **lower** number than the famous 4 g. See GAPS item 6 for the internal conflict.

> **Trap (plausible-but-adjacent).** **The combination-product overdose is the mechanism the chapter cares about.** Percocet, Vicodin, Norco, and Tylox all contain acetaminophen, so a patient taking one of these **plus** OTC Tylenol can pass the ceiling without ever taking "too much Tylenol." This is why the FDA capped combination products at **325 mg** in 2011 and why the chapter wants **APAP** retired as an abbreviation.

> **Trap (confusable pair).** **acetylcysteine timing has two different numbers.** Cue: *draw the **level** no sooner than **4 hours** after ingestion; give the **antidote** within **10 hours** for best effect.* And the override: **if you cannot get a level, treat anyway.** An option that delays acetylcysteine while waiting for a level is inverting the chapter's own instruction.

> **Trap (priority question).** **Review question 5 (p. 163) asks which patient must not receive acetaminophen,** offering a fever of 103.4°F, deep vein thrombosis, **severe hepatitis,** and post-abdominal-surgery. Held against p. 151, the listed contraindications are drug allergy, **severe liver disease,** and G6PD deficiency; fever is an **indication,** not a contraindication. Official key not in the pull; see GAPS item 10.

> **Trap (plausible-but-adjacent).** **Reye's syndrome is an aspirin problem, and acetaminophen is the answer to it,** not a cause of it. The chapter's sentence is constructed so that acetaminophen is the drug **of choice** in children and adolescents with flu syndromes precisely **because** aspirin is not. A distractor that withholds acetaminophen from a febrile child on Reye's grounds has the two drugs swapped.

> **Trap (confusable pair).** **tramadol vs. tapentadol.** One letter apart in the middle and structurally related. Cue: ***tram**adol = "nonopioid analgesic **with opioid-like activity**," oral only; **tapent**adol = **Schedule II narcotic,** and a **dual** mechanism (mu agonist **plus** norepinephrine reuptake inhibition).* The scheduling is the fastest discriminator.

## §13 Nursing administration: the numbers that discriminate

**The respiratory thresholds,** which are the chapter's most-cited nursing figures:

| Situation | Threshold as given | p. |
|---|---|---|
| **When to withhold an opioid** | "An opioid medication is usually **withheld if a patient's respirations are less than 12 breaths/min**" | 154 |
| **When to report** | "A respiratory rate of **10 breaths/min** (some protocols still adhere to the parameter of **12 breaths/min**) **may indicate respiratory depression and must be reported to the prescriber**" | 157 |
| **Expected outcome** | "Patient regains/maintains a **respiratory rate between 10 and 20 breaths/min** without respiratory depression" | 156 |
| **Reassessment trigger** | monitor "especially if the **respiratory rate is less than 10 breaths/min**" | 158 |

**Vital signs** (p. 154): "Always monitor and document **vital signs before, during, and after** the administration"; "The patient's baseline **age, weight, and height** are important to document, because **drug calculations are often based on these variables.**" For pediatric patients, "**check and double-check all mathematical calculations for accuracy** to avoid excessive dosages; **this is especially true for opioids.**"

**The pain-relief target** (p. 159): "A patient-rated pain level of **less than 4 on a scale of 1 to 10** is considered to indicate **effective pain relief.** However, this may vary depending on the health care provider, health care setting, and/or unit."

**Scheduling** (p. 159): "it is best to treat the patient's pain **before it becomes severe,** which is the rationale for considering **pain to be the fifth vital sign.** When pain is present for **more than 12 hours a day,** analgesic doses are individualized and are best **administered around the clock rather than on an as-needed basis**... **Around-the-clock (or scheduled) dosing maintains steady-state levels of the medication and prevents drug troughs and pain escalation.**"

**Table 10.7 Opioid Administration Guidelines** (p. 159), reproduced:

| Opioid | Nursing administration as given |
|---|---|
| **buprenorphine and butorphanol** | "When giving IV, **infuse over the recommended time (usually 3–5 min).** Always assess **respirations before, during, and after** use" |
| **codeine** | "Give **PO doses with food** to minimize GI tract upset; **ceiling effects** occur with oral codeine, resulting in **no increase in analgesia with increased dosage**" |
| **fentanyl** | "Administer parenteral doses over **1–2 min**... to prevent CNS depression and possible **cardiac or respiratory arrest.** Be sure to **remove residual amounts of the old patch** before application of a new patch. Patches are to be **folded and flushed down the toilet**" |
| **hydromorphone** | "May be given **SUBQ, rectally, IV, PO, or IM**" |
| **meperidine** | "IV, IM, or PO; **highly protein bound,** so watch for interactions and toxicity. Monitor **older adult** patients for increased sensitivity" |
| **morphine** | "SUBQ, IM, PO, IV, extended- and immediate-release; morphine sulfate (**Duramorph**) for **epidural** infusion. **Always monitor respiratory rate**" |
| **nalbuphine** | "IV doses of **10 mg given undiluted over 5 min**" |
| **naloxone** | "Antagonist given for opioid overdose; **0.4 mg usually given IV over 15 sec or less. Reverses analgesia as well**" |
| **oxycodone** | "Often mixed with acetaminophen or aspirin; **PO and suppository** dosage forms. Now available in both immediate and sustained-release tablets" |

**Route selection for chronic and cancer pain** (p. 160):

- "**Oral administration is always preferred**" but may not be tolerated.
- **Rectal:** "safe, inexpensive, effective, and helpful if the patient is experiencing **nausea or vomiting or altered mental status;** however, **not suitable for those with diarrhea, stomatitis, and/or low white blood cell counts.**"
- **Transdermal:** "may provide up to **7 days** of pain control but are **not for rapid dose titration** and are used **only when stable analgesia has been previously achieved.**"
- **Subcutaneous infusions** "are often used **when there is no intravenous access.**"
- **Intraspinal:** "The main reason for **long-term intraspinal opioid administration is intractable pain.**"
- **Transnasal:** "approved **only for butorphanol**... and this dosage form is **generally not used or recommended.**"
- "a **fast-acting rescue drug needs to be ordered and available** for patients with cancer pain."

**Suppository handling** (p. 157): a suppository "may be placed into a **medicine cup of ice** to prevent melting of the dosage form. Once the suppository is unwrapped, **cold water may be run over it.**"

**Older adults** (p. 162, key points): "these patients **experience pain the same as the general population does,** but they may be **reluctant to report pain** and may **metabolize opiates at a slower rate** and thus are at **increased risk for adverse effects such as sedation and respiratory depression. The best rule is to start with low dosages, reevaluate often, and go slowly during upward titration.**"

**Pediatrics** (p. 162, key points): "Pediatric dosages of morphine must be calculated **very cautiously** with close attention to the dose and **kilograms of body weight. Cautious titration of dosage upward is usually the standard.**"

**Patient teaching highlights** (p. 162):

- "Opioids are **not to be used with alcohol or with other central nervous system depressants,** unless ordered."
- Constipation: "**forcing fluids (up to 3 L/day unless contraindicated), increasing fiber consumption, and exercising as tolerated.** Stool softeners also may be necessary."
- "**Dizziness, difficulty breathing, low blood pressure, excessive sleepiness (sedation), confusion, or loss of memory must be promptly reported.**"
- "Any activities requiring **mental clarity or alertness** may need to be avoided if experiencing drowsiness or sedation."
- Tolerance: "if the level of pain increases while the patient remains on the prescribed dosage, the prescriber... must be contacted. **Dosages must not be changed, increased, or doubled unless prescribed.**"

> **Trap (level/stage swap).** **Less than 12 to hold, 10 to report, 10 to 20 as the goal.** Three numbers, one axis, and the chapter admits protocols disagree ("some protocols still adhere to the parameter of 12"). Cue: ***12 is the withholding threshold; 10 is the alarm.*** A stem giving a respiratory rate of 11 is deliberately placed between them.

> **Trap (priority question).** **Review question 2 (p. 163): a PCA patient is unresponsive, respirations 8, BP 102/58.** The stem has already stopped the infusion and asks what to do **next,** offering notify the charge nurse, draw arterial blood gases, **administer an opiate antagonist per standing orders,** and perform a thorough assessment. The chapter's escalation ladder (§9) goes stimulate → ventilate → **naloxone for severe respiratory depression,** and 8 breaths/min sits below every threshold in the table above. Note that "perform a thorough assessment" is the classic plausible-but-adjacent option: assessment is right in general and too slow here. Official key not in the pull.

> **Trap (confusable pair).** **Around-the-clock vs. prn, and which pain gets which.** Cue: *pain present **more than 12 hours a day** → **scheduled, around the clock,** to prevent troughs; **breakthrough** pain → **prn immediate-release** on top.* Review question 1 (p. 163) puts this to metastatic bone pain and offers "as needed," "when requested," "on schedule during waking hours only," and "**around the clock, with additional doses as needed for breakthrough pain.**" The waking-hours option is the trap: it reintroduces a nightly trough.

> **Trap (SATA, five options mapped to categories).** **Table 10.8 is a ready-made select-all.** Nine entries, five reasons: **short duration/CNS toxicity** (meperidine), **ceiling effect and precipitated withdrawal** (the agonists-antagonists and buprenorphine), **reverses analgesia** (naloxone), **no proven benefit** (Brompton, DPT), and **no analgesic property at all** (benzodiazepines, barbiturates). The last category is the one that surprises: benzodiazepines are **not analgesics,** so using one as monotherapy for cancer pain treats nothing and adds sedation risk.

> **Trap (setting vs. focus).** **Transdermal is for stable pain, not for titration.** "**Not for rapid dose titration** and are used **only when stable analgesia has been previously achieved.**" A stem escalating a patient's uncontrolled pain by switching them to a patch has chosen a route that cannot do the job, independent of the drug.
