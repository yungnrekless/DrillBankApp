## Key terms

All 6 terms from the chapter's KEY TERMS box, quoted as given (p. 684). This is a small box for a `full` chapter; the vocabulary load here is in the drug names and the two pathways, not in the terms.

| Term | p. | Definition as given |
|---|---|---|
| **Chemotaxis** | 684 | "The **chemical attraction of leukocytes to the site of inflammation,** which **worsens** an inflammatory response." |
| **Done nomogram** | 684 | "A standard data graph, originally published in **1960** in the journal *Pediatrics,* for **rating the severity of aspirin toxicity after overdose. Serum salicylate levels are plotted against time elapsed since ingestion.**" |
| **Gout** | 684 | "**Hyperuricemia (elevated blood uric acid level);** the **arthritis caused by tissue buildup of uric acid crystals.**" |
| **Inflammation** | 684 | "A **localized protective response** stimulated by injury to tissues that serves to **destroy, dilute, or wall off (sequester)** both the injurious agent and the injured tissue." |
| **Nonsteroidal antiinflammatory drugs (NSAIDs)** | 684 | "A large and chemically diverse group of drugs that possess **analgesic, antiinflammatory, and antipyretic (fever-reducing)** activity." |
| **Salicylism** | 684 | "The syndrome of **salicylate toxicity,** including symptoms such as **tinnitus** (ringing sound in the ears), **nausea, and vomiting.**" |

> **Trap (confusable pair).** **The NSAID definition has three properties; aspirin has four.** Cue: *all NSAIDs are **analgesic, antiinflammatory, and antipyretic**; **aspirin alone adds antiplatelet.*** The key points state it directly (p. 697): "NSAIDs have analgesic, antiinflammatory, and antipyretic activity; **aspirin also has antiplatelet activity.**" Compare **acetaminophen** from Ch 10, which is analgesic and antipyretic but **lacks the antiinflammatory** property, and is not an NSAID at all. Three drugs, three different property sets, and the exam builds options out of the differences.

> **Trap (confusable pair).** **Gout is defined twice in one entry.** The key term gives both the **lab finding** (hyperuricemia, elevated blood uric acid) and the **clinical disease** (arthritis from tissue buildup of urate crystals). A patient can be hyperuricemic without gouty arthritis; the chapter treats the two as one entry but the drugs target them differently (§7).

> **Trap (plausible-but-adjacent).** **Chemotaxis worsens inflammation.** It is not a healing or resolving process, and the definition says so ("which worsens an inflammatory response"). This matters because **colchicine's** mechanism is to inhibit "metabolism, mobility, and **chemotaxis** of polymorphonuclear leukocytes" (p. 693). An option describing chemotaxis as beneficial has the direction reversed.

## §1 Inflammation and the arachidonic acid pathway

**The classic signs and symptoms of inflammation** (p. 684): "**pain, fever, loss of function, redness, and swelling.**"

- **The mechanism behind them:** "These symptoms result from **arterial, venous, and capillary dilation; enhanced blood flow and vascular permeability; exudation of fluids, including plasma proteins; and leukocyte migration** into the inflammatory focus."
- **The mediators:** "proteins of the **complement system, histamine, serotonin, bradykinin, leukotrienes, and prostaglandins,** the latter two being **major contributors** to the symptoms of inflammation."

**The pathway** (pp. 684–685): "**Arachidonic acid is released from phospholipids in cell membranes** in response to a triggering event (e.g., an injury). It is metabolized in either the **prostaglandin pathway** or the **leukotriene pathway,** both of which are branches of the **arachidonic acid pathway.** Both of these pathways lead to **inflammation, edema, headache, and other pain.**"

**Fig. 44.1 Arachidonic acid pathway** (p. 685), reconstructed from the extracted labels:

| Branch | Enzyme | Products | Effects as labeled |
|---|---|---|---|
| **Leukotriene pathway** | **Lipoxygenase** | **Leukotrienes** | "**Vasoconstriction, bronchoconstriction, vascular permeability**" → Inflammation |
| **Prostaglandin pathway** | **Cyclooxygenase (COX)** → **prostacyclin synthetase** | **Prostacyclin (PGI2)**, in the **blood vessel wall** | "**↓ PLT aggregation, vasodilation**" → Inflammation |
| **Prostaglandin pathway** | **Cyclooxygenase (COX)** → **thromboxane synthetase** | **Thromboxane (TXA2)**, in **platelets** | "**↑ PLT aggregation, vasoconstriction**" → Inflammation |

**What each pathway does, in prose:**

- **Prostaglandins** (p. 685): "mediate inflammation by inducing **vasodilation and enhancing vasopermeability.** These effects in turn **potentiate the action of proinflammatory substances such as histamine and bradykinin** in the production of edema and pain."
- **Hyperalgesia:** "These symptoms arise as a result of **prostaglandin-induced hyperalgesia (excessive sensitivity).** In this situation, stimuli that normally would not be painful, such as simply **moving a joint through its natural range of motion,** become painful."
- **Fever:** "**Fever occurs when prostaglandin E2 is synthesized in the preoptic hypothalamic region,** the area of the brain that regulates temperature."
- **Leukotrienes** (p. 685): "more newly discovered than prostaglandins and **not as well studied,**" they promote "**vasoconstriction, bronchospasm, and increased vascular permeability with resultant edema**" (see Ch. 37).

> **Trap (confusable pair).** **Prostacyclin (PGI2) vs. thromboxane (TXA2).** Same pathway, same parent enzyme, **opposite effects on both platelets and vessels.** Cue: *prostacy**cl**in lives in the **blood vessel wall** and **decreases** platelet aggregation while **dilating**; thrombo**xane** lives in the **platelets** and **increases** aggregation while **constricting**. Thrombo- = thrombus = clot = sticky.* Aspirin's whole cardioprotective mechanism runs through suppressing **thromboxane**, so getting this pair backwards inverts the reason aspirin prevents MI.

> **Trap (confusable pair).** **Prostaglandin vs. leukotriene pathway, by enzyme.** Cue: ***lipo**xygenase → **leuko**trienes (both start with L); **cyclo**oxygenase → **prostaglandins**.* The therapeutic consequence is stated at p. 686: "**The leukotriene pathway is inhibited by some antiinflammatory drugs but not by salicylates.**" So aspirin does not touch the leukotriene arm at all, which is part of why it aggravates asthma.

> **Trap (plausible-but-adjacent).** **Both branches end in inflammation.** Fig. 44.1 labels all three terminal boxes "Inflammation," so an option contrasting one pathway as inflammatory and the other as protective is wrong on the figure. The branches differ in **how** they inflame (constriction and bronchospasm vs. dilation and permeability), not in **whether** they do.

> **Trap (confusable pair).** **Hyperalgesia here vs. allodynia in Ch 10.** Ch 10 (p. 142) defines **allodynia** as hypersensitivity to stimuli that are normally *not painful at all* (light touch, bed sheets). This chapter uses **hyperalgesia** for the same phenomenon in inflamed joints (range of motion becoming painful). Both chapters are describing lowered pain thresholds, from different causes: **inflammatory prostaglandins** here, **nerve damage** in Ch 10.

## §2 COX-1 vs. COX-2, and how NSAIDs work

**The mechanism** (p. 686): "NSAIDs work through inhibition of the **leukotriene pathway, the prostaglandin pathway, or both.** More specifically, NSAIDs relieve pain, headache, and inflammation by **blocking the chemical activity of the enzyme called cyclooxygenase (COX).**"

**The two isoforms, which is the highest-yield distinction in the chapter** (p. 686):

| | **COX-1** | **COX-2** |
|---|---|---|
| What it makes | prostaglandins with "primarily **beneficial** effects on various bodily functions" | prostaglandins "involved in **inflammatory processes**" |
| Named example | "their role in **maintaining an intact gastrointestinal mucosa**" | inflammation |
| Consequence of blocking it | **GI ulceration and bleeding**: "All NSAIDs can be **ulcerogenic** and induce gastrointestinal bleeding **due to their activity against tissue COX-1**" | the desired antiinflammatory effect |

- **COX-2 inhibitors** (p. 686): approved in **1998**, "the newest class of NSAIDs... work by **specifically inhibiting the COX-2 form** of COX and theoretically have **limited or no effects on COX-1. Previous NSAIDs nonspecifically inhibited both COX-1 and COX-2 activity.** This greater enzyme specificity... allows for the beneficial antiinflammatory effects **while reducing the prevalence of adverse effects** associated with the nonspecific NSAIDs, such as gastrointestinal ulceration."

**Aspirin's unique antiplatelet property** (p. 686): "One notable effect of aspirin is its **inhibition of platelet aggregation,** also known as its **antiplatelet activity. Aspirin has the unique property among NSAIDs of being an irreversible inhibitor of COX-1 receptors within the platelets themselves.** This in turn results in **reduced formation of thromboxane A2,** a substance that normally promotes platelet aggregation. This antiplatelet action has made aspirin a **primary drug in the treatment of acute MI** and many other thromboembolic disorders. **Other NSAIDs lack these antiplatelet effects.**"

**Aspirin in acute MI** (p. 685): "Aspirin has been shown to **reduce cardiac death after an MI and should be administered at the first sign of MI.** If not given before arriving in the emergency department, **aspirin is one of the first drugs given if there are no contraindications.**"

**The class's scale** (p. 685): "Currently **more than 23 different NSAIDs** are available in the United States... **A given patient may respond better to one NSAID than to others,** in terms of both symptom relief and adverse effects."

**Aspirin's history** (p. 685): "In **1899,** acetylsalicylic acid (ASA; aspirin) was marketed and rapidly became **the most widely used drug in the world.**" Its adverse effects (GI intolerance, bleeding, renal impairment) drove development of the other NSAIDs. **"If aspirin were to be a newly discovered drug today, it would most likely require a prescription."**

**Box 44.1 Chemical Categories of NSAIDs** (p. 686), reproduced:

| Category | Drugs |
|---|---|
| **Salicylates** | **aspirin,** diflunisal (Dolobid), salsalate (Salistab), choline salicylate |
| **Acetic acid derivatives** | diclofenac sodium (Voltaren), **indomethacin (Indocin),** sulindac (Clinoril), tolmetin (Tolectin), etodolac (Lodine), **ketorolac (Toradol)** |
| **Cyclooxygenase-2 inhibitors** | **celecoxib (Celebrex)** |
| **Enolic acid derivatives** | nabumetone (Relafen), meloxicam (Mobic), piroxicam (Feldene) |
| **Propionic acid derivatives** | fenoprofen (Nalfon), flurbiprofen (Ansaid), **ibuprofen (Motrin, Advil),** ketoprofen (Orudis KT), **naproxen (Naprosyn, Aleve),** oxaprozin (Daypro) |

> **Trap (confusable pair).** **COX-1 vs. COX-2, and which one you want to keep.** Cue: *COX-**1** is the **1** you need (protects the gut lining); COX-**2** is the **2**rouble (inflammation).* Every GI adverse effect in this chapter traces to COX-1 blockade, and the entire rationale for celecoxib is sparing it. An option attributing NSAID ulcers to COX-2 inhibition has the isoforms crossed.

> **Trap (confusable pair).** **Aspirin is irreversible; every other NSAID is not.** This single word carries the clinical difference. Cue: *irreversible → the platelet is disabled **for its lifetime** → antiplatelet effect persists after the drug clears → aspirin is the MI/stroke drug and the one you stop **a week** before surgery.* "**Other NSAIDs lack these antiplatelet effects.**" An option offering ibuprofen for thromboprophylaxis is substituting a drug that does not have the property.

> **Trap (plausible-but-adjacent).** **COX-2 inhibitors are not free of GI toxicity.** "They are **not totally devoid** of gastrointestinal toxicity. **Gastritis and upper gastrointestinal bleeding have been reported** with their use, although **much less frequently** than with the older NSAIDs" (p. 691). An option promising a patient there is no GI risk on celecoxib overstates the chapter.

> **Trap (level/stage swap).** **1899 (aspirin marketed) vs. 1998 (COX-2 inhibitors approved) vs. 2006 (black box required) vs. 2015 (warning strengthened).** Four dates, all in this chapter, all attached to different events. The two that matter clinically are the FDA ones (§4).

## §3 Indications

"NSAIDs are primarily used for their **analgesic, antiinflammatory, and antipyretic effects and for platelet inhibition**" (p. 686).

**Box 44.2 NSAIDs: FDA-Approved Indications** (p. 686), reproduced in full:

- **Acute gout**
- **Acute gouty arthritis**
- **Ankylosing spondylitis**
- **Bursitis**
- **Fever**
- **Juvenile rheumatoid arthritis**
- **Mild to moderate pain**
- **Osteoarthritis**
- **Primary dysmenorrhea**
- **Rheumatoid arthritis**
- **Tendonitis**
- **Various ophthalmic uses**

**Also used for** (pp. 684, 686): "relief of **mild to moderate headaches, myalgia, neuralgia, and arthralgia;** alleviation of **postoperative pain**"; "**rheumatic fever**"; and "as **adjunctive pain relief medications in patients with chronic pain syndromes, such as pain from bone cancer and chronic back pain.**"

**The opioid-sparing effect** (pp. 686–687), which is the direct link back to Chapter 10: "For pain relief, NSAIDs are sometimes **combined with an opioid (see Chapter 10). They tend to have an opioid-sparing effect** when given together with opioids, because the drugs **attack pain using two different mechanisms.** This often allows **less opioid to be used.**"

**The ceiling effect, stated as the contrast with opioids** (p. 687): "**Unlike opioids, NSAIDs show a ceiling effect that limits their effectiveness;** that is, any further **increase in the dosage beyond a certain level increases the risk for adverse effects without a corresponding increase in the therapeutic effect.** In contrast, **opioids may be titrated almost indefinitely** to increasingly higher levels, especially in terminally ill patients with severe pain."

**Table 44.1 Suggested NSAIDs for Patients With Various Medical Conditions** (p. 687), reproduced. This table is unusual in that it recommends drugs by **comorbidity**, not by diagnosis:

| Medical condition | Recommended NSAID |
|---|---|
| **Ankylosing spondylitis** | indomethacin, diclofenac |
| **Diabetic neuropathy** | sulindac |
| **Dysmenorrhea** | Fenamates, naproxen, ibuprofen |
| **Gout** | **indomethacin, naproxen, sulindac** |
| **Headaches** | aspirin, naproxen, ibuprofen |
| **Hepatotoxicity** | tolmetin, naproxen, ibuprofen, piroxicam, fenamates |
| **History of aspirin or NSAID allergy** | "**Avoid if possible;** if deemed necessary, consider a **nonacetylated salicylate**" |
| **Hypertension** | sulindac, nonacetylated salicylate, ibuprofen, etodolac |
| **Osteoarthritis** | diclofenac, oxaprozin, indomethacin |
| **Risk for gastrointestinal toxicity** | **COX-2 inhibitors (celecoxib),** nonacetylated salicylate, **enteric-coated aspirin,** diclofenac, nabumetone, etodolac, ibuprofen, oxaprozin |
| **Risk for nephrotoxicity** | sulindac, nonacetylated salicylate, nabumetone, etodolac, diclofenac, oxaprozin |
| **Warfarin therapy** | sulindac, tolmetin, naproxen, ibuprofen, oxaprozin |

**Selection criteria** (p. 687): "The appropriate selection of an NSAID is a **clinical judgment** based on consideration of the **patient's history, including any medical conditions; the intended use of the drug; the patient's previous experience with NSAIDs; the patient's preference; and the cost.**"

- Dosage forms: "Several of these drugs are available in **sustained-release formulations.** This allows **once- or twice-daily dosing, which is known to improve patients' adherence**" (p. 687). "**Diclofenac is available as a topical gel**" (p. 687).

> **Trap (confusable pair).** **NSAIDs have a ceiling; opioids do not.** This is the chapter's own explicit contrast with Ch 10 and is the cleanest cross-chapter item available. Cue: *past the NSAID ceiling you get **more adverse effects and no more relief**; opioids "may be titrated almost indefinitely."* Note that **codeine** (Ch 10, p. 148) is the exception on the opioid side, being "the only agonist to possess a ceiling effect," and the **agonists-antagonists** have one too. So "opioids have no ceiling" is a generalization with named exceptions in the other chapter.

> **Trap (setting vs. focus).** **Table 44.1 answers "which NSAID for this patient," not "which NSAID for this diagnosis."** Half its rows are comorbidities and risks (hypertension, warfarin therapy, GI toxicity risk, nephrotoxicity risk, prior allergy), not conditions being treated. A stem giving a patient on warfarin who needs an NSAID is asking a Table 44.1 question, and the answer set is **sulindac, tolmetin, naproxen, ibuprofen, oxaprozin.**

> **Trap (plausible-but-adjacent).** **NSAIDs treat gout and are first-line for it,** despite this chapter having a whole separate antigout section. "the **NSAIDs are considered first-line therapy for most patients with gout**" (p. 691), and **acute gout** and **acute gouty arthritis** are the first two entries in Box 44.2. Reaching for colchicine or allopurinol first is the trap; see §7.

## §4 Adverse effects and the FDA black box warnings

**The scale of harm** (p. 687): "their potential for serious adverse events **has been underemphasized. More than 100,000 hospitalizations occur each year due to NSAID use, with more than 16,500 deaths reported annually.**"

**Table 44.2 NSAIDs: Adverse Effects** (p. 687), reproduced:

| Body system | Adverse effects |
|---|---|
| **Cardiovascular** | "Noncardiogenic pulmonary edema, **increased risk for myocardial infarction and stroke**" |
| **Gastrointestinal** | "Dyspepsia, heartburn, epigastric distress, nausea, vomiting, anorexia, abdominal pain, **gastrointestinal bleeding, mucosal lesions (erosions or ulcerations)**" |
| **Hematologic** | "**Altered hemostasis through effects on platelet function**" |
| **Hepatic** | "**Acute reversible hepatotoxicity**" |
| **Renal** | "**Reduction in creatinine clearance, acute tubular necrosis with renal failure**" |
| **Other** | "Skin eruption, sensitivity reactions, **tinnitus, hearing loss**" |

**The three main ones, per the key points** (p. 697): "The **three main adverse effects** of NSAIDs are **gastrointestinal intolerance, bleeding (often gastrointestinal bleeding), and renal impairment.**"

**GI toxicity** (p. 687): "Symptoms can range from mild symptoms such as heartburn to the most severe gastrointestinal complication, gastrointestinal bleeding. **Most fatalities associated with NSAID use are related to gastrointestinal bleeding.**"

- **The mechanism and its antidote:** "Many of the adverse effects of NSAIDs are secondary to their **inactivation of protective prostaglandins that help maintain the normal integrity of the stomach lining.** The drug **misoprostol (Cytotec)** has proven successful in **preventing gastric ulcers and gastrointestinal bleeding when given in conjunction with an NSAID.** Misoprostol is a **synthetic prostaglandin E1 analogue** that inhibits gastric acid secretion and also has a **cytoprotective** component" (pp. 687–688).
- **Misoprostol's other property:** "This drug also has **abortifacient properties**" (see Ch. 34).

**Renal toxicity** (p. 688): "**Renal function depends partly on prostaglandins.** Disruption of prostaglandin function by NSAIDs is sometimes strong enough to **precipitate acute or chronic renal failure,** depending on the patient's current level of renal function."

- **Who is at risk:** "patients who are **dehydrated,** those with **heart failure or liver dysfunction,** and those taking **diuretics or angiotensin-converting enzyme inhibitors.**"
- "**acute renal failure is quite common with NSAID use, especially if the patient is dehydrated**" (p. 687).

Clinical Pearl (p. 688): "**NSAIDS can cause GI bleed and renal failure. Take with food and stay well hydrated.**"

**Box 44.3 FDA-Required Warnings on All NSAIDs** (p. 687), reproduced in full:

> **Cardiovascular Risk**
> - "NSAIDs may cause an **increased risk for serious cardiovascular thrombotic events, myocardial infarction, and stroke, which can be fatal.** Patients with cardiovascular disease or risk factors for cardiovascular disease **may be at greater risk. The risk for heart attack or stroke can occur as early as the first weeks** of using an NSAID. The risk **may increase with longer use** of the NSAID and **with higher doses.**"
> - "NSAIDs are **contraindicated for the treatment of perioperative pain in the setting of coronary artery bypass graft surgery.**"
>
> **Gastrointestinal Risk**
> - "NSAIDs cause an **increased risk for serious gastrointestinal adverse events, including bleeding, ulceration, and perforation of the stomach or intestines, which can be fatal. These events can occur at any time during use and without warning symptoms. Older adult patients are at greater risk** for serious gastrointestinal events."

**The two FDA actions** (p. 687):

| Year | Action |
|---|---|
| **2006** | "the FDA began requiring a **black box warning on all of the NSAIDs**" |
| **2015** | "the FDA **strengthened the warning** of increased chance of heart attack or stroke" |

The 2015 revision added: "The risk for heart attack or stroke **can occur as early as the first weeks.** The risk **increases with longer use and a higher dose. Patients treated with NSAIDs after a first heart attack are more likely to die in the first year** compared with patients who were not treated with an NSAID. There is also an **increased risk for heart failure** with NSAID use. **Patients without heart disease or risk factors are also at increased risk** for heart attack or stroke."

**The aspirin exemption, and the aspirin conflict** (p. 688): "**All NSAIDs (except aspirin)** share a black box warning regarding an increased risk for adverse cardiovascular thrombotic events, including fatal MI and stroke. **NSAIDs may counteract the cardioprotective effects of aspirin.**"

> **Trap (confusable pair).** **Aspirin is the NSAID that protects the heart; every other NSAID threatens it.** Cue: *aspirin is **exempted** from the cardiovascular black box and is given **at the first sign of MI**; the rest carry a black box for **causing** MI and stroke, and they **counteract aspirin's** protection.* A patient on low-dose aspirin for cardioprotection who starts ibuprofen for arthritis is losing the protection, which is the highest-value clinical item in this chapter.

> **Trap (level/stage swap).** **The cardiovascular risk is not dose-and-duration-only.** The 2015 language says the risk "can occur **as early as the first weeks**" and that "patients **without heart disease or risk factors are also at increased risk.**" An option reassuring a healthy patient on a short course has contradicted both clauses.

> **Trap (priority question).** **NSAIDs are contraindicated for perioperative pain in CABG.** This is a flat contraindication printed inside the black box, not a caution. If a stem orders an NSAID for post-CABG pain, that is the order to question.

> **Trap (confusable pair).** **misoprostol vs. the other "m" drugs.** Review question 1 (p. 698) asks which drug prevents serious GI adverse effects during long-term NSAID therapy, offering **misoprostol, metoprolol, metoclopramide, and magnesium sulfate.** Only misoprostol is in this chapter, and its logic is mechanistic: NSAIDs destroy protective prostaglandins, so you **replace** one with a **synthetic prostaglandin E1 analogue.** The other three are a beta blocker, a prokinetic, and an electrolyte. Official key not in the pull; see GAPS item 8.

> **Trap (plausible-but-adjacent).** **Misoprostol is an abortifacient.** The chapter flags this in the same paragraph that recommends it. Giving it to a pregnant patient for NSAID gastroprotection is the error the sentence exists to prevent.

> **Trap (confusable pair).** **Tinnitus and hearing loss appear in Table 44.2 as a general NSAID adverse effect AND in §5 as the hallmark of chronic salicylate toxicity.** Cue: *tinnitus on any NSAID = report it; tinnitus on **aspirin** = specifically suspect **salicylism.*** The same symptom, two levels of concern.

## §5 Salicylate toxicity

"**Salicylate toxicity, usually from aspirin, is not as common as it once was.** Chronic and acute manifestations of salicylate toxicity can occur" (p. 688).

**Chronic intoxication = salicylism** (p. 688): "**Chronic salicylate intoxication is also known as salicylism** and results from either **short-term administration of high dosages or prolonged therapy with high or even low dosages.**"

| Population | Most common manifestations as given |
|---|---|
| **Adults** | "**tinnitus and hearing loss**" |
| **Children** | "**hyperventilation and central nervous system (CNS) effects such as dizziness, drowsiness, and behavioral changes**" |

- **Metabolic complications:** "**metabolic acidosis and respiratory alkalosis** often occur to varying degrees in cases of chronic salicylate intoxication. **Metabolic acidosis can also occur with acute intoxication. Hypoglycemia may also arise and can be life threatening.**"
- **Treatment:** "The treatment of chronic intoxication is **based on the manifesting symptoms.**"

**Table 44.3 Acute or Chronic Salicylate Intoxication: Signs and Symptoms** (p. 688), reproduced:

| Body system | Signs and symptoms |
|---|---|
| **Cardiovascular** | "**Increased heart rate**" |
| **Central nervous** | "**Tinnitus, hearing loss,** dimness of vision, **headache,** dizziness, mental confusion, lassitude, drowsiness" |
| **Gastrointestinal** | "**Nausea, vomiting, diarrhea**" |
| **Metabolic** | "Sweating, thirst, **hyperventilation, hypoglycemia,** or hyperglycemia" |

**Acute intoxication** (p. 688): "the signs and symptoms are **similar to those of chronic intoxication, but the effects are often more pronounced and occur more quickly.** Acute salicylate overdose usually results from the **ingestion of a single toxic dose.**"

**Severity by dose ingested** (p. 688), reproduced:

| Severity | Dose |
|---|---|
| **Little or no toxicity** | "**less than 150 mg/kg**" |
| **Mild to moderate toxicity** | "**150 to 300 mg/kg**" |
| **Severe toxicity** | "**300 to 500 mg/kg**" |
| **Life-threatening toxicity** | "**greater than 500 mg/kg**" |

- **The override:** "**However, doses lower than 150 mg/kg have resulted in fatal toxicity.**"

**The Done nomogram** (p. 688): "A serum salicylate concentration measured **6 hours or longer after ingestion** may be used in conjunction with the Done nomogram to estimate the severity of intoxication... a **graphic plot of serum salicylate level as a function of time since salicylate ingestion.** It was first published in a **1960** issue of the journal *Pediatrics* and is still used currently."

- **Its limit:** "This nomogram is intended for estimating **only the severity of acute intoxications and not the severity of chronic salicylate intoxication.**"

**Table 44.4 Acute Salicylate Intoxication: Treatment** (p. 688), reproduced:

| Severity | Treatment |
|---|---|
| **Mild** | "1. **Dosage reduction or discontinuation** of salicylates. 2. **Symptomatic and supportive** therapy" |
| **Severe** | "1. **Discontinuation** of salicylates. 2. **Intensive symptomatic and supportive** therapy. 3. **Dialysis if:** high salicylate levels, unresponsive acidosis (**pH <7.1**), impaired renal function or renal failure, pulmonary edema, persistent CNS symptoms (e.g., seizures, coma), progressive deterioration despite appropriate therapy" |

**The treatment goals** (p. 688): "**removing salicylate from the gastrointestinal tract and/or preventing its further absorption; correcting fluid, electrolyte, and acid-base disturbances; and implementing measures to enhance salicylate elimination, including hemodialysis.**"

**Nonsalicylate NSAID overdose** (p. 688): "causes effects **similar to those of salicylate overdose, but they are generally not as extensive or as dangerous.**"

- **Symptoms:** "CNS toxicities such as **drowsiness, lethargy, mental confusion, paresthesias (abnormal touch sensations), numbness, aggressive behavior, disorientation, and seizures,** and gastrointestinal toxicities such as **nausea, vomiting, and gastrointestinal bleeding.** Intense headache, dizziness, cerebral edema, cardiac arrest, and death have also been known to occur in extreme cases."
- **Treatment:** "the administration of **activated charcoal,** with supportive and symptomatic treatment initiated thereafter."
- **The key difference:** "**Unlike in the case of salicylates, hemodialysis appears to be of no value in enhancing the elimination of NSAIDs because of their high protein binding.**"

> **Trap (confusable pair).** **Salicylism (chronic) vs. acute salicylate intoxication.** They share a symptom table, so the discriminators are elsewhere. Cue: *chronic = **salicylism,** builds over time, treated **by symptoms,** and the **Done nomogram does NOT apply**; acute = **single toxic dose,** graded by **mg/kg,** and the nomogram **does** apply from **6 hours** post-ingestion.* Applying the nomogram to a chronic case is the error its own definition warns against.

> **Trap (confusable pair).** **Adult vs. pediatric presentation of chronic salicylate toxicity.** Cue: *adults ring (**tinnitus, hearing loss**); children breathe (**hyperventilation**) and change behavior.* A stem describing a drowsy, hyperventilating child on chronic aspirin is salicylism, even without tinnitus.

> **Trap (confusable pair).** **Hemodialysis works for salicylates and not for other NSAIDs.** Cue: *NSAIDs are **highly protein bound,** so dialysis cannot pull them out; salicylate toxicity has dialysis written into its severe-treatment protocol.* The same overdose question with a different drug has a different answer, which is exactly how this pair gets tested.

> **Trap (level/stage swap).** **The mg/kg thresholds have a stated exception.** *Less than 150 = little or no toxicity, **but** "doses lower than 150 mg/kg have resulted in fatal toxicity."* An option that discharges a patient on the strength of a sub-150 estimate has taken the table as absolute when the text immediately qualifies it.

> **Trap (SATA, five options mapped to categories).** **Review question 6 (p. 698)** asks for symptoms of chronic salicylate intoxication, offering **tinnitus, headache, constipation, nausea, and bradycardia.** Held against Table 44.3: **tinnitus** and **headache** are CNS entries, **nausea** is GI. **Constipation** is not listed (the GI row says **diarrhea**), and **bradycardia** is not listed (the cardiovascular row says **increased heart rate**). Both distractors are **direction reversals** of a real entry. Official key not in the pull; see GAPS item 8.

> **Trap (plausible-but-adjacent).** **Review question 2 (p. 698)** asks about **nonsalicylate** NSAID toxicity, offering constipation, nausea and vomiting, numbness, drowsiness, and urinary retention. The p. 688 list gives **drowsiness, lethargy, mental confusion, paresthesias, numbness, aggressive behavior, disorientation, seizures, nausea, vomiting, and GI bleeding.** Constipation and urinary retention are not on it; both are **opioid** adverse effects from Ch 10, imported as distractors.

## §6 Contraindications, interactions, and lab interference

**Contraindications** (p. 687): "**known drug allergy** and conditions that place the patient at **risk for bleeding,** including **vitamin K deficiency and peptic ulcer disease. Patients with documented aspirin allergy must not receive NSAIDs.**"

Clinical Pearl (p. 686): "**If a patient has a true aspirin allergy, the patient cannot take an NSAID.**"

The key points add (p. 697): "gastrointestinal tract **lesions, peptic ulcers, and bleeding disorders.**"

**Pregnancy** (p. 687): "**generally categorized as pregnancy category C** drugs for use during the **first two trimesters**... but are categorized as **pregnancy category D (not recommended) for use during the third trimester.** This is because NSAID use has been associated with **excessive maternal bleeding, decreased amniotic fluid, and neonatal toxicity** during the perinatal period."

- "These drugs also are **not recommended for nursing mothers** because they are **known to be excreted into human milk.**"

**Elective surgery** (p. 687): "Because of the potential of NSAIDs to increase bleeding, patients undergoing elective surgery **need to stop taking NSAIDs at least 1 week before surgery.**"

**Table 44.5 Salicylates and Other NSAIDs: Drug Interactions** (p. 689), reproduced:

| Interacting drug | Mechanism | Result |
|---|---|---|
| **Alcohol** | Additive effect | **Increased GI bleeding** |
| **Anticoagulants** | Platelet inhibition, hypoprothrombinemia | **Increased bleeding tendencies** |
| **Aspirin and other salicylates with other NSAIDs** | Reduction of NSAID absorption, additive GI toxicities | "**Increased GI toxicity with no therapeutic advantage**" |
| **Bisphosphonates** | Additive GI toxicities | Increased GI bleeding risk |
| **Corticosteroids and other ulcerogenic drugs** | Additive toxicities | Increased ulcerogenic effects |
| **cyclosporine** | Inhibition of renal prostaglandin synthesis | "Increased **nephrotoxic** effects of cyclosporine, **renal failure**" |
| **Diuretics and ACE inhibitors** | Inhibition of prostaglandin synthesis | "**Reduced hypotensive and diuretic effects**" |
| **lithium** | Increased lithium absorption | "**Increased lithium concentrations**" |
| **Protein-bound drugs** | Competition for binding | More pronounced drug actions |
| **Uricosurics** | Antagonism | "**Decreased uric acid excretion**" |
| **Herbals: feverfew, garlic, ginger, gingko** | Interference with platelet function | "**Increased bleeding risk**" |

**Laboratory interference** (p. 689): "salicylates can cause what are usually **minor and transient elevations in the levels of liver enzymes (ALT, AST),** but, **unlike with acetaminophen (see Chapter 10), cases of severe hepatotoxicity are rare. Hematocrit, hemoglobin level, and RBC count can drop** if any drug-induced GI bleeding occurs, and **bleeding time may be prolonged. NSAID-induced hyperkalemia or hyponatremia can also occur.**"

> **Trap (confusable pair).** **Aspirin + another NSAID is a pure-loss combination.** Table 44.5 states the result as "increased GI toxicity **with no therapeutic advantage.**" Two drugs from the same class, doubled risk, no added benefit. An option combining ibuprofen and aspirin for better pain control is wrong on the table's own wording, and it also destroys aspirin's cardioprotection (§4).

> **Trap (confusable pair).** **NSAIDs + uricosurics antagonize each other,** which matters because both appear in this chapter. Giving an NSAID for gout pain while the patient is on **probenecid** for uric acid excretion **decreases uric acid excretion.** Two drugs aimed at the same disease working against each other.

> **Trap (setting vs. focus).** **NSAIDs blunt antihypertensives and diuretics.** "Reduced hypotensive and diuretic effects." A stem in which a controlled hypertensive patient's blood pressure rises after starting an NSAID is a drug-interaction item, not a treatment-failure item. Note the second layer: **hypertension** also appears in Table 44.1 as a comorbidity with recommended NSAIDs (sulindac, nonacetylated salicylate, ibuprofen, etodolac).

> **Trap (plausible-but-adjacent).** **Salicylates raise ALT and AST, but severe hepatotoxicity is rare** — the chapter draws the contrast with **acetaminophen** in Ch 10 explicitly. Cue: *acetaminophen = **hepatotoxicity is the defining danger**; salicylates = enzyme bumps that are "minor and transient."* An option treating a mild transaminase rise on aspirin as impending liver failure has imported Ch 10's drug.

> **Trap (priority question).** **Stop NSAIDs a week before elective surgery.** Review question 8 (p. 698) puts this to a patient scheduled for diagnostic laparoscopy in 2 weeks who wants to continue Motrin, and one distractor offers switching to **aspirin** — which is worse, because aspirin's COX-1 inhibition is **irreversible.** The "it's just minor surgery" framing in the stem is the bait.

## §7 Antigout drugs

**The pathophysiology** (p. 691): "Gout is caused by the **overproduction of uric acid, decreased uric acid excretion, or both.** This overproduction and/or decreased excretion often can result in **hyperuricemia** (too much uric acid in the blood). Persons with gout **either overproduce or underexcrete uric acid, which is the end product of purine metabolism.**"

- **How it hurts:** "deposits of uric acid crystals collect in tissues and joints. This causes an inflammatory response and **extreme pain because these crystals are like small needles that jab and stick into sensitive tissues and joints.**"
- **Purines:** "part of the normal dietary intake and are used to make the essential structural units of **DNA and RNA.** During purine metabolism, they are converted from **hypoxanthine to xanthine and eventually to uric acid.**"
- **Fig. 44.2 Uric acid production** (p. 692), reconstructed: **Hypoxanthine → [xanthine oxidase] → Xanthine → [xanthine oxidase] → Uric acid.** **Allopurinol** and its metabolite **oxypurinol** are shown blocking ("Stop") **both** xanthine oxidase steps.
- **The goals of treatment** (p. 691): "to **decrease the symptoms of an acute attack** and to **prevent recurrent attacks.**"

**Articular vs. abarticular** (p. 691): "**Not all gouty deposits occur within joints. Gouty arthritis** is the condition in which one or more joints are inflamed because [of] the collection of gouty deposits inside the joint anatomy. This is also called **articular gout,** whereas gout that occurs in **tissues outside of the joints is called abarticular gout.**"

**First-line therapy is NSAIDs, not antigout drugs** (p. 691): "**Although specific antigout drugs are available, the NSAIDs (described earlier) are considered first-line therapy for most patients with gout.** The specific antigout drugs — **allopurinol, febuxostat, colchicine, probenecid, lesinurad, and sulfinpyrazone** — are targeted at the **underlying defect in uric acid metabolism.**"

### The six antigout drugs, by mechanism

| Drug | Class / mechanism | Which defect it fixes |
|---|---|---|
| **allopurinol (Zyloprim)** | "**inhibits the enzyme xanthine oxidase,** which thereby **prevents uric acid production**" | **overproduction** |
| **febuxostat (Uloric)** | "a **nonpurine selective inhibitor of xanthine oxidase**... **more selective** for xanthine oxidase than is allopurinol" | **overproduction** |
| **probenecid** | "**inhibits the reabsorption of uric acid in the kidney** and thus **increases the excretion** of uric acid." A **uricosuric** | **underexcretion** |
| **lesinurad (Zurampic)** | "**uric acid transporter (URAT1) inhibitors**... inhibits the transporter proteins involved in **renal uric acid reabsorption**" | **underexcretion** |
| **colchicine** | "**reducing the inflammatory response to the deposits of urate crystals**"; inhibits "**metabolism, mobility, and chemotaxis of polymorphonuclear leukocytes**" | **neither** — it treats the inflammation, not the uric acid |
| **sulfinpyrazone** | named in the list only; no profile given | (uricosuric) |

### allopurinol (Zyloprim) (p. 691)

- **Indication:** "for patients whose gout is caused by the **excess production of uric acid (hyperuricemia).**"
- **Second use:** "also used to **prevent acute tumor lysis syndrome** (see Ch. 45)." The key points generalize it: antigout drugs "are often given to patients to avoid **goutlike syndromes and pain during cancer chemotherapy that causes cell death**" (p. 697).
- **Serious adverse effects:** "**agranulocytosis, aplastic anemia,** and serious and potentially fatal skin conditions such as **exfoliative dermatitis, Stevens-Johnson syndrome, and toxic epidermal necrolysis.**"
- **Interactions:** "**Azathioprine and mercaptopurine** both significantly interact with allopurinol, and, as a result, **their dosages may have to be adjusted.**"
- **Dosing:** "oral use only. The usual recommended adult dosage is **200 to 600 mg/day,** and the **maximum dosage is 800 mg/day.**" Pregnancy category **C**.

### febuxostat (Uloric) (p. 693)

- "the **first new drug approved for the treatment of gout since the 1960s.**"
- **The cardiac problem:** "Clinical trials suggest that it may pose a **greater risk for cardiovascular events than allopurinol** does... In **2017, the FDA issued a warning regarding an increased risk for cardiac death when compared with allopurinol.**"
- "**not to be given along with theophylline, azathioprine, or mercaptopurine.**"
- Dosing: "**40 to 80 mg/day,** with a maximum dose of **120 mg/day.**"

### colchicine (p. 693)

- "the **oldest available therapy for acute gout** and is considered **second-line therapy, after the NSAIDs.**"
- **Mechanism:** "**not clearly defined,** but it is thought to inhibit the metabolism, mobility, and **chemotaxis** of polymorphonuclear leukocytes."
- **Why it is short-term:** "a **powerful inhibitor of cell mitosis and can cause short-term leukopenia.** For this reason, it is generally used for the **short-term treatment of acute attacks of gout.**"
- **Prophylactic dosing:** "**0.6 mg once or twice a day.**"
- **Acute dosing:** "an initial dose of **0.6 to 1.2 mg, followed by 0.6 mg/hr until pain is relieved, the patient develops severe nausea and diarrhea, or a total of 6 mg has been administered.** Some practitioners choose to limit the cumulative dose to **3 mg.**"
- **The re-treatment interval:** "**3 days must pass before a second course of therapy is initiated.**"
- **Severe adverse effects:** "**bleeding into the gastrointestinal or urinary tracts,** and the drug needs to be **stopped** if such effects appear."
- **"There is no specific antidote for colchicine poisoning."**
- **Contraindications:** hypersensitivity, and "severe **renal, gastrointestinal, hepatic, or cardiac** disorders, and **blood dyscrasias.**"
- **Renal dosing,** with its own Clinical Pearl: "**Colchicine dose must be adjusted for decreased renal function.**" Pregnancy category **D**.
- **Regulatory history:** injectable form withdrawn in **2008** "because of the potential for life-threatening adverse effects"; in **2010** the FDA "required the withdrawal of all '**unapproved**' colchicine products that had been used for decades." Three FDA-approved products remain: "**Colcrys, Gloperba, and Mitigare.**"

### probenecid (p. 693)

- **Mechanism, in detail:** "works by **binding to the special transporter protein in the proximal convoluted renal tubule** that takes uric acid from the urine and places it back into the blood. **The probenecid is then reabsorbed into the bloodstream while the uric acid remains in the urine and is excreted.**"
- **The antibiotic interaction, which is the cross-chapter thread:** "it also has the ability to **delay the renal excretion of penicillin, which increases the serum levels of penicillin and prolongs its effect** (see Chapter 38)."
- **Dosing:** "**250 mg twice daily with food, milk, or antacids for 1 week, followed by 500 mg twice daily thereafter.**"
- **Contraindications:** "**peptic ulcer disease and blood dyscrasias. Probenecid is ineffective and is not to be used in patients with renal impairment.**" Pregnancy category **B**.

### lesinurad (Zurampic) (p. 693)

- **Must be combined:** "**Lesinurad must be given in combination with a xanthine oxidase inhibitor**," and its **black box warning** covers both "the risk for **acute renal failure**" and "that it **must be taken in conjunction with a xanthine oxidase inhibitor.**"
- Dosing: "**200 mg/day,** and it should be **taken with food.** Patients are advised to **stay well hydrated by taking in at least 2 L of fluid per day.**"
- Adverse effects: "**headache, influenza, and increased serum creatinine.**"
- "**All forms of hormonal contraceptives may be less effective during lesinurad therapy.**"

> **Trap (confusable pair).** **Overproduction vs. underexcretion, and which drug fixes which.** This is the organizing item of the whole antigout section. Cue: ***allo**purinol and febuxostat **block production** (xanthine oxidase, upstream); **probenecid** and lesinurad **push excretion** (renal tubule, downstream).* Probenecid "**is ineffective and is not to be used in patients with renal impairment**," which follows directly from its site of action — a drug that works in the kidney needs a working kidney.

> **Trap (confusable pair).** **Colchicine treats the attack; allopurinol and probenecid treat the uric acid.** Cue: *colchicine touches **inflammation only** and does nothing to uric acid levels.* Review question 3 (p. 698) asks how antigout drugs work and offers "**decreasing serum uric acid levels**" among options about oxygen, leukocytes, and rheumatoid factors — a class-level question that colchicine, strictly, does not satisfy.

> **Trap (level/stage swap).** **NSAIDs are first-line for gout; colchicine is second-line.** Stated explicitly at p. 691 and p. 693. An option starting colchicine for a first acute attack has skipped the first line, and the chapter names the NSAIDs for gout in Table 44.1: **indomethacin, naproxen, sulindac.**

> **Trap (confusable pair).** **allopurinol vs. febuxostat.** Same mechanism, same target enzyme. Cue: *febuxostat is **newer, more selective,** and carries a **2017 FDA warning for increased cardiac death compared with allopurinol.*** If a stem weighs the two, the cardiac risk is the discriminator.

> **Trap (priority question).** **Lesinurad cannot be given alone.** Its black box says so. An order for lesinurad monotherapy is incomplete by the drug's own labeling, and the risk named is **acute renal failure.**

> **Trap (setting vs. focus).** **Probenecid appears in three chapters for two different reasons.** Here it is a **uricosuric for gout**; in Ch 38 and Ch 39 it appears as the drug that **reduces renal excretion of penicillins, cephalosporins, quinolones, and nitrofurantoin,** raising their levels. Same renal-transporter mechanism, opposite framing: in gout it is the therapy, in antibiotics it is the interaction.

> **Trap (confusable pair).** **Colchicine's three dose numbers.** Cue: ***0.6 mg once or twice daily = prophylaxis; 0.6 to 1.2 mg then 0.6 mg/hr = acute attack; stop at 6 mg total (or 3 mg by some practitioners), or sooner if severe nausea and diarrhea appear.*** The GI symptoms are a **stopping endpoint**, not merely a side effect to manage. And **3 days** must elapse before a second course.

## §8 Aspirin, Reye's syndrome, and the individual NSAID profiles

### aspirin (p. 690)

- "known chemically as **ASA.** It is the **prototype salicylate and NSAID** and is the **most widely used drug in the world.**"
- **Prophylactic dosing:** "A daily aspirin tablet (**81 mg or 325 mg**) is now routinely recommended as prophylactic therapy for adults who have **strong risk factors for developing coronary artery disease or stroke.** It is also effective after an MI."
- **Which strength:** "The **81-mg strength and the 325-mg strength appear to be equally beneficial** for the prevention of thrombotic events. For this reason, the **lower strength is often chosen for patients who have any elevated risk for bleeding,** such as those with **stroke history or history of peptic ulcer disease and those taking the anticoagulant warfarin.**"
- **Other uses:** "headache, neuralgia, myalgia, and arthralgia... **arthritis, pleurisy, and pericarditis.** Patients with **systemic lupus erythematosus** may also benefit... because of its **antirheumatic** effects. Aspirin is also used for its **antipyretic** action."
- **Other salicylates** (p. 689): "**diflunisal (Dolobid), choline magnesium trisalicylate (Trilisate), and salsalate (Salsitab)**" require a prescription. Dosage forms include "a topical cream (**Aspercreme**), rectal suppositories, and oral liquids," combination products (**Excedrin** = aspirin/acetaminophen/caffeine; **Bufferin** = aspirin/antacid), and **enteric-coated aspirin (Ecotrin)**, "designed to protect the stomach mucosa by **dissolving in the duodenum.**"

**Reye's syndrome** (p. 690, and the lifespan box p. 692): "**Aspirin and other salicylates all have one very specific contraindication. This drug class is contraindicated in children with flulike symptoms,** because the use of these drugs has been **strongly associated with Reye's syndrome.**"

- **What it is:** "an **acute and potentially life-threatening condition involving progressive neurologic deficits that can lead to coma and may also involve liver damage.**" It "causes **swelling of the liver and brain.**"
- **Trigger:** "thought to be triggered by **viral illnesses such as influenza,** as well as by **salicylate therapy itself in the presence of a viral illness.**" More commonly in "children and teenagers who are **recovering from a viral infection, such as chickenpox or flu.**"
- **Timing:** "Signs and symptoms typically develop **within 12 hours to 3 weeks after recovery** from a viral illness, upper respiratory tract infection or gastroenteritis."
- **Incidence:** "currently a **rare diagnosis with fewer than two cases annually since 1994.**"
- **Prognosis:** "Survivors of this condition **may or may not suffer permanent neurologic damage.**"
- **Diagnostics:** "emergency **aspartate aminotransferase (AST), alanine aminotransferase (ALT), and serum ammonia** levels need to be drawn. **Hypoglycemia and hepatomegaly** may be present. **Elevated liver enzymes may occur in the absence of jaundice.**"
- **When to suspect it:** "**unexpected vomiting after any viral illness**... or signs of disturbed brain function characterized by **lethargy, stupor, agitated delirium, coma, screaming, and rigidity.**"

**The four stages** (p. 692), reproduced:

| Stage | Symptoms as given |
|---|---|
| **1** | "**Quiet, lethargic, sleepy; persistent, copious vomiting;** laboratory values show liver dysfunction" |
| **2** | "**Stupor, disorientation, combative;** deep lethargy; **hyperventilation; hyperreflexia**" |
| **3** | "**Obtunded;** seizures may be present; **decorticate posturing** with intact pupillary light reflexes" |
| **4** | "**Pupil dilation with minimal light reflexes; seizures; deepening coma; decerebrate posturing**" |

**The prevention teaching** (p. 692): "aspirin or medications that contain aspirin **must not be given to children or teenagers to treat viral illnesses or fever.** Other names for aspirin include **acetylsalicylic acid, acetylsalicylate, salicylic acid, and salicylate.** Drugs that may be used instead of aspirin... include **acetaminophen and ibuprofen. Avoid aspirin for several weeks after a child/teenager has received a varicella vaccine.**"

- **Hidden aspirin:** "aspirin is contained in many over-the-counter drugs. Some examples include **Alka-Seltzer, some Excedrin products, and Pepto-Bismol. Substances with oil of wintergreen may also contain salicylates.**"

### indomethacin (Indocin) (p. 690)

- "analgesic, antiinflammatory, antirheumatic, and antipyretic properties." Used for "**rheumatoid arthritis, osteoarthritis, acute bursitis or tendonitis, ankylosing spondylitis, and acute gouty arthritis.**"
- **Oral and rectal**; an injectable form "is used **intravenously to promote closure of patent ductus arteriosus,** a heart defect that sometimes occurs in premature infants. It is also used for the treatment of **preterm labor** (see Ch. 34)."

### ketorolac (Toradol) (p. 690)

- "somewhat unique in that, although it does have some antiinflammatory activity, it is used **primarily for its powerful analgesic effects. Its analgesic effects are comparable to those of narcotic drugs such as morphine.**"
- **Why that matters:** "can make it a desirable choice for **opiate-addicted patients who have acute pain-control needs, because ketorolac lacks the addictive properties of the opioids.**"
- **Indications:** "moderate to severe acute pain such as that resulting from **orthopedic injuries or surgery.**" Oral, injection, and ophthalmic (Ch. 57). Prescription only.
- **The 5-day rule,** with its own Clinical Pearl: "indicated for **short-term use (up to 5 days)**... **not indicated for treatment of minor pain or chronic pain**... **because of its potential adverse effects on the kidney and gastrointestinal tract.**" Clinical Pearl: "**Ketorolac can be used for only 5 days.**"
- **Adverse effects:** "**renal impairment, edema, gastrointestinal pain, dyspepsia, and nausea.**"
- Dosage table note: the **PO form "is recommended only when transitioning from injectable form to oral form.**"

### ibuprofen and the propionic acid derivatives (p. 690)

- "**Ibuprofen (Motrin, Advil) is the prototype NSAID in the propionic acid category,** which also includes **fenoprofen, flurbiprofen, ketoprofen, naproxen, and oxaprozin.**"
- "the **most commonly used** of the propionic acid drugs because of the numerous indications for its use and because of its **relatively safe adverse effect profile.**"
- Uses: "rheumatoid arthritis, osteoarthritis, **primary dysmenorrhea, gout,** dental pain, and musculoskeletal disorders; in addition... **antipyretic** actions."
- **Naproxen** is "the **second most commonly used NSAID,** with a reportedly **somewhat better adverse effect profile than ibuprofen,** as well as **fewer drug interactions with angiotensin-converting enzyme inhibitors.**"
- "In **2011, an injectable form of ibuprofen** became available."

### celecoxib (Celebrex) (p. 691)

- "the **first COX-2 inhibitor and is the only one remaining on the market.**"
- Indications: "**osteoarthritis, rheumatoid arthritis, acute pain symptoms, ankylosing spondylitis, and primary dysmenorrhea.** It is available **only for oral use.**"
- **The withdrawn siblings:** "Originally there were **three** COX-2 inhibitors; however, the use of **rofecoxib (Vioxx) and valdecoxib (Bextra)** was found to be associated with an **increased risk for adverse cardiovascular events, including MI, stroke, and death. They were removed from the US market.**"
- **Celecoxib's own risk:** "There is evidence that celecoxib **may pose a risk for cardiovascular events similar to that associated with rofecoxib and valdecoxib.** However, there is **inconsistency in the literature**... Celecoxib currently remains on the US market, although its use is being **monitored more closely by the FDA.**"
- Adverse effects: "headache, sinus irritation, diarrhea, fatigue, dizziness, **lower extremity edema, and hypertension. COX-2 inhibitors have little effect on platelet function.**"
- **The sulfa link, which is the Ch 38 cross-reference:** "**Celecoxib and sulfa antibiotics share a similar structure; therefore celecoxib should not be used in patients with known sulfa allergy.**"

### Enolic acid derivatives (p. 691)

- "**piroxicam, meloxicam, and nabumetone. Piroxicam and meloxicam are very potent drugs** commonly used in mild to moderate **osteoarthritis, rheumatoid arthritis, and gouty arthritis. Meloxicam is available in oral and injection** dosage formulations, whereas **piroxicam and nabumetone are available only orally.**"
- **Nabumetone (Relafen)** "is **better tolerated** than some of the others in terms of gastrointestinal adverse effects. It is **relatively nonacidic** compared with most of the other NSAIDs, which may account for its improved gastrointestinal tolerance. Currently it is indicated **only for the treatment of osteoarthritis and rheumatoid arthritis.**"

> **Trap (confusable pair).** **Reye's syndrome: which drug causes it and which drugs replace it.** Cue: ***aspirin (and any salicylate) is contraindicated in children and teenagers with viral illness; acetaminophen and ibuprofen are the substitutes.*** Review question 5 (p. 698) puts this to a 5-year-old with chickenpox, and two distractors offer aspirin either outright or as a fallback "if these do not work." A fourth offers letting the fever break untreated. Compare Ch 10 (p. 151), which frames the same fact from the acetaminophen side.

> **Trap (SATA, five options mapped to categories).** **Aspirin hides in products that do not say "aspirin."** The lifespan box names **Alka-Seltzer, some Excedrin products, Pepto-Bismol,** and **oil of wintergreen,** plus four alternate chemical names (**acetylsalicylic acid, acetylsalicylate, salicylic acid, salicylate**). This is the same trade-name trap Ch 38 builds around **Zosyn and Unasyn** not ending in "cillin."

> **Trap (level/stage swap).** **Reye's staging runs lethargy → stupor → obtunded → coma, and the posturing changes at stage 3 to 4.** Cue: ***decorticate** (stage 3, pupils still reactive) → **decerebrate** (stage 4, pupils dilated with minimal reflexes).* Hyperventilation appears at **stage 2**, which lines up with the pediatric presentation of salicylism in §5.

> **Trap (confusable pair).** **ketorolac's 5-day limit vs. every other NSAID.** Cue: *ketorolac = **opioid-strength analgesia, 5 days maximum, renal and GI toxicity is why.*** It is also the NSAID chosen **for opioid-addicted patients** needing acute pain control, which is a direct answer to a problem Ch 10 raises but does not solve.

> **Trap (confusable pair).** **celecoxib and sulfa allergy.** Ch 38 (p. 595) lists the drugs that are "relatively safe" in sulfonamide allergy (sulfonylureas, thiazides, loop diuretics, carbonic anhydrase inhibitors) and names **celecoxib** as the one that "**should not be used.**" This chapter gives the reason: **shared structure with sulfa antibiotics.** Two chapters, one fact, and the exam can ask it from either side.

> **Trap (plausible-but-adjacent).** **indomethacin's injectable form is not for pain.** It is for **closing a patent ductus arteriosus** in premature infants and for **preterm labor.** Same drug, entirely different therapeutic category, and it follows from prostaglandin inhibition rather than from analgesia.

> **Trap (confusable pair).** **COX-2 inhibitors have little effect on platelet function** — which is a benefit for bleeding risk and a **liability** if the patient needed antiplatelet action. Celecoxib is not a substitute for aspirin in a patient with cardiac risk, and it may add cardiovascular risk of its own.

## §9 Nursing process

**Assessment before any drug in this chapter** (p. 693): "assess **drug allergies, contraindications, cautions, and drug interactions**"; a "thorough **head-to-toe physical assessment and vital signs**"; a medication history including "**prescription, over-the-counter, herbal, and/or supplements.**"

- **Labs, if long-term use is indicated:** "**RBC count, hemoglobin level, hematocrit, WBC count, platelet count, BUN level, and liver enzyme levels such as ALP, AST, and LDH.**"
- **The exception:** "If NSAIDs are used **short-term** for other conditions (e.g., fever, acute pain), **laboratory studies are usually not indicated** because these drugs are available over the counter and often self-administered."

**Pain and joint assessment** (p. 694): "assess and document the **duration, onset, location, and type of inflammation and/or pain**... as well as any **precipitating, exacerbating, or relieving factors.** Note any interference with the patient's ability to perform **activities of daily living (ADLs). Inspect all joints,** with attention to **deformities, immobility or limitations in mobility, overlying skin condition, and presence of any heat or swelling** over the joint."

**Age as a critical factor** (p. 694): "**aspirin and many other NSAIDs... are not to be used in children and teenagers because of the increased risk for Reye's syndrome.** These drugs are to be used **very cautiously in older adult patients because of their decreased liver and renal functioning.**"

**Antigout assessment** (p. 695): "perform a thorough assessment of **hydration status and baseline serum uric acid levels.**"

**Administration and teaching** (pp. 695–697):

| Point | As given |
|---|---|
| **With food** | "Most oral NSAIDs are **better tolerated if taken with food** to minimize gastrointestinal upset" (key points, p. 697); "take NSAIDs with **food, milk, or antacids**" |
| **Do not crush** | "these drugs, if in **sustained-release or enteric-coated** dosage forms, are **not to be crushed or chewed**" |
| **Fluids** | "increase fluid intake to **3 L/day, unless contraindicated**" (gout) |
| **Alcohol and caffeine** | "must be avoided because these **will increase uric acid levels**" (gout) |
| **Onset of full effect** | "the **full antiinflammatory effect of the drug may not be apparent immediately**"; "may take **7 days for ibuprofen** or **30 to 60 minutes for its analgesic effects**" |
| **Arthritis timeline** | "When NSAIDs are used to decrease joint inflammation in arthritis patients, **full therapeutic effects may not be experienced for 1 week or longer**" (key points) |
| **What to report** | "**ringing in the ears,** persistent gastrointestinal problems, **black or tarry stools,** bleeding around the gums, **petechiae** (very small red-brown spots), **ecchymosis** (easy bruising), or **purpura** (large red spots)"; "**unusual bleeding, blood in the stool or vomitus**" |
| **Salicylate toxicity signs** | "**tinnitus, hearing loss, increased heart rate, dizziness**" |
| **Masking** | these drugs "**may mask symptoms**" of other conditions |
| **Celecoxib** | "take **only as ordered** and, as with other NSAIDs, **avoid alcohol, aspirin, salicylates, and OTC** [NSAIDs]" |

**Therapeutic response** (p. 696): "**decrease in acute pain; decrease in swelling, pain, stiffness, and tenderness of a joint or muscle area.**"

**Glucosamine and chondroitin** (p. 694): "**Glucosamine is chemically derived from glucose.**" The box notes that with these supplements "**there are no reports of any type of disease transmission from cows to humans**" and lists "**no specific contraindications**," with avoidance advised during pregnancy.

> **Trap (confusable pair).** **Analgesic effect vs. antiinflammatory effect have different timelines.** Cue: *ibuprofen relieves **pain in 30 to 60 minutes** but takes **up to 7 days** for full **antiinflammatory** effect; arthritis patients may need **1 week or longer.*** A patient who calls after a week saying "it isn't working" — as the chapter's own case study does — may be on schedule, not failing therapy. That case study is built on exactly this.

> **Trap (priority question).** **Black or tarry stools on an NSAID is GI bleeding.** It sits in the teaching list alongside gum bleeding, petechiae, ecchymosis, and purpura. Given that "most fatalities associated with NSAID use are related to gastrointestinal bleeding" and that GI events "**can occur at any time during use and without warning symptoms,**" this is the report-immediately finding.

> **Trap (confusable pair).** **Fluids are pushed for two different reasons in this chapter.** Cue: *NSAIDs → hydration protects the **kidney** (renal failure "especially if the patient is dehydrated"); antigout → **3 L/day** helps clear **uric acid.*** Same instruction, different rationale, and a stem may ask for the rationale rather than the action. Compare Ch 38, where **sulfonamides** get 2000 to 3000 mL/day to prevent **crystalluria** — a third reason for the same instruction.

> **Trap (plausible-but-adjacent).** **Alcohol and caffeine raise uric acid.** The gout teaching is not only about the drug. An option that addresses only medication adherence in a patient with recurrent attacks misses the dietary half the chapter names.

> **Trap (setting vs. focus).** **Critical thinking exercise 1 (p. 697)** has a 68-year-old on **aspirin 81 mg** after an MI asking whether it will also help his arthritis. The chapter's own numbers answer it: **81 mg is a thromboprevention dose**; the arthritis dose is "**3 g/day divided every 4–6 hr**" (dosage table, p. 689). Same drug, two indications, roughly a 37-fold dose difference. Official answers on Evolve; see GAPS item 8.

> **Trap (level/stage swap).** **Aspirin has four different dose ranges for four different jobs,** and they span two orders of magnitude. > **Trap (plausible-but-adjacent).** **The ibuprofen PK row is the timeline trap in numeric form.** Duration is printed as "**4–6 hr (analgesic)**" and "**7 days (antiinflammatory)**" in the same cell. Two effects, two timescales, one drug.

> **Trap (confusable pair).** **allopurinol's onset is 1 to 2 weeks; colchicine's is 12 hours.** Neither is a rescue drug for an attack in progress in the way an NSAID is. An option starting allopurinol to relieve an acute attack has chosen a drug that will not act for a fortnight.

> **Trap (level/stage swap).** **Ketorolac's IV/IM maximum is age-split: 120 mg/day under 65, 60 mg/day at 65 or older** — half the dose. Together with the 5-day cap and the renal warnings, ketorolac carries more numeric constraints than any other drug in the chapter.
