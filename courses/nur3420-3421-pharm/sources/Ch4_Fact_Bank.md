> **Trap (confusable pair).** **Blinded vs. double-blind.** Cue: ***blinded = the subject alone is unaware; double-blind = BOTH the investigator and the subject are unaware.*** The purpose differs accordingly: single-blind minimizes bias "on the part of research subjects"; double-blind minimizes it "on the part of **both**." Both definitions turn on the word **bias**, which is why the chapter gives bias its own key term.

> **Trap (confusable pair).** **Negligence vs. malpractice.** Cue: ***malpractice is negligence committed by a professional*** — the definition says "a **special type of negligence**" by someone "with **specialized education and training.**" Every malpractice case is negligence; not every negligence case is malpractice. A stem describing a layperson's failure to act prudently is negligence only.

> **Trap (confusable pair).** **Drug polymorphism vs. pharmacogenomics vs. ethnopharmacology.** All three concern why patients respond differently, and the chapter defines all three within one box. Cue: ***polymorphism = age, sex, size, body composition*** (the key term's own list, which notably does **not** include genetics); ***pharmacogenomics = genetics specifically***; ***ethnopharmacology = the effect of ethnicity across all four ADME phases, plus genetic variation.*** Note the internal tension: the body text at p. 48 broadens polymorphism to include "environmental factors (e.g., diet and nutritional status), cultural factors, and genetic (inherited) factors," which the key-term definition does not. See GAPS item 5.

> **Trap (confusable pair).** **Legend drugs vs. over-the-counter drugs.** Cue: ***legend = prescription*** (from the required legend on the label), ***OTC = nonprescription.*** The word "legend" is the trap: it means the printed caution, not "legendary" or "listed." Its origin is the Durham-Humphrey Amendment (§4).

> **Trap (plausible-but-adjacent).** **"Narcotic" is a legal term with two different current uses.** Cue: *in **clinical** settings it means any medically administered controlled substance; in **legal** settings it means any illicit or street drug.* Ch 10 says the same thing from the pharmacology side and adds that the term "**is falling out of use in favor of opioid.**" Both chapters agree that the pharmacologically correct word is **opioid**.

## §1 The nursing-relevant frame

The chapter's own summary of why this material sits in a pharmacology text (key points, pp. 57–58):

- "Drug therapy and subsequent patient responses **may be affected by racial and ethnic variations in levels of specific enzymes and metabolic pathways of drugs.**"
- "Various pieces of **federal legislation, as well as state law, state practice acts, and institutional policies,** have been established to help ensure the safety and efficacy of drug therapy."
- "The **Controlled Substance Act of 1970** provides nurses and other health care providers with information on drugs that cause **little to no dependence versus those associated with a high level of abuse and dependency.**"
- "Always **obtain informed consent** as needed, with complete understanding of your role and responsibilities **as patient advocate.**"
- "**Placebo use remains controversial,** and if a placebo is ordered, **question the prescriber about the specific cause for its use.**"
- "**HIPAA guidelines** have increased awareness concerning **patient confidentiality and privacy.**"

## §2 Ethnopharmacology and variation in drug response

**Ethnopharmacology's scope and its stated limitation** (p. 47): the field "provides an expanding body of knowledge for understanding the specific impact of cultural factors on patient drug response. **It is hampered by the lack of clarity in terms such as race, ethnicity, and culture.** For example, although some researchers have used the term **Hispanic** to encompass geographic groups as diverse as **Puerto Ricans, Mexicans, and Peruvians,** others have used it to denote a **specific racial group.**"

**Acetylator status** (p. 48), which is the chapter's clearest mechanistic example: patients "of [certain] descent are **more rapid acetylators and metabolize drugs more quickly, which predisposes the patient to subtherapeutic drug concentrations and may require higher drug dosages.**"

Clinical Pearl (p. 48): "**Ethnicity can have an impact on drug metabolism.**"

**Cultural Implications box: Examples of Varying Drug Responses in Different Racial or Ethnic Groups** (p. 49), reproduced as printed. The box carries its own footnote: "**Note: The comparison group for all responses is Whites.**"

| Group as labeled | Drug classification | Response as given |
|---|---|---|
| **African Americans** | Antihypertensive drugs | "Blacks respond... **better to diuretics** than to beta blockers and angiotensin-converting enzyme inhibitors; **less effectively to beta blockers**; **best to calcium channel blockers, especially diltiazem**; **less effectively to single-drug therapy**" |
| **Asians and Hispanics** | Antipsychotic and antianxiety drugs | "Asians... **need lower dosages of certain drugs such as haloperidol.** Asians and Hispanics... **respond better to lower dosages of antidepressants.** Chinese... **require lower dosages of antipsychotics.** Japanese... **require lower dosages of antimanic drugs**" |

The body text adds two more (pp. 48–49): "**Japanese and Taiwanese patients may require lower dosages of lithium**," and "for the treatment of hypertension, **thiazide diuretics appear to be more effective in Blacks than in Whites.**"

**Cultural Implications box: A Brief Review of Common Practices Among Selected Cultural Groups** (p. 48). This is a five-column table with the axes **Cultural Group / Common Health Beliefs and Alternative Healers / Verbal and Nonverbal Communication, Touch, Time / Family / Biological Variations.** Its cells are group-level generalizations and are **not transcribed here as study facts**; see the curation note and GAPS item 3. The two things worth carrying from it are:

- **The axes themselves,** because they tell you what a cultural assessment covers.
- **The "Biological Variations" column,** which names conditions with genuine pharmacologic weight and which recur elsewhere in the course: **lactose intolerance, sickle cell anemia, thalassemia,** and **"many drug interactions."**

**The chapter's own corrective, quoted because it is the most important sentence in the section** (p. 49): "It is important to remember that **these beliefs vary from patient to patient; therefore consult with the patient rather than assume that the patient holds certain beliefs because he or she belongs to a certain ethnic group.**"

**The cultural assessment questions** (p. 49), reproduced as the checklist it is. Before any medication is administered, complete a thorough cultural assessment including:

- "Languages spoken, written, and understood; **need for an interpreter**"
- "Health beliefs and practices"
- "Past uses of medicine"
- "Use of **herbal treatments, folk remedies, home remedies, or supplements**"
- "Use of **over-the-counter drugs**"
- "Usual responses to illness"
- "Responsiveness to medical treatment"
- "**Religious practices and beliefs** (e.g., many Christian Scientists believe in taking no medications at all)"
- "Support from the patient's cultural community"
- "**Dietary habits**"

**Barriers to care named** (p. 49): "**language, poverty, access, pride, and beliefs regarding medical practices.**"

**Adherence** (p. 47): "Medication response depends greatly on the level of the patient's **adherence** with the therapy regimen. Yet adherence may vary depending on the patient's **cultural beliefs, experiences with medications, personal expectations, family expectations and influence, and level of education.**"

- **The alternative-therapy warning:** "some patients use alternative therapies, such as **herbal and homeopathic remedies, which can inhibit or accelerate drug metabolism** and therefore alter a drug's response."
- **Diet:** "a **diet high in fat** has been documented to **increase the absorption of some drugs.**"

**Communication and drug teaching** (pp. 49–50), a concrete instruction that is unusually specific: "some languages, such as the Chinese language, **do not have numerous verb tenses** as compared to the English language. Therefore **very precise instructions** must be included in patient education... **Avoiding the use of contractions such as can't, won't, and don't is important with patients from other countries to prevent confusion. Instead, use of cannot, will not, and do not is recommended.**"

**Box 4.3 Cultural Assessment Tools** (p. 56): "**Madeline Leininger's Sunrise Model** focuses on **seven major areas** of cultural assessment, including **educational, economic, familial and social, political, technologic, religious and philosophic, and cultural values, beliefs, and practices.**" Other tools named: "**Andrews and Bowls, 1999; Friedman, Bowden, and Jones, 2003; Giger and [Davidhizar]**."

> **Trap (confusable pair).** **Fast vs. slow acetylator, and which one needs a higher dose.** Cue: ***fast/rapid acetylator = metabolizes quickly = subtherapeutic levels = may need HIGHER doses; slow acetylator = drug accumulates = toxicity risk.*** This is the same pair Ch 2 lists in its Table 2.6 (fast acetylator → increased metabolism; slow acetylator → decreased) and that Ch 38 names as one of its two genetic host factors alongside **G6PD deficiency**. Three chapters, one mechanism.

> **Trap (level/stage swap).** **The comparison group in the drug-response box is Whites, and every entry is relative to it.** The footnote says so explicitly. An option stating an absolute dose requirement rather than a relative one has dropped the comparator that makes the claim meaningful.

> **Trap (confusable pair).** **Antihypertensive response is the row with the most internal structure.** Cue: *better to **diuretics**, best to **calcium channel blockers (especially diltiazem)**, less effectively to **beta blockers and ACE inhibitors**, and less effectively to **single-drug therapy.*** Four claims, and a distractor need only swap one drug class. The body text reinforces the diuretic half separately with thiazides.

> **Trap (priority question).** **The chapter's instruction is to assess, not to assume.** Review question 2 (p. 58) asks which cultural issue may influence care for an older adult Asian patient and offers, among options about x-rays, hospital diets, and hospitalization as socialization, one about "**the use of herbal products may be an important practice for this patient.**" The chapter's assessment checklist names "use of herbal treatments, folk remedies, home remedies, or supplements" as a required question, and the body text warns that herbals "**can inhibit or accelerate drug metabolism.**" Official key not in the pull; see GAPS item 9.

> **Trap (SATA, five options mapped to categories).** **Review question 6 (p. 59)** asks which factors to consider when evaluating polymorphism, offering **nutritional status, drug route, genetic factors, cultural factors, and age.** Held against p. 48, the chapter's own list of contributing factors is "**environmental factors (e.g., diet and nutritional status), cultural factors, and genetic (inherited) factors**," and the key term adds "**age, sex, size, and/or body composition.**" **Drug route** appears in neither list — it is a pharmacokinetic variable, not a polymorphism one.

## §3 US drug legislation

**Why the laws exist** (p. 50): "Until the beginning of the twentieth century, there were **no federal rules and regulations in the United States to protect consumers from the dangers of medications.** The various legislative interventions that have occurred **have often been prompted by large-scale serious adverse drug reactions.**"

**The two tragedies that drove the two most important acts** (p. 50):

| Tragedy | What happened | What it produced |
|---|---|---|
| **Sulfanilamide, 1937** | "**Over 100 deaths** occurred in the United States when people ingested a **diethylene glycol** solution of sulfanilamide that had been marketed as a therapeutic drug. Diethylene glycol is a component of **automobile antifreeze** solution, and the drug **was never tested for its toxicity**" | **Federal Food, Drug, and Cosmetic Act (1938)** |
| **Thalidomide, 1940s–1960s in Europe** | "Many pregnant women who took this **sedative-hypnotic** drug **gave birth to seriously deformed infants**" | **Kefauver-Harris Amendments (1962)** |

**Table 4.1 Summary of Major US Drug and Related Legislation** (p. 50), reproduced in full:

| Legislation (year) | Provisions as given |
|---|---|
| **Federal Food and Drugs Act (FFDA, 1906)** | "Required drug manufacturers to **list on the drug product label the presence of dangerous and possibly addicting substances;** recognized the **US Pharmacopeia and National Formulary** as printed reference standards" |
| **Sherley Amendment (1912)** | "**Prohibited fraudulent claims** for drug products" |
| **Harrison Narcotic Act (1914)** | "**Established the legal term narcotic** and **regulated the manufacture and sale of habit-forming drugs**" |
| **Federal Food, Drug, and Cosmetic Act (FFDCA, 1938)** | "Required drug manufacturers to **provide data proving drug safety** with FDA review; **established the investigational new drug application process** (prompted by the **sulfanilamide elixir tragedy**)" |
| **Durham-Humphrey Amendment (1951)** | "**Established legend drugs or prescription drugs;** drug labels must carry the legend, '**Caution—Federal law prohibits dispensing without a prescription**'" |
| **Kefauver-Harris Amendments (1962)** | "Required manufacturers to **demonstrate both therapeutic efficacy and safety** of new drugs (prompted by the **thalidomide tragedy**)" |
| **Controlled Substance Act (1970)** | "**Established 'schedules' for controlled substances;** promoted **drug addiction education, research, and treatment**" |
| **Orphan Drug Act (1983)** | "Enabled the FDA to **promote research and marketing of orphan drugs used to treat rare diseases**" |
| **Accelerated Drug Review Regulations (1991)** | "Enabled **faster approval** by the FDA of drugs to treat **life-threatening illnesses** (prompted by the **HIV/AIDS epidemic**)" |
| **Health Insurance Portability and Accountability Act (1996)** | "More commonly known by its acronym, **HIPAA;** officially required **all health-related organizations as well as schools to maintain privacy of protected health information**" |
| **Medicare Prescription Drug Improvement and Modernization Act (2003)** | "More commonly known as **Medicare Part D;** provides seniors and persons with disabilities with an **insurance benefit program for prescription drugs;** the cost is **shared by the patient and the federal government**" |

**HIPAA in detail** (p. 50): it "requires **all health care providers, health and life insurance companies, public health authorities, employers, and schools** to maintain patient privacy regarding **protected health information.** Protected health information includes **any individually identifying information** such as patients' **health conditions, account numbers, prescription numbers, medications, and payment information.**"

**The Dietary Supplement Health and Education Act (1994)** (p. 51), which is in the body text but not in Table 4.1: "the FDA has had **less regulatory authority over vitamin, herbal, and homeopathic preparations because they are designated as dietary supplements rather than drugs.** In 1994, Congress passed the Dietary Supplement Health and Education Act, which requires manufacturers of such products **at least to ensure their safety (although not necessarily their efficacy)** and prohibits them from making **any unsubstantiated claims** in the product labeling."

- **The worked example:** "a product label **may read 'For depression' but cannot read 'Known to cure depression.'**"
- **1998:** "Congress established the **National Center for Complementary and Alternative Medicine** as a new branch of the **National Institutes of Health**" to "conduct rigorous scientific studies of alternative medical treatments."

**Who may prescribe** (p. 50): "Traditionally, only **medical doctors (MD) and doctors of osteopathy (DO)** had the privilege of prescribing medications. **Dentists and podiatrists** are also allowed to prescribe... so long as it is within the scope of their practice. In some states, other health care professionals may prescribe, including **licensed physician's assistants (PAs), advanced practice registered nurses (APRNs), and most recently optometrists.**"

**The three regulating bodies** (p. 50): "the **Food and Drug Administration (FDA), Drug Enforcement Agency (DEA), and individual state laws.**"

> **Trap (confusable pair).** **Which tragedy produced which act.** Cue: ***sulfanilamide (1937) → FFDCA (1938) → proved SAFETY; thalidomide → Kefauver-Harris (1962) → proved EFFICACY (as well as safety).*** The pairing is chronological and mnemonic: the earlier disaster produced the earlier, weaker requirement. An option crediting Kefauver-Harris with establishing the IND process has the acts crossed; that was the **1938** FFDCA.

> **Trap (level/stage swap).** **Three acts are commonly confused because all three concern controlled or habit-forming drugs.** Cue: ***1906 FFDA = merely LIST addicting substances on the label; 1914 Harrison = coined "narcotic" and REGULATED manufacture and sale; 1970 CSA = created the SCHEDULES.*** Sixty-four years, three escalating levels of control.

> **Trap (confusable pair).** **Dietary supplements are regulated as supplements, not drugs.** Cue: *manufacturers must ensure **safety but NOT efficacy,** and may make **structure claims but not cure claims.*** The chapter's own example ("For depression" is allowed; "Known to cure depression" is not) is the cleanest way to hold it. Ch 7 covers this class in depth.

> **Trap (plausible-but-adjacent).** **HIPAA covers schools and employers, not just clinicians.** The definition names "health care providers, health and life insurance companies, public health authorities, **employers, and schools.**" Critical thinking exercise 1 (p. 58) puts a caller claiming to be a patient's grandson on the phone asking about her diagnosis and prognosis — a HIPAA item wearing a communication-skills costume.

## §4 New drug development and the four clinical phases

**The overall shape** (p. 51): "It begins with **preclinical testing phases,** which include **in vitro studies (using tissue samples and cell cultures) and animal studies. Clinical (human) studies follow** the preclinical phase. There are **four clinical phases.** The drug is **put on the market after phase III is completed** if an investigational new drug application submitted by the manufacturer is approved by the FDA."

- **The collective goal:** "to provide information on the **safety, toxicity, efficacy, potency, bioavailability, and purity** of the new drug."
- **The FDA's purpose:** "to **protect the patient and ensure drug effectiveness.**"
- "This US system of drug research and development is **one of the most stringent in the world.**"

**Preclinical** (p. 51): "**all new drugs undergo laboratory testing using both in vitro (cell or tissue) and animal studies before any testing in human subjects can be done.** In vitro studies help researchers determine early on **if a substance might be too toxic for human patients. Many prospective new drugs are ruled out for human use during this preclinical phase.**"

**The four clinical phases** (pp. 51–52), reproduced with the chapter's own defining details:

| Phase | Subjects | Purpose as given |
|---|---|---|
| **I** | "**small numbers of healthy subjects** rather than those who have the disease" | "to determine the **optimal dosage range and the pharmacokinetics** of the drug and to ascertain if further testing is needed." Blood tests, urinalyses, vital signs, and specific monitoring tests performed |
| **II** | "**small numbers of volunteers who have the disease** that the drug is designed to diagnose or treat" | "to determine the drug's **effectiveness** and identify any adverse effects. **Therapeutic dosage ranges are refined** during this phase" |
| **III** | "**large numbers of patients** who are followed by medical research centers" | "to provide information about **infrequent or rare adverse effects.**" The three objectives: "to establish the drug's **clinical effectiveness, safety, and dosage range**" |
| **IV** | the general population, post-approval | "**postmarketing studies that are voluntarily conducted** by pharmaceutical companies to obtain **further proof of the therapeutic and adverse effects.** Data are gathered for **at least 2 years** after the drug's release. Often these studies **compare the safety and efficacy of the new drug with that of another drug in the same therapeutic category**" |

- **Why phase IV matters:** "Some medications make it through all phases of clinical trials **without causing any problems among study patients. However, when they are used in the larger general population, severe adverse effects may appear for the first time.**"
- **The NDA:** "After phase III is completed, the FDA receives a report from the manufacturer, at which time the drug company submits a **new drug application (NDA).**"

**Placebos and blinding** (p. 52): "To enhance objectivity, many studies are designed to incorporate a **placebo**... given to a portion of the research subjects to **separate out the real benefits of the investigational drug from the apparent benefits arising out of researcher or subject bias.**"

- **Blinded:** "If the study subject **does not know** if the drug he or she is administered is a placebo or the investigational drug, **but the investigator does know,** the study is referred to as a **blinded** investigational drug study."
- **Double-blind:** "neither the research staff nor the subjects being tested know which subjects are being given the real drug... because **both the researcher and the subject are 'blinded.'**"
- **The code:** "Drug and placebo dosage forms given to patients often **look identical except for a secret code**... At the completion of the study, this code is revealed... The code **also can be broken before study completion by the principle investigator in the event of a clinical emergency.**"

**Informed consent** (p. 51), which is objective 7 and 8 material:

- **What must be explained:** "the **purpose of the study, the procedures to be used, the possible benefits, and the risks involved.**"
- **The document:** "must be **written in a language understood by the patient** and must be **dated and signed by the patient and at least one witness.**"
- **Timing and competence:** "**informed consent must be obtained more than a given number of days or hours before certain procedures are performed and must always be obtained when the patient is fully mentally competent.**"
- **Voluntariness:** "**Informed consent is always voluntary**... participants in experimental drug studies be **informed volunteers and not be coerced to participate in any way.**"
- **The right to leave:** "any individual can either **decline to participate or quit the study at any time without affecting the delivery of any previously agreed-upon health care services.**"
- **Who obtains it:** "may be carried out by a **nurse or other health care professional,** depending on how a given study is designed."

**Expedited (fast-track) approval** (p. 51): "**Acquired immunodeficiency syndrome (AIDS) was the first major public health crisis for which the FDA began granting expedited drug approval.** This process allowed pharmaceutical manufacturers to shorten the approval process and allowed prescribers to give medications that showed promise **during early phase I and phase II clinical trials** to qualified patients with AIDS."

- **The controversy:** "The concept of expedited drug approval became controversial after the FDA-initiated manufacturer recall of the antiinflammatory drug **rofecoxib (Vioxx) in 2004.** This recall followed multiple case reports of **severe cardiovascular events, including fatalities**... This unfortunate example has **reduced the number of drugs approved by the expedited approval process.**"
- **COVID-19:** "the FDA approved vaccines and some drugs under **Emergency Use Authorization (EUA). Before an EUA is approved, the drug or vaccine must first be proven safe and effective.**"

**Patent economics** (p. 52): exclusivity runs "**normally 17 years after discovery** of the molecule and includes the **10- to 12-year period** generally required to complete drug research. Therefore the manufacturer typically has **5 to 7 years after drug marketing to recoup research costs,** which are usually in the **hundreds of millions of dollars** for a single drug."

**Black box warnings** (p. 52): "the **strictest warning from the FDA**" indicating "that **serious adverse effects have been reported** with the drug. **The drug can still be prescribed;** however, the prescriber must be aware of the potential risk and **the patient must be warned.** Black box warnings are included in the prescribing information, and **the text of the warning has a solid black border, thus the name black box.**"

**The three recall classes** (p. 52), reproduced:

| Class | Definition as given |
|---|---|
| **Class I** | "**The most serious type of recall** — use of the drug product carries a **reasonable probability of serious adverse health effects or death**" |
| **Class II** | "**Less severe** — use may result in **temporary or medically reversible health effects,** but the probability of lasting major adverse health effects is **low**" |
| **Class III** | "**Least severe** — use is **not likely to result in any significant health problems**" |

**MedWatch** (p. 52): "a **voluntary program** in which professionals are encouraged to **report any adverse events seen with newly approved drugs.**"

> **Trap (four-options-three-levels).** **The four clinical phases are the chapter's most orderable list.** Cue: ***I = small, HEALTHY, dosage range and pharmacokinetics; II = small, DISEASED, effectiveness; III = LARGE, rare adverse effects, then market; IV = POSTmarketing, at least 2 years, compares against an existing drug.*** Review question 7 (p. 59) asks you to place them in order, and review question 4 (p. 58) describes severe reports arriving in the first 6 months after a drug goes on sale, which is by definition **after** phase III. Official keys not in the pull; see GAPS item 9.

> **Trap (confusable pair).** **Phase I uses HEALTHY volunteers.** The single most counterintuitive fact in the sequence, and the one distractors exploit: the first humans to receive a new drug do **not** have the disease it treats. Diseased volunteers enter at **phase II**.

> **Trap (confusable pair).** **IND application vs. new drug application (NDA).** Cue: ***IND comes FIRST and permits human study*** ("must be submitted to the FDA **before a drug can be studied in humans**"); ***NDA comes after phase III and permits marketing.*** Both are "applications to the FDA," which is what makes them swappable. Note the chapter's own wording is loose here; see GAPS item 6.

> **Trap (level/stage swap).** **Recall classes run I (worst) to III (least severe), the same direction as the controlled-substance schedules.** Cue: ***in both systems, I is the most serious.*** That parallel is a memory aid, not a coincidence to be over-read: they are unrelated systems that happen to share a direction.

> **Trap (plausible-but-adjacent).** **A black box warning does not remove a drug from the market.** "**The drug can still be prescribed.**" It is a warning, not a withdrawal. Contrast with the **recall**, which is the withdrawal mechanism, and with **rofecoxib (Vioxx)**, which was actually recalled in 2004 — Ch 44 covers that recall and notes rofecoxib and valdecoxib "were removed from the US market" while celecoxib remains.

> **Trap (priority question).** **Informed consent is voluntary and revocable at any time.** Review question 3 (p. 58) asks what informed consent indicates and offers, among others, that the patient **cannot withdraw** once therapy begins and that the patient **cannot sue.** Both contradict the chapter directly: any individual "can either decline to participate or **quit the study at any time without affecting the delivery of any previously agreed-upon health care services.**"

> **Trap (confusable pair).** **Patent arithmetic disagrees between Ch 2 and Ch 4.** Ch 2 (p. 15) gives **17 years total, ~10 in research, 7 remaining for profit.** Ch 4 (p. 52) gives **17 years total, 10 to 12 in research, 5 to 7 remaining.** Same total, different split. Both are quoted in their own fact banks; see GAPS item 7.

## §5 Controlled substances

"**The Controlled Substance Act requires the scheduling of every controlled drug.** There are **five classes of controlled substances, designated from C-I to C-V**" (p. 52).

**Table 4.2 Controlled Substances: Schedule Categories** (p. 53), reproduced:

| Schedule | Abuse potential | Medical use | Dependency potential |
|---|---|---|---|
| **C-I** | **High** | **None** | **Severe physical and psychological** |
| **C-II** | **High** | **Accepted** | **Severe physical and psychological** |
| **C-III** | Less than C-II | Accepted | **Moderate to low physical or high psychological** |
| **C-IV** | Less than C-III | Accepted | Limited physical or psychological |
| **C-V** | Less than C-IV | Accepted | Limited physical or psychological |

The body text restates the definitions (p. 52): C-I drugs have "**no currently accepted medical use and a high potential for abuse**"; Schedule II drugs have "**a medical use and a high potential for abuse**"; Schedule III drugs have "a **moderate to low potential for physical and psychological dependence**"; Schedule IV drugs have "a **low potential for abuse and low risk for dependence**"; Schedule V drugs have "**lower potential for abuse than Schedule IV**" and "consist of preparations containing **limited quantities of certain opioids.**"

**Table 4.3 Controlled Substances: Categories, Dispensing Restrictions, and Examples** (p. 53), reproduced:

| Schedule | Dispensing restrictions | Examples |
|---|---|---|
| **C-I** | "**Only with approved protocol**" | "Heroin, lysergic acid diethylamide (LSD), marijuana, mescaline, peyote, psilocybin, and methaqualone" |
| **C-II** | "**Written prescription only**; **no prescription refills**; container must have warning label" | "**Codeine, cocaine, hydrocodone, hydromorphone, meperidine, morphine, methadone,** secobarbital, pentobarbital, **oxycodone,** amphetamine, methylphenidate" |
| **C-III** | "Written or oral prescription that **expires in 6 months**; **no more than five refills** in a 6-month period; container must have warning label" | "**Codeine with selected other medications (e.g., acetaminophen),** pentobarbital rectal suppositories, dihydrocodeine combination products" |
| **C-IV** | "Written or oral prescription that **expires in 6 months**; **no more than five refills** in a 6-month period; container must have warning label" | "Phenobarbital, chloral hydrate, meprobamate, **benzodiazepines (e.g., diazepam, temazepam, lorazepam), tramadol**" |
| **C-V** | "**Written prescription or over the counter** (varies with state law)" | "Medications generally for **relief of coughs or diarrhea** containing limited quantities of certain opioid controlled substances" |

Table 4.3's footnote on C-II: "**Legally permitted to be telephoned in for major emergencies only. If telephoned in, written prescription is required within 72 hours.**"

**Two reschedulings the chapter flags** (p. 53), quoted as printed: "in **2014,** the popular pain medicine **hydrocodone (Vicodin, Lortab) was rescheduled from C-III to C-II,** and **tramadol (Ultram) was rescheduled to C-IV; before the change it was a Schedule V.**"

- **Read that tramadol baseline with care.** "Schedule V" is what the page prints, and it is preserved here unaltered, but it conflicts with the widely held understanding that tramadol was federally **unscheduled** before 2014, and the sentence reads like a dropped negation. **See GAPS item 10.** Do not build an exam answer on the pre-2014 baseline in either direction; the rescheduling *to* C-IV is the uncontested half.

- **State variation:** "certain medications (e.g., **gabapentin**) may have a **higher schedule in certain states than in others.**"

> **Trap (level/stage swap).** **C-I and C-II both have HIGH abuse potential and SEVERE dependency; only medical use separates them.** Cue: ***C-I = NO accepted medical use; C-II = accepted medical use.*** Every other row differs on abuse potential too, which makes this top pair the one that must be memorized on a single axis. Read Table 4.2 across, not down.

> **Trap (confusable pair).** **C-III and C-IV share identical dispensing restrictions.** Both are "written or oral prescription that expires in 6 months, no more than five refills." So a dispensing-rules question **cannot** distinguish them; only abuse and dependency potential can. Cue: *if the stem is about refills, the answer is "III or IV"; if it is about **no refills at all,** the answer is **C-II**.*

> **Trap (confusable pair).** **Codeine appears in two different schedules, and Ch 10 says why.** Cue: ***codeine alone = C-II; codeine combined with acetaminophen = C-III.*** Ch 10 (p. 148) states it from the drug side: the combination "is classified as a **Schedule III** controlled substance," while "when codeine is not combined with other drugs, it is classified as a **Schedule II.**" The combination is the **lower** schedule.

> **Trap (level/stage swap).** **Both 2014 reschedulings tightened by exactly one level, and the ladder runs backwards from the numbers.** Cue: ***a LOWER numeral is MORE restricted,*** so **hydrocodone C-III → C-II** and, as the page prints it, **tramadol Schedule V → C-IV** are both a one-step tightening. The reflex error is reading "moved from V to IV" as a loosening because the number went down. Ch 10 confirms the hydrocodone half — "In 2014, hydrocodone was rescheduled as a CII drug" — and lists tramadol among the drugs with opioid-like activity. **The tramadol starting point is disputed (GAPS item 10); the safe fact is where it landed, C-IV.**

> **Trap (priority question).** **A C-II prescription cannot be refilled and normally cannot be phoned in.** The emergency exception is narrow and time-bound: "**major emergencies only**," with a **written prescription required within 72 hours.** A stem in which a C-II order is called in routinely, or refilled, is the order to question.

> **Trap (plausible-but-adjacent).** **C-V may be available over the counter, depending on state law.** The lowest schedule is not necessarily prescription-only, which surprises people who assume "controlled substance" means "requires a prescription." Its typical products are **antitussives and antidiarrheals** containing limited opioid quantities — which connects to Ch 10's note that **codeine** is used as an antitussive and that **diphenoxylate/atropine (Lomotil)** and **paregoric** are opioid-containing antidiarrheals.

## §6 Legal considerations in nursing practice

**Nurse Practice Acts** (Box 4.1, p. 53): "**state laws** that are instrumental in **defining the scope of nursing practice** and protect **public health, safety, and welfare.** In each state, the law **directs entry into nursing practice, defines the scope of practices, and identifies disciplinary actions. State boards of nursing oversee this statutory law.** NPAs are **the most significant part of legislation as related to professional nursing practice.** Together, it is **NPAs and common law that define nursing practice.**"

**What NPAs further define** (p. 53), reproduced as the numbered list it is: "(1) the **scope of nursing practice,** (2) **expanded nursing roles,** (3) **educational requirements** for nurses, (4) **standards of care,** (5) **minimally safe nursing practice,** and (6) **differences between nursing and medical practice.**"

**Other sources of legal boundary** (p. 53): "guidelines from **professional nursing groups** (e.g., the **American Nurses Association [ANA]**) and nursing specialty groups, as well as **institutional policies and procedures and state/federal hospital licensing laws.** There is also **case law or common law** consisting of **prior court rulings.**"

**The consequence of falling below standard** (p. 54): "**If standards are not met, the nurse becomes liable for negligence and malpractice.**"

**Box 4.2 Areas of Potential Liability for Nurses** (p. 54), reproduced by category:

| Area | Examples related to drug therapy as given |
|---|---|
| **Failure to assess/evaluate** | Failure to "see **significant changes in patient's condition after taking a medication**"; "report the changes"; "take a **complete medication history**"; "**monitor patient after medication administration**" |
| **Failure to ensure safety** | "Lack of adequate monitoring"; "failure to **identify patient allergies** and other risk factors"; "**inappropriate drug administration technique**"; "failure to implement appropriate nursing actions based on a lack of proper assessment" |
| **Medication errors** | Failure to "**clarify unclear medication order**"; "identify and react to **adverse drug reactions**"; "be **familiar with medication before its administration**"; "maintain level of professional nursing skills"; "**identify patient's identity before drug administration**"; "**document drug administration** in medication profile" |
| **Fraud** | "**Falsification of documentation** on the medication profile or patient's record"; "failure to provide the nursing care that **was documented**" |

**The four elements of liability for nursing malpractice** (p. 54), reproduced with the chapter's own worked IV-extravasation example:

| Element | Example as given |
|---|---|
| **Duty** | "Being responsible for **accurate assessment of a patient's intravenous (IV) line and access site,** monitoring the site during caustic drug infusion, and the **timely reporting of changes**" |
| **Breach of duty** | "Nurse **does not notice** that the IV site is **swollen, red, painful, and warm to touch** or that the IV has **quit infusing properly**" |
| **Causation** | "Nurse **fails to note the signs and symptoms of extravasation** at IV site (with a chemotherapy drug or other caustic drug) that **results in the need for skin grafting**" |
| **Damage** | "**Extensive skin and nerve damage** with several surgical skin grafts resulting in **limited use of arm**" |

**Accrediting bodies** (p. 54): "**The Joint Commission (TJC), Healthcare Facilities Accreditation Program (HFAP), and Det Norske Veritas (DNV).**"

- **TJC:** "For years TJC, as the **number one choice,** has been providing accreditation to hospitals in the United States... requires that accredited hospitals fulfill certain standards that essentially **define how high-quality, safe patient care should be delivered.** There is a focus on **leadership standards and quality management.**"
- **DNV:** "emerged in **2008**"; "seen as more '**facility friendly**' but within a stringent accreditation philosophy and with a more **quality-management** approach."
- **HFAP:** "meets or exceeds the standards required by the **Centers for Medicare and Medicaid Services (CMS).** It provides accreditation to **all hospitals, ambulatory care/surgical facilities, mental health facilities, physical rehabilitation facilities, clinical laboratories, and critical access.**"
- **Why nurses should care:** "because of their **priority of monitoring standard of patient care.** Nurses need to not only understand their own credentialing/licensing/nurse practice acts but also understand the credentialing process and activities associated with **their place of employment.**"

> **Trap (four-options-three-levels).** **The four elements of malpractice must ALL be present, and they are sequential.** Cue: ***Duty (you were responsible) → Breach (you failed) → Causation (your failure caused it) → Damage (harm resulted).*** A stem describing a nurse who missed an infiltrated IV that was then caught by someone else with no injury has **breach without damage,** which does not complete the set.

> **Trap (confusable pair).** **Nurse Practice Acts are STATE law; the drug legislation in §3 is FEDERAL.** Cue: *scope of nursing practice = **state** (NPAs, boards of nursing); drug scheduling and approval = **federal** (FDA, DEA).* Both regulate what a nurse may do with a drug, from different directions. The chapter says both "state and federal legislation dictate the boundaries for professional nursing practice."

> **Trap (plausible-but-adjacent).** **Documenting care that was not given is filed under FRAUD, not medication error.** Box 4.2 separates them. Cue: *an **error** is a failure of practice; **fraud** is a falsification of the record.* The second is a different category of liability with different consequences.

> **Trap (setting vs. focus).** **Accrediting bodies accredit the facility; nurse practice acts license the nurse.** TJC, HFAP, and DNV set institutional standards; the state board sets individual scope. A stem asking who defines whether a nurse may start an IV is asking about the **state board**, not TJC.

## §7 Ethical considerations

**The framing** (p. 54): "Decisions in health care are **seldom made independently of other people** and are made with consideration of the **patient, family, nurses, and other members of the health care team.** All members must make a concentrated effort to **recognize and understand their own values** and be **considerate, nonjudgmental, and respectful of the values of others.**"

- **What drug therapy has become:** "The use of drug therapy has evolved from **just administering whatever was prescribed** to **providing responsible drug therapy for the purpose of achieving defined outcomes that improve a patient's quality of life.**"
- **When principles apply:** "When answers to ethical dilemmas remain unclear and ethical conflict occurs, **the appropriate action must be based on ethical principles.**"

**Ethical Terms Related to Nursing Practice** (p. 55), reproduced in full with the chapter's own related nursing actions:

| Principle | Definition as given | Related nursing actions as given |
|---|---|---|
| **Autonomy** | "**Self-determination and the ability to act on one's own**" | "promoting a patient's **decision making,** supporting **informed consent,** and assisting in decisions or making a decision when a patient is **posing harm to himself or herself**" |
| **Beneficence** | "The ethical principle of **doing or actively promoting good**" | "determining **how the patient is best served**" |
| **Confidentiality** | "The duty to **respect privileged information** about a patient" | "**not talking about a patient in public or outside the context of the health care setting**" |
| **Justice** | "The ethical principle of **being fair or equal** in one's actions" | "ensuring **fairness in distributing resources** for the care of patients and **determining when to treat**" |
| **Nonmaleficence** | "The duty to **do no harm** to a patient" | "**avoiding doing any deliberate harm** while rendering nursing care" |
| **Veracity** | "The duty to **tell the truth**" | "telling the truth with regard to **placebos, investigational new drugs, and informed consent**" |

**The ANA Code of Ethics** (pp. 55–56): "**The Code of Ethics for Nurses (The Code) with Interpretive Statements.** The latest revision (**2016**) was made in response to the complexity of contemporary nursing practice... There are **nine provisions** within The Code... to serve as the profession's **nonnegotiable ethical standard,** provide very clear statements of '**ethical values, obligations and duties**' of everyone entering the nursing profession."

**The International Council of Nurses (ICN)** (p. 56): "a federation of **over 130 national nurses associations (NNAs)** and represents some **16 million nurses** throughout the world. It was **founded in 1899** and works to ensure quality of nursing care for all and sound health care policies globally. The ICN works with agencies of the **United Nations, specifically the World Health Organization.**"

**The nurse's right of conscience, and its limit** (p. 55): "The nurse also has the **right to refuse to participate in any treatment or aspect of a patient's care that violates the nurse's personal ethical principles. However, this must be done without deserting the patient,** and in some facilities the nurse may be **transferred to another patient care assignment only if the transfer is approved by the nurse manager or nurse supervisor.**"

- **The overriding duty:** "The Code requires the nurse to **provide nonjudgmental nursing care from the start of the patient's treatment until the time of the patient's discharge.** If transferring... is not an option... **the nurse must always act in the best interest of the patient while remaining an objective patient advocate.**"
- **The boundary on values:** "it is **not within the nurse's realm of ethical and professional responsibility to impose his or her own values or standards on the patient,** [but] it **is** within the nurse's realm to **provide information and assist the patient in making decisions.**"

**Placebo ethics** (p. 56), which is the chapter's designated controversy: a placebo is "a drug dosage form **without any pharmacologic activity** because of a lack of active ingredients. However, **there may be reported therapeutic responses,** and placebos have been found to be beneficial in certain patients, such as those being treated for **anxiety.**"

- **The rule:** "**Except in new drug studies, however, placebo use is often considered to be unethical and deceitful, possibly creating mistrust** among the nurse, the prescriber, and the patient."
- **The professional guidance:** "the **American Pain Society** and the **Agency for Health Care Policy and Research** recommend the **avoidance of placebos** because their use is thought to be **deceitful and to violate a patient's rights to the highest-quality care possible.** Many health care agencies **limit the use of placebos to research only.**"
- **The nursing action:** "If an order is received for a placebo for a patient, it is **within the legal purview of a professional nurse to inquire about the order and to ask why a placebo is being prescribed; the order must never be taken lightly.**"
- **The four research-participant rights** attached to placebo use in a study: the right to "(1) **leave the study at any time without any pressure or coercion to stay,** (2) **leave the study without consequences to medical care,** (3) **receive full and complete information about the study,** and (4) **be aware of all alternative options and receive information on all treatments, including placebo therapy.**"

> **Trap (confusable pair).** **Beneficence vs. nonmaleficence.** The classic pair, one letter of Latin apart in effect. Cue: ***bene = actively DO good; non-male = do NO harm.*** One is a positive duty, the other a negative one. A stem about withholding a harmful intervention is nonmaleficence; a stem about providing a beneficial one is beneficence.

> **Trap (confusable pair).** **Autonomy vs. veracity vs. beneficence, in a patient-refusing-treatment stem.** Review question 1 (p. 58) gives a patient with late-stage cancer who says he wants to live to see a graduation "but after that I don't want any extra treatments," and offers **veracity, beneficence, maleficence, and autonomy.** Held against the box: **autonomy** is "self-determination and the ability to act on one's own," with related actions including "promoting a patient's decision making." Note that one option, "**maleficence**," is not a principle in the chapter at all — the principle is **non**maleficence, and the distractor drops the negation. Official key not in the pull; see GAPS item 9.

> **Trap (setting vs. focus).** **Veracity is the principle explicitly tied to placebos, INDs, and informed consent.** The box names all three in its "related nursing actions" cell. So a stem about whether to tell a patient they are receiving a placebo is a **veracity** item, even though it also touches autonomy.

> **Trap (priority question).** **A placebo order outside a research study is questioned, not simply administered.** The chapter grants the nurse explicit standing: it is "within the legal purview of a professional nurse to inquire about the order and to ask why." Compare the general rule from Box 4.2 that failing to "clarify unclear medication order" is an area of liability.

> **Trap (plausible-but-adjacent).** **The right to refuse participation does not include the right to abandon the patient.** Both halves are in one sentence, and the second half is the one distractors omit: refusal "must be done **without deserting the patient**," and transfer requires supervisor approval. If no transfer is possible, the duty to act in the patient's best interest stands.
