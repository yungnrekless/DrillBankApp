> **Trap (confusable pair).** **Sedative vs. hypnotic vs. sedative-hypnotic.** Cue: ***sedative** = calms **without** sleep; **hypnotic** = causes sleep; **sedative-hypnotic** = does **either, depending on dose**.* The Overview states it both ways: "**a sedative can become a hypnotic if it is given in large enough doses.** Hypnotics cause sleep and have a **much more potent effect** on the CNS" (p. 184). The label is not a property of the molecule: a drug is one or the other "**depending on the degree to which it inhibits the transmission of nerve impulses to the CNS**" (p. 184). An option calling a drug "a sedative" with no dose qualifier fights the chapter's framing.

> **Trap (plausible-but-adjacent).** **The printed *Hypnotics* definition contradicts the body text one page later.** The box (p. 183) says hypnotics at low to moderate doses act "**without inducing sleep**"; the Overview (p. 184) says "**Hypnotics cause sleep.**" The box wording actually describes the *continuum,* not the hypnotic end. Both quoted as printed; see GAPS item 3. Use the body-text meaning.

> **Trap (confusable pair).** **REM interference vs. REM rebound.** Cue: ***interference happens **while on** the drug = **less** REM; rebound happens **after stopping** = **more** REM.*** Consequences differ: interference gives "**daytime fatigue** because REM sleep provides a certain component of the 'restfulness' of sleep"; rebound gives "**frequent and vivid dreams**" (p. 184), and with barbiturates "**nightmares often ensue**" (p. 189).

> **Trap (confusable pair).** **REM rebound vs. rebound insomnia.** Both are withdrawal phenomena. Cue: ***REM rebound = too much **dreaming**; rebound insomnia = too much **wakefulness**.*** Rebound insomnia is "**greater insomnia than pretreatment**," occurring with "**abrupt discontinuation**" (p. 186). Fix for both: "**Gradual weaning-off periods** are recommended for benzodiazepines and all CNS depressants" (p. 194).

> **Trap (confusable pair).** **Sleep vs. sleep architecture.** Cue: ***sleep = the **state**; architecture = the **structure of the cycle**, "including normal and abnormal patterns."*** An option defining architecture as "the state of rest" took the wrong half.

> **Trap (plausible-but-adjacent).** **Therapeutic index is in this box but belongs to Chapter 2.** It is here for one sentence: barbiturates "are physiologically habit forming and **have a low therapeutic index**" (p. 188). Low index = small gap between therapeutic and toxic = more hazardous. That is the discriminator in any benzodiazepine-vs-barbiturate safety item.

| **Nonbenzodiazepines** | **zolpidem, eszopiclone, zaleplon** (p. 184) | schedule IV | none named | "function **much like benzodiazepines** but are **chemically distinct** from them" (p. 184) |
| **Orexin receptor antagonists** | **suvorexant** (Belsomra); "**Lemborexant (DayVigo) is a similar drug**" | "schedule IV drug" (p. 188) | none named | "**first drug in a new class**"; "currently... the **only orexin receptor antagonist available**" (p. 188) |

| **OTC hypnotics** | **doxylamine (Unisom), diphenhydramine (Sominex)**; melatonin (p. 190) | no | n/a | "often contain **antihistamines**... These drugs have a **CNS depressant effect**" (p. 190) |

**Sites of action, one per class:**

| Class | Site, as given | p. |
|---|---|---|
| **Benzodiazepines** | "the **hypothalamic, thalamic, and limbic systems** of the brain" | 185 |
| **Barbiturates** | "the **brainstem** in an area called the **reticular formation**"; they reduce "nerve impulses traveling to... the **cerebral cortex**" | 189 |
| **Muscle relaxants, centrally acting** | "the **brainstem, thalamus, and basal ganglia** and also at the **spinal cord**" | 191 |
| **Dantrolene, direct acting** | "the **excitation-contraction coupling of muscle fibers** and **not at the level of the CNS**" | 191 |

**GABA appears three different ways.** Benzodiazepines: their receptors "are thought to be either **GABA receptors or other adjacent receptors,**" and like GABA they "**inhibit stimulation of the brain**" (p. 185). Barbiturates: they "**potentiate the action of the inhibitory neurotransmitter GABA**" (p. 189). Dantrolene: "It **closely resembles GABA**" (p. 191).

> **Trap (confusable pair).** **Benzodiazepine vs. nonbenzodiazepine ("Z-drug").** Table 12.2 prints both **in one table,** separated only by a footnote. Cue: ***-pam or -lam = benzodiazepine (Clinical Pearl, p. 186: "Many benzodiazepines end in 'pam'"); zolpidem, eszopiclone, zaleplon are **not** benzodiazepines despite sharing the table and the schedule IV status.*** The footnote is explicit: "**These drugs share many characteristics with the benzodiazepines but are classified as miscellaneous hypnotic drugs**" (p. 185). The chapter's own Key Points violate this — see GAPS item 4.

> **Trap (confusable pair).** **Benzodiazepines vs. barbiturates: which is safer, and why.** Cue: ***benzodiazepines = **favorable** adverse effect profile and an **antidote**; barbiturates = **low therapeutic index, physiologically habit forming, no antidote**.*** Stated three times: barbiturates fell out of use "due to the **favorable safety profile and proven efficacy of the benzodiazepines**" (p. 188); "dependence may be a problem with the benzodiazepines, **but not to the same degree as with the barbiturates**" (p. 194); hangover occurs "**less frequently with benzodiazepines and nonbenzodiazepines than with barbiturates**" (p. 194).

> **Trap (setting vs. focus).** **What the drug is *for* vs. where it *acts*.** Centrally acting muscle relaxants are "**similar in structure and action to other CNS depressants such as diazepam**" and work "from their **sedative effects rather than from direct muscle relaxation**" (p. 191) — so mechanism will not separate them from benzodiazepines. Cue: ***the axis is **indication**: muscle relaxants "have different indications... and are **not used to treat insomnia**" (p. 194).***

## §2 Sleep architecture and the sleep stages

"Sleep is defined as a transient, reversible, and periodic state of rest... **Normal sleep is cyclic and repetitive,** and a person's **responses to sensory stimuli are markedly reduced** during sleep." Architecture "consists of **two basic elements that occur cyclically: rapid eye movement (REM) sleep and non–rapid eye movement (non-REM) sleep**" (p. 184).

**Table 12.1 Stages of Sleep** (p. 185), full. The percentage column is "Average Percentage of Sleep Time in Stages (for Young Adult)."

| Stage | Characteristics as given | % |
|---|---|---|
| **Non-REM 1** | "Dozing or feelings of **drifting off to sleep**; person can be **easily awakened**; **slow, side-to-side eye movements**; **insomniacs have longer stage 1 periods** than normal." | **2%–5%** |
| **Non-REM 2** | "Sleep **deepening** and a **higher arousal threshold** being required to awaken the patient." | **45%–55%** |
| **Non-REM 3** | "**Deep sleep**; difficult to wake person; **respiratory rates, pulse, and blood pressure may decrease**; stages 3 and 4 often combined and referred to as '**delta sleep**' or '**slow-wave sleep**'; delta sleep associated with the **highest arousal threshold**." | **3%–8%** |
| **Non-REM 4** | "**Very difficult to wake person**; person may be **very groggy** if awakened; **dreaming occurs, especially about daily events**; **sleepwalking or bedwetting** may occur." | **10%–15%** |
| **REM** | "**REMs occur; vivid dreams occur; breathing may be irregular.**" | **25%–33%** |

Table note (p. 185): "**Four to five cycles** are completed during normal sleep for adults. **Non-REM sleep constitutes approximately the first third of the night,** and **REM sleep is more prominent during the last third of the night.**"

> **Trap (level/stage swap).** **Four non-REM stages, one cue each.** ***Stage 1 = **drifting off,** easily awakened, the stage **insomniacs spend longer in**; stage 2 = **deepening,** the **biggest slice** at 45%–55%; stage 3 = **delta / slow-wave,** vitals **decrease,** **highest arousal threshold**; stage 4 = **very difficult to wake, groggy,** and owns **sleepwalking and bedwetting**.***

> **Trap (level/stage swap).** **Which stage owns dreaming?** Both stage 4 and REM do, which is why an item can be built here. Cue: ***stage 4 dreaming is "especially about **daily events**"; REM dreaming is "**vivid**" and comes with **rapid eye movements and irregular breathing**.*** The key term names all three REM features together, so "vivid dreams" alone is half the term.

> **Trap (level/stage swap).** **The percentages do not rank the way the stage numbers do.** Cue: ***stage 2 is the **largest** non-REM slice, stage 3 the **smallest,** and REM (25%–33%) is **second largest overall**.*** A distractor assigning the biggest percentage to the deepest stage is following intuition, not the table. Separately testable: non-REM as a whole is "the **largest portion** of the sleep cycle" and "**precedes REM sleep**" (p. 183).

> **Trap (confusable pair).** **First third vs. last third of the night.** Cue: ***non-REM front-loads the night; REM is "more prominent during the **last third**."*** This is why hypnotics that wear off early still leave REM interference, and why hangover complaints attach to the drugs still on board at dawn.

## §3 Benzodiazepines and the nonbenzodiazepines

"**Historically, benzodiazepines were the most commonly prescribed sedative-hypnotic drugs; however, the nonbenzodiazepine drugs are now more frequently prescribed**" (p. 184). Other drugs commonly used for sleep: "**diphenhydramine** (see Chapter 36), **trazodone,** and **amitriptyline** (see Chapter 16)." Benzodiazepines are "classified as either **sedative-hypnotics or anxiolytics, depending on their primary use**," but "**any of these drugs can function along a continuum as a sedative and/or hypnotic and/or anxiolytic, depending on the dosage and patient sensitivity**" (p. 184). "**There are five benzodiazepines used as sedative-hypnotic drugs**" — which five is never stated; see GAPS item 5.

**Table 12.2 Sedative-Hypnotic Benzodiazepines and Miscellaneous Drugs** (p. 185):

| Duration | Generic → Trade |
|---|---|
| **Long acting** | clonazepam → Klonopin; diazepam → Valium; flurazepam → Dalmane |
| **Intermediate acting** | alprazolam → Xanax; lorazepam → Ativan; **suvorexant → Belsomra**; temazepam → Restoril |
| **Short acting** | **eszopiclone → Lunesta**; midazolam → Versed; **ramelteon → Rozerem**; triazolam → Halcion; **zaleplon → Sonata**; **zolpidem → Ambien** |

Footnote a (on eszopiclone, ramelteon, zaleplon, zolpidem, suvorexant): "**These drugs share many characteristics with the benzodiazepines but are classified as miscellaneous hypnotic drugs.**"

**Indications** (p. 185): "**sedation, relief of agitation or anxiety, treatment of anxiety-related depression, sleep induction, skeletal muscle relaxation, and treatment of acute seizure disorders.**" Also combined with "**anesthetics, analgesics, and neuromuscular blocking drugs in balanced anesthesia and also moderate sedation** (see Chapter 11) for their **amnesic properties to reduce memory of painful procedures.**" And alcohol withdrawal, with its reason: benzodiazepine receptors "are in the **same area as those that play a role in alcohol addiction,**" so "**diazepam, chlordiazepoxide**" are used "in the **treatment and prevention of the symptoms of alcohol withdrawal**."

**For insomnia, short term only:** "it is recommended that they be used **short term, if clinically feasible, to avoid dependency**" (p. 185).

**Contraindications** (p. 185): "**known drug allergy, narrow-angle glaucoma, and pregnancy.**"

**Adverse effects** (pp. 185–186): "a **relatively favorable adverse effect profile**; however, they can be harmful **if given in excessive doses or when mixed with alcohol.**" Commonly reported: "**headache, drowsiness, paradoxical excitement or nervousness, dizziness or vertigo, cognitive impairment, and lethargy.**" Plus: a "**significant fall hazard in older adults,** and the **lowest effective dose must be used**"; a "**hangover**" effect "(e.g., **daytime sleepiness**)"; and "**rebound insomnia**... with **abrupt discontinuation.**"

> **Trap (plausible-but-adjacent).** **A paradoxical reaction is not allergic, teratogenic, or idiopathic.** Review question 2 (p. 196) uses exactly those four options for an older adult given a benzodiazepine for sleep who stays "awake most of the night, watching television and reading in bed." The chapter lists "**paradoxical excitement or nervousness**" for benzodiazepines (p. 185), "**paradoxical restlessness or excitement**" for barbiturates (p. 189), and "**paradoxical excitement in children**" (p. 193). Cue: ***the drug did the **opposite** of its purpose = paradoxical.*** (Reasoned from body text; not the official key — GAPS item 11.)

> **Trap (priority question).** **Older adult + benzodiazepine = fall risk; the answer is the lowest effective dose.** Cue: ***benzodiazepine = "**lowest effective dose**" (p. 185); barbiturate = "the usual dose is **reduced by half whenever possible**" (p. 189).*** The half-dose number belongs to the barbiturates only.

### Toxicity, overdose, and flumazenil

"An overdose of benzodiazepines may result in one or all of the following symptoms: **somnolence, confusion, diminished reflexes, and coma.** ... **Overdose of benzodiazepines alone rarely results in hypotension and respiratory depression.** These effects are **more commonly seen when benzodiazepines are taken with other CNS depressants such as alcohol or barbiturates.** In the **absence** of the concurrent ingestion of alcohol or other CNS depressants, **benzodiazepine overdose rarely results in death**" (p. 186).

"**Flumazenil, a benzodiazepine antidote,** can be used to **acutely reverse the sedative effects** of benzodiazepines. It **antagonizes the action of benzodiazepines on the CNS by directly competing with the benzodiazepine for binding at the receptors.** Flumazenil is used in cases of **oral overdose or excessive intravenous sedation**" (p. 186).

**Table 12.3 Flumazenil Treatment Regimen** (p. 186):

| Indication | Regimen | Duration |
|---|---|---|
| **Reversal of moderate sedation or general anesthesia** | "**0.2 mg (2 mL) IV over 15 sec,** then **0.2 mg** if consciousness does not occur; may be repeated at **60-sec intervals** prn up to **4 additional times** (**maximum total dose, 1 mg**)" | **1–4 hr** |
| **Suspected benzodiazepine overdose** | "**0.2 mg (2 mL) IV over 30 sec;** wait 30 sec, then give **0.3 mg (3 mL) over 30 sec**...; further doses of **0.5 mg (5 mL)** at intervals of **1 min** up to a **cumulative dose of 3 mg**" | **1–4 hr** |

Table note: "**Flumazenil has a relatively short half-life and a duration of effect of 1 to 4 hours;** therefore, if flumazenil is used to reverse... a **long-acting benzodiazepine,** the dose of the reversal drug **may wear off and the patient may become sedated again, requiring more flumazenil.**"

> **Trap (confusable pair).** **Flumazenil vs. naloxone.** Review question 1 (p. 196) is built on it: overdose of "an **oral benzodiazepine**," drowsy but responsive, options hemodialysis / **flumazenil** / naloxone / intubation. Cue: ***flumazenil reverses **benzodiazepines**; naloxone reverses **opioids** (Chapter 10).*** Intubation tempts, but the chapter says benzodiazepine overdose **alone** "rarely results in... respiratory depression," and the patient is still responsive. (Reasoned from body text — GAPS item 11.)

> **Trap (level/stage swap).** **Flumazenil's two regimens are different numbers for different jobs.** Cue: ***sedation reversal = **0.2 mg over 15 sec,** repeats at **60 sec,** **max 1 mg**; overdose = **0.2 mg over 30 sec,** escalating 0.3 then 0.5 mg, **max 3 mg**.*** The **1 mg vs. 3 mg** ceiling is the cleanest discriminator.

> **Trap (plausible-but-adjacent).** **Giving flumazenil is not the end of the intervention.** Its duration is **1 to 4 hours** and long-acting benzodiazepines outlast it, so "**the patient may become sedated again.**" An option that stops monitoring after a successful reversal is defeated by the table's own note.

### Interactions

Interactions matter "**because of their intensity,** particularly when they involve **other CNS depressants (e.g., alcohol, opioids, muscle relaxants),**" producing "**reduced blood pressure, reduced respiratory rate, sedation, confusion, and diminished reflexes**" (p. 186).

**Table 12.4 Benzodiazepines: Drug/Food Interactions** (p. 186):

| Drug | Mechanism | Result |
|---|---|---|
| **Azole antifungals, verapamil, diltiazem, protease inhibitors, macrolide antibiotics, grapefruit juice** | "**Decreased** benzodiazepine metabolism" | "**Prolonged** benzodiazepine action" |
| **CNS depressants** | "**Additive effects**" | "**Increased CNS depression**" |
| **olanzapine** | "**Unknown**" | "**Increased benzodiazepine effects**" |
| **rifampin** | "**Increased** metabolism" | "**Decreased** benzodiazepine effects" |

Herbals: "**kava and valerian,** which may also lead to further CNS depression." Food: "**grapefruit and grapefruit juice,** which alter drug metabolism by **inhibition of the cytochrome P-450 system** and can result in **prolonged effect, increased effect, and toxicity**" (p. 186).

> **Trap (confusable pair).** **Enzyme inhibition vs. induction — both are in this chapter, running opposite ways.** Cue: ***inhibitors (grapefruit, azoles, macrolides, protease inhibitors, verapamil, diltiazem) **slow** metabolism → **more** drug, **longer** action, **toxicity**; inducers (rifampin here, and **the barbiturates themselves,** p. 189) **speed** metabolism → **less** drug, **treatment failure**.*** Rifampin is the single row of Table 12.4 that runs against the rest of the table, which is why it is there.

> **Trap (SATA, five options mapped to categories).** **What potentiates a benzodiazepine, and what blunts it.** Sort every option into three buckets: **prolongs/increases** — azole antifungals, verapamil, diltiazem, protease inhibitors, macrolides, grapefruit juice, olanzapine, and any CNS depressant (alcohol, opioids, muscle relaxants, antihistamines, barbiturates); **decreases** — rifampin, alone; **not named in this chapter** — anticoagulants, thyroid hormone, beta blockers. Cue: ***only **rifampin** goes down.***

### The three FDA black box warnings

| Year | Applies to | Content as given | p. |
|---|---|---|---|
| **2016** | "**all opioids and all benzodiazepines** regarding the risk of **combined use**" | "should be used **only if no other alternatives are available.** Risks include **extreme sleepiness, respiratory depression, coma, and death.**" | 186 |
| **2019** | the nonbenzodiazepines **zolpidem, eszopiclone, zaleplon** | "**complex sleep behaviors including sleep-walking, sleep-driving and engaging in other activities while not fully awake**" | 184 |
| **late 2020** | "the **entire class of benzodiazepine**" | "the risk for **abuse, misuse addiction, physical dependence, and withdrawal**" | 184 |

> **Trap (level/stage swap).** **Three warnings, three years, three scopes.** Cue: ***2016 = the **combination** warning (opioid + benzodiazepine); 2019 = **complex sleep behaviors,** belonging to the **nonbenzodiazepines**; 2020 = **abuse and dependence,** belonging to the **whole benzodiazepine class**.*** A stem attaching sleep-driving to benzodiazepines as a class, or dependence to the 2019 box, has the years crossed. The printed "abuse, misuse addic-tion" is quoted as-is; see GAPS item 2.

> **Trap (priority question).** **Opioid + benzodiazepine ordered together.** The governing principle is the FDA's own conditional: use the combination "**only if no other alternatives are available.**" The assessment section operationalizes it: watch "the concurrent use of other CNS depressants (e.g., **opioids**)" because it "may lead to **severe decreases in blood pressure, respiratory rate, reflexes, and level of consciousness**" (p. 192). Cue: ***the priority is **respiratory** — human need statement 1 is "Altered oxygenation, decreased, to respiratory depression associated with CNS depressants" (p. 193).***

> **Trap (confusable pair).** **Half-life vs. duration of action — diazepam breaks the intuition.** IV diazepam has a **20–50 hour** half-life but a duration of only **15 to 60 minutes.** Cue: ***a long half-life does not mean a long clinical effect; it means a long time to clear,*** which is what matters for accumulation, hangover, and flumazenil wearing off first. Half-life is defined in Chapter 2.

> **Trap (confusable pair).** **Onset drives the timing instruction, not duration.** "if... the drug's **onset of action is 30 to 60 minutes, the drug needs to be dosed 60 minutes before bedtime**" (p. 194). Cue: ***match the teaching time to the **onset** column*** — temazepam **1 hour** before bed, suvorexant and ramelteon **30 minutes** before bed.

**diazepam (Valium)** (p. 187): "the **first clinically available benzodiazepine** drug; as such, it is the **prototypical benzodiazepine.**" Uses: "**anxiety, procedural sedation and anesthesia adjunct, anticonvulsant therapy, and skeletal muscle relaxation following orthopedic injury or surgery.**" Available "in **oral, rectal, and injectable** forms."

**midazolam (Versed)** (p. 187), the chapter's only **HIGH-ALERT DRUG** (flagged p. 184): "most commonly used **preoperatively and for moderate sedation.** It is useful... because of its ability to cause **amnesia and anxiolysis (reduced anxiety) as well as sedation.** The drug is normally given **by injection in adults.** However, a **liquid oral dosage form is also available for children.**"

**temazepam (Restoril)** (p. 187): "an **intermediate-acting** benzodiazepine, is actually **one of the metabolites of diazepam** and normally **induces sleep within 20 to 40 minutes.** Temazepam has a **long onset of action,** so it is recommended that patients **take it approximately 1 hour before going to bed.** Although it is still an effective hypnotic, **it has been replaced by the newer drugs.**"

**eszopiclone (Lunesta)** (p. 188): "the **first hypnotic to be FDA approved for long-term use.** It is designed to provide a **full 8 hours of sleep.** It is considered a **short- to intermediate-acting** agent." Patients "should **allot 8 hours of sleep time** and should **avoid taking hypnotics when they must awaken in less than 6 to 8 hours.**" From assessment (p. 192): "**Do not use eszopiclone in those younger than 18 years,** and use extreme caution if there is a history of **compromised respiratory status or drug, alcohol, or other substance abuse.**"

**ramelteon (Rozerem)** (p. 188): "**structurally similar to the hormone melatonin,** which is thought to regulate **circadian rhythms**... Ramelteon works as an **agonist at melatonin receptors in the CNS. Technically it is not a CNS depressant,** but it is included here because of its use as a hypnotic. It is also **not classified as a controlled substance because of its lack of observed dependency risk.** It has a **shorter duration of action than do other hypnotics** and is therefore **indicated primarily for patients who have difficulty with sleep onset rather than sleep maintenance.** Its use is **contraindicated in cases of severe liver dysfunction.** It is best **avoided in patients receiving fluconazole or ketoconazole** (see Chapter 42)... **Rifampin** (see Chapter 41) can **reduce the efficacy** of ramelteon by **speeding its metabolism.**" The nursing sections add: it is "**not associated with CNS depression, does not carry the potential for abuse or dependence, and does not lead to withdrawal symptoms**," so it "**may be an option for patients who are likely to be abusers of CNS depressants**"; **liver function studies** are needed first; it is **not indicated** in COPD, sleep apnea, or children; and it must not be "**mixed with alcohol**," is taken "**30 minutes before bedtime**," and "**not... along with or immediately following a high-fat meal**" (pp. 193–194).

> **Trap (confusable pair).** **Ramelteon vs. every other drug in this chapter.** It is the exception on four axes at once. Cue: ***ramelteon is **not a controlled substance,** **not technically a CNS depressant,** works on **melatonin receptors** rather than GABA, and is for **sleep onset, not maintenance**.*** The case study is built on this: "P.S. receives a prescription for ramelteon (Rozerem). **How is this drug different from alprazolam?**" (p. 195).

> **Trap (plausible-but-adjacent).** **"Not a controlled substance" is not "no contraindications."** Ramelteon is still contraindicated in **severe liver dysfunction,** still needs **baseline liver studies,** is still **not indicated in COPD, sleep apnea, or children,** and still must not be combined with **alcohol, fluconazole, or ketoconazole.** Treating the lack of abuse potential as general safety clearance answers a different question than the stem asks.

**zolpidem (Ambien)** (p. 188): "a **short-acting nonbenzodiazepine hypnotic.** Its **short half-life,** and its **lack of active metabolites** contribute to a **lower incidence of daytime sleepiness**...; however, the **FDA now recommends doses of 5 mg as a maximum dose for women or 5 to 10 mg for men,** because of risk for **next-morning impairment.**" **Ambien CR** is "a **longer-acting form with two separate drug reservoirs**," one fast to "induce hypnosis (sleep) more rapidly," the second releasing "more slowly throughout the night to help maintain sleep"; its special concern is "**somnambulation, or sleepwalking.**" It is "currently **one of only two hypnotics to be FDA approved for long-term use; the other is eszopiclone.**" Administration (p. 194): "**optimal absorption if taken at bedtime on an empty stomach with no crushing, chewing, or breaking**"; it "may infrequently lead to **temporary memory loss**," so do not dose it "**without a full night's sleep (e.g., at least 7 to 8 hours) the previous night.**"

> **Trap (confusable pair).** **The zolpidem dose is sex-specific; almost nothing else here is.** Cue: ***women **5 mg max**; men **5 to 10 mg**; the reason is **next-morning impairment**.*** Review question 8 (p. 197) uses it: a patient discharged after a **hysterectomy** has "**zolpidem, 10 mg at bedtime,**" with options to review adverse effects, suggest a glass of wine, **contact the prescriber to question the dose,** or find a pharmacy. Two options fail on chapter content independently: **wine** is an additive CNS depressant, and reviewing adverse effects leaves a dose the FDA capped at half. (Reasoned from body text — GAPS item 11.)

> **Trap (confusable pair).** **Empty stomach vs. with food — the chapter gives both, for different reasons.** Cue: ***the **pharmacokinetic** rule is empty stomach for faster onset; the **tolerability** rule is with food to prevent GI upset*** (both stated together, p. 194). Zolpidem comes down firmly on **empty stomach**; **barbiturates** get the opposite ("take oral doses **with food or a light snack**"); **ramelteon** has a third rule, not with or right after a **high-fat meal**.

**suvorexant (Belsomra)** (p. 188): "the **first drug in a new class, called orexin receptor antagonists. Orexin A and orexin B are hypothalamic neuropeptides that play a key role in promoting wakefulness and regulating the sleep-wake cycle.** Suvorexant is an **oral dual orexin receptor antagonist with a 12-hour half-life.** Adverse effects include **drowsiness, headache, dizziness, diarrhea, dry mouth, increased serum cholesterol, and cough. Many of the adverse effects are more common in females.** ...there are **safety concerns regarding daytime somnolence and unconscious nighttime behaviors.** Suvorexant is a **schedule IV** drug. **Lemborexant (DayVigo) is a similar drug.**" Class note: "**Orexins, also referred to as hypocretins,**... **regulate transitions between wakefulness and sleep by promoting cholinergic/monoaminergic neural pathways. Orexin antagonists compete with the physiologic effects of orexin.**" From p. 184: "**Tasimelteon is indicated only for disturbances of sleep-wake cycle in patients who are totally blind.**"

> **Trap (SATA, five options mapped to categories).** **Review question 3 (p. 197), five-option select-all on suvorexant teaching.** **(a) take about 30 minutes before bedtime** — matches the profile's **30-min onset** and the onset-based dosing rule (p. 194). **(b) take 2 hours before bedtime because of slow onset** — contradicted by that same 30-min onset. **(c) caution about daytime somnolence and unconscious nighttime behaviors** — stated verbatim (pp. 188, 196). **(d) less likely to interact with alcohol** — nothing exempts suvorexant from the additive CNS-depressant rule. **(e) available over the counter** — contradicted by "**schedule IV.**" Cue: ***schedule IV is never OTC, and the "slow onset" option is a fabricated pharmacokinetic.*** (Reasoned from body text — GAPS item 11.)

> **Trap (confusable pair).** **Orexin antagonist vs. melatonin agonist.** Cue: ***suvorexant **blocks a wake-promoting** peptide, half-life **12 hr,** **schedule IV,** adverse effects worse in **females**; ramelteon **agonizes a sleep-promoting** receptor, half-life **1–2.5 hr, not scheduled,** for **sleep onset**.*** Two one-word neighbors sit nearby: **tasimelteon (Hetlioz)** for **totally blind** patients only, and **lemborexant (DayVigo),** the second orexin antagonist.

> **Trap (plausible-but-adjacent).** **Suvorexant carries the chapter's only sex-linked *adverse-effect* statement, made three times** (pp. 188, 194, 196). Do not merge it with the **zolpidem** sex rule, which is about **dose,** not adverse-effect frequency.

## §5 Barbiturates

"Barbiturates were **first introduced into clinical use in 1903**... Chemically they are **derivatives of barbituric acid.** Although **50 different barbiturates are approved for clinical use in the United States, only a few are currently in clinical use.** This is due to the **favorable safety profile and proven efficacy of the benzodiazepines.**... They are **physiologically habit forming and have a low therapeutic index**" (p. 188).

**Mechanism** (p. 189): "**CNS depressants that act primarily on the brainstem in an area called the reticular formation.** Their **sedative and hypnotic effects are dose related,** and they act by **reducing the nerve impulses traveling to... the cerebral cortex.**" They "**potentiate the action of the inhibitory neurotransmitter GABA**" and "**raise the seizure threshold and can be used to treat seizures** (see Chapter 14)."

**Table 12.5 (p. 189) merged with the Indications paragraph:**

| Duration | Drugs (generic → trade) | Therapeutic purposes, as given |
|---|---|---|
| **Ultrashort acting** | methohexital → Brevital; thiopental → Pentothal | "**anesthesia for short surgical procedures, anesthesia induction, control of convulsions, and reduction of intracranial pressure in neurosurgical patients**" |
| **Short acting** | pentobarbital → Nembutal; secobarbital → Seconal | "**sedation and control of convulsive conditions**" |
| **Intermediate acting** | butabarbital → Butisol | "**sedation and control of convulsive conditions**" |
| **Long acting** | phenobarbital → Generic; mephobarbital → Mebaral | "**epileptic seizure prophylaxis**" |

Framing sentence (p. 189): "**All barbiturates have the same sedative-hypnotic effects but differ in their potency, time to onset of action, and duration of action.** It is important to note that **the use of barbiturates is no longer recommended for sleep induction.**"

**Table 12.7 Barbiturates: Controlled Substance Schedule** (p. 190): **C-II** = pentobarbital, secobarbital. **C-III** = butabarbital, thiopental. **C-IV** = mephobarbital, methohexital, phenobarbital.

> **Trap (level/stage swap).** **Four durations, only two distinct indication sets.** Cue: ***ultrashort = **anesthesia** (plus ICP reduction); short **and** intermediate = **sedation and convulsive conditions,** worded identically; long = **seizure prophylaxis**.*** Since short and intermediate share the exact printed indication, any item separating them must do it on the **drug name**: ultrashort = methohexital and thiopental; short = pentobarbital and secobarbital; intermediate = butabarbital alone; long = phenobarbital and mephobarbital.

> **Trap (level/stage swap).** **Controlled-substance schedule does NOT track duration of action.** The intuitive rule — shorter acting, tighter schedule — fails on two of seven. Cue: ***thiopental is **ultrashort** but only **C-III**; methohexital is **ultrashort** but only **C-IV**; **pentobarbital and secobarbital, both short acting, are C-II**,*** the tightest in the chapter. Phenobarbital, the long-acting prototype, is **C-IV**, the same as the benzodiazepines. Read Tables 12.5 and 12.7 as independent lists.

> **Trap (confusable pair).** **Barbiturates raise the seizure threshold; they do not lower it.** Raising the threshold means **harder** to trigger a seizure, which is why phenobarbital is an anticonvulsant. Withdrawal runs the other way: abrupt withdrawal after prolonged therapy produces "**nightmares, hallucinations, and delirium to seizures**" (p. 194). Cue: ***on the drug = seizure protection; off it abruptly = seizure risk.***

**Adverse effects.** Body text (p. 189): "drowsiness, lethargy, dizziness, **hangover,** and **paradoxical restlessness or excitement.**" **Table 12.6** (p. 189): **Cardiovascular** "**Vasodilation and hypotension, especially if given too rapidly**"; **GI** "Nausea, vomiting, diarrhea, constipation"; **Hematologic** "**Agranulocytosis, thrombocytopenia**"; **Nervous** "Drowsiness, lethargy, vertigo"; **Respiratory** "**Respiratory depression,** cough"; **Other** "**Hypersensitivity reactions: urticaria, angioedema, rash, fever, Stevens-Johnson syndrome.**"

**Sleep architecture** (p. 189): "**Barbiturates deprive people of REM sleep, which can result in agitation.** When any barbiturate is stopped, a **rebound phenomenon** may occur. During this rebound, **the proportion of REM sleep is increased and nightmares often ensue.**"

**Contraindications** (p. 189): "known **drug allergy, pregnancy, significant respiratory difficulties,** and **severe kidney or liver disease.**" Caution in older adults because of "**sedative properties and increased fall risk.**"

**Toxicity and overdose** (p. 189): "**mainly symptomatic and supportive.** The mainstays... are **maintenance of an adequate airway, assisted ventilation, and oxygen administration if needed,** along with **fluid and pressor support.** **Activated charcoal may be given; however, recent clinical data do not support its use because no improvement in clinical outcome has been shown.**" And: "**Phenobarbital and mephobarbital are relatively acidic and can be eliminated more quickly by the kidneys when the urine is alkalized (pH is raised).** This **keeps the drug in the urine and prevents it from being resorbed back into the circulation. Alkalization, along with forced diuresis using diuretics (e.g., furosemide** [see Chapter 28]**), can hasten elimination.**"

> **Trap (confusable pair).** **Only one class here has an antidote.** Cue: ***benzodiazepine → **flumazenil**; barbiturate → **alkalinize the urine and force diuresis**; muscle relaxant → "**There is no specific antidote (or reversal drug)**" (p. 191).*** An item offering flumazenil for a barbiturate or muscle-relaxant overdose trades on all three being CNS depressants.

> **Trap (plausible-but-adjacent).** **Activated charcoal is named and then dismissed** in the same sentence. Choosing it means picking a true statement about what is sometimes done, against the chapter's own evidence verdict.

> **Trap (confusable pair).** **Alkalinize, do not acidify.** Cue: ***the barbiturate is **acidic,** so **raise** the urine pH to trap it there.*** The printed mechanism — "keeps the drug in the urine and prevents it from being **resorbed**" — is the discriminator; an option saying alkalinization "neutralizes" the drug has the wrong mechanism on the right action.

**Interactions and enzyme induction** (pp. 189–190): "Barbiturates as a class are **notorious enzyme inducers.**... they cause **many drugs to be metabolized more quickly, which usually shortens their duration of action.** Barbiturates **increase the activity of hepatic microsomal or cytochrome P-450 enzymes** (see Chapter 2). This process is called **enzyme induction.**... **Other drugs that are enzyme inducers are rifampin and phenytoin.**" Clinical Pearl (p. 190): "**Barbiturates are enzyme inducers — meaning they increase metabolism of other drugs.**" The chapter also notes the reverse case: "if **two drugs are competing for the same enzyme system,** the result can be **inhibited drug metabolism and possibly increased toxicity.**"

| Interacting drug | Result as given |
|---|---|
| **alcohol, antihistamines, benzodiazepines, opioids, tranquilizers** | "**Additive CNS depression**" |
| **MAOIs** | "**prolonged barbiturate effects**" |
| **anticoagulants** (Ch. 26) | "**decreased anticoagulation response and possible clot formation**" |
| **oral contraceptives** (Ch. 34) | "**accelerated metabolism of the contraceptive drug and possible unintended pregnancy**"; advise "an **additional method of contraception as a backup**" |
| **tricyclic antidepressants** (Ch. 16), **glucocorticoids** | among "Drugs most likely to have marked interactions with the barbiturates" |

> **Trap (confusable pair).** **Enzyme induction lowers the *other* drug's level, not the barbiturate's.** Cue: ***the barbiturate is the **inducer**; the victim drug is the one that **fails**.*** Anchors: the anticoagulant stops working (**clot formation,** not bleeding) and the contraceptive stops working (**unintended pregnancy**). An option predicting increased bleeding from warfarin plus a barbiturate has the direction inverted.

> **Trap (plausible-but-adjacent).** **MAOIs run against the pattern.** Every other row is additive CNS depression or induction-driven failure of the other drug. MAOIs instead cause "**prolonged barbiturate effects**" — the barbiturate is the affected drug. Cue: ***MAOI is the row where the arrow points back at the barbiturate.***

**pentobarbital (Nembutal)** (p. 190): "a **short-acting** barbiturate. **Formerly prescribed as a sedative-hypnotic for insomnia,** pentobarbital is now **principally used preoperatively to relieve anxiety and provide sedation.** In addition, it is used occasionally to **control status epilepticus,**" and "to **treat withdrawal symptoms in patients who are physically dependent on barbiturates or nonbarbiturate hypnotics.**" Available "in **oral, injectable, and rectal**" forms. IV: onset 30–60 min, peak 1–2 hr, half-life **15–50 hr**, duration 3–4 hr.

**phenobarbital** (p. 190): "the **prototypical barbiturate** and is classified as a **long-acting** drug... used for the **prevention of generalized tonic-clonic seizures and fever-induced convulsions.** In addition, it has been useful in the treatment of **hyperbilirubinemia in neonates.** Currently it is **only rarely used as a sedative and is no longer recommended to be used as a hypnotic drug.**" Available "in **oral and injectable** forms." IV onset 5 min, peak 30 min; PO onset 60 min, peak 2–4 hr; half-life **50–120 hr**; duration 6–12 hr.

**Dosages: Selected Barbiturates** (p. 190): **pentobarbital (Nembutal),** short acting, **IM 150–200 mg; IV 100 mg,** preoperative sedative. **phenobarbital,** long acting, **PO 30–120 mg/day divided; IM/IV 100–200 mg 60–90 min before surgery,** sedative and preoperative sedative.

**Phenobarbital drug levels** (p. 194): "**the therapeutic level of phenobarbital must range between 15 and 40 mcg/mL. Patients with serum levels greater than 40 mcg/mL may experience toxicity manifested by cold and clammy skin, respiratory rate of less than 10 breaths/min, and other signs of severe CNS depression.**"

> **Trap (level/stage swap).** **Phenobarbital's numbers: 15–40 therapeutic, above 40 toxic, and the toxic signs are specific.** Cue: ***the triad is **cold and clammy skin, respiratory rate under 10, severe CNS depression**.*** An option offering a rate of 10 as the threshold is off by one in the unsafe direction — the chapter says **less than 10**.

> **Trap (confusable pair).** **pentobarbital vs. phenobarbital, one syllable apart.** Cue: ***pento- = **short** acting, **C-II**, **preoperative** sedation and **status epilepticus,** half-life **15–50 hr**; pheno- = **long** acting, **C-IV**, **seizure prophylaxis** and **neonatal hyperbilirubinemia,** half-life **50–120 hr,** and the **prototype**.*** Both are no longer recommended as hypnotics; routes differ, pentobarbital adding a **rectal** form.

> **Trap (confusable pair).** **Two "prototypical" drugs in one chapter.** Cue: ***diazepam = **prototypical benzodiazepine** (and the first clinically available); phenobarbital = **prototypical barbiturate**.*** They share seizure ground — status epilepticus for diazepam, tonic-clonic prophylaxis for phenobarbital — which is what makes the pair usable.

**Over-the-counter hypnotics** (p. 190): "Nonprescription sleeping aids often contain **antihistamines** (see Chapter 36). These drugs have a **CNS depressant effect.** The most common... are **doxylamine (Unisom) and diphenhydramine (Sominex). Analgesics (e.g., acetaminophen** [see Chapter 10]**) are sometimes added**... (e.g., **Extra Strength Tylenol PM**). As with other CNS depressants, **concurrent use of alcohol can cause additive CNS depression.** The herbal product **melatonin** is commonly used as a sleep aid."

> **Trap (plausible-but-adjacent).** **"Over the counter" is not "safe to add."** Diphenhydramine appears twice as a hazard: as an OTC hypnotic ingredient, and in planning outcome 4 as "other **sedating over-the-counter products such as diphenhydramine**" (p. 193). The rule: "**Always check with the prescriber or pharmacist before taking any over-the-counter medications** because of the many drug interactions with CNS depressants" (p. 196).

## §6 Muscle relaxants

**The two-way split, the section's spine** (p. 191): "**Most muscle relaxants are known as centrally acting skeletal muscle relaxants because their site of action is the CNS.** Centrally acting skeletal muscle relaxants are **similar in structure and action to other CNS depressants such as diazepam.**... **Only one of these compounds, dantrolene, acts directly on skeletal muscle.** It belongs to a group of relaxants known as **direct-acting skeletal muscle relaxants. It closely resembles GABA.**" Spasms follow "**trauma, inflammation, anxiety, and pain**" (p. 190).

**Mechanism** (p. 191). Centrally acting: "Their **beneficial effects are thought to come from their sedative effects rather than from direct muscle relaxation**"; they have "**no direct effects on muscles, nerve conduction, or muscle-nerve junctions.**" Dantrolene: "acts directly on the **excitation-contraction coupling of muscle fibers** and **not at the level of the CNS**... by **decreasing the amount of calcium released from storage sites in the sarcoplasmic reticula of muscle fibers.**"

**Effects** (p. 191): "**relaxation of striated muscles, mild weakness of skeletal muscles, decreased force of muscle contraction, and muscle stiffness,**" plus "**generalized CNS depression manifested as sedation, somnolence, ataxia, and respiratory and cardiovascular depression.**"

**Indications** (p. 191): "**relief of painful musculoskeletal conditions such as muscle spasms, often following injuries such as low back strain.** They are **most effective when used in conjunction with physical therapy.**" Also "**spasticity associated with severe chronic disorders such as multiple sclerosis and other types of cerebral lesions. Intravenous dantrolene is used for the management of skeletal muscle spasms that accompany the crisis condition known as malignant hyperthermia** (see Chapter 11). **Baclofen has been shown to be effective in relieving hiccups.**"

**Contraindications** (p. 191): "The **only usual contraindication**... is **known drug allergy,** but contraindications for some drugs may include **severe renal impairment.**" The drug profiles paragraph on the same page instead says use is contraindicated with "a **hypersensitivity reaction**... or **compromised pulmonary function, active hepatic disease, or impaired myocardial function.**" Not reconciled; see GAPS item 7.

**Adverse effects** (p. 191): "an **extension of their effects on the CNS and skeletal muscles. Euphoria, lightheadedness, dizziness, drowsiness, fatigue, confusion, and muscle weakness** are often experienced **early in treatment. These adverse effects are generally short lived because patients grow tolerant to them over time.**" Less common: "diarrhea, GI upset, headache, **slurred speech,** muscle stiffness, constipation, **sexual difficulties in males, hypotension, tachycardia,** and weight gain."

**Toxicity** (p. 191): "primarily involve the CNS. **There is no specific antidote (or reversal drug)**... best treated with **conservative supportive measures.**... **An adequate airway must be maintained**... **Electrocardiographic monitoring** needs to be instituted, and **large quantities of intravenous fluids are administered to avoid crystalluria.**"

**Interactions** (p. 191): with "**alcohol and benzodiazepines,**" watch for "**Mental confusion, anxiety, tremors, and additive hypoglycemic activity.**" A "**dosage reduction and/or discontinuance of one or both drugs**" is recommended.

**The drugs.** Centrally acting (p. 191): "**baclofen (Lioresal), carisoprodol (Soma), chlorzoxazone (Paraflex), cyclobenzaprine (Flexeril), metaxalone (Skelaxin), methocarbamol (Robaxin), and tizanidine (Zanaflex).**" Direct acting: **dantrolene (Dantrium)** alone. Abuse note: "**Carisoprodol has become a popular drug of abuse.** When combined with an **opioid and a benzodiazepine,** it is known as '**The Holy Trinity.**' This combination produces a **heroin-like effect.**"

**Dosages: Selected Muscle Relaxants** (p. 191): **baclofen (Lioresal) (C),** centrally acting, **PO 5–20 mg 3 times daily (max 20 mg PO qid),** spasticity. **cyclobenzaprine (Flexeril) (B),** centrally acting, **PO 5–10 mg TID,** spasticity.

**baclofen** (p. 192): "available in **both oral and injectable** dosage forms. The **injectable form is for use with an implantable baclofen pump device**... With this route, **a test dose needs to be administered initially to test for a positive response.**" PO: onset 0.5–1 hr, peak 2–3 hr, half-life 2.5–4 hr, duration **8 hr or longer**.

**cyclobenzaprine** (p. 192): "available in a **5- and 10-mg dose** and an **extended-release formulation (Amrix).** ...**structurally and pharmacologically related to the tricyclic antidepressants.** It is the **most commonly used drug in this class to reduce spasms after musculoskeletal injuries.** It is **very common for patients to exhibit marked sedation from its use.**" PO: onset 1 hr, peak 3–8 hr, half-life 8–37 hr, duration 12–24 hr.

> **Trap (confusable pair).** **Centrally acting vs. direct acting.** Cue: ***every muscle relaxant here works in the **CNS** except **dantrolene,** which works **on the muscle fiber itself** by cutting **calcium release from the sarcoplasmic reticulum**.*** Two consequences are separately testable: the centrally acting drugs relieve spasm **through sedation,** and dantrolene is the one given **IV for malignant hyperthermia.** Crediting a centrally acting relaxant with direct muscle action, or giving baclofen for malignant hyperthermia, swaps the axis.

> **Trap (priority question).** **The priority adverse effect of a muscle relaxant is CNS depression.** Review question 4 (p. 197) offers **CNS depression** / hypertension / peripheral edema / blurred vision; critical thinking exercise 2 (p. 196) asks the same in essay form. The chapter's answer is structural — the class's effects are "an **extension of their effects on the CNS**" — and implementation escalates it: "**frequent monitoring of airway, breathing, and circulation is warranted. Early identification of toxicity is critical**" (p. 194). Note the direction of the vital-sign distractor: the chapter lists **hypotension,** not hypertension. (Reasoned from body text — GAPS item 11.)

> **Trap (confusable pair).** **baclofen vs. cyclobenzaprine.** Both centrally acting, both three times daily, both listed for spasticity. Cue: ***baclofen = the **implantable pump** (and therefore a **test dose**) and **hiccups**; cyclobenzaprine = related to the **tricyclic antidepressants,** **most commonly used** after musculoskeletal injury, and known for **marked sedation**.*** Printed pregnancy categories also differ: baclofen **(C)**, cyclobenzaprine **(B)** — obsolete labeling, GAPS item 8.

> **Trap (plausible-but-adjacent).** **Muscle relaxant tolerance is to the adverse effects, not to the benefit.** "generally **short lived because patients grow tolerant to them over time**" (p. 191) is a reassurance about early euphoria, dizziness, and drowsiness. Do not generalize it to the benzodiazepine sense, where the patient "may **require larger dosages to produce the same therapeutic effect**" (p. 194). Same word, opposite implication.

> **Trap (plausible-but-adjacent).** **Muscle relaxants are not insomnia drugs, even though they sedate.** They "have **different indications**... and are **not used to treat insomnia**" (p. 194). The sedation is a mechanism and an adverse effect, not an indication.

> **Trap (priority question).** **Muscle relaxant hypotension has a time window.** "**The greatest risk for hypotension... is usually within 1 hour of dosing,** so caution must be taken with activity" (p. 194). Cue: ***first hour = fall risk;*** teach the patient to "**change positions purposefully and slowly to prevent syncope or dizziness**" and to ambulate cautiously.

## §7 Nursing process and administration safety

### Assessment, by class (pp. 192–193)

**Common baseline** (p. 192), condensed from the seven-item list: insomnia complaints with **onset, duration, frequency** and what has been tried; family-reported sleep patterns and awakenings; **time to fall asleep and energy on awakening**; vital signs including **supine and standing blood pressure** and **respiratory rate, rhythm, and depth**; a full physical exam for baseline; **neurologic findings** — mental status, memory, cognition, alertness, **orientation to person, place, and time,** level of sedation, mood; and history including allergies, **alcohol, smoking, caffeine intake (especially 6 hours before bedtime),** all prescription, OTC, and **herbal** products, and **life stressors including loss and grief.**

**Benzodiazepines** (p. 192): "**Closely monitor those who are anemic or suicidal or who have a history of abusing drugs, alcohol, or other substances.**" Cautions in "the **very young or in older adults** because of their **increased sensitivity**" and in "the **pregnant or lactating** patient"; these patients "may require **lower dosages because of potential ataxia and excessive sedation.**" Baselines: "**blood studies, such as CBC.** **Renal function studies (BUN or creatinine levels)** and/or **hepatic function studies (ALP level)**... to **rule out organ impairment and prevent potential toxicity.**"

**Nonbenzodiazepines** (p. 192): "**Assess and document allergies to these drugs and to aspirin. If the patient is allergic to aspirin, there is an associated risk for allergies to nonbenzodiazepines.**" Also neurologic assessment for "**confusion and lightheadedness, especially in older adults**," and "**Gender of the patient is also important to consider... because of the reduced dosage recommended by the FDA for female patients.**"

**Barbiturates** (p. 193): "**not to be used by pregnant or lactating women. These drugs cross the placenta and breast-blood barriers, posing a risk for respiratory depression in the fetus and neonate. Withdrawal symptoms may appear in neonates born to women who have taken barbiturates during their last trimester.** ...**Barbiturates may also produce paradoxical excitement in children; confusion and mental depression may occur in the older adult.**" Assess **renal and liver function** in compromised patients and older adults.

**Muscle relaxants** (p. 192): note **drug allergies,** do a head-to-toe with a **neurologic** focus; in older adults there is "**increased risk for CNS toxicity with possible hallucinations, confusion, and excessive sedation.**"

> **Trap (confusable pair).** **Which baseline goes with which class.** Cue: ***benzodiazepine = **CBC, BUN/creatinine, ALP** plus a suicide/substance screen; nonbenzodiazepine = **aspirin allergy** and **patient sex**; barbiturate = **pregnancy/lactation** and **renal/liver function**; ramelteon = **liver studies** and a **respiratory history**; muscle relaxant = **drug allergy** and a **neurologic** exam.*** The **aspirin** cross-allergy is the most surprising item on the list and therefore the most likely tested.

> **Trap (level/stage swap).** **Age extremes cut both ways, with different findings.** Cue: ***children on barbiturates → **paradoxical excitement**; older adults on barbiturates → **confusion and mental depression**; older adults on muscle relaxants → **hallucinations, confusion, excessive sedation**; older adults on benzodiazepines → **falls,** answered with the **lowest effective dose**.*** All four rest on "increased sensitivity" but land differently.

**Human need statements** (p. 193), abbreviated: 1. **Altered oxygenation** related to **respiratory depression.** 2. **Decreased self-determination** from **inadequate information** about first-time use. 3. **Risk for injury** from **decreased sensorium.** 4. **Risk for injury** from **overdose or drug-drug interactions** (alcohol, tranquilizers, analgesics), **decreased alertness,** and **unsteady gait.** 5. **Risk for injury** from **physical or psychological dependence.**

**Planning outcomes** (p. 193), abbreviated: normal **gas exchange** and no respiratory depression; **adequate knowledge** of sedating properties and interactions; **free from self-injury and falls,** with safety measures — "**removing all throw rugs from walking areas (especially at night), changing positions slowly, ambulating with caution, and well-lit rooms at night**"; free from injury via knowledge of interactions including "**other sedating over-the-counter products such as diphenhydramine**"; and **no drug dependence,** reporting "**drug resistance, excessive sedation, or the need for more medication.**"

### Implementation: the administration rules that carry safety weight

**IV diazepam** (p. 194): "**do not mix or dilute with other solutions/drugs in syringe or infusion container. Intravenous diazepam must be injected slowly, at least 1 minute for each 5 mg given,** through the infusion tubing **as close as possible to the vein insertion site. Do not use small veins,** and avoid **intraarterial administration or extravasation.**"

**IV barbiturates** (p. 194): "requires **dilution of the drug with normal saline**... **Most of the drugs are not to be given any faster than 1 mg/kg/min**... **Too rapid an infusion of a barbiturate may produce profound hypotension and marked respiratory depression.**" **Infiltration:** the site becomes "**swollen, erythematous, and tender. Tissue necrosis may occur**"; for phenobarbital, "the solution must be **discontinued, a 0.5% procaine solution injected into the affected area, and moist heat applied**," and "**in certain situations, the intravenous catheter may be left in place until antidotes are administered.**" **Incompatibilities:** "**amphotericin B, hydrocortisone, and hydromorphone**"; give them "only after the intravenous line has been adequately flushed with normal saline." **IM:** "**deep into a large muscle mass to prevent tissue sloughing;** however, **avoid this route and use only when absolutely necessary.**" **Monitoring:** "**red blood cell count and hemoglobin and hematocrit levels** because of the possible adverse effect of **anemia.**"

**Timing** (pp. 194, 196): "avoid administering these drugs **late in the night**... **dosing before midnight may help prevent daytime drowsiness or difficulty waking**"; teaching repeats "**Medications taken for sleep are to be given before midnight.**"

**REM interference** (p. 194): "**REM interference is less problematic with flurazepam, quazepam, and estazolam,** primarily because they **produce fewer active metabolites.** Educate patients about the **REM interference and rebound insomnia that may occur with just a 3- to 4-week regimen**... benzodiazepines and other drugs are used **only when nonpharmacologic methods fail**... and **for short periods of time.**"

**Tolerance** (p. 194): "the patient may **require larger dosages to produce the same therapeutic effect,** at some point. **Interrupting therapy helps to decrease drug tolerance.**"

**Safety measures** (p. 194): "**side rails or bed alarms. Ambulation needs to occur safely and with assistance.**" Patients must "**avoid driving or participating in any activities that require mental alertness.**"

> **Trap (confusable pair).** **Two different IV rate rules, one page apart.** Cue: ***IV diazepam = **at least 1 minute per 5 mg**; IV barbiturates = **no faster than 1 mg/kg/min**.*** One is fixed time per fixed dose, the other weight-based. Dilution reverses too: diazepam must **not** be mixed or diluted; barbiturates **require** dilution with normal saline. Diluting the diazepam or pushing the barbiturate undiluted exchanges the two drugs' rules.

> **Trap (priority question).** **Too-rapid IV administration is the chapter's recurring near-miss.** Three statements converge: hypotension "**especially if given too rapidly**" (Table 12.6); "**profound hypotension and marked respiratory depression**" (p. 194); and Key Points, "**too rapid an infusion may precipitate respiratory and/or cardiac arrest**" (p. 196). Cue: ***with an IV CNS depressant in the stem, **rate** is the intervention.***

> **Trap (plausible-but-adjacent).** **Do not pull the IV catheter on a barbiturate infiltration.** The reflex — stop and remove the line — is half right. The chapter stops the solution but may keep the catheter "**until antidotes are administered.**" The named protocol is **0.5% procaine plus moist heat** for phenobarbital.

### Evaluation (p. 195)

CNS depressant response: "an **increased ability to sleep at night, fewer awakenings, shorter sleep induction time, few adverse effects such as hangover effects,** and an **improved sense of well-being.**" Muscle relaxant response: "**decreased spasticity, reduction of choreiform movements in Huntington chorea, decreased rigidity in parkinsonian syndrome,** and **relief of pain from trigeminal neuralgia.**" Toxicity: watch for "**severe CNS depression of all body systems, especially respiratory and circulatory collapse,** with **decrease in respiratory rate/depth and decrease in blood pressure.**"

> **Trap (plausible-but-adjacent).** **The muscle relaxant *evaluation* criteria name three conditions the *indications* section does not.** Huntington chorea, parkinsonian rigidity, and trigeminal neuralgia appear only on p. 195; p. 191 names muscle spasm, low back strain, spasticity in MS and cerebral lesions, malignant hyperthermia, and hiccups. Cue: ***indications → answer from p. 191; evaluating the response → p. 195 adds three more.*** See GAPS item 6.

### Box 12.1, nonpharmacologic measures (p. 195)

The **sleep diary** records bed and wake times, how long and how well the patient sleeps, night awakenings, ease of falling asleep and waking, caffeine and alcohol intake, last food or drink after dinner, bedtime snacks, emotions and stressors, daily medications, smoking, OTC drugs, and herbals.

**Interventions,** as listed: **set sleep pattern** with fixed bed and wake times, to "**reset your internal clock**"; "**Sleep only as much as you need to feel refreshed**... **Too much sleep may lead to fragmented sleep patterns and shallow sleep**"; **moderate bedroom temperature**; "**Decrease exposure to loud noises**"; "**Avoid daytime napping**"; "**Avoid exercise late in the evening (i.e., not past 7 pm)**"; "**Avoid any stimulants, such as caffeine, nicotine, and alcohol, close to bedtime. Avoid alcohol in the evening because rather than putting you to sleep, it actually results in fragmented sleep**"; **no caffeine within 6 hours of bedtime** (energy drinks up to **160 mg**, chocolate beverage about **4 mg** per ounce, OTC pain relievers/cold medicines/diet pills **16 to 200 mg**, and "**Decaffeinated coffee also contains some caffeine**"); "**Avoid tobacco at bedtime**"; **relax before bed** with soft music, yoga, relaxation therapy, deep breathing, or light reading; **a warm decaffeinated beverage 30 minutes to 1 hour before bed**; "**If you are still awake 20 minutes after going to bed, get up and engage in a relaxing activity**... and go back to bed once you feel drowsy"; **adequate natural light exposure**; and "**Associate your bed with sleep**" and not with television, radio, or reading.

**Caffeine elimination** (p. 195): "approximately **5 to 7 hours for approximately 50% elimination and 8 to 10 hours for 75% of elimination.** **Caffeine consumption in the morning will not interfere with sleep at night**... sleep will most likely not be affected **if caffeine is avoided at least 6 hours before bedtime.**"

> **Trap (priority question).** **Nonpharmacologic measures come first — stated four times.** Key Points open with it (p. 196); implementation repeats it (p. 194); patient teaching repeats it (p. 195); and the REM section gives the reason, drugs "used **only when nonpharmacologic methods fail**" (p. 194). Cue: ***on a "what does the nurse do first" sleep stem, the default is the **nondrug** measure.*** The override is a prescriber order plus a failed nondrug trial, not patient request alone — which is the tension in critical thinking exercise 1 (p. 196), a patient awake at **4 am** asking for prn temazepam. Relevant facts there: temazepam has a **long onset,** is taken **about 1 hour before bed,** lasts **7 to 8 hours,** and "**avoid administering these drugs late in the night**" / "**dosing before midnight.**" (Reasoned from body text — GAPS item 11.)

> **Trap (confusable pair).** **Alcohol is a sedative that worsens sleep.** Box 12.1 says it outright: "**rather than putting you to sleep, it actually results in fragmented sleep.**" It is also additive with every class here. Cue: ***alcohol is never a correct sleep intervention in this chapter, in any form,*** which is what makes "suggest a glass of wine at bedtime" a clean wrong answer in review question 8.

> **Trap (level/stage swap).** **Caffeine elimination is a two-tier fact.** Cue: ***50% gone in **5 to 7 hours**; 75% gone in **8 to 10 hours**; practical rule, avoid caffeine **at least 6 hours** before bed.*** The 6-hour figure also appears in the assessment list, so it is the number the chapter uses operationally.

**Patient teaching, remaining items** (pp. 195–196): keep a **sleep journal**; keep drugs **out of the reach of children**; take **only as prescribed** and "if one dose does not work, **not to double up on the dosage**"; observe **driving and machinery** restrictions; get **assistance with ambulation**; "**Do not abruptly discontinue**... to **avoid rebound insomnia**"; sedative-hypnotics "are **not intended for long-term use** because of their **adverse effects, interference with REM sleep, and addictive properties**"; **hangover effects** are "more problematic in older adults or in patients with **altered renal and hepatic function**"; avoid "**smoking in bed or when lounging**"; and **grapefruit** causes "**decreased drug metabolism by inhibition of the cytochrome P-450 system**" with "a **prolonged effect and possible toxicity.**"

## §8 The chapter's own questions

The chapter poses **2 critical thinking exercises,** a **3-question case study,** and **8 review questions.** Their answers live in two places, neither in this pull; all reasoning below comes from the chapter's body text. See GAPS item 11.

**Case study, P.S.** (p. 195): a 68-year-old retired secretary "feeling 'so tired' during the day," taking **alprazolam (Xanax)** prn "for nerves" but in fact "using alprazolam **almost every night for a week** to help her get to sleep," who says "**I am sleeping very well, but I'm so tired during the day... I don't understand how I can get such good sleep and still feel tired!**" The questions: explain the tiredness; how ramelteon differs from alprazolam; what nonpharmacologic measures she can try.

> **Trap (confusable pair).** **"Sleeping well but tired all day" is the printed signature of REM interference,** not of insufficient sleep. "Prolonged sedative-hypnotic use may **reduce the cumulative amount of REM sleep;** this is known as **REM interference. This can result in daytime fatigue because REM sleep provides a certain component of the 'restfulness' of sleep**" (p. 184). The compatible alternative is the **hangover effect,** "**daytime sleepiness**" (p. 186), worse in older adults. Cue: ***sleep quantity preserved but **quality** degraded → REM interference; grogginess concentrated in the **morning** → hangover.*** Alprazolam is **intermediate acting,** and the teaching note says REM interference and rebound insomnia can appear "with just a **3- to 4-week** regimen" — P.S. is one week in.

**Review questions 1–5 and 8** are handled in the trap notes where their content lives: **RQ1** flumazenil (§3), **RQ2** paradoxical reaction (§3), **RQ3** suvorexant SATA (§4), **RQ4** muscle relaxant CNS depression (§6), **RQ5** nonpharmacologic first (§4 and §7), **RQ8** zolpidem dose in a female patient (§4).

**Review question 6** (pp. 196–197), five-option SATA on administering a benzodiazepine as a sedative-hypnotic:

| Option | What the chapter says | p. |
|---|---|---|
| **a.** intended for **long-term** management of insomnia | "**not intended for long-term use**"; use "**short term, if clinically feasible, to avoid dependency**" | 196, 185 |
| **b.** can be given **safely with other CNS depressants** | additive CNS depression; the 2016 opioid-plus-benzodiazepine **black box**; "**Increased CNS depression**" (Table 12.4) | 186 |
| **c.** dose **1 hour before bedtime** | "**dosed 60 minutes before bedtime**"; temazepam "approximately **1 hour before going to bed**" | 194, 187 |
| **d.** used as a **first choice** for sleeplessness | "**Nonpharmacologic measures... before resorting to treatment with medications**"; drugs "**only when nonpharmacologic methods fail**" | 196, 194 |
| **e.** evaluate for **drowsiness the morning after** | the "**hangover**" effect, "e.g., **daytime sleepiness**," worse in older adults | 186, 196 |

> **Trap (SATA, five options mapped to categories).** **RQ6 sorts into two chapter principles.** Cue: ***a, b, and d each contradict a stated rule (**short-term use, no combining with CNS depressants, nondrug measures first**); c and e each restate a stated rule (**onset-based timing, hangover monitoring**).*** For an all-or-nothing item, the discriminator is that three of five are absolute prohibitions in the text, not judgment calls.

**Review question 7** (p. 197) is arithmetic: "**pentobarbital 100 mg** slow IV push... available for injection with a concentration of **1 g/20 mL.** How many milliliters?" 1 g = 1000 mg, so 20 mL holds 1000 mg = **50 mg/mL**; 100 mg ÷ 50 mg/mL = **2 mL.** That is my computation, not the printed key.

> **Trap (confusable pair).** **The unit conversion is the trap in RQ7, not the division.** Cue: ***the order is in **mg,** the vial is labeled in **g** — convert first.*** Reading 1 g/20 mL as 1 mg/20 mL yields 2000 mL; the opposite slip yields 0.002 mL. The answer, 2 mL, matches the dosages table's "**IV: 100 mg**" preoperative dose (p. 190), and the drug is a **slow IV push** subject to the **1 mg/kg/min** barbiturate rate rule (p. 194).
