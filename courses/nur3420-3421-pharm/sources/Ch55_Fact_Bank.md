> **Trap (confusable pair).** **Anabolism vs. catabolism.** Cue: ***ana** = building **up** (tissue building, the stated purpose of supplements); **cata** = tearing down, **liberating energy**.* Amino acids "**reduce the breakdown of proteins (catabolism)**" (p. 863), so an option saying supplements *promote* catabolism is inverted. "Accelerated catabolic status" (p. 860) is an **indication** for supplementation, not a goal of it.

> **Trap (confusable pair).** **Casein vs. whey.** Both are milk proteins used in protein formulations (p. 861) and both carry the egg-white/whey allergy caution (p. 864). Cue: ***casein = the curd***; ***whey = what is left over*** (the thin serum after casein and fat are removed).

> **Trap (confusable pair).** **Malnutrition has two printed definitions.** The key-terms box says "**Any disorder of undernutrition**" (p. 858); the Overview says "a condition in which the **body's essential need for nutrients is not met by nutrient intake**" (p. 859). Both are the book's words, so an option quoting either is defensible.

> **Trap (plausible-but-adjacent).** **Hyperalimentation is not overfeeding.** It is the **older name for parenteral nutrition,** retired precisely because it reads like overfeeding. If a stem offers it as a complication or an error, that is the wrong sense of the word.

## §1 Enteral vs. parenteral: the central confusable pair

The chapter's schematic (p. 859): nutritional support → **enteral** → gastrointestinal tract; nutritional support → **parenteral** → circulation.

| Axis | **Enteral** | **Parenteral** |
|---|---|---|
| Definition as given | "the provision of food or nutrients **through the gastrointestinal tract**" (p. 859) | "the **intravenous administration of nutrients**" (p. 859) |
| GI tract | nutrients are digested and absorbed | "**bypasses the entire gastrointestinal system,** which eliminates the need for **absorption, metabolism, and excretion**" (p. 861) |
| Prerequisite | "used **when the patient has a functioning gastrointestinal tract**" (p. 859) | "**only when oral or enteral support is impossible** or when the **gastrointestinal absorptive or functional capacity is not sufficient**" (p. 862) |
| Standing preference | "the **preferred route** of administration of nutritional supplements" (p. 859) | "**preferred ... for patients who are unable to tolerate and/or maintain adequate enteral or oral intake**" (p. 861) |
| Routes | **six**: oral plus five tube routes | **two**: peripheral vein or central vein |
| Product groups | elemental, polymeric, modular, altered amino acid, impaired glucose tolerance (p. 859) | amino acids, carbohydrates, lipids, trace elements, vitamins, fluids, electrolytes (p. 863) |
| Usual contraindication | "**known allergy to a specific product** or **genetic disease that renders a patient unable to metabolize** certain nutrients" (p. 860) | same rule, worded "unable to **assimilate**" (p. 862); plus egg allergy may **cross-react with lipid formulations** (p. 863) |
| Most common adverse effect | "**gastrointestinal intolerance, manifesting as diarrhea**" (p. 860) | PPN: "**phlebitis**"; central: those "associated with the **use of the central vein**" (pp. 862–863) |
| Complication set (p. 867) | "**hyperglycemia, dumping syndrome, and aspiration**" | "**air embolism, fever, infection, fluid volume overload, hyperglycemia, or hypoglycemia.** If ... discontinued abruptly, **rebound hypoglycemia** may result" |

> **Trap (confusable pair).** **Enteral vs. parenteral.** Cue: ***enteral = enteron = gut*** (the oral route counts, and it is "the most common and least invasive"); ***parenteral = para + enteral = around the gut,*** meaning intravenously. The discriminating question in every stem is **"does this patient have a working GI tract?"** Working gut → enteral; non-functioning, obstructed, or inaccessible → parenteral. A distractor that starts TPN on a patient with a functioning gut is wrong on the chapter's own rule even if every other detail is right.

> **Trap (SATA, five options mapped to categories).** Review question 6 (p. 868) asks for adverse effects of **enteral** feeding, and the five options split by route:
>
> | **Enteral** | **Parenteral** (the distractors here) |
> |---|---|
> | diarrhea; aspiration; hyperglycemia; dumping syndrome; infection | **air embolism**; phlebitis (PPN); catheter-induced trauma; rebound hypoglycemia |
>
> The one that trips people is **hypoglycemia**: the chapter attaches it to **parenteral** nutrition (abrupt discontinuation, p. 866), not to enteral feeding. **Air embolism** is the giveaway parenteral option because it requires a vascular line.

> **Trap (setting vs. focus).** Distractors swap **where the nutrition goes** (gut vs. vein, peripheral vs. central) for **how much of the need it covers** (supplemental vs. total). Independent axes: enteral nutrition can "**supplement an oral diet** ... or **[be] used alone to meet all** of the patient's nutrient needs" (p. 860), and PPN is "**Supplemental (total if moderate to low needs)**" while central is "**Total**" (Table 55.2). Read the stem for which axis it asks about.

## §2 Enteral routes and formula groups

### The six routes (Table 55.1, p. 859)

| Route | Description as given | Surgical? |
|---|---|---|
| **Oral** | "Nutritional supplements delivered by mouth" | no |
| **Nasogastric** | "Feeding tube placed **from the nose to the stomach**" | no |
| **Nasoduodenal** | "Feeding tube placed **from the nose to the duodenum**" | no |
| **Nasojejunal** | "Feeding tube placed **from the nose to the jejunum**" | no |
| **Esophagostomy** | "Feeding tube **surgically inserted into the esophagus**" | **yes** |
| **Gastrostomy** | "Feeding tube **surgically inserted directly into the stomach**" | **yes** |
| **Jejunostomy** | "Feeding tube **surgically inserted into the jejunum**" | **yes** |

"**A feeding tube is used in the other five enteral routes**" (p. 859). Tube delivery benefits patients with "**abnormal esophageal or stomach peristalsis, altered anatomy secondary to surgery, depressed consciousness, or impaired digestive capacity**" (p. 859).

> **Trap (confusable pair).** **Naso- vs. -ostomy.** Review question 1 (p. 867) is built entirely on this one feature. Cue: ***naso- = through the nose, no surgery; -ostomy = a surgically created opening.*** The second half names the destination organ (*-gastric* = stomach, *-duodenal* = duodenum, *-jejunal* = jejunum, *esophago-* = esophagus). A nasogastric tube is "**inserted through the nose into the stomach**," never "surgically inserted." That item's three distractors are the other three cells of this table.

### The five formula groups (Box 55.1, p. 860; drug profiles p. 861)

| Group | Contents as given | Indication as given | Products |
|---|---|---|---|
| **Elemental** | "**dipeptides, tripeptides, or crystalline amino acids;** glucose oligosaccharides"; "**minimal digestion; minimal residue**" | "**malabsorption, partial bowel obstruction, irritable bowel disease, radiation enteritis, bowel fistula, short bowel syndrome**" | Peptamen, Vital HN, **Vivonex Plus**, Vivonex TEN |
| **Polymeric** | "**complex nutrients (proteins, carbohydrates, and fat)**" | "**preferred over elemental formulations for patients with a fully functional gastrointestinal tract and few specialized nutrient requirements**" | **Ensure** (lactose-free), Ensure Plus, Isocal, Osmolite, Portagen, Jevity, Sustacal |
| **Modular** | "**single-nutrient formulas (protein, carbohydrate, or fat)**" | "**added to a monomeric or polymeric formulation** to provide a more individualized nutrient formulation" | *carb:* Moducal, Polycose; *fat:* MCT oil, Microlipid (4.5 kcal/mL); *protein:* Casec, ProMod, Stresstein |
| **Altered amino acid** | "**varying amounts of specific amino acids**" | "**altered metabolic capacity**": **phenylketonuria, homocystinuria, maple syrup urine disease**; also renal impairment, eclampsia, heart failure, liver failure | **Amin-Aid**, Hepatic-Aid, Travasorb Renal, Traum-Aid HBC |
| **Impaired glucose tolerance** | "protein, carbohydrate, fat, sodium, potassium" | "**impaired glucose tolerance (e.g., diabetic patients)**" | **Glucerna** |

Box 55.2 (p. 860) sorts indications three ways: **complete** formulations for inability to consume or digest normal foods, accelerated catabolic status, or undernourishment because of disease; **incomplete** formulations for genetic metabolic enzyme deficiency or hepatic/renal impairment; **infant** formulations as sole or supplemental intake for infants.

> **Trap (level/stage swap).** **Elemental vs. polymeric swaps on how far the food is already broken down, and the swap runs one way.** Cue: ***elemental = pre-digested to di/tripeptides and amino acids, for a gut that barely works; polymeric = whole complex nutrients, for a gut that fully works.*** The stated preference is counterintuitive: **polymeric is preferred** for the *fully functional* GI tract because it "most closely resembles normal dietary intake" and is "**less hyperosmolar** than elemental formulations and therefore causes fewer gastrointestinal problems" (p. 861). A distractor reaching for elemental "because it is gentler" has the levels backwards.

> **Trap (plausible-but-adjacent).** **Modular is not a complete feeding.** Modular products are **single-nutrient add-ons** mixed into an elemental or polymeric base. An option offering Polycose, Microlipid, or ProMod as a patient's sole nutrition is a true statement about the product attached to the wrong question.

## §3 Peripheral vs. central parenteral nutrition

**Table 55.2 (p. 862), in full:**

| Characteristic | **Peripheral** | **Central** |
|---|---|---|
| Goal (total vs. supplemental) | "Supplemental (total if moderate to low needs)" | "Total" |
| Length of therapy | "**Short (fewer than 14 days)**" | "**Long (7 days or longer)**" |
| Osmolarity | "Hyperosmolar (600–900 mOsm/L)" | "Hyperosmolar (600–900 mOsm/L)" |
| Fluid tolerance | "**Must be high**" | "**Can be fluid-restricted**" |
| Dextrose | "**Less than 10%**" (note a: many institutions cap at 12.5%) | "**10%–35%**" |
| Amino acids | "Less than 3%" | "Greater than 3%–7%" |
| Fats | "10%–20%" | "10%–20%" |
| Calories per day | "**Less than 2000 kcal/day**" | "**Greater than 2000 kcal/day**" |

> **Two rows above carry a conflict the chapter never resolves. Quoted as printed, not repaired:**
> - **Central dextrose.** Table 55.2 gives "**10%–35%**"; the drug profile one page later (p. 863) gives "**10% to 50%,** but ... commonly 25% to 35%." Both are printed. The **overlap, 25% to 35%, is the only figure surviving both** and is the safe one to hold.
> - **Length of therapy.** Peripheral is "fewer than 14 days" and central is "7 days or longer," so **days 7 through 13 satisfy both** and the row cannot decide a route on its own. The deciding facts are the other rows — fluid tolerance, dextrose concentration, and total-versus-supplemental goal.
>
> See GAPS item 3.

What the table omits:

- **PPN** (p. 862) is "meant only as a **temporary means (less than 2 weeks)**," for patients who "do not have large nutritional needs, **can tolerate moderately large fluid loads,** and need nutritional supplements only temporarily." Indications: a **procedure that restricts oral feedings**; **anorexia from radiation or chemotherapy**; a **GI illness that prevents oral food ingestion**; **surgery of any type**; **minimal** deficits with oral nutrition delayed **longer than 5 days**.
- **PPN adverse effects** (p. 862): "The **most devastating adverse effect of PPN is phlebitis** ... it can lead to the **loss of a limb,** although this is rare." Plus **fluid overload**, because PPN "is limited to ... **generally less than 10%, to avoid sclerosing the vein.** Thus **large volumes are needed** ... patients ... with **renal failure or heart failure, cannot tolerate large fluid volumes.**"
- **Central TPN** (pp. 862–863) uses "a **large central vein** ... usually the **subclavian or internal jugular**," for a "**prolonged period, usually longer than 7 to 10 days**," and can be used **at home**. Other indications: **large nutritional requirements (metabolic stress or hypermetabolism)** and inability to **tolerate large fluid loads**. Disadvantages: "**greater potential for infection, more serious catheter-induced trauma** ... **metabolic alterations**," and "a **greater chance for metabolic complications such as hyperglycemia.**" Why central at all (p. 867): "**because of the hyperosmolarity of the substances used and the need for dilution provided by a larger-diameter vein.**"
- The bags are not different in kind (p. 863): components "are **the same. The difference lies in the concentrations and amounts.**"

> **Trap (confusable pair).** **Peripheral vs. central TPN.** Every row of Table 55.2 is a candidate distractor, but the exam-usable cue is one number set: ***peripheral = dextrose under 10%, under 2000 kcal/day, under 14 days; central = dextrose 10% to 35%, over 2000 kcal/day, over 7 to 10 days.*** Review question 4 (p. 867) is exactly this: "fewer than 14 days" fits PPN, while "longer than 2 weeks," "a dextrose concentration of 20%," and "3000 kcal/day" each violate one PPN limit. **Osmolarity is the trap that is *not* a difference** — both are 600–900 mOsm/L, so an option distinguishing them on osmolarity contradicts the table.

> **Trap (confusable pair).** **Fluid tolerance runs opposite to what the vein size suggests.** Cue: ***peripheral must tolerate HIGH volume; central can be fluid-RESTRICTED.*** The logic is the dextrose cap: PPN is held under 10% to avoid sclerosing the vein, so large volumes are the only way to deliver the calories. Heart failure and renal failure patients are therefore pushed **toward central**. A distractor assigning the fluid-restricted patient to PPN "because it is less invasive" is the classic miss.

> **Trap (priority question).** **Peripheral TPN + a painful site + a high-dextrose bag = phlebitis, and the priority is to stop the infusion.** The chapter's case study (p. 865, question 4) builds this stem: peripheral TPN, a tender site with nothing visibly wrong, and a bag reading **20% dextrose**, a central-line concentration in a peripheral vein. Governing principle: **phlebitis is the most devastating PPN adverse effect and the solution is causing it, so the solution comes off first.** Override: a hemodynamically unstable patient, where circulation outranks the line.

> **Trap (plausible-but-adjacent).** **The chapter's own duration ranges overlap, and both are printed.** Peripheral is "fewer than 14 days," central is "7 days or longer," so day 10 satisfies both. If a stem hinges on duration alone, look for the second discriminator (dextrose concentration, kcal/day, or fluid tolerance).

## §4 What is in the bag, and what goes wrong with the glucose

**Caloric density** (Fig. 55.2, p. 864; p. 863): dextrose **3.4 kcal/g**, protein/amino acids **4 kcal/g**, fat **9 kcal/g**.

**Amino acids** (p. 863): "The primary role is **protein synthesis, or anabolism.**" Aminosyn (3%, 5%, 10%) and FreAmine III (8.5%, 10%) work in "**either peripheral or central TPN**" and "**differ only in their respective concentrations.**" Table 55.3 dosing: **healthy 0.9 g/kg**; **malnourished or trauma/burn up to 2 g/kg**.

**Box 55.3, amino acid classification (p. 863):**

| Category | Members as listed |
|---|---|
| **Essential** (8) | isoleucine, leucine, lysine, methionine, phenylalanine, threonine, tryptophan, valine |
| **Semiessential** (2) | **arginine, histidine** |
| **Nonessential** | alanine, asparagine, aspartic acid, cysteine, glutamine, glutamic acid, glycine, proline, serine, tyrosine |

The body "is able to manufacture ... **all but eight**"; histidine and arginine "are **not manufactured by the body in large enough quantities during rapid growth periods such as infancy or childhood**" (p. 863).

> **Trap (level/stage swap).** **Essential / semiessential / nonessential is a three-level set, and the middle level is what gets swapped away.** Cue per level: ***essential = the body cannot make it at all, diet required (8 of them); semiessential = the body makes it, but not fast enough in infancy and childhood (arginine and histidine only); nonessential = the body makes it without dietary help.*** Distractors promote arginine or histidine to "essential" or demote them to "nonessential." "Nonessential" does not mean unnecessary, only not dietarily required.

**Carbohydrate** (p. 863): dextrose is "normally the **greatest source of calories.**" "In PPN, dextrose concentrations are kept **below 10% to decrease the possibility of phlebitis.** In central TPN ... **10% to 50%,** but ... **commonly 25% to 35%.** Because dextrose is a sugar, **supplemental insulin may be given simultaneously.**" A supplement carrying dextrose **and** lipids "**decreases the need for large amounts of insulin.**"

**Fat** (pp. 863–864): emulsions "serve **two functions**: they **supply essential fatty acids** and **are a source of energy or calories.**" **Linoleic acid** "cannot be synthesized by the body. It is needed to produce **linolenic and arachidonic acid.**" **Intralipid** (soybean oil) and **Liposyn** (safflower oil) come as **10%, 20%, or 30%** emulsions and "normally deliver **20% to 30% of the total daily calories and must not exceed 60% of daily caloric intake.**" Covering part of the load with fat "**prevents ... hyperglycemia, hyperinsulinemia, and hyperosmolarity ... when a patient's entire caloric needs are being met solely by dextrose.**"

**Essential fatty acid deficiency, signs** (p. 864): "**hair loss, scaly dermatitis, growth retardation, delayed wound healing, decreased platelet levels, and fatty liver.**" **Trace elements** (p. 864): chromium, copper, iodine, manganese, molybdenum, selenium, zinc; "A common multivitamin combination is **multivitamin infusion (MVI).**"

> **Trap (SATA, five options mapped to categories).** **Essential fatty acid deficiency signs** (review question 8, p. 868) run five options against that six-item list, and the wrong ones are **directional inversions**: the chapter says **decreased** platelet levels, so "increased platelet levels" is out, and "excessive bruising" is not on the list at all.

> **Trap (SATA, five options mapped to categories).** **Lipid emulsions do exactly two things** (review question 2, p. 867): supply **essential fatty acids** and supply **calories/energy**. Amino acids, minerals, and immunoglobulins are all real parts of nutrition but none is a function of the lipids. Per-option-rationale pattern: each distractor is true of *something* in the bag, just not this component.

> **Trap (confusable pair).** **Hyperglycemia vs. hypoglycemia in parenteral nutrition** (p. 866). Sort by **cause first**:
>
> | | Cause in this chapter | Signs as given |
> |---|---|---|
> | **Hyperglycemia** | high dextrose; **accelerating the infusion rate** ("Never accelerate these infusion rates to increase plasma volume") | "**polydipsia**, **polyuria**, **polyphagia**, headache, dehydration, nausea, vomiting, and weakness" |
> | **Hypoglycemia** | **abrupt discontinuation** (rebound hypoglycemia) | "**cold clammy skin, dizziness, tachycardia, and tingling of the extremities**"; teaching adds "restlessness, nervousness, fainting" |
>
> The prevention rules mirror each other: hypoglycemia is prevented "by a **gradual reduction of the intravenous feeding rate** to allow the pancreas time to adapt," and if TPN must stop immediately, "by providing **infusions of 5% to 10% glucose.**"

> **Trap (priority question).** **A TPN line that runs out, disconnects, or clogs is a hypoglycemia problem first.** Review questions 3 and 5 and critical thinking exercise 1 (pp. 867–868) build the same stem three ways. Governing principle: **abrupt cessation causes rebound hypoglycemia, so check the blood glucose and bridge with 5% to 10% dextrose** rather than hanging saline, hanging lipids, or waiting hours for a new bag. Override: a stem giving the signs of a *different* emergency (fever and chills → catheter infection; sudden dyspnea and chest pain → air embolism or lipid reaction).

> **Trap (plausible-but-adjacent).** **Dumping syndrome vs. aspiration.** Both are enteral and both follow from *how* the feeding is delivered. Cue: ***too FAST = dumping*** ("rapid feeding or bolus doses can result in dumping syndrome," p. 860); ***high residual, flat bed, or absent gag reflex = aspiration*** ("tube feeding places the patient at significant risk for **aspiration pneumonia** ... especially ... where **mental status, gag reflexes, and general mobility are compromised**," p. 860).

## §5 Nutrients interact with drugs: the numbers to hold

**Interactions** (p. 860). "With some exceptions, food usually **delays the absorption of drugs** when administered simultaneously."

| Interaction as given | Direction |
|---|---|
| "High gastric acid content or prolonged emptying time can result in **decreased effects of certain antibiotics (cephalosporins, erythromycin, and penicillins)**" | decreased |
| "**increased absorption rate** ... when **corticosteroids or vitamins A and D** are given with nutritional supplements" | increased |
| "**tetracyclines and quinolones are decreased, as a result of chemical inactivation** ... must be given **at least 2 hours before or after tube feedings**" | decreased |
| "Tube feedings can also **reduce the absorption of phenytoin** ... **which may result in seizures** ... tube feedings be **held for at least 2 hours before and after**" | decreased |

**Clinical Pearl** (p. 860): "**Enteral feedings can interact with quinolone and tetracycline antibiotics and phenytoin and need to be separated by 2 hours.**" The chapter then hedges its own phenytoin rule (p. 861): the interaction is "**more theoretical than actual**" to some, so "**some institutions have decided to ignore**" it while "**others continue to hold**" the feedings; often the patient "will require **intravenous phenytoin** when continuous tube feedings are necessary."

**Administration numbers** (pp. 865–866):

| Parameter | Value as given |
|---|---|
| NG placement check | test the pH of the aspirate: "**With a pH of 5.5 or lower, the tube is in the correct position (within the stomach)**"; may also be confirmed **by x-ray** |
| Residual, **continuous** | "**more than the volume delivered over the previous 2 hours**" → **return it, hold the feeding, contact the prescriber, head of bed elevated** |
| Residual, **intermittent bolus** | "**more than 50% of the volume previously infused**" → **return, withhold, contact the prescriber** |
| Flush | "**at least 15 to 30 mL of tepid water** ... **before and after ... each dose of medication, before and after checking gastric residuals, and every 4 to 12 hours**" |
| Infusion rate | "**20 mL/hr, increased at 10 mL/hr every 8 hours** ... **goal of 50 mL/hr**"; formula at **room temperature**, "**never ... cold or warm**" |
| Head of bed | "**at least 30 to 45 degrees**"; "**more critical with nasogastric ... than with gastrostomy tube feedings**" |
| Tubes | newer tubes are "**5 to 10 Fr**," which "makes checking for gastric aspiration **more difficult**"; clogs get a **pancrelipase tablet** or declogging stylus; for a **PEG**, "**Placement need not be checked**" |
| Parenteral monitoring | check **every hour**; change tubing **with every new bag,** or daily; **1.2-micron filter** "to trap bacteria, including *Pseudomonas* species"; report temperature **over 100°F (37.8°C)** immediately |

Incorrect NG placement shows as "**coughing, choking, difficulty speaking, cyanosis, and subsequent respiratory distress**"; fluid overload as "**weak pulse, hypertension, tachycardia, confusion, decreased urine output, and pitting edema**," answered by "**slow the infusion rate, measure vital signs, contact the prescriber, and remain with the patient**" (p. 866).

> **Trap (level/stage swap).** **The 2-hour rule has two shapes and they are easy to swap.** Cue: ***tetracyclines and quinolones = move the DRUG (give it 2 hours before or after the feeding); phenytoin = move the FEEDING (hold it 2 hours before and after the dose).*** Same interval, different thing being moved. Critical thinking exercise 2 (p. 867) is built on the phenytoin version, with Glucerna running through a nasogastric tube.

> **Trap (priority question).** **Before any drug goes down a feeding tube, confirm the tube and confirm the dosage form.** Two governing rules collide, and one lives in another chapter: the tube must be in the stomach (pH 5.5 or lower), and the drug must be crushable. Chapter 2 (p. 17) warns that "**Granules contained in capsules are usually for extended drug release and normally should not be crushed or chewed,**" with pharmacist consultation required if any question exists. An option that crushes an extended-release product to get it down the tube is wrong even when placement is correct.
