## Key terms

All 14 terms from the chapter's KEY TERMS box, quoted as given (pp. 614–615).

| Term | p. | Definition as given |
|---|---|---|
| **Carbapenem-resistant organism (CRO)** | 614 | "Refers to bacteria that possess an enzyme, **carbapenemase,** which renders the organism **resistant to all carbapenem antibiotics as well as beta-lactam antibiotics and monobactams.** Such organisms produce a very serious resistant infection. CRO used to be known as ***Klebsiella pneumoniae* carbapenemase (KPC)** and **carbapenem-resistant *Enterobacteriacae* (CRE).**" |
| **Concentration-dependent killing** | 614 | "A property of some antibiotics, **especially aminoglycosides,** whereby achieving **high plasma drug concentrations, even if briefly,** results in the most effective bacterial kill (compare time-dependent killing)." |
| **Extended-spectrum beta-lactamases (ESBLs)** | 614 | "A group of beta-lactamase enzymes produced by some organisms that makes the organism **resistant to all beta-lactam antibiotics (penicillins and cephalosporins) and aztreonam.** Patients who are infected by such organisms must be in **contact isolation;** proper handwashing is key to preventing the spread of these organisms." |
| **Methicillin-resistant *Staphylococcus aureus* (MRSA)** | 614 | "A strain of *Staphylococcus aureus* that is resistant to the beta-lactamase penicillin known as **methicillin.** Originally the abbreviation MRSA referred exclusively to methicillin-resistant *S. aureus.* It is now used more commonly to refer to strains of *S. aureus* that are **resistant to several drug classes,** and therefore, depending on the context or health care institution, it may also stand for '**multidrug-resistant *S. aureus.***'" |
| **Microgram** | 614 | "One **millionth** of a gram. Be careful not to confuse it with **milligram** (one **thousandth** of a gram), which is **1000 times greater** than 1 microgram. Confusion of these two units sometimes results in drug dosage errors." |
| **Minimum inhibitory concentration (MIC)** | 614 | "A laboratory measure of the **lowest concentration of a drug needed to kill a certain standardized amount of bacteria.**" |
| **Multidrug-resistant organisms** | 614 | "Bacteria that are **resistant to one or more classes of antimicrobial drugs.** These include multidrug-resistant *Staphylococcus aureus,* extended-spectrum beta-lactamase–producing organisms, and carbapenemase-resistant *Enterobacteriaceae.*" |
| **Nephrotoxicity** | 614 | "Toxicity to the **kidneys,** often drug induced and manifesting as compromised renal function; **usually reversible** upon withdrawal of the offending drug." |
| **Ototoxicity** | 614 | "Toxicity to the **ears,** often drug induced and manifesting as varying degrees of hearing loss that is **likely to be permanent.**" |
| **Postantibiotic effect** | 614 | "A period of **continued bacterial suppression that occurs after brief exposure** to certain antibiotic drug classes, especially **aminoglycosides** (discussed in this chapter) and **carbapenems** (see Chapter 38). **The mechanism of this effect is uncertain.**" |
| **Pseudomembranous colitis** | 614–615 | "A necrotizing inflammatory bowel condition that is often associated with antibiotic therapy. Some antibiotics (**e.g., clindamycin**) are more likely to produce it than others. More commonly referred to as **antibiotic-associated colitis** or ***Clostridioides* (formerly *Clostridium*) *difficile* diarrhea** or ***C. difficile* infection.**" |
| **Synergistic effect** | 615 | "Drug interaction in which the **bacterial killing effect of two antibiotics given together is greater than the sum of the individual effects** of the same drugs given alone." |
| **Therapeutic drug monitoring** | 615 | "Ongoing monitoring of **plasma drug concentrations and dosage adjustment based on these values** as well as other laboratory indicators such as kidney and liver function test results; it is often carried out by a **pharmacist** in collaboration with medical, nursing, and laboratory staff." |
| **Time-dependent killing** | 615 | "A property of **most antibiotic classes** whereby **prolonged high plasma drug concentrations are required** for effective bacterial kill (compare concentration-dependent killing)." |
| **Vancomycin-resistant *Enterococcus* (VRE)** | 615 | "*Enterococcus* species that are **resistant to beta-lactam antibiotics and vancomycin.** Most commonly refers to ***Enterococcus faecium.***" |

> **Trap (confusable pair).** **Concentration-dependent vs. time-dependent killing.** This is the pair the key term box itself cross-references ("compare time-dependent killing" / "compare concentration-dependent killing"), which is as close to a printed warning as a textbook gives. Cue: ***concentration** = how **high** you get, briefly, and that is the **aminoglycosides**; **time** = how **long** you stay above the MIC, and that is **most other classes,** including the beta-lactams.* The clinical consequence is the dosing schedule: concentration-dependent drugs move toward **once-daily** dosing, time-dependent drugs toward **continuous or frequent** infusion.

> **Trap (confusable pair).** **Nephrotoxicity vs. ototoxicity.** Both are aminoglycoside and vancomycin toxicities, both are monitored by drug levels, and they are the single most swappable pair in this chapter. Cue: ***nephro** = kidneys, more **common,** and **usually reversible**; **oto** = ears, **less common,** and **likely permanent**.* The chapter's own definitions carry that reversibility contrast, and the incidence figures at p. 617–618 confirm the frequency contrast.

> **Trap (confusable pair).** **Microgram vs. milligram.** The chapter gives this its own key term, with the reason attached: "**Confusion of these two units sometimes results in drug dosage errors.**" A microgram is **1/1000** of a milligram. Every aminoglycoside and vancomycin level in this chapter is in **mcg/mL**; every dose is in **mg** or **mg/kg**. An option that reports a trough in mg/mL is off by a factor of 1000.

> **Trap (plausible-but-adjacent).** **Pseudomembranous colitis has four names in this book.** Pseudomembranous colitis = antibiotic-associated colitis = *C. difficile* diarrhea = *C. difficile* infection. Chapter 38 uses "*C. difficile* infection" as the headword; this chapter uses "pseudomembranous colitis." They are the same condition, so an option using the unfamiliar name is not thereby wrong.

## §1 The four resistant organisms

This is the chapter's organizing spine and its most testable block, because each abbreviation has a definition, a resistance mechanism, and a named treatment.

| Organism | Resistant to | Treatment (Box 39.1, p. 615) |
|---|---|---|
| **MRSA** (methicillin-resistant *S. aureus*) | methicillin; now "several drug classes" | "vancomycin, daptomycin, linezolid, tedizolid, televancin, ortivancin, dalbavancin, ceftaroline (see Chapter 38)" |
| **VRE** (vancomycin-resistant *Enterococcus,* usually *E. faecium*) | beta-lactams **and** vancomycin | "daptomycin, linezolid, tedizolid" |
| **ESBL** (extended-spectrum beta-lactamase producers) | "all beta-lactam antibiotics (penicillins, cephalosporins) and aztreonam" | "**Carbapenems** (see Chapter 38)" |
| **CRO** (carbapenem-resistant organism) | "all carbapenems," plus beta-lactams and monobactams | "**colistin, tigecycline** (see Chapter 38)" |

**The escalation story, in the chapter's own words** (p. 615): "Organisms that produce ESBL are resistant to all beta-lactam antibiotics (i.e., penicillins, cephalosporins) and aztreonam and can be treated **only with carbapenems or sometimes fluoroquinolones.** In our noble effort to treat infection with ESBL-producing organisms, the use of carbapenems increased, and unfortunately in response, bacteria created a new means of resistance, namely, the ability to produce the enzyme **carbapenemase,** which renders all carbapenems ineffective. When patients become infected with CRO, there are **few known antibiotics** that can be used to treat such infections. Reports of resistance to these antibiotics have been described, **which leaves the patient untreatable.**"

**MRSA in the community** (p. 615): "MRSA is **no longer seen just in hospitals;** it has spread to the community setting, and **more than 50% of staphylococcal infections contracted in the community involve MRSA,** depending on location."

**VRE's usual site** (p. 615): "VRE is usually seen in **urinary tract infections (UTIs).**"

**The WHO Global Priority List, February 2017** (p. 615):

| Tier | Organisms |
|---|---|
| **Most critical** | **CRO, ESBL, *Pseudomonas aeruginosa,* and *Acinetobacter baumannii*** |
| **High priority** | **MRSA, VRE, *Neisseria gonorrhoeae*** (fluoroquinolone and third-generation cephalosporin resistant), and ***Salmonella* species** (fluoroquinolone resistant) |

**The nursing response, stated three separate times in the chapter** (pp. 614, 615, 626): "When patients become infected with such an organism, they must be placed in **contact isolation.** Proper handwashing is of the utmost importance. These organisms are spread by contact, so **all health care professionals must wash their hands before and after all patient contact. The importance of handwashing cannot be overemphasized.**"

> **Trap (confusable pair).** **ESBL vs. CRO: what is left to treat with.** Cue: *ESBL knocks out the **beta-lactams,** so you escalate **to** carbapenems; CRO knocks out the **carbapenems** too, so you fall back to **colistin and tigecycline**.* The two are a sequence, not a parallel pair, and the chapter tells the sequence as a cause-and-effect story. An option treating a CRO infection with a carbapenem is naming the exact drug the organism is defined by resisting.

> **Trap (confusable pair).** **MRSA vs. VRE treatment lists.** They overlap in **daptomycin, linezolid, and tedizolid,** and the discriminator is what is on the MRSA list **only**: **vancomycin** (obviously, since VRE resists it), **telavancin, oritavancin, dalbavancin, and ceftaroline.** Cue: *if the option is vancomycin and the organism is VRE, it is wrong by definition of the name.*

> **Trap (confusable pair).** **What MRSA stands for now.** The key term says the abbreviation "may also stand for **multidrug-resistant *S. aureus***," and the chapter's own case study (p. 626) writes "multidrug-resistant *Staphylococcus aureus* (MRSA)." Both expansions appear in this chapter. An option using either is defensible; the clinical meaning does not change.

> **Trap (priority question).** For any multidrug-resistant organism stem, the two answers the chapter repeats are **contact isolation** and **handwashing.** The vancomycin case study asks directly, "What is the **single best action** you can take to prevent the spread of Mr. M's MRSA infection?" (p. 626), and the chapter's superlative language ("cannot be overemphasized," "of the utmost importance") is written for exactly that key.

## §2 Aminoglycosides (pp. 616–619)

**Class action** (p. 616): "a group of **natural and semisynthetic** antibiotics that are classified as **bactericidal** drugs. They are **potent antibiotics,** which makes aminoglycosides the **drugs of choice for the treatment of particularly virulent infections.**"

**Mechanism** (pp. 616–617): "Aminoglycosides work in a way that is **similar to that of the tetracyclines,** in that they bind to ribosomes (specifically the **30S ribosome**) and thereby prevent protein synthesis in bacteria."

**The route rule, and its one exception** (p. 616): "These drugs can be given by several different routes, but **they are not given orally because of their poor oral absorption. An exception to this is neomycin.**" They "can also be given by **inhalation** for the treatment of lung infections."

**The three used systemically** (p. 616): "The three aminoglycosides most commonly used for the treatment of systemic infections are **amikacin, gentamicin, and tobramycin.**" A new one, **plazomicin (Zemdri),** "was recently approved for the treatment of complicated UTI."

### Therapeutic drug monitoring, the highest-yield block

"For the aminoglycosides to be effective, the serum level needs to be **at least eight times higher than the minimum inhibitory concentration (MIC)**" (p. 616).

**Table 39.1 Aminoglycoside Antibiotics** (p. 616), reproduced:

| Drug | Peak, multiple daily dosing | Peak, once-daily dosing | Trough, multiple daily dosing | Trough, once-daily dosing |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| **amikacin** | 15–30 mcg/mL | "Usually not measured" | 5–10 mcg/mL | **Less than 10 mcg/mL** |
| **gentamicin** and **tobramycin** | 4–10 mcg/mL | "Usually not measured" | 1–2 mcg/mL | **Less than 1 mcg/mL** |

**Once-daily dosing** (p. 616). The chapter's rationale is a direct consequence of concentration-dependent killing:

- Doses: "**5 to 7 mg/kg per day** of gentamicin or tobramycin," and "**15 mg/kg** of amikacin."
- Evidence: "once-daily dosing provides a sufficient plasma drug concentration for bacterial kill, along with **equal or lower risk for toxicity** compared with multiple daily dosing regimens."
- Practical benefits: "reduces the **nursing care time** required and often allows for **outpatient or even home-based** aminoglycoside drug therapy."
- **Peaks are not drawn:** "Peak (highest) drug levels for once-daily regimens are usually **not measured,** as it is assumed that the peak level for a single daily dose will be short-lived and will drop within a reasonable time frame."
- **Troughs are drawn:** "at least **8 to 12 hours after completion of dose administration.** The therapeutic goal is a trough concentration **at or below 1 mcg/mL** (which is considered undetectable for gentamicin and tobramycin). **Trough levels above 2 mcg/mL are associated with a greater risk for both ototoxicity and nephrotoxicity.**"

**Traditional (three-times-daily) dosing** (p. 616): "**both peak and trough levels are measured.** Samples for measurement of **peak** levels are drawn **30 to 60 minutes after a 30-minute infusion,** and samples for measurement of **trough** levels are drawn **just before the next dose.**"

- "the desired peak levels vary depending on the type of organism and the site of infection. **Higher levels are needed when treating pneumonia,** as opposed to treating a UTI. Because the aminoglycosides are eliminated by the kidney, **the drug concentrates in the urine, so lower dosages can be used to treat UTIs.**"
- "Regardless of the infection being treated, however, it is desirable to keep gentamicin and tobramycin **trough levels below 2 mcg/mL.**"

**Monitoring schedule** (p. 616): "Trough levels are normally monitored initially, and then **once every 5 to 7 days** until drug therapy is discontinued. The patient's **serum creatinine level is measured at least every 3 days** as an index of renal function."

**Renal function is the dosing basis** (p. 616): "dosing is adjusted to the patient's level of renal function, based on **estimates of creatinine clearance calculated from serum creatinine values.** This task is often carried out by a hospital pharmacist." A **rising serum creatinine** "suggests reduced creatinine clearance by the kidneys and is indicative of declining renal function."

### Synergy and sequence

**Synergistic effect** (p. 617): aminoglycosides "are often used in combination with other antibiotics such as **beta-lactams or vancomycin**... because the combined effect of the two antibiotics is **greater than the sum** of the effects of each drug acting separately."

**The administration sequence, and the reason for it** (p. 617): "When aminoglycosides are used in combination with beta-lactam antibiotics (i.e., penicillins, cephalosporins, monobactams), **the beta-lactam antibiotic is given first.** This is because the **beta-lactams break down the cell wall of the bacteria and allow the aminoglycoside to gain access to the ribosomes,** where they work."

**Postantibiotic effect** (p. 617): "a period of **continued bacterial growth suppression that occurs after short-term antibiotic exposure,** as in once-daily aminoglycoside dosing. **Carbapenems** are another antibiotic class with a postantibiotic effect. The postantibiotic effect is **enhanced with higher peak drug concentrations and concurrent use of beta-lactam antibiotics.**"

### Indications and the per-drug spectra

**Table 39.2 Aminoglycosides: Comparative Spectra of Antimicrobial Activity** (p. 617), condensed to what discriminates the drugs:

| Drug | Spectrum note as given |
|---|---|
| **amikacin** | *Acinetobacter, Enterobacter aerogenes, E. coli, Klebsiella pneumoniae, Proteus, Providencia, Pseudomonas, Serratia, Staphylococcus.* "generally **reserved for resistant infections**" and "often used to treat infections that are **resistant to gentamicin or tobramycin**" |
| **gentamicin** | *E. aerogenes, E. coli, K. pneumoniae, Proteus, Pseudomonas, Salmonella, Serratia* (nonpigmented), *Shigella* |
| **neomycin** | "**Toxicity limits use to gastrointestinal tract** (hepatic coma, *E. coli* diarrhea, and antisepsis) and as a **topical** antibacterial" |
| **streptomycin** | *Klebsiella granulomatis* (granuloma inguinale), *Yersinia pestis* (**plague**), *Francisella tularensis* (**tularemia**), *Mycobacterium tuberculosis* (**tuberculosis**), *Streptococcus* (nonhemolytic endocarditis) |
| **tobramycin** | *Citrobacter, Enterobacter, E. coli, Klebsiella, Proteus, Providencia, P. aeruginosa, Serratia.* Also "commonly used to treat **recurrent pulmonary infections in patients with cystic fibrosis**" by injectable and inhaled dosing |

**What aminoglycosides never cover** (p. 618): "Aminoglycosides are **inactive against fungi, viruses, and most anaerobic bacteria.**"

**Gram-positive use is always combination therapy** (pp. 617–618): "gentamicin is usually given **in combination with penicillin**... A regimen of **three daily doses is more common when treating gram-positive infections,** because this often enhances synergy with other antibiotics that are used. **Aminoglycosides are never used alone to treat gram-positive infections.**"

- Prophylaxis: "used for prophylaxis in procedures involving the **gastrointestinal or genitourinary tract,** because such procedures carry a **high risk for enterococcal bacteremia**," and "commonly given in combination with either **ampicillin or vancomycin (for penicillin-allergic patients)** to surgical patients with a **history of valvular heart disease.**"
- Serious pediatric infections treated: "**pneumonia, meningitis, and UTIs.**"
- Two drugs with narrow, specific roles: "**Streptomycin** is active against *Mycobacterium* species (see Chapter 41), whereas **paromomycin** is used to treat **amebic dysentery,** a protozoal intestinal disease (see Chapter 43)."

**Neonates** (p. 618): "administered with caution in **premature and full-term neonates.** Because of the **renal immaturity** of these patients, prolonged actions of the aminoglycosides and a greater risk for toxicities may result."

**Pregnancy** (p. 618): categories "either **C or D**." "There have been several case reports of **total irreversible bilateral congenital deafness** in the children of women receiving aminoglycosides during pregnancy. Therefore aminoglycosides are used in pregnant women **only in the event of life-threatening infections when safer drugs are ineffective.**"

### Toxicity

| | **Nephrotoxicity** | **Ototoxicity** |
|---|---|---|
| Incidence | "typically occurs in **5% to 25%** of patients" | "**less common,** occurring in **3% to 14%** of patients" |
| Reversible? | "**usually reversible,** but the patient's renal function test results must be monitored throughout therapy" | "**often is not reversible.** It can result in varying degrees of **permanent hearing loss**" |
| Manifestations | "**urinary casts** (visible remnants of destroyed renal cells), **proteinuria, and increased BUN and serum creatinine levels**" | "injury to the **eighth cranial nerve (CN VIII,** also called the cochleovestibular nerve or auditory nerve)"; involves "both **cochlear damage (hearing loss)** and **vestibular damage (disrupted sense of balance)**" |
| Symptoms to report | rising creatinine | "**dizziness, tinnitus, a sense of fullness in the ears, and hearing loss**" |

Other, less common effects (p. 618): "headache, paresthesia, vertigo, skin rash, fever, overgrowth of nonsusceptible organisms, and **neuromuscular paralysis (very rare and reversible).**"

**Who is at greatest risk** (p. 618): "patients with **preexisting renal impairment,** patients already **receiving other renally toxic drugs,** and patients receiving **high-dose or prolonged** aminoglycoside therapy."

**Interactions** (p. 618):

| Interacting drug | Effect |
|---|---|
| **vancomycin, cyclosporine, amphotericin B** (other nephrotoxic drugs) | increased risk for **nephrotoxicity** |
| **Loop diuretics** | increased risk for **ototoxicity** |
| **warfarin** | aminoglycosides "kill intestinal bacterial flora" and so "**reduce the amount of vitamin K** produced by these gut bacteria... Therefore aminoglycosides can **potentiate warfarin toxicity**" |
| **Neuromuscular blocking drugs** | "may **prolong the duration** of action of the neuromuscular blockade" |
| **IV contrast media for CT scans** | named separately in the nursing section (p. 625) as a nephrotoxicity risk; "alert the prescriber to the situation so that the dose may be adjusted" |

**Administration** (pp. 626–627): give "with adequate hydration. **Encourage fluid intake of up to 3000 mL/day unless contraindicated.**" Monitor BUN and **glomerular filtration rate (GFR).** "**Alteration in auditory, vestibular, or renal function may indicate the need for a possible dosage adjustment or withdrawal of the drug.**" For gentamicin specifically: "**intramuscular**, give deep and inject slowly into muscle mass (**ventrogluteal**) to minimize discomfort"; "**intravenous**, check for incompatibilities with other drugs. Only use/administer solutions that are clear." Check IM sites for **induration**: "If noted, report it immediately to the prescriber, and **do not reuse the site.**"

**Patient teaching** (p. 628): "**Hearing loss may occur even after completion of therapy.**" Report "any persistent headache, nausea, or vertigo," and "**any change in hearing.**" The implementation section adds: "**Nausea, vomiting with motion, ataxia, nystagmus, and dizziness** should also be reported immediately and may indicate issues with the **vestibular nerve**" (p. 627).

> **Trap (confusable pair).** **Peak vs. trough, and when each is drawn.** This is the densest number cluster in the chapter, and it splits by dosing regimen, not by drug. Cue: *once-daily = **trough only,** drawn **8 to 12 hours after** the dose ends, goal **at or below 1**; traditional three-times-daily = **both,** peak drawn **30 to 60 minutes after a 30-minute infusion,** trough drawn **just before the next dose**.* An option drawing a peak "just before the next dose" has the two reversed.

> **Trap (level/stage swap).** **The trough thresholds are three different numbers.** *Goal **at or below 1** mcg/mL (once-daily gentamicin/tobramycin); **1 to 2** mcg/mL is the traditional-dosing trough range; **above 2** mcg/mL raises the risk of both toxicities.* Amikacin runs an order of magnitude higher (trough 5 to 10 traditional, less than 10 once-daily), so an amikacin number applied to gentamicin looks alarmingly toxic and vice versa.

> **Trap (priority question).** **Give the beta-lactam FIRST, then the aminoglycoside.** Critical thinking exercise 2 (p. 630) is built on exactly this: gentamicin 300 mg IV daily and ceftazidime/avibactam 2.5 g IV both due at 0900, and it asks which to infuse first. The chapter's reason is mechanistic: the beta-lactam breaks the cell wall so the aminoglycoside can reach the ribosome. (The official answer is on Evolve; see GAPS item 9.)

> **Trap (confusable pair).** **neomycin is the aminoglycoside that breaks every class rule.** Cue: *neomycin is the **only oral** one, and it is oral **because** it is too toxic to give systemically.* Its uses are all local: preoperative bowel decontamination (oral and rectal enema), *E. coli* diarrhea, hepatic encephalopathy, bladder irrigation, topical skin, and eye infections. **It is not available in injectable form.** Its mechanism in hepatic encephalopathy is worth holding separately: it "helps reduce the number of **ammonia-producing bacteria** in the gastrointestinal tract," and "the subsequent **reduced blood ammonia levels** sometimes result in improvement of neurologic symptoms."

> **Trap (confusable pair).** **amikacin vs. gentamicin vs. tobramycin.** All three treat serious gram-negative infections and all three are injectable. Cue: *am**i**kacin = the **reserve** drug, for organisms **resistant** to the other two; **tob**ramycin = cystic fibrosis lungs, including **inhaled** dosing; gentamicin = the default workhorse, and the one paired with penicillin for gram-positive synergy.*

> **Trap (confusable pair).** **Aminoglycosides bind 30S, like tetracyclines; macrolides bind 50S.** The chapter states the tetracycline similarity outright. But the two 30S classes are not interchangeable: **aminoglycosides are bactericidal, tetracyclines are bacteriostatic.** Same binding site, opposite killing behavior.

> **Trap (plausible-but-adjacent).** **"Aminoglycosides are never used alone to treat gram-positive infections."** An option giving gentamicin as monotherapy for a gram-positive infection is wrong on an absolute statement, not a judgment call. Note also the reversal: for **gram-positive** infections the chapter says **three daily doses** is more common, precisely because synergy, not peak concentration, is the goal there.

## §3 Quinolones / fluoroquinolones (pp. 619–621)

**Class action** (p. 619): "very potent **bactericidal broad-spectrum** antibiotics." Available drugs: "**norfloxacin, ciprofloxacin, levofloxacin, moxifloxacin, gemifloxacin,** and most recently **delafloxacin (Baxdela).**"

**Mechanism** (p. 619): "destroy bacteria by **altering their deoxyribonucleic acid (DNA).** They accomplish this by interfering with the bacterial enzymes **DNA gyrase and topoisomerase IV. Quinolones do not inhibit the production of human DNA.**"

**Oral absorption is the class's defining practical feature** (p. 619): "With the exception of **norfloxacin,** these antibiotics have **excellent oral absorption.** In most cases, the extent of oral absorption is **comparable to that of intravenous injection.**" Ciprofloxacin "can work orally as well as many intravenous antibiotics" (p. 621).

**Why they suit UTIs** (p. 619): "Most are excreted primarily by the kidneys as **unchanged drug.** This characteristic, together with the fact that they have extensive gram-negative coverage, makes them suitable for treating **complicated UTIs.**" Also used for "respiratory, skin, gastrointestinal, and bone and joint infections."

**Table 39.3 Quinolones: Common Indications for Specific Drugs** (p. 620), reproduced:

| Drug | Spectrum as given | Common indications |
|---|---|---|
| **norfloxacin (Noroxin)** | "Extensive gram-negative and selected gram-positive coverage" | UTIs, prostatitis, STIs. **Oral only, limited absorption, use limited to genitourinary tract infections** |
| **ciprofloxacin (Cipro)** | "Comparable to that of norfloxacin" | **Anthrax (inhalational, postexposure);** respiratory, skin, urinary tract, prostate, intraabdominal, GI, bone, and joint infections; typhoid fever; selected health care–associated pneumonias |
| **levofloxacin (Levaquin)** | "Comparable to that of ciprofloxacin **with better gram-positive coverage**" | Respiratory infections and UTIs; prophylaxis in transrectal and transurethral prostate surgical procedures. **Once-daily dosing** |

**Box 39.2 Overview of Quinolone-Susceptible Organisms** (p. 619), condensed:

- **Gram-positive:** *Streptococcus* (including *S. pneumoniae*), *Staphylococcus, Enterococcus, Listeria monocytogenes*
- **Gram-negative:** *Neisseria gonorrhea, N. meningitidis, Haemophilus influenzae, H. parainfluenzae,* Enterobacteriaceae (*E. coli, Enterobacter, Klebsiella, Proteus mirabilis, Salmonella, Shigella*), *Acinetobacter, Pseudomonas aeruginosa, Pasteurella multocida, Legionella, Mycoplasma pneumoniae, Chlamydia*
- **Anaerobes:** *Bacteroides fragilis, Peptococcus, Peptostreptococcus* (**moxifloxacin is strongest**)
- **Other:** *Rickettsia* (**ciprofloxacin only**)
- Footnote: "**Most *Pseudomonas* infections are now resistant to quinolone therapy.**"

**Resistance** (p. 619): identified in "*P. aeruginosa, S. aureus, Pneumococcus* species, *Enterococcus* species, and the broad Enterobacteriaceae family that includes *E. coli.*" And more bluntly at p. 619: "**Unfortunately, resistance to all quinolones has become widespread.**"

### The safety warnings, which is where the exam items live

**The black box warning** (p. 620): "The US Food and Drug Administration (FDA) requires a **black box warning for all quinolones** because of the increased risk for **tendinitis and tendon rupture** with use of these drugs, as well as **peripheral neuropathy, central nervous system (CNS) effects (seizures), and exacerbation of myasthenia gravis.**"

- **Who ruptures tendons:** "**older adult patients, patients with renal failure, and those receiving concurrent glucocorticoid therapy (e.g., prednisone).**"
- Table 39.4 dates it: "Ruptured tendons and tendonitis (**black box warning added in 2008**)."
- "New evidence suggests that they may also cause **liver injury in older adults.**"

**The cardiac effect** (p. 620): "More worrisome is a cardiac effect that involves **prolongation of the QT interval** on the electrocardiogram (ECG). **Dangerous cardiac dysrhythmias are more likely to occur when quinolones are taken by patients who are also receiving class Ia and class III antidysrhythmic drugs, such as disopyramide and amiodarone.** For this reason, such drug combinations are best avoided."

**Two later FDA actions** (p. 620):

| Year | Action |
|---|---|
| **2016** | "safety alert stating that the **risks outweigh the benefits** of quinolone use in **uncomplicated UTIs, acute sinusitis, and acute bronchitis** if other options are available" |
| **2018** | "required a change to all quinolone labeling to strengthen existing warnings about **mental health side effects** and **serious blood glucose disturbances** and **aortic rupture,** and they should be **reserved when no other antibiotic is effective**" |

**Table 39.4 Quinolones: Reported Adverse Effects** (p. 620):

| Body system | Adverse effects |
|---|---|
| **Central nervous** | Headache, dizziness, insomnia, depression, restlessness, **convulsions,** neuropathy |
| **Gastrointestinal** | Nausea, constipation, increased AST and ALT levels, flatulence, heartburn, vomiting, diarrhea, oral candidiasis, dysphagia |
| **Integumentary** | Rash, pruritus, urticaria, flushing |
| **Other** | **Ruptured tendons and tendonitis** (black box warning added in 2008), fever, chills, blurred vision, tinnitus, **QT prolongation, hypo- or hyperglycemia, aortic rupture** |

**Infusion rate** (p. 620): "Quinolones given via the intravenous route must be **infused over 1 to 1.5 hours.**"

**Children** (p. 619): "The use of quinolones in **prepubescent children is not generally recommended,** because these drugs have been shown to **affect cartilage development in laboratory animals.** However, more recent evidence suggests that judicious use in children might be less of a risk than previously thought, and in fact these drugs are **used commonly in children with cystic fibrosis.**" The dosage table footnote reads "Not normally recommended for children **younger than 18 years of age.**"

**Interactions** (p. 620):

| Interacting agent | Effect and the fix |
|---|---|
| **antacids, calcium, magnesium, iron, zinc preparations, sucralfate** | "causes a **reduction in the oral absorption** of the quinolone. Patients need to take the interacting drugs **at least 1 hour before or after** taking quinolones" |
| **Dairy products** | "also reduce the absorption of quinolones and should be separated as stated previously" |
| **Enteral tube feedings** | "can also reduce the absorption of quinolones" |
| **probenecid** | "can reduce the renal excretion of quinolones" |
| **nitrofurantoin** | "can **antagonize the antibacterial activity** of the quinolones" |
| **warfarin** | use with caution "because of the antibiotic-induced **alteration of the intestinal flora, which affects vitamin K synthesis**" |

**Photosensitivity** (p. 628, patient teaching): quinolones are named as a photosensitizing class. "The chemicals in the drug, when taken orally or applied topically, **increase the sensitivity of the skin to ultraviolet (UV) light.** Photosensitized skin will **burn, possibly blister,** when exposed to the sun or other UV rays such as with **tanning beds/booths.**" Recommend "**daily and year-long** use of sunglasses and sunscreen protection."

> **Trap (confusable pair).** **The 1-hour rule (quinolones) vs. the 2-hours-before / 3-hours-after rule (tetracyclines, Ch. 38).** Both classes are blocked by antacids, calcium, magnesium, and iron, and both are fixed by spacing, but **the spacing intervals are different.** Cue: *quinolone = **1 hour** either side; tetracycline = **2 before, 3 after**.* An option applying one class's interval to the other is the most economical distractor available here.

> **Trap (confusable pair).** **QT prolongation vs. dysrhythmia.** The chapter names the **ECG finding** (QT prolongation) as the quinolone effect, and describes **dangerous dysrhythmias** as what happens when a quinolone is combined with a **class Ia or class III antidysrhythmic.** Review question 1 (p. 630) offers bradycardia, dysrhythmias, tachycardia, and prolonged QT interval as options for "which potential cardiac effect," which is precisely this distinction. Cue: *the effect **of the drug** is the QT interval; the dysrhythmia is what the **interaction** produces.*

> **Trap (confusable pair).** **Which quinolone for which job.** Cue: *nor**floxacin** = the **weak oral one,** GU tract only; **cipro** = anthrax and the broadest general use; **levo** = once daily, **better gram-positive**; **moxi** = the only one with real **anaerobic** coverage; **dela** = the one that reaches **MRSA**.* The table builds each drug's spectrum by reference to the one above it, which is a chain a distractor can break at any link.

> **Trap (level/stage swap).** **Three separate FDA actions, three different years, three different contents.** *2008 = **tendon** black box; 2016 = **risks outweigh benefits** in uncomplicated UTI, sinusitis, bronchitis; 2018 = **mental health, blood glucose, aortic rupture,** and reserve-use language.* A stem attaching aortic rupture to the 2008 box, or tendon rupture to 2018, has the years crossed.

> **Trap (plausible-but-adjacent).** **Nitrofurantoin antagonizes quinolones.** Two urinary anti-infectives that look like a sensible pair for a stubborn UTI actually work against each other. It appears in the quinolone interaction list and again in the patient teaching list.

> **Trap (setting vs. focus).** **Photosensitivity now belongs to three classes across these two chapters:** sulfonamides (Ch. 38), tetracyclines (Ch. 38), and quinolones (Ch. 39). Naming a class as "the" photosensitizing one is wrong; if a stem needs one drug, look for the drug-level cue (demeclocycline is the most photosensitizing tetracycline).

### clindamycin (pp. 621–622)

- **Semisynthetic**; the key points add it is "a semisynthetic **derivative of lincomycin,** an older antibiotic" (p. 629).
- **"Can be either bactericidal or bacteriostatic, depending on the concentration of the drug at the site of infection and on the infecting bacteria."** It "inhibits protein synthesis in bacteria."
- Indications: "chronic **bone** infections, genitourinary tract infections, intraabdominal infections, **anaerobic pneumonia,** septicemia caused by streptococci and staphylococci, and serious skin and soft-tissue infections."
- **Its special advantage:** "being active against several **anaerobic** organisms and is **most often used for this purpose.**"
- **Resistance:** "**all Enterobacteriaceae are resistant to clindamycin.**"
- **Contraindications:** known hypersensitivity, "those with **ulcerative colitis or enteritis,** and **infants younger than 1 month of age.**"
- **Adverse effects:** GI are most common: "nausea, vomiting, abdominal pain, diarrhea, **pseudomembranous colitis,** and anorexia." Pseudomembranous colitis "is often associated with antibiotic therapy, **especially clindamycin therapy.**"
- **Neuromuscular blockade:** "known to have some **neuromuscular blocking properties** that may enhance the actions of neuromuscular drugs. Patients receiving both drugs need to be monitored for **excessive neuromuscular blockade and respiratory paralysis,** and appropriate ventilatory support provided as needed."
- **Administration** (p. 627): oral forms with "**at least 6 to 8 oz of water or other fluid.**" IV "by **piggyback** technique... **Most references state never to give these drugs by intravenous push.** Too rapid an intravenous infusion may lead to **severe hypotension and possible cardiac arrest.**" IM "deep into a large muscle mass." Topical forms: avoid "near the eyes or near any abraded areas," and avoid simultaneous "peeling or abrasive acne products, soaps, or alcohol-containing cosmetics."

### colistimethate (Coly-Mycin), "colistin" (p. 622)

- **Polypeptide** that "**penetrates and disrupts the bacterial membrane**" of gram-negative bacteria.
- "an **old drug that fell out of clinical use** when newer, less toxic drugs became available. Unfortunately, because of the emergence of **CRO** infections, it is now being used again, often as **one of the only drugs available to treat CRO.**"
- Routes: intravenous, intramuscular, inhalational. **"Colistin is infused over 3 to 5 minutes."**
- Adverse effects: "**renal failure** and **neurotoxic effects** such as paresthesia, numbness, tingling, vertigo, dizziness, and impairment of speech. **It can cause acute respiratory failure when administered by inhalation.**" Crosses the placenta.

### daptomycin (Cubicin) (p. 622)

- "currently the **only** drug of the new class known as **lipopeptides.**" Mechanism "is not completely known, but it binds to gram-positive cells in a **calcium-dependent process** and disrupts the cell membrane potential."
- Treats "complicated skin and soft-tissue infections caused by susceptible gram-positive bacteria, **including MRSA and VRE.** It also can be used to treat **vancomycin-intermediate or vancomycin-resistant strains of MRSA.**"
- **"Daptomycin cannot be used to treat pneumonia, because it is inactivated by lung surfactant."**
- Injectable only. **"Monitor creatine phosphokinase (CPK) levels throughout therapy because daptomycin may increase these levels."** Theoretical "risk for increased **myopathy**" with **statins.**

### linezolid (Zyvox) (pp. 622–623)

- "the **first** antibacterial drug in a class of antibiotics known as **oxazolidinones.**" Works "by inhibiting bacterial protein synthesis."
- "**originally developed to treat infections associated with vancomycin-resistant *Enterococcus faecium,* more commonly referred to as VRE.**" Also used for health care–associated pneumonia; complicated skin and skin structure infections "including cases caused by **MRSA**"; gram-positive infections in infants and children; community-associated pneumonia; uncomplicated skin infections.
- Adverse effects: "headache, nausea, diarrhea, and vomiting. It also has been shown to **decrease platelet count.**"
- **"It has excellent oral absorption, which allows patients to continue oral therapy at home for serious infections that would otherwise require hospitalization."**
- **Three interactions, and they are the reason linezolid is tested:**

| Interaction | Result |
|---|---|
| **Vasopressors** (e.g., **dopamine**) | "potential to **strengthen the vasopressor (prohypertensive) effects**... by an unclear mechanism" |
| **Serotonergic drugs (SSRIs)** | "postmarketing case reports of this drug causing **serotonin syndrome.** It is recommended that the SSRI be **stopped** while the patient is receiving linezolid therapy; however, often this is not realistic and patients must be **watched carefully**" |
| **Tyramine-containing foods** | "**aged cheese or wine, soy sauce, smoked meats or fish, and sauerkraut** can interact with linezolid to **raise blood pressure**" |

- **The tyramine mechanism, as the chapter explains it** (p. 626): "Tyramine is normally broken down to an enzyme called **monoamine oxidase (MAO).** When MAO inhibitors are taken then large amounts of tyramine are released into the bloodstream. This leads to the release of **adrenalin-like substances** in the body and results in an **increased blood pressure.**"
- **Administration** (p. 627): "Oral doses are to be evenly spaced **around the clock**... and given with **food or milk.** **Oral suspension forms must be used within 21 days of reconstitution.** Protect intravenous doses **from light** and infuse over **30 to 120 minutes**; **do not mix with any other medication.**"
- **tedizolid (Sivextro),** approved 2014: "similar to linezolid, and it is **effective against some linezolid-resistant strains.** Tedizolid is available in oral and intravenous forms and is **dosed once a day.**"

### metronidazole (Flagyl) (p. 623)

- **Nitroimidazole.** "It has especially good activity against **anaerobic** organisms and is widely used to treat **intraabdominal and gynecologic infections.**" Anaerobes named: "*Peptostreptococcus, Eubacterium, Bacteroides,* and *Clostridioides.*"
- Also for **protozoal infections:** "**amebiasis and trichomoniasis**" (see Ch. 43).
- Mechanism: "works by **interfering with microbial DNA synthesis** and in this regard **is similar to the quinolones.**"
- ***C. difficile* status has changed:** "Historically, it was used as the **first-line treatment for *C. difficile* diarrhea; however, because of resistance, it is no longer recommended as first line.**"
- Pregnancy category **B**, "although it is **not recommended for use during the first trimester.**"
- Adverse effects: "dizziness, headache, gastrointestinal discomfort, nasal congestion, and **reversible neutropenia and thrombocytopenia.**"
- **The alcohol rule, with its two numbers** (p. 623): "**acute alcohol intolerance** when it is taken with alcoholic beverages, because of the accumulation of **acetaldehyde,** the principal alcohol metabolite. Patients must **avoid alcohol for 24 hours before initiation of therapy and for at least 36 hours after the last dose.**"
- Other interactions: increases toxicity of "**lithium, benzodiazepines, cyclosporine, calcium channel blockers, various antidepressants (e.g., venlafaxine), warfarin.**" In the other direction, "**phenytoin and phenobarbital may reduce the effects of metronidazole,**" via cytochrome P-450 effects.
- **Administration** (p. 627): give oral forms "**with food or meals.** Educate patients **not to chew extended-release dosage forms.** Intravaginal doses are recommended to be administered **at bedtime.**" Topical forms applied thinly, gloves worn, and "**do not apply topical forms close to the eyes.**"
- **secnidazole (Solosec)** is "similar to metronidazole, that is used as a **single 2-g dose for bacterial vaginosis.**"

### nitrofurantoin (Macrodantin, Furadantin) (p. 623)

- **Nitrofuran,** "indicated **primarily for urinary tract infections** caused by ***E. coli, S. aureus, Klebsiella* species, and *Enterobacter* species.**"
- Mechanism: "interfering with the activity of **enzymes that regulate bacterial carbohydrate metabolism** and also by **disrupting bacterial cell wall formation.**"
- **Contraindicated in "significant renal function impairment, because the drug concentrates in the urine."** Dose "must be reduced for older adult patients or those with decreased renal function."
- **Oral only.**
- Adverse effects: "gastrointestinal discomfort, dizziness, headache, skin reactions (mild to severe reported), blood dyscrasias, ECG changes, **possibly irreversible peripheral neuropathy,** and **hepatotoxicity. Although hepatotoxicity is rare, it is often fatal.**"
- Interactions: **probenecid** reduces renal excretion; **antacids** reduce GI absorption.
- **Administration** (p. 627): "must be given with **plenty of fluids, food, or milk.** **Avoid crushing of tablets** to help decrease gastric upset and **prevent staining of teeth.**" Watch for "**jaundice, itching, rash, and liver enlargement**" (liver toxicity) and "**numbness and tingling**" (peripheral neuropathy).
- **fosfomycin (Monurol)** is also approved for UTIs: "given as a **one-time dose** and maintains **high concentrations in the urine for up to 48 hours.**"

### quinupristin and dalfopristin (Synercid) (p. 623)

- "two **streptogramin** antibacterials marketed in a **30:70 fixed combination.**"
- Approved for "intravenous treatment of **bacteremia and life-threatening infection caused by VRE**" and complicated skin and skin structure infections caused by *S. pyogenes* and *S. aureus*, "**including MRSA.**"
- Adverse effects: "**arthralgias and myalgias, which may become severe.**" Infusion-site reactions "including pain, inflammation, edema, and thrombophlebitis, have developed in **approximately 75% of patients treated through a peripheral intravenous line.**"
- **The diluent rule:** "must be infused with **5% dextrose in water (D5W) only** and **cannot be mixed with saline or heparin, including heparinized flushes.**"
- **Administration** (p. 628): "Reconstitute the drug using **only D5W.** To minimize **foaming** of the solution, mix the drug with a **gentle swirling action instead of shaking.** A diluted infusion bag of the drug is stable for up to **6 hours,** or **54 hours if refrigerated.** Infusions are generally given over **at least 60 minutes.**"
- Interaction: "potential **increase in levels of cyclosporine.**"

### The lipoglycopeptides: telavancin, dalbavancin, oritavancin (pp. 623–624)

| Drug | What is distinctive |
|---|---|
| **telavancin (Vibativ)** | "in the class called the **glycopeptides**." Indicated for **skin and skin structure infections and pneumonia** caused by gram-positive organisms. **"It is effective against MRSA."** "**must not be used in pregnant women,**" and the dose "needs to be adjusted for renal dysfunction." Common adverse effects: "**renal toxicity, infusion-related reactions, and QT prolongation.**" Give with "close monitoring of the patient's **ECG for changes in the QT segment**" (p. 628) |
| **dalbavancin (Dalvance)** | indicated **only** for skin and skin structure infections including MRSA. **"extremely long half-lives."** Dosed as "a **1000-mg single dose followed by a 500-mg single dose 1 week later**"; the implementation section describes it as "**dosed once weekly**" (p. 626). Side effects: nausea, diarrhea, headache; also "hypersensitivity reactions, elevated liver function tests, and infusion reactions that **resemble 'red man syndrome'**" |
| **oritavancin (Orbactiv)** | indicated **only** for skin and skin structure infections including MRSA. "**a single dose of 1200 mg IV over 3 hours.**" Side effects: hypersensitivity reactions, infusion reactions, tachycardia, headache, dizziness, anemia. **"It is contraindicated with use of unfractionated heparin for 48 hours after oritavancin administration, as it can falsely elevate aPTT. Co-administration with warfarin may increase bleed risk"** |

### vancomycin (p. 624)

- "a **bactericidal** antibiotic in the **glycopeptide** class. It destroys bacteria by **binding to the bacterial cell wall,** producing **immediate inhibition of cell wall synthesis and death.** **This mechanism differs from that of the beta-lactam antibiotics.**"
- **"Vancomycin is the antibiotic of choice for the treatment of MRSA infection"** and other gram-positive infections. **"It is not active against gram-negative bacteria, fungi, or yeast."**
- **The oral/parenteral split, which is the chapter's cleanest route trap:**

| Route | Indication |
|---|---|
| **Oral** | "**antibiotic-induced colitis (*C. difficile*)** and... **staphylococcal enterocolitis.** Because the oral formulation is **poorly absorbed** from the gastrointestinal tract, it is used **for its local effects on the tract's surface**" |
| **Parenteral** | "**bone and joint infections and bacterial bloodstream infections** caused by *Staphylococcus* species" |

- Implementation restates it (p. 628): "Oral dosage forms are **only** used to treat microbes **in** the gastrointestinal tract. **Parenteral dosage forms must be used to treat infection outside of the intima of the gastrointestinal tract.**"
- **Resistance:** "noted with increasing frequency in patients with infections caused by ***Enterococcus*** organisms... **Resistance to MRSA has been rarely reported to occur with vancomycin.**"
- **Cautions:** "preexisting **renal dysfunction or hearing loss,** as well as in **older adult patients and neonates.**"

**Vancomycin blood levels** (p. 624):

| Level | Value as given |
|---|---|
| **Subtherapeutic** | "**less than 5 mcg/mL**" |
| **Optimal peak** | "**18 to 50 mcg/mL**" |
| **Optimal trough** | "**10 to 20 mcg/mL**" |
| **Practitioners' working goal** | "many practitioners use a trough level of **15 to 20 mcg/mL** depending on indication" |
| **Toxic** | "**over 50 mcg/mL**" |

- **"Measurement of peak levels is no longer routinely recommended, and only trough levels are commonly monitored. Blood samples for measurement of trough levels are drawn immediately before administration of the next dose."**
- When to start monitoring: the assessment section says troughs "are generally done **before the fourth dose** of a new regimen and need to be obtained **within 30 minutes** before the next scheduled dose" (p. 625); the implementation section says "**before the third or fourth dose**" (p. 628).
- **Toxicities:** "the two most severe of which are **ototoxicity (hearing loss)** and **nephrotoxicity (kidney damage).** Nephrotoxicity is more likely to occur with concurrent therapy with other nephrotoxic drugs such as **aminoglycosides, cyclosporine, and contrast media used for CT scans.**" Also "additive **neuromuscular blocking** effects in patients receiving neuromuscular blockers."

**Red man syndrome** (p. 624): "another common adverse effect that is **bothersome, but usually not harmful**... characterized by **flushing and/or itching of the head, face, neck, and upper trunk area.** It is **most commonly seen when the drug is infused too rapidly.** It usually can be alleviated by **slowing the rate of infusion of the dose to at least 1 hour. Rapid infusions may also cause hypotension.**"

- **Infusion rule:** "**Vancomycin must be infused over at least 60 minutes, and longer for higher doses.**"
- Implementation adds (p. 628): "Too rapid an infusion of vancomycin **or administration by intravenous push** may lead to **severe hypotension and red man syndrome. Extravasation may cause local skin irritation and damage;** therefore frequently monitor the infusion and in particular the intravenous site."
- **Hydration:** "adequate hydration (**at least 2 L of fluids every 24 hours** unless contraindicated) is important to prevent nephrotoxicity."

> **Trap (confusable pair).** **Red man syndrome vs. an allergic reaction.** This is the chapter's flagship confusable, and its own case study (p. 626) builds a stem on it: two days into vancomycin, Mr. M "complains of feeling 'hot' in his face and neck, and itching in those same areas. His face and neck are flushed." Cue: *red man = **rate-related, not immune,** confined to **head, face, neck, upper trunk,** "bothersome but usually not harmful," and the fix is to **slow the infusion to at least 1 hour** rather than to stop the drug and treat anaphylaxis.* Hypotension can accompany it, which is what makes the anaphylaxis reading tempting.

> **Trap (confusable pair).** **Oral vancomycin vs. IV vancomycin are not substitutes in either direction.** Cue: *oral is **not absorbed,** so it works **only inside the gut** (*C. diff,* staphylococcal enterocolitis); IV works **everywhere but the gut lumen.*** An option treating MRSA bacteremia with oral vancomycin, or *C. difficile* colitis with IV vancomycin, is confusing local effect with systemic effect. The dosage table footnote states it flatly: "Oral is not absorbed and is used only for *C. difficile* diarrhea."

> **Trap (confusable pair).** **Vancomycin trough vs. aminoglycoside trough.** Both are drawn immediately before the next dose and both guard against the same two toxicities, but the **numbers are an order of magnitude apart.** Cue: *aminoglycoside trough goal is **at or below 1 to 2** mcg/mL; vancomycin trough goal is **10 to 20** mcg/mL.* A vancomycin trough of 1 is dangerously subtherapeutic; a gentamicin trough of 15 is frankly toxic. The same number means opposite things depending on the drug.

> **Trap (level/stage swap).** **Vancomycin peaks are no longer routinely drawn,** and neither are aminoglycoside peaks on once-daily dosing. But the chapter still **prints** an optimal peak range for vancomycin (18 to 50 mcg/mL). An option that has the nurse drawing a vancomycin peak is out of date by the chapter's own statement, even though the number exists in the same paragraph.

> **Trap (confusable pair).** **Which drug cannot treat pneumonia, and why.** Two drugs across these two chapters carry a pneumonia exclusion, for entirely different reasons. Cue: *dapto**mycin** cannot treat pneumonia because it is **inactivated by lung surfactant**; **doripenem** (Ch. 38) must not be used for pneumonia because of **increased mortality risk.*** A stem that gives the wrong reason is testing mechanism, not recall.

> **Trap (confusable pair).** **Metronidazole vs. cefotetan: two disulfiram-like reactions, two different windows.** Cue: *cefotetan (Ch. 38) = avoid alcohol **within 72 hours after**; metronidazole = avoid alcohol **24 hours before starting** and **at least 36 hours after** the last dose.* Metronidazole is the only drug in these two chapters with a **pre**-therapy alcohol restriction. Critical thinking exercise 1 (p. 630) is built on it: a patient discharged on oral metronidazole says, "Our annual office party is tomorrow night."

> **Trap (confusable pair).** **Linezolid's two blood-pressure interactions have different sources and the same direction.** Cue: *SSRIs → **serotonin syndrome**; tyramine foods and vasopressors → **hypertension**.* Both must be assessed, and review question 2 (p. 630) is a SATA item listing oral anticoagulant, SSRI antidepressant, thyroid replacement hormone, vasopressor, and beta blocker. Of those five, the chapter names **SSRIs** and **vasopressors** in linezolid's interaction paragraph; it names no interaction with anticoagulants, thyroid hormone, or beta blockers. (Official key not in the pull; see GAPS item 9.)

> **Trap (SATA, five options mapped to categories).** **The three drugs whose class name is the answer.** Cue: *linezolid = **oxazolidinone** (the **first** in its class); daptomycin = **lipopeptide** (currently the **only** one); quinupristin/dalfopristin = **streptogramins** (a **30:70** fixed combination).* Clindamycin's class, **lincosamide,** appears only in the dosage table, while the key points instead call it "a semisynthetic derivative of **lincomycin.**" Both are correct and they point at the same family.

> **Trap (priority question).** **Never IV push: clindamycin and vancomycin, same consequence.** Clindamycin by rapid IV "may lead to severe hypotension and **possible cardiac arrest**"; vancomycin by IV push "may lead to severe hypotension and red man syndrome." If an option pushes either drug, that is the order to question.

> **Trap (plausible-but-adjacent).** **D5W only for Synercid.** Normal saline is the reflex diluent for almost everything else in this chapter, including vancomycin ("either D5W or NS") and gentamicin ("either normal saline or D5W"). ## §5 Nursing process, gathered

**Baseline diagnostic studies before starting any drug in this chapter** (p. 625), quoted as a numbered set because it reads as a checklist:

1. "**AST and ALT** levels for assessing liver function"
2. "**urinalysis, BUN, and serum creatinine** levels for assessing renal function"
3. "**ECG, echocardiography, ultrasonography, and/or cardiac enzyme** levels for assessing cardiac function"
4. "**culture and sensitivity** of infected tissue or blood samples"
5. "**WBC count, hemoglobin level, hematocrit, red blood cell count, platelet count, and clotting values**"

**Hypersensitivity spectrum** (p. 625): "Symptoms may range from **mild reactions with rash, pruritus, or hives** to **severe reactions with laryngeal edema, bronchospasm, hypotension, and/or possible cardiac arrest.**"

**Class-specific assessment, condensed** (pp. 625–626):

| Class or drug | Assess specifically for |
|---|---|
| **Aminoglycosides** | **baseline hearing tests and vestibular function**; renal function studies (BUN, urinalysis, serum and urine creatinine); **hydration status**; other nephrotoxic drugs including **CT contrast**; neuromuscular status (myasthenia gravis or Parkinson's disease patients "may experience worsening of muscle weakness because of the drug's neuromuscular blockade") |
| **Quinolones** | preexisting **CNS conditions (seizure or stroke disorders)**; existing **neuropathies**; **cardiac history and antidysrhythmic use**; baseline **liver** function |
| **Clindamycin** | hypersensitivity; **not for ulcerative colitis or age younger than 1 month**; bowel sounds and bowel patterns before giving; concurrent **neuromuscular blocking drugs** ("these patients, if in need of clindamycin, would need ventilator support") |
| **Linezolid** | concurrent **serotonergic drugs (SSRIs)**; intake of **tyramine-containing foods** |
| **Metronidazole** | allergy to nitroimidazole derivatives; baseline **neurologic, GI, and genitourinary** systems; **alcohol intake**; interactions with benzodiazepines, calcium channel blockers, antidepressants, warfarin |
| **Nitrofurantoin** | **renal and liver** function; baseline sensory/motor exam for **peripheral neuropathy** ("this may be irreversible"); skin color, turgor, intactness, rash |
| **Quinupristin/dalfopristin** | vital signs; **muscle aches and pain** (arthralgia, myalgia) |
| **Vancomycin** | other **nephrotoxic or ototoxic** drugs; history of renal disease and hearing loss; **baseline hearing status**; **skin color** (red man syndrome); **blood pressure** (rapid infusion causes hypotension); "multiple drug and diluent incompatibilities" |

**Culture timing, restated with the practical exception** (p. 626): "it is important for patient safety to **review culture and sensitivity reports before therapy is initiated.** However, it may be necessary to start the prescribed medication regimen (because of clinical presentation), **before results being obtained but after a specimen has been collected.** The medication can then be **changed as dictated by the culture and sensitivity results.**"

**Evaluation** (p. 628): therapeutic goals are "a **return to normal of all blood counts and vital signs, negative results on culture and sensitivity testing,** and improved appetite, energy level, and sense of well-being." Monitor for "superinfections, antibiotic-associated colitis, **nephrotoxicity, ototoxicity, neurotoxicity, hepatotoxicity,** and other drug-specific adverse effects."

**Superinfection** (p. 625, cross-referenced to Ch. 38): "**Fungal superinfections may be manifested by fever, lethargy, perineal itching, and other anatomically related symptoms.**" Prevention: "Consumption of **yogurt or buttermilk and/or probiotics** may help prevent antibiotic-induced superinfections" (p. 627).

**Handwashing, in the chapter's own detail** (p. 628): engage in frequent handwashing "especially during cold and flu season, before and after preparing food, and after using the bathroom or changing diapers; touching bare body parts; coughing, sneezing, or using a handkerchief or disposable tissue; eating or drinking; using tobacco; using the telephone; shaking hands; and playing with pets."

> **Trap (confusable pair).** **Which baseline test goes with which drug.** Cue: *aminoglycosides and vancomycin = **hearing + creatinine**; quinolones = **ECG + neuro + liver**; clindamycin = **bowel sounds**; nitrofurantoin = **liver + peripheral sensation**; daptomycin = **CPK**; linezolid = **platelets**.* Review question 3 (p. 630) asks what is **most important** to assess before giving vancomycin, offering renal function, WBC count, liver function, and platelet count. The chapter pins vancomycin's two severe toxicities to the **kidneys and ears,** not the liver or marrow.

> **Trap (priority question).** **A patient on an IV aminoglycoside reports ringing in the ears.** Review question 4 (p. 630) offers reassurance, reducing the rate, increasing the rate, and stopping the infusion. The chapter's relevant facts: tinnitus is a **symptom of ototoxicity**, ototoxicity is "**often not reversible**," hearing loss "**may occur even after completion of therapy,**" and "alteration in auditory, vestibular, or renal function may indicate the need for a possible **dosage adjustment or withdrawal of the drug.**" Note that unlike red man syndrome, **rate is not the mechanism here,** so the rate-adjustment options are answering the wrong drug's problem.

> **Trap (level/stage swap).** **A tobramycin trough of 3 mcg/mL.** Review question 8 (p. 630) gives that value on daily dosing with the next dose due now. Held against Table 39.1, the once-daily goal for tobramycin is "**less than 1 mcg/mL**" and levels "**above 2 mcg/mL are associated with a greater risk for both ototoxicity and nephrotoxicity.**" The distractor set includes repeating the test and skipping only one dose, both of which delay a decision the numbers already support.
