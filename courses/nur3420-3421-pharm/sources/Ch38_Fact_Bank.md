> **Trap (confusable pair).** **Antiseptic vs. disinfectant.** Both are "topical antimicrobial agents," so the definitions differ on exactly two axes. Cue: *antisep**t**ic = living **t**issue, s**t**atic (inhibits)*; ***d**isinfectant = **d**ead/nonliving objects, ci**d**al (kills)*. Table 38.1 (p. 592) adds a third axis, **potency: antiseptics lower, disinfectants higher.** A distractor that puts an antiseptic on an instrument tray, or a disinfectant on a wound, is swapping the *where*.

> **Trap (confusable pair).** **Bactericidal vs. bacteriostatic.** *Cid**al** = **kill**s.* *Stat**ic** = holds **stat**ionary, only inhibits growth.* The key point at p. 611 states it plainly: "Bacteriostatic antibiotics inhibit the growth of bacteria but do not kill them. Bactericidal antibiotics kill the bacteria." Memorize which classes are which (see §3), because a stem naming a class and asking for its action is the most common form of this item.

> **Trap (confusable pair).** **Beta-lactamase vs. beta-lactamase inhibitor.** One is a **bacterial enzyme** that destroys the drug; the other is a **medication** added to the drug to block that enzyme. Cue: *if bacteria make it, it is the **-ase**; if a pharmacist dispenses it, it is the **inhibitor**.* **Penicillinase** is the subset of beta-lactamases that specifically inactivates penicillins (p. 596).

## §1 Infection, colonization, and where the infection came from

**The classic signs and symptoms of infection** (p. 592): "fever, chills, sweats, redness, pain and swelling, fatigue, weight loss, increased white blood cell (WBC) count, and the formation of pus."

- **"Not all patients will exhibit signs of the infection. This is especially true in older adults and immunocompromised patients"** (p. 592). This is the override clause for any "which patient could still be infected" stem.

**Gram stain** (p. 590, Clinical Pearl): **"Gram stain purple = Gram positive. Gram stain red = Gram negative."**

| | Gram-positive | Gram-negative |
|---|---|---|
| Gram stain | **Purple** | **Red** |
| Cell wall | "very thick cell wall, known as peptidoglycan," thick outer capsule | "more complex," smaller outer capsule and peptidoglycan layer, **two** cell membranes (outer and inner) |
| Consequence for therapy | easier to penetrate | "gram-negative bacterial infections **more difficult to treat,** because the drug molecules have a harder time penetrating the more complex cell walls" |

**Colonization vs. infection** (p. 591): in colonization "bacteria are present in open wounds, in secretions, on mucous membranes, or on the skin," but "these patients do not have any overt signs of infection." **"Colonization does not require antibiotic treatment. However, it is not uncommon for these colonizations to be treated, which may be one way in which drug-resistant organisms emerge."**

**Community-associated vs. health care-associated infection** (p. 591):

| | Community-associated | Health care-associated (formerly **nosocomial**) |
|---|---|---|
| Definition as given | "acquired by a person who has **not recently (within the past year)** been hospitalized or had a medical procedure (e.g., dialysis, surgery, catheterization)" | "an infection that a patient acquires during the course of receiving treatment for another condition in a health care institution" |
| Timing test | no recent health care contact | "not present or incubating at the time of admission but occurs **more than 48 hours after admission**" |
| Why it matters | | "one of the top 10 leading causes of death in the United States"; harder to treat because the organisms "have been exposed to strong antibiotics in the past and are the most drug resistant and the most virulent" |

**Health care-associated infection facts worth holding** (p. 591):

- Most common organism: **MRSA** is "now being one of the most common." Other serious pathogens "include *Enterococcus, Klebsiella, Acinetobacter,* and *Pseudomonas aeruginosa.*"
- Incidence: **"develop in 1 in 25 hospitalized patients in a given day."**
- Most common types: **"urinary tract infections (UTIs), surgical site infections, bloodstream infections, and pneumonia."**
- Highest-risk units: **"the critical care, dialysis, oncology, transplant, and burn units,"** because "the host defenses of the patients in these areas are typically compromised."
- **"Over 70% of health care–associated infections are preventable."**
- Most common mode of transmission: **"direct contact."**
- **"Handwashing is the single most important thing health care professionals can do to prevent the spread of these potentially deadly infections."**

> **Trap (confusable pair).** **Community-associated vs. health care-associated.** The discriminator is **timing plus setting, not severity**. Cue: *"within the past year" for community-associated; "more than 48 hours after admission" for health care-associated.* A patient who develops pneumonia on hospital day 4 is health care-associated even though they walked in from the community; a patient admitted **with** pneumonia is not, because it was incubating at admission.

> **Trap (confusable pair).** **Colonization vs. infection.** Cue: *bacteria present + **no** overt signs = colonization = **no antibiotic**.* The distractor is always the option that treats the colonized patient, and the chapter pre-arms you against it by naming that exact error as a driver of resistance.

> **Trap (priority question).** If a stem asks the **single most important** nursing action to prevent health care-associated infection, the answer is **handwashing**, not gloves, isolation, or disinfectants. The chapter states it in superlative form, which is how a "priority" key is written.

## §2 The three uses of antibiotics

"Antibiotics have three general uses: empiric therapy, definitive therapy, and prophylactic or preventive therapy" (p. 592).

| | **Empiric** | **Definitive** | **Prophylactic** |
|---|---|---|---|
| Trigger | signs and symptoms appear "long before a causative organism can be identified," and risk of severe complications is high (e.g., "suspected acute meningitis") | "Once the results of culture and sensitivity testing are available (usually in **48 to 72 hours**)" | patient "scheduled to undergo a procedure (i.e., surgery) in which the likelihood of dangerous microbial contamination is high" |
| Basis of choice | the practitioner's judgment of "the microorganisms known to be the most common causes of the infection" | the identified organism, "using the most narrow-spectrum, least toxic drug based on sensitivity results" | the expected contamination of the surgical field |
| Timing rule | **"culture specimens must be obtained before drug therapy is initiated whenever possible"** | after culture results return | **"generally 30 minutes before the incision to ensure adequate tissue penetration"** |

- **Why culture first:** "Otherwise, the presence of antibiotics in the tissues may result in misleading culture results." The nursing section restates it as the consequence: "Taking antibiotics before a culture would lead to false negative results" (p. 608).
- **Broad- vs. narrow-spectrum** (p. 592): broad-spectrum drugs "are active against numerous organisms (gram-positive, gram-negative, and anaerobic)"; narrow-spectrum drugs "are effective against only a few organisms." **"Overuse of broad-spectrum antibiotics contributes to resistance. The goal of therapy is to use the most narrow-spectrum drug when possible based on sensitivity results."**
- **SCIP** (p. 592): the Surgical Care Improvement Project is "a national performance improvement project that provides hospitals with evidence-based recommendations on the administration of prophylactic antibiotics."
- The chapter's own worked example of why the risk varies: coronary artery bypass surgery is "relatively low" risk, while intraabdominal surgery after a motor vehicle accident is a "contaminated or 'dirty' surgical field," so "antibiotic therapy would likely be required for a longer period after the procedure."

**Response categories** (p. 592). These three are a graded set, and the chapter defines each against the same baseline:

| Response | Definition as given |
|---|---|
| **Therapeutic** | "a decrease in the specific signs and symptoms of infection compared with the baseline findings (e.g., fever, elevated WBC count, redness, inflammation, drainage, pain)" |
| **Subtherapeutic** | "when these signs and symptoms do not improve." Causes: "an incorrect route of drug administration, inadequate drainage of an abscess, poor drug penetration to the infected area, insufficient serum levels of the drug, or bacterial resistance" |
| **Toxic** | "when the serum levels of the antibiotic are too high or when the patient has an allergic or other major adverse reaction." Reactions: "rash, itching, hives, fever, chills, joint pain, difficulty breathing, or wheezing" |

> **Trap (confusable pair).** **Empiric vs. definitive.** Cue: *e**m**piric = **m**aybe, best guess, **before** culture results; **d**efinitive = **d**ocumented, **after** culture and sensitivity.* Both are treatments of an existing infection, which is what makes them swappable in distractors. The tell in the stem is whether culture results are back.

> **Trap (confusable pair).** **Prophylactic vs. empiric.** Both happen before an organism is identified, so timing alone will not separate them. Cue: *prophylactic = there is **no infection yet** (surgery tomorrow); empiric = there **is** an infection, we just do not know its name.* Review question 1 (p. 612) is exactly this item: a patient scheduled for colorectal surgery, normal WBC, no fever, antibiotic ordered "on call just before he goes to surgery." The distractors offered are empiric therapy, superinfection treatment, and reducing resistant organisms; the key is **prophylactic**.

> **Trap (plausible-but-adjacent).** "Reduce the number of resistant organisms" is a **true statement about antibiotic stewardship** but it is not why a single preoperative dose is ordered. Anchor the fact to what it answers: the pre-op dose prevents *this patient's* surgical site infection.

> **Trap (level/stage swap).** **Subtherapeutic is not a synonym for resistant.** Resistance is only **one** of five listed causes of a subtherapeutic response. A stem describing no improvement is not automatically a resistance item; wrong route, undrained abscess, poor penetration, and low serum levels are all in the definition.

## §3 How antibiotics work: the four mechanisms

"The four most common mechanisms of antibiotic action are (1) interference with bacterial cell wall synthesis, (2) interference with protein synthesis, (3) interference with replication of nucleic acids (deoxyribonucleic acid [DNA] and ribonucleic acid [RNA]), and (4) antimetabolite action that disrupts critical metabolic reactions inside the bacterial cell" (p. 594).

Fig. 38.3 (p. 594) maps classes onto those mechanisms. The classes named in **this** chapter:

| Class | Mechanism | Cidal or static | Ribosome subunit |
|---|---|---|---|
| **Sulfonamides** | **antimetabolite**: block folic acid synthesis | **bacteriostatic** | n/a |
| **Trimethoprim** | antimetabolite (folic acid pathway); **not a sulfonamide** | | n/a |
| **Penicillins** | cell wall synthesis | **bactericidal** | n/a |
| **Cephalosporins** | cell wall synthesis | **bactericidal** | n/a |
| **Carbapenems** | cell wall synthesis | **bactericidal** | n/a |
| **Monobactams (aztreonam)** | cell wall synthesis | **bactericidal** | n/a |
| **Macrolides** | protein synthesis | **bacteriostatic** ("in high enough concentrations they may be bactericidal to some susceptible bacteria") | **50S** |
| **Tetracyclines** | protein synthesis | **bacteriostatic** | **30S** |

**Sulfonamide mechanism, in the chapter's own words** (p. 594): "They inhibit the growth of susceptible bacteria by preventing bacterial synthesis of folic acid... in a process known as **competitive inhibition,** sulfonamides compete with **para-aminobenzoic acid (PABA)** for the bacterial enzyme tetrahydropteroic acid synthetase."

- Why this is safe in humans: **"Microorganisms that require exogenous folic acid (not synthesized by the bacterium itself) are not affected by sulfonamide antibiotics. Therefore these drugs do not affect folic acid metabolism in human cells."**
- Trimethoprim "works via a similar mechanism, inhibiting dihydrofolic acid reduction to tetrahydrofolate."

**Beta-lactam mechanism** (pp. 596–597): all four subclasses "inhibit the synthesis of the bacterial peptidoglycan cell wall." Penicillins "bind to specific proteins called **penicillin-binding proteins**... This causes the formation of defective cell walls that are unstable and easily broken down... Bacterial death usually results from **lysis** (rupture) of the bacterial cells."

- **Time-dependent killing** (p. 596): "The beta-lactams exhibit time-dependent killing (meaning their concentration must be above the minimum inhibitory concentration to kill bacteria). Because of this property, it is not unusual to see them given by **continuous intravenous infusion.** They are also given by intermittent infusion (i.e., every 4 to 6 hours)."

> **Trap (confusable pair).** **50S vs. 30S.** *Ma**c**rolides = **5**0S. Te**t**racyclines = **3**0S.* Both are protein-synthesis inhibitors and both are bacteriostatic, so a distractor will simply swap the number. The chapter adds one more discriminator: macrolides bind **reversibly** to the 50S subunit.

> **Trap (confusable pair).** **Sulfonamide vs. penicillin mechanism.** Both are "how does this drug kill bacteria" items, but sulfonamides are **antimetabolites that inhibit growth** and penicillins are **cell wall inhibitors that kill.** A stem mentioning folic acid or PABA is never a beta-lactam item.

> **Trap (plausible-but-adjacent).** The key point at p. 611 says **"Most antibiotics work by inhibiting bacterial cell wall synthesis in some way."** That is the chapter's own generalization, but it does **not** apply to the sulfonamides, macrolides, or tetracyclines covered in this same chapter. Do not carry the generalization onto a specific-class stem.

## §4 Host factors

"Some of these host factors are age, allergy history, kidney and liver function, pregnancy status, genetic characteristics, site of infection, and host defenses" (p. 593).

| Host factor | What the chapter says about it |
|---|---|
| **Age (children)** | "infants and children may not be able to take certain antibiotics such as **tetracyclines,** which affect developing teeth or bones; **quinolones,** which may affect bone or cartilage development in children; and **sulfonamides,** which may displace bilirubin from albumin and precipitate **kernicterus** (hyperbilirubinemia) in neonates" |
| **Age (older adults)** | "a gradual decline in the function of the kidneys and liver, the organs primarily responsible for metabolizing and eliminating antibiotics. Therefore... **dosage adjustments** may be necessary" |
| **Allergy history** | "**Penicillins and sulfonamides** are two broad classes of antibiotic to which many people have allergic anaphylactic reactions" |
| **Pregnancy** | "several antibiotics can pass through the placenta and cause harm to the developing fetus. Drugs that cause developmental abnormalities in the fetus are called **teratogens**" |
| **Genetics: G6PD deficiency** | "administration of antibiotics such as **sulfonamides, nitrofurantoin, and dapsone**... may result in the **hemolysis, or destruction, of red blood cells**" |
| **Genetics: slow acetylation** | "certain drugs to be metabolized more slowly than usual... This can lead to **toxicity from drug accumulation**" |
| **Site of infection** | "Some antibiotics do not penetrate into the site of infection, such as the **lung or bone or abscesses,** which can lead to treatment failures" |

**Anaphylaxis symptoms as given** (p. 593): "flushing, itching, hives, anxiety, fast irregular pulse, and throat and tongue swelling. The most dangerous such reaction is **anaphylactic shock,** in which a patient can go into respiratory arrest."

**The allergy-history interview** (p. 593). This is a nursing-judgment item, not a pharmacology one:

- "Many patients will say that they are 'allergic' to a medication when in fact what they experienced was a **common mild adverse effect such as stomach upset or nausea.**"
- "Patients who report drug allergies need to be asked **open-ended questions** to elicit descriptions of prior allergic reactions so that the actual severity of the reaction can be assessed."
- The three severe reactions that must be charted: "**any difficulty breathing; significant rash, hives, or other skin reaction; and severe gastrointestinal intolerance.**"

**Hypersensitivity timing, from the implementation section** (p. 610). Assessment does not stop after the first dose:

| Reaction type | Onset |
|---|---|
| **Immediate** | "may not occur for up to **30 minutes**" |
| **Accelerated** | "may occur within **1 to 72 hours**" |
| **Delayed** | "may occur **after 72 hours**" |

Manifestations: "wheezing; shortness of breath; swelling of the face, tongue, or hands; itching; or rash."

**Other interactions named in the host-factor discussion** (p. 593): the most common food-drug interaction is "**milk or cheese and tetracycline,** which results in decreased gastrointestinal absorption"; the drug-drug example is "**quinolone antibiotics and antacids or multivitamins with iron,** which leads to decreased absorption of quinolones."

> **Trap (confusable pair).** **G6PD deficiency vs. slow acetylation.** Both are named as "genetic host factors" in the same sentence, so they are built to be swapped. Cue: *G6PD = **red blood cells burst** (hemolytic anemia); slow acetylation = **drug piles up** (toxicity from accumulation).* One is a blood problem, the other is a metabolism problem.

> **Trap (confusable pair).** **Allergy vs. adverse effect.** Nausea and stomach upset are **adverse effects,** not allergy. The chapter names this misreport explicitly. A stem in which the patient says "I'm allergic to erythromycin, it upsets my stomach" is testing whether you document an allergy or an intolerance.

> **Trap (confusable pair).** **Which drug harms which pediatric structure.** Cue: *tetracyclines = **teeth** (and bone); quinolones = **cartilage**; sulfonamides = **bilirubin/kernicterus** in neonates.* Three drugs, three different pediatric harms, listed in one sentence, which is exactly the setup for an option swap.

> **Trap (priority question).** **If signs of hypersensitivity occur, the first action is to stop the drug** (p. 610): "the first thing to do is stop the dosage form immediately (if intravenously, stop the infusion; **leave the intravenous line in place,** contact the prescriber, and monitor the patient closely." Note the override embedded in the key: you stop the **infusion** but you do **not** remove the IV, because that line is the route for emergency drugs.

## §5 Sulfonamides (pp. 594–596)

**What is actually in clinical use** (p. 594): "although there are many compounds in the sulfonamide family, only **sulfamethoxazole combined with trimethoprim** (a nonsulfonamide antibiotic), known as **Bactrim, Septra, or co-trimoxazole** and often abbreviated as **SMX-TMP,** is used commonly in clinical practice."

- **Sulfisoxazole + erythromycin** is "occasionally used in pediatrics."
- **Sulfasalazine** "is used to treat ulcerative colitis and rheumatoid arthritis and **is not used as an antibiotic.**"
- **Co-trimoxazole composition** (p. 596): "a fixed-combination drug product containing a **5:1 ratio** of sulfamethoxazole to trimethoprim (i.e., each single-strength tablet contains **sulfamethoxazole 400 mg and trimethoprim 80 mg**)." Available in oral and injectable forms.

**Indications** (pp. 594–595). Sulfonamides "achieve very high concentrations in the kidneys, through which they are eliminated. Therefore **SMX-TMP is often used in the treatment of UTIs.**" Also:

- Respiratory tract infections, though "it is now **less effective against streptococci** infecting the upper respiratory tract and pharynx."
- "**prophylaxis and treatment of opportunistic infections in patients with human immunodeficiency virus (HIV) infection, especially infection by *Pneumocystis jirovecii,*** a common cause of HIV-associated pneumonia."
- "a drug of choice for infection caused by the bacterium *Stenotrophomonas maltophilia.*"
- "common treatment for **outpatient *Staphylococcus* infections,** because of the high rate of community-associated MRSA infections."
- The combination "allows for a **synergistic** antibacterial effect."
- **Phenazopyridine (Pyridium)**, a urinary analgesic available **over the counter**, "is given along with an antibiotic to help with the pain associated with a UTI." It is an analgesic, not an antibiotic.

**Contraindications** (p. 595): known sulfonamide allergy; **"pregnant women at term and in infants younger than 2 months of age."**

**The sulfa cross-allergy rules** (p. 595). This paragraph is unusually exam-shaped, because it splits chemically related drugs into safe and unsafe:

| Chemically related to sulfonamides | Status in a sulfonamide-allergic patient |
|---|---|
| **sulfonylureas** (diabetes, Ch. 32) | "generally considered **relatively safe**" |
| **thiazide and loop diuretics** (Ch. 28) | "generally considered **relatively safe**" |
| **carbonic anhydrase inhibitors** (Ch. 28) | "generally considered **relatively safe**" |
| **celecoxib (Celebrex)**, a COX-2 inhibitor (Ch. 44) | **"should not be used"** |
| **sulfites** (preservatives) | a different substance: "A person allergic to sulfonamide drugs **may or may not** also be allergic to sulfite preservatives" |

**Adverse effects** (pp. 595–596): "Sulfonamide drugs are a common cause of allergic reaction." The characteristic pattern is **delayed**, not immediate: "sulfonamides typically cause **delayed cutaneous reactions.** These reactions frequently begin with **fever followed by a rash** (morbilliform eruptions, erythema multiforme, or toxic epidermal necrolysis)." **Photosensitivity** reactions are "induced by exposure to sunlight during sulfonamide drug therapy" and "in some cases, such reactions can result in severe sunburn."

**Table 38.2 Sulfonamides: Reported Adverse Effects** (p. 595), reproduced in full:

| Body system | Adverse effects |
|---|---|
| **Blood** | Agranulocytosis, aplastic anemia, hemolytic anemia, thrombocytopenia |
| **Gastrointestinal** | Nausea, vomiting, diarrhea, pancreatitis, hepatotoxicity |
| **Integumentary** | Epidermal necrolysis, exfoliative dermatitis, **Stevens-Johnson syndrome**, photosensitivity |
| **Other** | Convulsions, **crystalluria**, toxic nephrosis, headache, peripheral neuritis, urticaria, cough |

**Interactions** (p. 595). Sulfonamides may potentiate:

- "the **hypoglycemic** effects of **sulfonylureas** in diabetes treatment"
- "the **toxic** effects of **phenytoin**"
- "the **anticoagulant** effects of **warfarin,** which can lead to hemorrhage"
- "may increase the likelihood of **cyclosporine-induced nephrotoxicity**"
- "Sulfonamides, **and all antibiotics,** may also reduce the efficacy of **oral contraceptives.**"

Clinical Pearls: **"Sulfonamides have many drug interactions!"** and **"Sulfonamides need to be taken with 8 ounces of water"** (p. 595).

**Nursing implementation** (p. 610): avoid in G6PD "because they antagonize folate synthesis and may cause hemolytic anemia." **"Encourage an increase in fluids (2000 to 3000 mL/24 hr), preferably water, to prevent drug-related crystalluria."** Oral forms "are to be taken with food to minimize GI upset." Sun precautions: **"stay out of direct sunlight, especially between 10:00 am and 3:00 pm,"** protective clothing, hat, sunglasses, and **"a sun blocking product with an SPF of at least 15. Lip protection is also needed."**

**Assessment** (p. 608): "Assess complete blood cell count before beginning sulfonamide therapy because of the possibility of drug-related **anemias/blood dyscrasias.**" With frequent or long-term therapy, "assess renal function studies, including BUN, creatinine, and urinalysis because of the potential for **drug-related crystalluria.**" Perform "a thorough skin assessment before and during drug therapy because of the potential for occurrence of the adverse effect of **Stevens-Johnson syndrome.**"

> **Trap (confusable pair).** **Sulfonamides vs. sulfites vs. sulfonylureas vs. "sulfur."** The chapter warns that patients say "sulfa allergy" or even "**sulfur** allergy" (p. 595). These are four different things. Cue: *the only chemically related drug the chapter forbids outright in sulfa allergy is **celecoxib**; sulfonylureas, thiazides, loop diuretics, and carbonic anhydrase inhibitors are "relatively safe."* A distractor that withholds a thiazide from a sulfa-allergic patient is over-applying the allergy.

> **Trap (confusable pair).** **Crystalluria vs. photosensitivity: same drug, two teaching points, two different interventions.** Cue: *crystalluria is answered with **fluids** (2000 to 3000 mL/24 hr); photosensitivity is answered with **sun protection**.* A stem asking why fluids are pushed has a renal answer, not a skin answer.

> **Trap (plausible-but-adjacent).** **Trimethoprim is not a sulfonamide,** and **sulfasalazine is not used as an antibiotic.** Both are stated explicitly, and both are exactly the sort of "true, but you assumed otherwise" detail an item writer builds an option from.

> **Trap (level/stage swap).** Sulfonamide skin reactions are **delayed** and open with **fever, then rash**. A distractor describing an immediate rash within 30 minutes of the first dose is describing the penicillin pattern, not the sulfonamide one.

## §6 Beta-lactams: the shared frame

"This broad group of drugs includes four major subclasses: **penicillins, cephalosporins, carbapenems, and monobactams**" (p. 596). All four "share a common structure and mechanism of action: they inhibit the synthesis of the bacterial peptidoglycan cell wall."

**How resistance works against them** (p. 596): "Some bacterial strains produce the enzyme **beta-lactamase**... The enzyme can break the chemical bond between the carbon (C) and nitrogen (N) atoms in the structure of the beta-lactam ring. When this happens, **all beta-lactam drugs lose their antibacterial efficacy.**"

**The four beta-lactamase inhibitors** (p. 596): "**clavulanic acid** (also called clavulanate), **tazobactam, sulbactam,** and **avibactam.**" They "bind with the beta-lactamase enzyme itself to prevent the enzyme from breaking down the penicillin molecule, **although they are not always effective.**"

**The combination products, by trade name** (p. 596):

| Trade name | Penicillin | Beta-lactamase inhibitor |
|---|---|---|
| **Unasyn** | ampicillin | sulbactam |
| **Augmentin** | amoxicillin | clavulanic acid |
| **Timentin** | ticarcillin | clavulanic acid |
| **Zosyn** | piperacillin | tazobactam |

Two more combinations appear elsewhere in the chapter: **Avycaz** = ceftazidime + avibactam (p. 602); **Zerbaxa** = ceftolozane + tazobactam (p. 600).

> **Trap (confusable pair).** **The four trade names.** This is the chapter's own designated medication-error trap, in a Safety and Quality Improvement box titled "Do You Know Your Penicillins?" (p. 597): "Medication errors have occurred when nurses gave penicillin products to patients who were allergic to penicillin. In these cases, the drugs were referred to by their **trade names, which lack the 'cillin' suffix.**" Clinical Pearl (p. 597): **"Trade names don't always end in 'cillin,' for example Zosyn."** Cue: *Un**a**syn = **a**mpicillin; **A**ugmentin = **a**moxicillin; Zosyn and Timentin give you no letter hint, so memorize those two outright.* The chapter's stated fix: "use **both trade and generic names** when ordering medications."

> **Trap (confusable pair).** **Beta-lactamase inhibitor vs. cilastatin.** Both are helper drugs packaged with an antibiotic, and both have names that look like nothing you would recognize. But **cilastatin is not a beta-lactamase inhibitor**: it "inhibits an enzyme in the kidneys called **dehydropeptidase**" that would otherwise break down imipenem (p. 603). Cue: *inhibitors block a **bacterial** enzyme; cilastatin blocks a **kidney** enzyme.*

## §7 Penicillins (pp. 596–599)

**Origin and class action:** "first derived from a **mold (fungus)** often seen on bread or fruit" (p. 596). Penicillins are **bactericidal**, "meaning they kill a wide variety of gram-positive and some gram-negative bacteria."

**Table 38.3 Classification of Penicillins** (p. 596), reproduced:

| Subclass | Generic drugs | Description as given |
|---|---|---|
| **Natural penicillins** | **penicillin G, penicillin V** | "these are the only two in current clinical use. **Penicillin G is the injectable form** for intravenous or intramuscular use; **penicillin V is an oral dosage form** (tablet and liquid)" |
| **Penicillinase-resistant drugs** | **cloxacillin, dicloxacillin, nafcillin, oxacillin** | "**Stable against hydrolysis by most staphylococcal penicillinases** (enzymes that normally break down the natural penicillins)" |
| **Aminopenicillins** | **amoxicillin, ampicillin** | "Have an **amino group** attached to the basic penicillin structure that **enhances their activity against gram-negative bacteria** compared with natural penicillins" |
| **Extended-spectrum drugs** | **piperacillin, ticarcillin, carbenicillin, piperacillin/tazobactam** | "Have **wider spectra of activity** than do all other penicillins" |

**Indications by subclass:**

- General: "gram-positive bacteria, including *Streptococcus* species, *Enterococcus* species, and *Staphylococcus* species. **Most natural penicillins have little if any ability to kill gram-negative bacteria**" (p. 597).
- **Penicillinase-resistant** (p. 598): also called **antistaphylococcal penicillins**. "The chemical structure of these drugs features a **large, bulky side chain** near the beta-lactam ring. This side chain serves as a **barrier to the penicillinase enzyme.**" Caveat: certain strains of *S. aureus* "are resistant to these drugs" and "require alternative antibiotic regimens." **Methicillin** "is part of this group but is **no longer used clinically.**"
- **Aminopenicillins** (p. 598): named "because of the presence of a free amino group (−NH2) in their chemical structure." "**Amoxicillin is an analogue of ampicillin.**"
- **Extended-spectrum** (p. 599): "excellent gram-positive, gram-negative, and anaerobic coverage," used "to treat many health care–associated infections, including pneumonia, intraabdominal infections, and sepsis." **Zosyn** "is commonly used in hospitalized patients with suspected or documented serious infections... it is often used as **empiric therapy.** Zosyn and Timentin are available for **intravenous use only.**"

**Route facts worth memorizing** (pp. 598–599):

| Drug | Routes |
|---|---|
| **penicillin G** | injectable (IV or IM) |
| **penicillin V potassium** | **oral only** |
| **nafcillin** | **injectable only** |
| **cloxacillin, dicloxacillin** | **oral only** |
| **oxacillin** | oral **and** injectable |
| **amoxicillin** | **oral only**, "can be given with or without food" |
| **ampicillin** | PO/IV/IM (three salt forms: anhydrous and trihydrate oral, sodium parenteral) |
| **Zosyn, Timentin** | **IV only** |

**Penicillin G salt forms** (p. 598), and the fatal-error warning attached to them: "Penicillin G has three salt forms: **benzathine, procaine, and potassium.** The benzathine and procaine salts are used as **longer-acting intramuscular injections**... **Never give these preparations intravenously, because their consistency is too thick for intravenous administration, and such use can be fatal.**" The IM formulations are "especially helpful for treating the sexually transmitted disease **syphilis,** because often only one injection is needed."

**Adverse effects** (p. 597). Penicillins are "usually safe and well-tolerated" and "the only usual contraindication is known drug allergy."

- Allergic reactions occur in **"0.7% to 4% of treatment courses."** Most common: "urticaria, pruritus, and angioedema."
- **Maculopapular rash: "about 2% of treatment courses with natural penicillin and 5.2% to 9.5% of those with ampicillin."**
- **Anaphylactic reactions: "0.004% to 0.015% of patients."**
- **"Severe reactions are much more common with injected than with orally administered penicillin."**
- "The intravenous formulations of some penicillins contain **large amounts of sodium and/or potassium.** Doses must be adjusted for patients with **renal dysfunction.**"

**Table 38.4 Penicillins: Reported Adverse Effects** (p. 597):

| Body system | Adverse effects |
|---|---|
| **Central nervous** | Lethargy, anxiety, depression, seizures |
| **Gastrointestinal** | Nausea, vomiting, diarrhea, taste alterations, oral candidiasis |
| **Hematologic** | Anemia, bone marrow depression, granulocytopenia |
| **Metabolic** | **Hyperkalemia, hypernatremia,** alkalosis |
| **Skin** | Pruritus, hives, rash |

**Table 38.5 Penicillins: Drug Interactions** (p. 598), reproduced:

| Interacting drug | Mechanism | Result |
|---|---|---|
| Aminoglycosides (IV) and clavulanic acid | Additivity | **More effective killing of bacteria** (a beneficial interaction) |
| methotrexate | Decreased renal elimination of methotrexate | Increased methotrexate levels |
| NSAIDs | Compete for protein binding | More free and active penicillin (**may be beneficial**) |
| Oral contraceptives | Uncertain | May decrease efficacy of the contraceptive |
| **probenecid** | Competes for elimination | **Prolongs the effects of penicillins** |
| rifampin | Inhibition | May inhibit the killing activity of penicillins |
| warfarin | Reduced vitamin K from gut flora | **Enhanced anticoagulant effect of warfarin** |

**The sodium and potassium load** (p. 608): "**penicillin G contains 1.7 mEq of potassium ion per million units and 2 mEq of sodium ion per million units.**" In heart failure, fluid overload, or dysrhythmias, "a high sodium or potassium level (hypernatremia or hyperkalemia) could lead to exacerbation of these health issues."

**Risk factors for penicillin allergy to assess for** (p. 608): "a history of **asthma, sensitivity to multiple allergens, aspirin allergy, and sensitivity to cephalosporins;** these factors are associated with a **higher risk for penicillin allergy.**" If procaine penicillin is ordered, "assess for **procaine hypersensitivity.**"

**Penicillin-cephalosporin cross-reactivity** (p. 597). The chapter gives both the historical and the current numbers, and they disagree:

| Source of the figure | Incidence |
|---|---|
| "historically was reported to be" | **1% to 4%** |
| "Current data suggest" | **less than 1%** without positive skin test |
| "Current data suggest" | **2%** for those with a positive skin test to penicillin |

- Current thinking attributes cross-reactivity to "the **structure of the cephalosporin side chain,** versus the beta-lactam ring which pencillins and cephalosporins share."
- **The clinical rule:** "In general, **only those patients with a history of throat swelling or hives from penicillin should not receive cephalosporins.**" "Some patients may require **skin testing and desensitization.**"

> **Trap (confusable pair).** **The four penicillin subclasses.** Cue: *natural = only two drugs, **G is the shot, V is the pill**; penicillinase-resistant = **anti-staph**, bulky side chain; **amino** adds **gram-negative** reach; extended = widest of all, the Zosyn tier.* A distractor that gives a natural penicillin for gram-negative coverage is swapping tiers.

> **Trap (setting vs. focus).** **Penicillinase-resistant does not mean beta-lactamase-proof.** These drugs resist **staphylococcal penicillinases** by their own structure, with no inhibitor added. The combination products (Unasyn, Augmentin, Zosyn, Timentin) resist by carrying a **separate inhibitor drug.** Two different solutions to the same problem, and item writers swap the mechanism.

> **Trap (confusable pair).** **amoxicillin vs. ampicillin.** Both aminopenicillins, one letter apart in the middle. Cue: *am**ox**icillin = **o**ral only, with or without food; **amp**icillin = **amp**le routes (PO/IV/IM), three salt forms.* Ampicillin also carries the higher rash rate (5.2% to 9.5% vs. about 2%).

> **Trap (priority question).** **Never give benzathine or procaine penicillin G intravenously.** The chapter says such use "can be fatal." If any option in a stem routes a thick, paste-like penicillin preparation IV, that is the answer to "which order does the nurse question."

> **Trap (confusable pair).** **Hyperkalemia and hypernatremia are penicillin adverse effects, not cephalosporin ones.** They come from the salt content of the IV formulations, which is why the chapter bothers to give the mEq figures. A stem about a heart failure patient on high-dose IV penicillin is an electrolyte item.

> **Trap (level/stage swap).** Cross-reactivity: the memorable number is **1% to 4%**, but the chapter labels that figure **historical** and gives **<1% / 2%** as current. The same 1% to 4% figure is repeated for cephalosporins at p. 600, so both live in this chapter. The clinical rule (throat swelling or hives) is the safer thing to reason from than either number.

## §8 Cephalosporins (pp. 599–603)

"Structurally and pharmacologically related to the penicillins... **bactericidal** and work by interfering with bacterial cell wall synthesis. They also bind to the same **penicillin-binding proteins**" (p. 599).

**What they never cover** (p. 599): "They are **not active against fungi, viruses, or enterococci.**"

**Table 38.6 Cephalosporins: Parenteral and Oral Preparations** (p. 600), reproduced as a generation grid:

| Generation | IV | PO |
|---|---|---|
| **First** | cefazolin, cephradine | cefadroxil, cephalexin, cephradine |
| **Second** | cefoxitin, cefuroxime, cefotetan | cefaclor, cefuroxime axetil, cefprozil |
| **Third** | cefotaxime, ceftizoxime, ceftriaxone, ceftazidime, ceftazidime/avibactam | cefpodoxime, ceftibuten, cefdinir |
| **Fourth** | cefepime | (none) |
| **Fifth** | ceftaroline, ceftolozane/tazobactam | (none) |

**The generation rule** (p. 600, Clinical Pearl): **"As the generations of cephalosporins increase, their gram-positive coverage decreases and gram-negative coverage increases."** The body text states the same thing: "the level of gram-negative coverage increases with each successive generation. The first-generation drugs have the most gram-positive coverage, and the later generations have the most gram-negative coverage."

Two additional rules the chapter states as absolutes:

- **"Anaerobic coverage is found only with the second-generation drugs"** (p. 600, repeated verbatim in the key points at p. 611).
- **Ceftaroline is "the only cephalosporin that treats MRSA"** (p. 602).

**Generation by generation, with the profiled drugs:**

| Gen | Coverage as given | Profiled drugs and their distinguishing feature |
|---|---|---|
| **1st** | "usually active against gram-positive bacteria and have **limited activity against gram-negative**" | **cefazolin (Ancef, Kefzol)**, "a prototypical first-generation cephalosporin," parenteral only, "used commonly for **surgical prophylaxis** and for susceptible staphylococcal infections." **cephalexin (Keflex)**, "a prototypical **oral** first-generation cephalosporin" |
| **2nd** | gram-positive similar to 1st, "but have **enhanced coverage against gram-negative**"; **the only generation with anaerobic coverage** | **cefoxitin (Mefoxin)** and **cefotetan** are the **cephamycins**, with "better coverage against various anaerobic bacteria such as *Bacteroides fragilis, Peptostreptococcus* species, and *Clostridioides* species"; cefoxitin "used extensively as a prophylactic antibiotic in patients undergoing **abdominal surgery** because it can effectively kill intestinal bacteria, including anaerobes." **cefuroxime**: the IV form is **cefuroxime sodium (Zinacef)**, the oral form is a different salt, **cefuroxime axetil (Ceftin)**, a **prodrug** "hydrolyzed in the liver to its active cefuroxime form"; unlike the cephamycins "**it does not kill anaerobic bacteria**" |
| **3rd** | "the **most potent of the first three generations** in fighting gram-negative bacteria, but they generally have **less activity than first- and second-generation drugs against gram-positive** organisms" | **ceftriaxone (Rocephin)**: "extremely long-acting," given **once a day**, "**able to pass easily through the blood-brain barrier**," so "one of the few cephalosporins that is indicated for the treatment of **meningitis**"; **93% to 96% protein bound**; "metabolized in the intestine after biliary excretion." **ceftazidime (Fortaz, Tazidime)**: activity against **Pseudomonas**; "generally given in combination with an aminoglycoside" |
| **4th** | | **cefepime (Maxipime)**, "the prototypical fourth-generation cephalosporin," "most closely resembles ceftazidime," but with "**increased activity against many *Enterobacter* species (gram-negative) as well as gram-positive organisms**"; indicated for uncomplicated and complicated UTIs, uncomplicated skin and skin structure infections, and pneumonia; **injectable only** |
| **5th** | | **ceftaroline (Teflaro)**: "the newest cephalosporin," covers **MRSA**, indicated for "acute skin and skin structure infections and community-associated pneumonia"; dose "needs to be adjusted for decreased renal function"; injectable only. **ceftolozane/tazobactam (Zerbaxa)**: intraabdominal infections "**in combination with metronidazole**" and complicated UTIs "including pyelonephritis" |

**Ceftriaxone's two contraindication clauses** (p. 602), both frequently tested:

- "**not given to hyperbilirubinemic neonates** or to patients with severe liver dysfunction"
- "**should not be administered with calcium infusions**"

**Table 38.7 Cephalosporins: Drug Interactions** (p. 600):

| Interacting drug | Mechanism | Result |
|---|---|---|
| **Ethanol (alcohol)** | "Accumulation of acetaldehyde metabolite of ethanol" | **"Acute alcohol intolerance (disulfiram-like reaction) after drinking alcoholic beverages within 72 hr of taking cefotetan."** Symptoms: "stomach cramps, nausea, vomiting, diaphoresis, pruritus, headache, and hypotension" |
| Antacids, iron | Decreased absorption of certain oral cephalosporins (**cefdinir, cefditoren**) | Decreased effectiveness of drug |
| probenecid | Decreased renal excretion | Increased cephalosporin levels |
| Oral contraceptives | Unknown | Increased risk for unintended pregnancy |

**Adverse effects and contraindications** (p. 600): "The most commonly reported adverse effects are **mild diarrhea, abdominal cramps, rash, pruritus, redness, and edema.**" Use "is contraindicated in patients with a known hypersensitivity to them and in patients with a **history of life-threatening allergic reaction to penicillins.**"

**Administration** (p. 610): "Orally administered cephalosporins **may be given with food** to decrease gastrointestinal upset." Alcohol must be avoided. "Many of the cephalosporin drug names **sound alike,** potentially leading to medication errors."

> **Trap (confusable pair).** **The generation rule vs. cefepime.** The Clinical Pearl says gram-positive coverage **decreases** as generations rise, but the chapter's own Selected Cephalosporins table says cefepime (4th gen) provides "**better gram-positive coverage than third generation**," and ceftaroline (5th) covers MRSA. The pearl describes generations **1 through 3**. If a stem asks for the general trend, use the pearl; if it asks about cefepime or ceftaroline specifically, use the drug fact. Do not let the pearl override a named drug. (Flagged as a source contradiction in the GAPS list.)

> **Trap (confusable pair).** **Anaerobic coverage.** Cue: *anaerobes = **second** generation only, and specifically the **cephamycins** cefoxitin and cefotetan.* The chapter states this twice, once in the body text and once in the key points, which is the textbook signaling that it will be tested. The near-miss distractor is **cefuroxime**, which **is** second generation but which the chapter explicitly says "does not kill anaerobic bacteria."

> **Trap (confusable pair).** **cefuroxime sodium (Zinacef) vs. cefuroxime axetil (Ceftin).** Same drug name, different salt, different route, and only one is a prodrug. Cue: *a**x**etil = ta**b**let (oral prodrug, activated in the liver); sodium = the shot.* Ordering "cefuroxime" without the salt is exactly the sound-alike error the chapter warns about.

> **Trap (confusable pair).** **ceftriaxone vs. ceftazidime.** Both third generation, both injectable, similar names. Cue: *ceftria**x**one cro**ss**es into the brain, so it is the **meningitis** drug, and it is **once daily**; cefta**z**idime hits **Pseudomonas**.* The dosage table states ceftriaxone's coverage is "comparable to those for ceftazidime, **except for *Pseudomonas* species,**" which is the entire discriminator.

> **Trap (confusable pair).** **cefotetan vs. cefoxitin.** Both are second-generation cephamycins with anaerobic coverage, so they are interchangeable in most stems. The one thing that is **not** interchangeable: the **disulfiram-like reaction is pinned to cefotetan** in Table 38.7, with a **72-hour** window. Cue: *cefo**tet**an = the **tee-total** drug.*

> **Trap (confusable pair).** **ceftaroline vs. ceftolozane/tazobactam.** Both fifth generation, both new, both injectable, both start with "cefto/cefta." Cue: *ceftaro**line** = MR**SA**, the only cephalosporin that treats it; ceftolo**zane**/tazobactam (**Zerbaxa**) = intraabdominal **with metronidazole,** plus complicated UTI.*

> **Trap (plausible-but-adjacent).** **Cephalosporins do not cover enterococci,** in any generation. An option treating an *Enterococcus* infection with a cephalosporin is wrong on a class fact, not a generation fact. Compare ertapenem, which is also inactive against enterococci; these two facts are easy to fuse into a false rule about beta-lactams generally, which does not hold (penicillins do cover *Enterococcus*).

## §9 Carbapenems and monobactams (pp. 603–604)

**Carbapenems** "have the **broadest antibacterial action of any antibiotics to date.** They are bactericidal and inhibit cell wall synthesis. Because of this, they are often **reserved for complicated body cavity and connective tissue infections in acutely ill hospitalized patients**" (p. 603).

- **"Carbapenems must be infused over 60 minutes."**
- **Seizures** are the class hazard: "One hazard of carbapenem use is drug-induced seizure activity, which occurs in a relatively small percentage of patients. However, the risk for seizures can be reduced by **proper dosage adjustment in impaired patients.**"
- Cross-allergenicity: "There is a **small risk** for cross-allergenicity in patients with penicillin allergies. **Only those patients with anaphylactic-type reactions to penicillins must avoid a carbapenem.**"

**The four carbapenems, by distinguishing feature:**

| Drug | Distinguishing feature as given |
|---|---|
| **imipenem/cilastatin (Primaxin)** | **cilastatin** "inhibits an enzyme in the kidneys called **dehydropeptidase,** which would otherwise quickly break down the imipenem," and "also blocks the renal tubular secretion of imipenem." **Highest seizure risk:** "reported in up to **1.5%** of patients receiving **less than 500 mg every 6 hours**"; at "**more than 500 mg every 6 hours**... there is a **10% incidence of seizures.**" Seizures "more likely in **older adults and renally impaired patients.**" **The IM form contains lidocaine,** so its use "is therefore contraindicated in patients with a known drug allergy to lidocaine or related local anesthetics." Interacts with cyclosporine, ganciclovir, and probenecid, "all of which may potentiate the central nervous system (CNS) adverse effects (including seizures)" |
| **meropenem (Merrem)** | "somewhat **less** active against gram-positive organisms, **more** active against *Enterobacteriaceae,* and **equally** active against *P. aeruginosa*" than imipenem/cilastatin. **"the only carbapenem currently indicated for treatment of bacterial meningitis"** |
| **ertapenem (Invanz)** | spectrum "comparable to that of imipenem/cilastatin, although it is **not active against enterococcal or *Pseudomonas* species.** It has the advantage of being given **once a day**" |
| **doripenem (Doribax)** | "the newest carbapenem. It has **less seizure potential** than imipenem/cilastatin." Indicated for intraabdominal infections, pyelonephritis, UTIs. **"Doripenem must not be used for pneumonia because of an increased mortality risk"** |

Newer combinations named in the same paragraph: **meropenem/vaborbactam (Vabomere)** and **imipenem/cilastatin with relebactam (Recarbrio)**.

**Imipenem/cilastatin indications** (p. 603): "bone, joint, skin, and soft-tissue infections; bacterial endocarditis caused by *S. aureus*; intraabdominal bacterial infections; pneumonia; UTIs and pelvic infections; and bacterial septicemia."

**Monobactams** (pp. 603–604): "Currently, there is **only one** monobactam in clinical use." **Aztreonam (Azactam)**:

- "primarily active against **aerobic gram-negative bacteria,** including *Escherichia coli, Klebsiella* species, and *Pseudomonas* species"
- **bactericidal**; "destroys bacteria by inhibiting bacterial cell wall synthesis, which results in lysis"
- indicated "for the treatment of moderately severe systemic infections and UTIs"; "often combined with other antibiotics for the treatment of intraabdominal and gynecologic infections"
- **"most commonly used when gram-negative coverage is needed in patients with a penicillin allergy"**; "it is thought to have **less allergic cross-reactivity** with other beta-lactam antibiotics. In fact, it is **commonly given to patients who have a penicillin allergy**"
- **injectable only**
- Common adverse effects: "rash, nausea, vomiting, and diarrhea"

**Assessment** (p. 609): "Assess patients for a **history of seizure activity** because of the potential seizure-type drug-induced reactions."

> **Trap (confusable pair).** **Which carbapenem for meningitis.** Cue: *m**e**ropenem = m**e**ningitis.* Imipenem/cilastatin looks like the all-purpose answer because it is the best-known carbapenem, but its seizure risk is precisely why it is not the CNS drug.

> **Trap (confusable pair).** **Seizure risk ranking within the class.** Cue: *imipenem/cilastatin = **most** seizures; doripenem = **less** seizure potential.* The chapter states that "seizures have been associated with **all** of the carbapenems, but the data suggest that they are **most likely to occur with imipenem/cilastatin.**" An option saying carbapenems as a class do not cause seizures is wrong; an option pinning the highest risk anywhere but imipenem is also wrong.

> **Trap (level/stage swap).** **The two imipenem seizure numbers go the direction you would expect, but the doses do not look far apart.** *Less than 500 mg q6h = up to **1.5%**. More than 500 mg q6h = **10%**.* A distractor will swap them or blur them into a single figure.

> **Trap (plausible-but-adjacent).** **Doripenem is contraindicated for pneumonia.** A carbapenem with the broadest spectrum of any antibiotic class looks like the obvious choice for severe pneumonia, which makes this a "which order does the nurse question" item. The reason given is **increased mortality risk,** not lack of coverage.

> **Trap (confusable pair).** **Aztreonam vs. the other beta-lactams in penicillin allergy.** All four subclasses share the beta-lactam ring, so the instinct is that all four are unsafe in penicillin allergy. Cue: *a**z**treonam is the beta-lactam you **can** give.* Compare the three different thresholds this chapter sets: **cephalosporins** are withheld only from patients with a history of **throat swelling or hives**; **carbapenems** only from patients with **anaphylactic-type** reactions; **aztreonam** is "commonly given" to penicillin-allergic patients outright.

> **Trap (setting vs. focus).** **Cilastatin has no antibacterial activity of its own.** It is a kidney-enzyme inhibitor that keeps imipenem intact. An option describing cilastatin as "a second antibiotic for broader coverage" is describing a product that does not exist.

## §10 Macrolides (pp. 604–606)

**Class action:** "**bacteriostatic** drugs that **inhibit protein synthesis by binding reversibly to the 50S ribosomal subunits** of susceptible microorganisms"; "however, in high enough concentrations they may be bactericidal to some susceptible bacteria" (pp. 604–605).

**The four available macrolides** (p. 604): **azithromycin, clarithromycin, erythromycin, fidaxomicin.**

**Erythromycin vs. the two semisynthetics** (pp. 604, 605–606):

| | **erythromycin** | **azithromycin (Zithromax)** and **clarithromycin (Biaxin)** |
|---|---|---|
| Duration | shorter | "**longer durations of action** than erythromycin, which allows them to be given less often" |
| GI effects | "cause many gastrointestinal-related adverse effects, especially **nausea and vomiting**" | "**fewer and milder** GI tract adverse effects"; "a **lower incidence** of these gastrointestinal tract complications" |
| Tissue penetration | | "capable of **better tissue penetration**"; azithromycin "has **excellent tissue penetration,** so that it can reach high concentrations in infected tissues" |
| CYP450 | "a **strong inhibitor** of cytochrome P450 enzymes," and so "associated with many drug interactions" | **azithromycin** "is **not as prone** to such interactions... because of its **minimal effects** on the cytochrome P-450 enzymes" |
| Routes | oral (several salt and dosage forms), injectable, topical (Ch. 56), ophthalmic (Ch. 57) | azithromycin: oral **and** injectable. **clarithromycin: oral only** |
| Dosing | | azithromycin "**500 mg on day 1 and then 250 mg/day for 4 days**," once daily. clarithromycin "**twice daily** in adults and children older than 6 months of age" |
| With food | absorption "enhanced if it is taken on an **empty stomach,** but because of the high incidence of stomach irritation associated with its use, many of these drugs are taken with a meal or snack" | azithromycin: "Taking the drug with food **decreases both the rate and extent** of gastrointestinal absorption." clarithromycin: "can be given with or without food" |

- **"The extended-release preparation must not be crushed"** (clarithromycin, p. 606).
- **FDA caution on clarithromycin** (p. 604): "In 2018 the US Food and Drug Administration issued a cautionary statement regarding clarithromycin. There is a **potential increased risk for heart problems or death when given to patients with heart disease.**"

**Indications** (p. 605): upper and lower respiratory tract, skin, and soft tissue infections; **spirochetal infections (syphilis and Lyme disease)**; gonorrhea; *Chlamydia, Mycoplasma,* and *Corynebacterium* infections. Infections caused by ***Streptococcus pyogenes* (group A beta-hemolytic streptococci)** are inhibited by macrolides, as are "mild to moderate upper and lower respiratory tract infections caused by *Haemophilus influenzae.*"

- **Macrolides reach intracellular organisms:** "especially effective against several bacterial species that often reproduce **inside host cells** instead of just in the bloodstream or interstitial spaces," namely *Listeria, Chlamydia, Legionella* (Legionnaires' disease), *Neisseria* (gonorrhea), and *Campylobacter*.
- **Gonorrhea caveat:** "increasingly difficult to treat with macrolide monotherapy, so these drugs are sometimes used **in combination with other antibiotics such as cephalosporins.**"
- **Azithromycin and clarithromycin** are "approved for the prevention and treatment of ***Mycobacterium avium-intracellulare* complex** infections," "a common opportunistic infection often associated with HIV infection/AIDS."
- **Clarithromycin** is approved "for use in combination with **omeprazole and amoxicillin** for the treatment of patients with active ulcer associated with ***Helicobacter pylori*** infection."
- **Macrolides are the beta-lactam alternative:** "Macrolides are often used as **alternative drugs for patients with allergies to beta-lactam antibiotics**" (p. 605).

**Erythromycin's non-antibiotic effect** (p. 605), a favorite item: "A therapeutic effect of erythromycin **outside its antibiotic actions** is its ability to **irritate the gastrointestinal tract, which stimulates smooth muscle and gastrointestinal motility.** This may be of benefit to patients who have decreased gastrointestinal motility, such as **delayed gastric emptying in diabetic patients (known as diabetic gastroparesis).** It has also been shown to be helpful in **facilitating the passage of feeding tubes** from the stomach into the small bowel."

**Fidaxomicin (Dificid)** (p. 604): "the newest macrolide antibiotic. It is indicated **only** for the treatment of ***C. difficile*–associated diarrhea.**" Most common adverse effects: "nausea, vomiting, and gastrointestinal bleed." Pregnancy category **B**. "It has minimal absorption."

**Bezlotoxumab (Zinplava)** (p. 604): "**Although, not a macrolide antibiotic,**" it is a new drug for *C. difficile*–associated diarrhea, "a **monoclonal antibody** given as a **single intravenous dose.**"

**Interactions** (p. 605). Macrolides have two properties that generate interactions: "they are **highly protein bound,** and they are **metabolized in the liver**" via cytochrome P-450. "The result is a **delay in the metabolic clearance** of one or more interacting drugs and thus a **prolonged and possibly toxic drug effect.**"

- Drugs that compete for hepatic metabolism: "**carbamazepine, cyclosporine, theophylline, and warfarin.**" Result: "enhanced effects and possible toxicity of the latter drugs, and patients must be monitored."
- The nursing section adds **digoxin** to that list (p. 609).
- "Macrolides can also **reduce the efficacy of oral contraceptives.**"
- **QT drugs:** "Clarithromycin and erythromycin are **not to be used with moxifloxacin, pimozide, thioridazine, or other drugs that prolong the QT interval, because malignant dysrhythmias can occur.**"
- **Statins:** "Concurrent use of **simvastatin or lovastatin** with clarithromycin or erythromycin **is not recommended.**"

**Table 38.8 Macrolides: Reported Adverse Effects** (p. 605):

| Body system | Adverse effects |
|---|---|
| **Cardiovascular** | Palpitations, chest pain, **QT prolongation** |
| **Central nervous** | Headache, dizziness, vertigo |
| **Gastrointestinal** | Nausea, hepatotoxicity, heartburn, vomiting, diarrhea, flatulence, cholestatic jaundice, anorexia, abnormal taste |
| **Integumentary** | Rash, urticaria, phlebitis at intravenous site |
| **Other** | **Hearing loss, tinnitus** |

**Assessment and administration** (pp. 609–610): "assess **baseline cardiac function and vital signs** because of the potential adverse effects of palpitations, chest pain, and electrocardiographic changes. Note **baseline hearing status** because of drug-induced hearing loss and tinnitus. Assess **liver function** and history of liver disease." **"Macrolides are not to be given with or given immediately before or after fruit juices."** Report immediately: "**chest pain, palpitations, dizziness, jaundice, rash, and hearing loss.**"

> **Trap (confusable pair).** **erythromycin vs. azithromycin vs. clarithromycin.** Cue: *e**r**ythromycin = the **r**ough one (most GI upset, strongest CYP450 inhibitor, most interactions); **a**zithromycin = the **a**greeable one (minimal CYP450 effect, once daily, 500-then-250 taper); **c**larithromycin = **c**ardiac caution (2018 FDA warning in heart disease) and **oral only**.* A distractor giving IV clarithromycin is wrong on route alone.

> **Trap (plausible-but-adjacent).** **Erythromycin for gastroparesis is not an infection order.** If a stem gives erythromycin to a diabetic patient with delayed gastric emptying and asks the rationale, the answer is **motility**, not infection. This is the chapter's clearest example of a drug used *for* one of its adverse effects.

> **Trap (confusable pair).** **fidaxomicin vs. bezlotoxumab.** Both treat *C. difficile*–associated diarrhea and are introduced in the same paragraph. Cue: *fidaxomicin is a **macrolide antibiotic,** oral, minimal absorption; bezlotoxumab is **not a macrolide and not an antibiotic,** it is a **monoclonal antibody** given as a **single IV dose**.* The chapter flags the distinction itself with "Although, not a macrolide antibiotic."

> **Trap (confusable pair).** **Hearing loss is a macrolide adverse effect.** Ototoxicity is normally taught with the aminoglycosides (Chapter 39), which makes "hearing loss and tinnitus" look misplaced in Table 38.8. It is genuinely there, and the assessment section reinforces it by requiring a baseline hearing status. A stem in which a patient on azithromycin reports ringing in the ears is a real macrolide item, not a distractor.

> **Trap (level/stage swap).** Macrolides are the **alternative** for beta-lactam-allergic patients, not a beta-lactam themselves. They have no beta-lactam ring and no cross-reactivity with penicillins, which is exactly why they fill that role.

## §11 Tetracyclines (pp. 606–608)

**Class action:** "**bacteriostatic** drugs that **inhibit bacterial protein synthesis by binding to the 30S bacterial ribosome**" (p. 606).

**Box 38.1 Available Tetracycline Antibiotics** (p. 607): **demeclocycline, tetracycline, doxycycline, minocycline, tigecycline.**

- **Three naturally occurring:** "demeclocycline, oxytetracycline, and tetracycline."
- **Two semisynthetic:** "doxycycline and minocycline," which "have **excellent absorption and tissue penetration.**"
- **Tigecycline (Tygacil)** is "structurally related to minocycline, but is the **first in a new class called glycylcyclines.**"

**Chelation, the defining property** (p. 606): "The most significant chemical characteristic of these drugs is their ability to **bind to (chelate) divalent (Ca++, Mg++) and trivalent (Al3+) metallic ions to form insoluble complexes.** Therefore their coadministration with **milk, antacids, or iron salts** causes a **considerable reduction in the oral absorption** of the tetracycline."

**The age and pregnancy rule** (p. 606): "their strong affinity for calcium usually **precludes their use in pediatric patients younger than 8 years of age,** because it can result in **significant tooth discoloration.** These drugs must be **avoided in pregnant women and nursing mothers.** The drugs **do pass into breast milk,** and this can be another route of exposure leading to tooth discoloration in nursing children."

- The adverse-effect section adds the skeletal risk: tetracyclines "can cause discoloration of the permanent teeth and **tooth enamel hypoplasia** in both fetuses and children and possibly **retard fetal skeletal development** if taken during pregnancy" (p. 607).

**Indications** (pp. 606–607). "Traditionally used to treat **acne** in adolescents and adults," they are "the drugs of choice for the treatment of the following infections caused by susceptible organisms":

- ***Chlamydia:*** "lymphogranuloma venereum, psittacosis, and nonspecific endocervical, rectal, and urethral infections"
- ***Mycoplasma:*** "*Mycoplasma* pneumonia"
- ***Rickettsia:*** "Q fever, rickettsial pox, **Rocky Mountain spotted fever,** and typhus"
- **Other bacteria:** "acne, brucellosis, chancroid, cholera, granuloma inguinale, shigellosis, spirochetal relapsing fever, **Lyme disease,** *H. pylori* infections associated with peptic ulcer disease (used as part of the treatment regimen), **syphilis** (used as an **alternative drug to treat patients with penicillin allergy**)"
- ***Protozoa:*** "balantidiasis"
- **Not gonorrhea:** "tetracyclines now **unreliable in treating gonorrhea** because the development of resistant bacterial strains"

They also "inhibit the growth of and kill a wide range of *Rickettsia, Chlamydia,* and *Mycoplasma* organisms, as well as a variety of gram-negative and gram-positive bacteria," and are "useful in the treatment of spirochetal infections, such as syphilis and Lyme disease, and **pelvic inflammatory disease**" (p. 606).

**Demeclocycline's unique effect** (p. 606): "Demeclocycline possesses a unique drug effect in that it **inhibits the action of antidiuretic hormone,** which makes it useful in the treatment of the **syndrome of inappropriate secretion of antidiuretic hormone (SIADH).**" The drug profile (p. 607) adds that it "is derived from strains of *Streptomyces*" and "is available only for oral use."

**Doxycycline** (p. 607): "a semisynthetic tetracycline antibiotic. It is useful in the treatment of rickettsial infections such as **Rocky Mountain spotted fever,** chlamydial and mycoplasmal infections, spirochetal infections, and many infections with gram-negative organisms. It also can be used for the **prevention and treatment of anthrax and malaria.** Doxycycline may also be used in the treatment of **acne.**" Available in oral and injectable forms.

**Tigecycline (Tygacil)** (p. 608): "the newest tetracycline, referred to as a **glycylcycline.** It differs from other tetracyclines in that it is **effective against many organisms resistant to others in its class.**" Indicated for complicated skin and skin structure infections "including **MRSA and vancomycin-sensitive *Enterococcus faecalis,***" complicated intraabdominal infections, and community-associated pneumonia. **Given by injection only.** "**Nausea and vomiting are the most common adverse effects, occurring in 20% to 30% of patients.**"

Two newer agents named in passing: **eravacycline (Xerava)**, injectable, for intra-abdominal infections; **omadacycline (Nuzyra)**, for community-acquired pneumonia and skin infections, "available both orally and for injection."

**Adverse effects** (p. 607): "All tetracyclines cause similar adverse effects." Beyond teeth and bone: "**photosensitivity,** which is **most frequent in patients taking demeclocycline;** alteration of the intestinal and vaginal flora, which can result in **diarrhea or vaginal candidiasis**; **reversible bulging fontanelles in neonates**; thrombocytopenia, possible coagulation irregularities, and hemolytic anemia; and **exacerbation of systemic lupus erythematosus.** Other effects include gastric upset, enterocolitis, and maculopapular rash."

Clinical Pearl (p. 607): **"Tetracyclines cause photosensitivity."**

**Interactions** (p. 607):

- Reduced oral absorption when taken with "**antacids, antidiarrheal drugs, dairy products, calcium, enteral feedings, or iron preparations**"
- "can **potentiate the effects of oral anticoagulants,** which necessitates more frequent monitoring of anticoagulant effect and possible dosage adjustment"
- "can also **antagonize the effects of bactericidal antibiotics** and oral contraceptives"
- "depending on the dosage, they can cause **increased blood urea nitrogen levels**"

**Administration** (pp. 610–611):

- "take oral doses with fluids (**at least 8 oz of water**) and/or food to minimize gastrointestinal upset"
- **"warn the patient not to take tetracyclines with calcium, magnesium, and/or iron"**; avoid "dairy products, antacids, or iron"
- **The spacing rule: "take any of these foods, fluids, or drugs 2 hours before or 3 hours after the tetracycline."**
- "**Intravenous doxycycline is very irritating to the veins,** so frequent monitoring of the patency of the intravenous line is necessary to prevent/avoid patient injury"
- Photosensitivity teaching (p. 611): "avoid exposure to **tanning beds** and direct sunlight or to use sunscreen and/or wear protective clothing... These photosensitive effects may be noticed within a **few minutes to hours** after taking the drug and **may last up to several days after the drug has been discontinued.**"
- Monitor continually "for **diarrhea and/or vaginal yeast infections** because of altered intestinal and/or vaginal flora"

**Class-wide resistance** (p. 607): **"Resistance to one tetracycline implies resistance to all tetracyclines."**

> **Trap (confusable pair).** **The chelation spacing numbers.** Cue: ***2** hours **b**efore, **3** hours **a**fter.* The numbers are asymmetric, which is precisely why a distractor will make them both 2 or both 1. Contrast with penicillin V and amoxicillin, which are given "**1 hour before or 2 hours after meals**" (p. 610). Two different drugs, two different spacings, both about absorption.

> **Trap (confusable pair).** **Photosensitivity: sulfonamides *and* tetracyclines.** Both classes carry it, and the chapter says sulfonamide photosensitivity reactions "are also common with the **tetracycline** class of antibiotics" (p. 596). Within tetracyclines it is "**most frequent** in patients taking **demeclocycline.**" An item asking which class causes photosensitivity may have two correct-looking options; read for whether it wants the class or the specific drug.

> **Trap (confusable pair).** **demeclocycline has two entirely different uses.** It is an antibiotic **and** a treatment for SIADH by inhibiting ADH. A stem giving demeclocycline to a patient with hyponatremia and SIADH is not an infection item. Cue: *de**me**clocycline = **me**tabolic second job.*

> **Trap (confusable pair).** **tigecycline vs. the rest of the class.** Cue: *tigecycline is a **glycylcycline,** injectable only, works against **resistant** organisms including **MRSA,** and its signature adverse effect is **nausea and vomiting in 20% to 30%**.* The class rule "resistance to one implies resistance to all" is exactly what tigecycline is built to escape, so the two facts must be held together rather than as a contradiction.

> **Trap (level/stage swap).** **Younger than 8 years,** not 2, 6, 12, or 18. Compare the other age numbers in this chapter so they do not blur: **sulfonamides** are contraindicated in **infants younger than 2 months**; **clarithromycin** is dosed in **children older than 6 months**; **tetracyclines** are contraindicated **younger than 8 years.**

> **Trap (plausible-but-adjacent).** **Tetracyclines antagonize bactericidal antibiotics.** A bacteriostatic drug that halts bacterial growth blunts a bactericidal drug that needs actively dividing bacteria. An option combining a tetracycline with a penicillin "for broader coverage" is working against itself.

> **Trap (SATA, five options mapped to categories).** Critical thinking exercise 1 (p. 612) orders doxycycline 200 mg PO every morning, multivitamin **with iron** 1 tablet PO every morning, and **Mylanta** 30 mL PO twice a day, then asks the priority action "when considering whether these medications can be given together." All three are due at once, and **two of them chelate the doxycycline.** The category being tested is which agents bind tetracyclines, and the answer applies the 2-hours-before / 3-hours-after rule. (The official answer key is on Evolve; see GAPS item 11.)

## §12 Superinfection and C. difficile

This is the chapter's own objective 10 ("Briefly discuss superinfection, including its cause and prevention") and it draws items reliably.

**Cause** (p. 592): "Superinfection can occur when antibiotics **reduce or completely eliminate the normal bacterial flora.** Normal flora consist of certain bacteria and fungi that are needed to maintain normal function in various organs. When these bacteria or fungi are killed by antibiotics, **other bacteria or fungi are permitted to take over and cause infection.**"

- The textbook's canonical example: "the development of a **vaginal yeast infection** when the normal vaginal bacterial flora is reduced by antibiotic therapy and yeast growth is no longer kept in balance."

**The escalation from diarrhea to *C. difficile*** (pp. 592–593): "**Antibiotic-associated diarrhea** is a common adverse effect of antibiotics. However, it becomes a **serious superinfection** when it causes infection, known as ***C. difficile* infection.**"

- Symptoms: "**watery diarrhea, abdominal pain, and fever.**"
- **The nursing rule:** "Whenever a patient who was previously treated with antibiotics develops **watery diarrhea, the patient needs to be tested for *C. difficile* infection.** If the results are positive, the patient will need to be treated for this serious superinfection and **placed in isolation.**"
- "Infections with *C. difficile* are increasingly becoming **resistant to standard therapy.** There is some evidence to suggest **probiotics** may help prevent *C. difficile* infections."

**The second sense of superinfection** (p. 593): "a second infection closely follows the initial infection and comes **from an external source** (as opposed to normal body flora). A common example is a case in which a patient who has a **viral respiratory infection develops a secondary bacterial infection.** This is likely due to weakening of the patient's immune system function by the primary viral infection."

- The diagnostic caution attached to it: "**The presence of colored sputum (e.g., green or yellow) may or may not be a sign of a bacterial superinfection** during a viral respiratory illness." The prescriber "needs to avoid prescribing unnecessary antibiotics for a viral infection."

**Signs of superinfection to assess for** (pp. 608, 611): "fever, lethargy, mouth sores, perineal itching, and other system-related symptoms." The key points list is "**fever, perineal itching, oral lesions, vaginal irritation and discharge, cough, and lethargy.**" For tetracyclines specifically, assess for "**whitish, sore patches on the oral mucosa** (from candidiasis or yeast infection), as well as any **vaginal itching, pain, and/or cottage cheese–like discharge**" (p. 609).

**Prevention** (p. 610): "This process may be prevented by the consumption of **probiotics, such as products containing *Lactobacillus*; supplements; or cultured dairy products such as yogurt, buttermilk, and kefir.**"

> **Trap (confusable pair).** **The two definitions of superinfection are not the same event.** Sense (1) is **overgrowth of your own flora** during treatment; sense (2) is a **new organism from outside** on top of an earlier infection. The key term box gives both, numbered. A stem describing thrush during antibiotic therapy is sense (1); a stem describing bacterial pneumonia after influenza is sense (2).

> **Trap (confusable pair).** **Antibiotic-associated diarrhea vs. *C. difficile* infection.** Cue: *diarrhea alone = **common adverse effect**; watery diarrhea + abdominal pain + fever after antibiotics = **test for *C. diff,* then isolate**.* Review question 5 (p. 612) runs the parallel version on the genital tract: a woman on an antibiotic for UTI reports severe vaginal itching and thick whitish discharge, with distractors "expected response," "UTI worse," and "UTI resistant." The key is **superinfection.**

> **Trap (plausible-but-adjacent).** **"This is an expected response to antibiotic therapy"** is the seductive option, because nausea, vomiting, and diarrhea genuinely are "quite common with antibiotic therapy and are usually not severe enough to require drug discontinuation" (p. 592). The discriminator is whether the finding is a **new infection** (candidiasis, *C. diff*) or a **direct GI adverse effect.**

> **Trap (priority question).** Colored sputum does **not** establish bacterial superinfection. The chapter's framing here is about **avoiding unnecessary antibiotics for viral illness,** so an option that starts an antibiotic on sputum color alone is the distractor. Note the related pressure the chapter describes: patients "will often expect to receive an antibiotic prescription even when they show no signs of a bacterial superinfection."

## §13 Administration rules, gathered by class

The nursing implementation section (pp. 609–611) is where most "what will the nurse teach" items come from. Collected here because the raw text scatters them across two columns.

**All antibiotics** (p. 609): take "as ordered, for the prescribed length of time and **around the clock** to maintain effective blood levels"; "**Doses are not to be omitted or doubled-up**"; do not give oral antibiotics "at the same time as antacids, calcium supplements, iron products, laxatives containing magnesium, or some of the antilipemic drugs."

| Class | The rules the chapter states |
|---|---|
| **Sulfonamides** | **8 oz water** per dose; total fluids **2000 to 3000 mL/24 hr** to prevent crystalluria; **take with food**; sun precautions (**10:00 am to 3:00 pm,** SPF **15**+, hat, sunglasses, lip protection); report "worsening abdominal cramps, stomach pain, diarrhea, blood in the urine, severe or worsening rash, shortness of breath, and fever" |
| **Penicillins** | oral forms with **at least 6 oz of water, not juices** ("juices are acidic and may nullify the drug's antibacterial action"); **penicillin V, amoxicillin, and amoxicillin/clavulanate 1 hour before or 2 hours after meals,** though a small snack is allowed for GI upset; procaine and benzathine salts are **thick,** give IM with a **21- to 23-gauge needle into a large muscle mass,** and **"dorsogluteal site is NOT to be used"**; rotate sites; have **epinephrine** and airway/oxygen available |
| **Cephalosporins** | may be given **with food**; **avoid alcohol and alcohol-containing products** (disulfiram-like reaction, up to **72 hours** after cefotetan); check sound-alike names carefully |
| **Carbapenems** | infuse over **60 minutes**; reconstitute IM imipenem/cilastatin "in sterile saline, with plain lidocaine, as ordered and if the patient has no allergy to it," into a large muscle mass |
| **Macrolides** | **not with, immediately before, or immediately after fruit juices**; report chest pain, palpitations, dizziness, jaundice, rash, **hearing loss** |
| **Tetracyclines** | **at least 8 oz water,** with food for GI upset; **no calcium, magnesium, or iron**; space those foods and drugs **2 hours before or 3 hours after**; IV doxycycline is **vein-irritating,** monitor patency; avoid sun and tanning beds |

**Evaluation: what a therapeutic response looks like** (p. 611): "a decrease in the signs and symptoms of the infection; a return to normal vital signs, including temperature; **negative results on culture and sensitivity tests**; normal results for CBC; and improved appetite, energy level, and sense of well-being."

**Oral contraceptives** (p. 611): "The effectiveness of oral contraceptives may be **decreased** with certain antibiotics because of the interaction or from altered metabolism. **Reliable backup methods of contraception must be used, in addition to the oral contraceptive, during antibiotic use.**" The chapter attaches this warning to sulfonamides, penicillins, cephalosporins, macrolides, and tetracyclines, which is every class it covers.

**Medical alert** (p. 611): counsel the patient "to wear a **medical alert bracelet or necklace** at all times and to keep a medical card with diagnoses and list of medications and allergies, **especially if anaphylactic,** on his or her person at all times."

> **Trap (confusable pair).** **Which drink with which drug.** Cue: *penicillins = **water, not juice** (acid destroys them); macrolides = **not with fruit juice**; tetracyclines and sulfonamides = **water, and lots of it**.* Juice is wrong for three of the classes in this chapter and right for none of them.

> **Trap (confusable pair).** **6 oz vs. 8 oz.** Penicillins get "**at least 6 oz** of water"; sulfonamides and tetracyclines get "**8 ounces.**" A distractor swapping the volumes is testing whether you read the number or just the class.

> **Trap (priority question).** **The dorsogluteal site is not to be used** for IM penicillin. If a stem lists injection sites, the dorsogluteal option is wrong by explicit prohibition, not by clinical preference.

> **Trap (setting vs. focus).** **"With food" vs. "on an empty stomach"** splits by class, not by comfort: **cephalosporins, sulfonamides, and tetracyclines** may be taken **with food**; **penicillin V, amoxicillin, and amoxicillin/clavulanate** are timed **around** meals for absorption; **erythromycin** absorbs best on an empty stomach but is usually given with food anyway because of irritation. Erythromycin is the trap, because the absorption rule and the tolerance rule point in opposite directions and the chapter chooses tolerance.

> **Trap (plausible-but-adjacent).** **The half-lives explain the dosing intervals you already memorized, so use them as a check rather than a separate fact.** *Ceftriaxone's 4.3 to 8.7 hr half-life with a **24-hour duration** is why it is once daily; **azithromycin's 60 to 70 hr** half-life is why a 5-day course keeps working after the last dose; **tigecycline's 42.4 hr** is why it loads at 100 mg before dropping to 50.* If a dosing-frequency option contradicts the duration column, it is wrong.

> **Trap (confusable pair).** **The SMX-TMP dose is expressed in trimethoprim, the smaller component.** The 5:1 ratio means a single-strength tablet is 400 mg SMX + 80 mg TMP, but the weight-based order reads "8–20 mg/kg per day" of **TMP**. An option computing the dose off the sulfamethoxazole number is five times high.
