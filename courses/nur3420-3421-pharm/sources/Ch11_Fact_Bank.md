## Key terms

All 12 terms from the chapter's KEY TERMS box, quoted as given (p. 165).

| Term | p. | Definition as given |
|---|---|---|
| **Adjunct anesthetics** | 165 | "Drugs used in **combination with anesthetic drugs** to **control the adverse effects** of anesthetics or to help **maintain the anesthetic state** in the patient. (See balanced anesthesia.)" |
| **Anesthesia** | 165 | "The **loss of the ability to feel pain** resulting from the administration of an anesthetic drug." |
| **Anesthetics** | 165 | "Drugs that **depress the central nervous system (CNS) or peripheral nerves** to produce decreased or loss of consciousness or muscle relaxation." |
| **Anesthesia provider** | 165 | "A health care professional who is **licensed to provide anesthesia.** Can be an **anesthesiologist (MD),** a **certified registered nurse anesthetist (CRNA),** or an **anesthesia assistant.**" |
| **Balanced anesthesia** | 165 | "The practice of using **combinations of different drug classes rather than a single drug** to produce anesthesia." |
| **General anesthesia** | 165 | "A drug-induced state in which the **CNS nerve impulses are altered** to reduce pain and other sensations **throughout the entire body.** It involves **complete loss of consciousness and depression of respiratory drive.**" |
| **Local anesthesia** | 165 | "A drug-induced state in which **peripheral or spinal nerve impulses are altered** to reduce or eliminate pain and other sensations **in tissues innervated by these nerves.**" |
| **Malignant hyperthermia** | 165 | "A **genetically linked** major adverse reaction to general anesthesia characterized by a **rapid rise in body temperature** as well as **tachycardia, tachypnea, and sweating.**" |
| **Moderate sedation** | 165 | "A **milder form of general anesthesia** that causes **partial or complete loss of consciousness** but **does not generally reduce normal respiratory drive** (also referred to as **conscious sedation**)." |
| **Monitored anesthesia care** | 165 | "Monitored anesthesia care (MAC) is a **planned procedure** in which the patient undergoes **local anesthesia along with sedation and analgesia.**" |
| **Overton-Meyer theory** | 165 | "A theory describing the relationship between the **lipid solubility** of anesthetic drugs and their **potency.**" |
| **Spinal anesthesia** | 165 | "**Local anesthesia** induced by injection of an anesthetic drug **near the spinal cord** to anesthetize nerves that are **distal to the site of injection.**" |

> **Trap (confusable pair).** **General anesthesia vs. local anesthesia.** The two definitions are built on the same sentence frame and differ in exactly two slots, which is what makes them swappable. Cue: *general = **CNS** nerve impulses, **entire body,** and it **takes the respiratory drive with it**; local = **peripheral or spinal** nerve impulses, only the **tissues those nerves innervate,** and **consciousness and breathing stay.*** The overview states the respiratory consequence flatly: general anesthesia's "loss of normal respiratory function requires mechanical or manual ventilatory support to avoid brain damage and suffocation (death from respiratory arrest)," while "Local anesthesia does not involve paralysis of respiratory function but only elimination of pain sensation in the tissues innervated by anesthetized nerves" (p. 166).

> **Trap (confusable pair).** **Anesthesia vs. anesthetics vs. anesthesia provider.** Three terms one word apart, and the key-term box prints them consecutively. Cue: ***anesthesia** = the **state** (loss of the ability to feel pain); **anesthetics** = the **drugs** that produce it; **anesthesia provider** = the **person** licensed to give them.* Only the third has a membership list attached, and it is a three-item list: **anesthesiologist (MD), CRNA, anesthesia assistant.** A stem that adds "surgeon" or "staff registered nurse" to that list has widened it.

> **Trap (confusable pair).** **Adjunct anesthetics vs. balanced anesthesia.** The key term for adjuncts sends you straight to the other term, which is the book flagging its own confusable. Cue: *an **adjunct** is a **drug** ("helper drugs"); **balanced anesthesia** is the **practice** of combining them.* The chapter's own sentence joins them: "The simultaneous use of both general anesthetics and adjuncts is called **balanced anesthesia**" (p. 166). Adjuncts have two stated jobs, and distractors drop one: **control the adverse effects** of anesthetics *and* **help maintain the anesthetic state.**

> **Trap (setting vs. focus).** **Monitored anesthesia care (MAC) vs. moderate (conscious) sedation.** These are the two "sedated but not under" entries and they are separated by what the sedation is added *to*, not by depth. Cue: ***MAC** = **local anesthesia** plus sedation plus analgesia, a **planned procedure** with a named element list; **moderate sedation** = a **milder form of general anesthesia** that spares the respiratory drive.* MAC's three fundamental elements are "**safe sedation, control of anxiety, and pain control,**" and its defining patient behaviours are that patients "are able to **answer questions appropriately and protect their airways**" and can be "**discharged soon after the procedure**" (p. 166). Name the axis: MAC is defined by what is *combined*; moderate sedation is defined by *how deep*.

> **Trap (plausible-but-adjacent).** **Moderate sedation is classified as a form of general anesthesia, not of local anesthesia.** Both the key term ("a **milder form of general anesthesia**," p. 165) and the key points ("**Conscious or moderate sedation is a form of general anesthesia** resulting in partial or complete loss of consciousness but without reducing normal respiratory drive," p. 181) put it under the general heading. Its taxonomic home is the counterintuitive part, because everything about its clinical use — outpatient, awake, self-ventilating — reads local.

> **Trap (confusable pair).** **Spinal anesthesia is a kind of LOCAL anesthesia.** Its key-term definition begins with the words "**Local anesthesia** induced by injection..." and the key points repeat it: "**Spinal anesthesia, or regional anesthesia, is a form of local anesthesia**" (p. 181). The body text hedges the same point differently: because spinal anesthesia "does not depress the CNS at a level that causes loss of consciousness, it can be thought of as a **large-scale type of local rather than general anesthesia**" (p. 170). Cue: *a needle near the **spinal cord** still means **local,** and the block runs **distal to** the injection site.*

> **Trap (confusable pair).** **Overton-Meyer: lipid solubility, and the direction of the relationship.** Cue: *potency varies **directly** with **lipid** solubility, so **fat-soluble drugs are stronger anesthetics than water-soluble drugs.*** The chapter gives the anatomic reason, which is the part a distractor omits: "Nerve cell membranes have **high lipid content,** as do the **brain, spinal cord, and blood-brain barrier,**" so lipid-soluble anesthetics "can therefore easily **cross the blood-brain barrier** to concentrate in nerve cell membranes" (p. 167). An option pairing potency with water solubility, or with molecular weight, has inverted or replaced the axis.

> **Trap (SATA, five options mapped to categories).** **Malignant hyperthermia's sign list has three versions in this chapter, and the longest one is the assessment version.** The key term (p. 165) gives four: **rapid rise in body temperature, tachycardia, tachypnea, sweating.** The adverse-effects paragraph (p. 168) gives four, swapping sweating for **muscular rigidity.** The assessment section (p. 176) gives the full set: "**rapid rise in body temperature, tachycardia, tachypnea, muscle rigidity, cyanosis, irregular heartbeat, mottling of the skin, diaphoresis (profuse sweating), and an unstable blood pressure.**" For an all-or-nothing SATA, work from the p. 176 list; nothing in this chapter puts **bradycardia, bradypnea, hypothermia, or seizure activity** on it.

## §1 The anesthesia vocabulary map (pp. 165–170)

This chapter is unusually definition-heavy, and the 6 blueprint items are far more likely to land on which-word-means-what than on any drug's dose. The whole vocabulary resolves onto two axes: **how much of the body** is affected, and **whether the respiratory drive survives.**

| Term | Scope | Consciousness | Respiratory drive | p. |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| **General anesthesia** | Entire body, via CNS | "Complete loss of consciousness" | "Depression of respiratory drive"; ventilatory support required | 165–166 |
| **Moderate (conscious, procedural) sedation** | CNS, but milder | "Partial or complete loss of consciousness" | "Does not generally reduce normal respiratory drive"; "does not normally cause respiratory arrest" | 165, 169 |
| **Monitored anesthesia care (MAC)** | Local anesthesia plus sedation and analgesia | Patient can answer questions appropriately | Patient protects own airway | 166 |
| **Spinal / neuraxial / central anesthesia** | All peripheral nerves distal to the injection site | Preserved | Preserved, but the drug can "move upward in the spinal cord and breathing may be affected" | 165, 170, 178 |
| **Local anesthesia** | Only tissues innervated by the anesthetized nerves | Preserved | Preserved | 165, 166 |

Three synonym sets the chapter states outright, each a free distractor if not held:

- "**Moderate sedation, conscious sedation, and procedural sedation** are synonymous terms" (p. 169).
- "**Spinal or neuraxial or central anesthesia**" are one entry in Box 11.2 (p. 170).
- **Epidural anesthesia** is "also called **regional anesthesia** in some textbooks" (p. 179) — while the key points instead equate regional anesthesia with **spinal** anesthesia ("Spinal anesthesia, or regional anesthesia," p. 181). See GAPS item 5.

> **Trap (level/stage swap).** **The stages of anesthesia are declared obsolete in this chapter, and then used anyway.** Box 11.1 (p. 166) says the observable stages belonged to the "ether-based inhaled anesthetic drugs," that newer drugs "often have a much more rapid onset of action and body distribution," and that "the specific stages of anesthesia once observed with older drugs are **no longer sufficiently well defined to be observable.** Thus the concept of stages of anesthesia is **an outdated one in most modern surgical institutions.**" But p. 167 still narrates a sequence and calls it "These are the classic '**stages**' of anesthesia": the senses of **sight, touch, taste, smell, and hearing** go first along with consciousness, and "**cardiac and pulmonary functions are usually the last to be interrupted because they are controlled by the medulla of the brainstem.**" Cue: *the **order** is still testable; the **numbered stage names** are not, and the book will not supply them.*

> **Trap (priority question).** **Whose job is it.** The chapter draws the line twice. General anesthetics "are given **only under controlled conditions by anesthesia providers**" (p. 166), and drugs for moderate sedation are "given **only under controlled situations by anesthesia providers**" (p. 171). The assessment section repeats it for the RN: "anesthetics are **not drugs that are typically given by the registered nurse** unless the nurse is licensed and certified as an anesthesia provider, that is, a nurse anesthetist. **Exceptions** to this statement are orders for **topical forms,** such as oral swish-and-swallow solutions that may be used during chemotherapy and **lidocaine patches** for pain relief" (p. 175). Propofol is the one drug the chapter treats as genuinely contested: "**Some states,** specifically under a state's nurse practice act, **allow nurses to administer propofol** as part of a moderate sedation protocol; however, **many states prohibit** administration by nurses" (p. 169). Cue: *if the option has a staff RN pushing an anesthetic, the only defensible ones are **topical** — and propofol's answer is "**it depends on the state nurse practice act,**" not a flat yes or no.*

> **Trap (plausible-but-adjacent).** **Three requirements for administering moderate sedation, all of them people-and-equipment rules rather than drug rules** (p. 169): personnel "are required to have **advanced cardiac life support training**"; "**one professional must have no duties other than to monitor the patient**"; and "**someone with the ability to intubate the patient must be present** in case the patient slips into a deeper state of sedation and is unable to maintain an open airway." The guidelines are published by "the **American Society of Anesthesia**" at www.asahq.org (printed as given; see GAPS item 8).

## §2 General anesthetics: the class (pp. 166–168)

**Definition and route split.** "General anesthetics are drugs used to produce **profound neurosensory depression** to allow for surgical procedures" (p. 166). Two delivery families:

| Family | What it is | Members named | p. |
|---|---|---|---|
| **Inhalational** | "Volatile liquids or gases that are **vaporized or mixed with oxygen or medical air** to induce anesthesia" | **Inhaled gas:** nitrous oxide (laughing gas). **Inhaled volatile liquid:** desflurane (Suprane), isoflurane (Forane), sevoflurane (Ultane) | 166 |
| **Parenteral** | "Given **intravenously** and are used for **induction and/or maintenance** of general anesthesia, induction of amnesia, and as **adjuncts to inhalation-type anesthetics**" | etomidate (Amidate), ketamine (Ketalar), methohexital (Brevital), propofol (Diprivan), thiopental (Pentothal) | 166 |

"Often a **synergistic combination** of drugs is used, which allows for **smaller doses of each drug** and **better control** of the patient's anesthetized state" (p. 166). "Common intravenous anesthetic drugs include drugs classified **solely as general anesthetics, such as etomidate and propofol**" (p. 166).

> **Trap (confusable pair).** **Table 11.1 vs. Table 11.2 — parenteral vs. inhalational.** Five drugs are in one table and four in the other, and the two tables sit on the same page. Cue: *if it ends in **-flurane** or is a **gas,** it is **inhalational**; the **IV** list is **etomidate, ketamine, methohexital, propofol, thiopental.*** Watch **ketamine**, which is on the parenteral list but is the chapter's most-routed drug (IV, IM, and subcutaneous, p. 168), and **nitrous oxide,** the only **gas** where the other three inhaled agents are **volatile liquids.**

**Adjuncts.** "**Adjunct** is a general term for **any drug that enhances clinical therapy when used simultaneously with another drug.** Adjunct drugs can be thought of as '**helper drugs**'" (p. 166). Their stated purposes with general anesthetics: "**initiation (induction), sedation, reduction of anxiety, and amnesia**" (p. 166). The classes named in the text, each with its cross-reference:

| Adjunct class | Example given | Chapter | p. |
|---|---|---|---|
| Neuromuscular blocking drugs (NMBDs) | — | this chapter | 166 |
| Sedative-hypnotics or anxiolytics | propofol (this chapter) | 12 | 166 |
| Benzodiazepines | diazepam, midazolam | 12 | 166 |
| Barbiturates | thiopental | 12 | 166 |
| Opioid analgesics | fentanyl | 10 | 166 |
| Anticholinergics | atropine | 21 | 166 |
| Antiemetics | ondansetron | 52 | 166 |

**Table 11.3, Adjunctive Anesthetic Drugs** (p. 167) is a separate list keyed to *indications*, and the pairing of drug to purpose is the testable part:

| Drug | Pharmacologic class | Indication/use as given |
|---|---|---|
| alfentanil (Alfenta), **fentanyl (Sublimaze)**, sufentanil (Sufenta) | Opioid analgesic | **Anesthesia induction** |
| diazepam (Valium), midazolam (Versed) | Benzodiazepine | **Amnesia and anxiety reduction** |
| atropine, glycopyrrolate (Robinul) | Anticholinergic | **Drying up of excessive secretions** |
| meperidine (Demerol), morphine | Opioid analgesic | **Pain prevention and pain relief** |
| hydroxyzine (Atarax, Vistaril), promethazine (Phenergan) | Antihistamine | **Sedation, prevention of nausea and vomiting, anxiety reduction** |
| pentobarbital (Nembutal), secobarbital (Seconal) | Sedative-hypnotic | **Amnesia and sedation** |
| dexmedetomidine (Precedex) | Alpha agonist *(printed with an orphaned subscript "2" on the following line; intended reading is **alpha-2 agonist**)* | **Sedation** |

> **Trap (confusable pair).** **The two opioid rows in Table 11.3 are not interchangeable.** Both are "Opioid analgesic," and a distractor can swap the indication across the two lines. Cue: *the **short-acting synthetics** — alfentanil, **fentanyl**, sufentanil — are listed for **anesthesia induction**; **meperidine and morphine** are listed for **pain prevention and pain relief.*** Same class, different job in the table.

> **Trap (plausible-but-adjacent).** **The anticholinergic adjunct is there to dry secretions, not to sedate.** Atropine and glycopyrrolate are the only row whose indication is not sedation, amnesia, anxiety, or pain. An option giving atropine "for sedation" or "for nausea" has taken a neighbouring row's purpose. Conversely **promethazine and hydroxyzine** are the row that carries **three** purposes at once (sedation, antiemesis, anxiety reduction), so an option naming any one of the three is defensible.

**Mechanism of action.** The chapter is candid that there is no single mechanism: "**Many theories have been proposed**"; the drugs "vary widely in their chemical structures," and "their mechanisms of action are **not easily explained by a structure-receptor relationship**" (p. 167). The Overton-Meyer theory "has been used to explain **some of** the properties of anesthetic drugs" (p. 167) — a partial explanation by the book's own framing.

**Overall effect:** "a **progressive reduction of sensory and motor CNS functions,**" whose "degree and speed... vary with the anesthetics and adjuncts used along with their dosages and routes of administration" (p. 167). "In more extensive surgical procedures, especially those **involving the heart,** pharmacologic cardiac support involving **adrenergic drugs (see Chapter 18)** and **inotropic drugs (see Chapter 24)** also may be required" (p. 167).

**Table 11.4, Effects of Inhaled and Intravenous General Anesthetics** (p. 168) — the organ-by-organ reaction list:

| Organ/system | Reaction as given |
|---|---|
| Respiratory system | "Impaired oxygenation, depressed airway-protective mechanisms, **airway irritation and possible laryngospasm**" |
| Cardiovascular system | "**Depressed myocardium, hypotension and tachycardia,** bradycardia in response to vagal stimulation" |
| Cerebrovascular system | "**Increased intracranial pressure**" |
| Gastrointestinal system | "**Reduced hepatic blood flow** and thus reduced hepatic clearance" |
| Renal system | "**Decreased glomerular filtration**" |
| Skeletal muscles | "Skeletal muscle relaxation" |
| Cutaneous circulation | "**Vasodilation**" |
| Central nervous system (CNS) | "CNS depression; **blurred vision; nystagmus;** progression of CNS depression to decreased alertness, sensorium, and decreased level of consciousness" |

> **Trap (confusable pair).** **The cardiovascular row contains both tachycardia and bradycardia, with different triggers.** Cue: *the **drug effect** is depressed myocardium, hypotension, and **tachycardia**; the **bradycardia** is specifically "**in response to vagal stimulation.**"* An option offering bradycardia as a routine general-anesthetic effect has dropped the trigger clause.

**Indications** (p. 167): "to produce **unconsciousness** as well as **some degree of relaxation of skeletal and visceral smooth muscles** for surgical procedures as well as in **electroconvulsive therapy for severe depression** (see Chapter 16)."

**Contraindications** (p. 167): "**known drug allergy.** Depending on the drug type, contraindications may also include **pregnancy, narrow-angle glaucoma, acute porphyria, and known susceptibility to malignant hyperthermia.**"

**Adverse effects** (p. 167): "**dose dependent** and vary with the individual drug." Sites primarily affected: "the **heart, peripheral circulation, liver, kidneys, and respiratory tract.**" "One **major complication** of general anesthesia is **hypotension,** affecting perfusion of the organs mentioned earlier." Newer drugs have made **hepatotoxicity and myocardial depression** "less frequent," and balanced anesthesia has made **nausea, vomiting, and confusion** "less common."

**PONV.** "Even with the use of the newer anesthetic agents, the incidence of **postoperative nausea and vomiting (PONV)** remains **one of the most common reasons children and adults have extended/protracted stays in the postanesthesia care unit (PACU)/recovery room.** There is **no exact cause** of PONV; it is thought to be due to **multiple factors.** **Pain is associated with PONV; therefore the adequate treatment of pain frequently decreases nausea.**" And: "**Substance abuse can predispose a patient to anesthetic-induced complications**" (p. 167).

> **Trap (priority question).** **The intervention for PONV that the chapter actually names is treating the pain.** Given options like "administer an antiemetic," "restrict oral intake," "reposition the patient," and "treat the patient's pain," the sentence the chapter supplies is "**adequate treatment of pain frequently decreases nausea**" (p. 167). Cue: *PONV has **no single cause** in this book, so the answer it supports is the **one causal link it does state** — pain.*

**Toxicity and management of overdose** (p. 168). "In large doses, anesthetics are **potentially life threatening,** with **cardiac and respiratory arrest** as the ultimate causes of death. **However,** these drugs are **almost exclusively administered in a very controlled environment** by personnel trained in **advanced cardiac life support.** General anesthetics are **very quickly metabolized.** The newer drugs are much **more lipophilic** than the older drugs, contributing to the '**fast on**' and '**fast off**' action of these drugs. These factors combined make an anesthetic **overdose rare and easily reversible.**"

> **Trap (plausible-but-adjacent).** **"Overdose is rare and easily reversible" is a statement about the setting, not about an antidote.** The chapter names **no reversal agent for general anesthetics** — the reversal agents in this chapter belong to opioids (naloxone), benzodiazepines (flumazenil), and nondepolarizing NMBDs (neostigmine, pyridostigmine, sugammadex). Cue: *reversibility here comes from **rapid metabolism plus a controlled environment,** not from a drug.* See GAPS item 4.

**Interactions** (p. 168): "**antihypertensives and beta blockers,** which have **additive effects**... (i.e., **increased hypotensive effects from antihypertensives** and **increased myocardial depression from beta blockers**). **No significant interactions from laboratory tests have been reported.**"

> **Trap (confusable pair).** **The two interacting classes produce two different additive problems.** Cue: ***antihypertensives → more hypotension; beta blockers → more myocardial depression.*** They point the same direction clinically but the chapter names them separately, and a distractor can cross them.

**Older adult considerations** (p. 167 lifespan box). "The older adult patient is affected **more adversely** by anesthesia than the young or middle-aged adult." The stated mechanism is organ decline: "A decline in **liver function** results in the **decreased metabolism** of drugs. A decline in **renal function** leads to **decreased drug excretion.** Either of these can lead to **drug toxicity, unsafe levels, and/or overdose. If both of these organs are not functioning properly, the risk... is even greater.** In addition, older adult patients are **more sensitive to the effects of drugs affecting the central nervous system.**" The box also names a postanesthesia risk list — "**cardiac dysrhythmias, hypotension, respiratory depression, atelectasis, and/or pneumonia**" — driven by "the presence of **cardiac and respiratory diseases**," and flags **polypharmacy**: "The more drugs a patient is taking, the **higher the risk for adverse reactions and drug-drug interactions,** including interactions with anesthetics."

> **Trap (SATA, five options mapped to categories).** **Who is more prone to complications from general anesthesia.** Review question 2 (p. 181) is a select-all offering: a **79-year-old** for hip replacement; a **49-year-old athlete who quit heavy smoking 12 years ago**; a **19-year-old with a history of substance and alcohol abuse**; a **30-year-old in perfect health who has never had anesthesia**; a **50-year-old scheduled for outpatient laser vision correction.** The chapter supplies risk statements for exactly two of those categories: **advanced age** ("affected more adversely by anesthesia," p. 167) and **substance abuse** ("Substance abuse can predispose a patient to anesthetic-induced complications," p. 167; excessive alcohol "may alter the patient's response to general anesthesia," p. 175). Its smoking statement is about **current or historical smoking** and nicotine's paralyzing effect on respiratory cilia (p. 175); it attaches **no** risk to never having had anesthesia, and none to minor outpatient procedures. (Official key is at the back of the book; see GAPS item 3.)

> **Trap (level/stage swap).** **Age changes pharmacokinetics in two separate places, and the box names both.** Cue: *declining **liver** → decreased **metabolism**; declining **kidney** → decreased **excretion**.* Both end in accumulation, but they are different steps, and Chapter 2 is where those steps are defined. An option that blames "increased absorption" or "increased distribution" has named a step this box does not.

## §3 Malignant hyperthermia and dantrolene (pp. 165, 168, 173–176, 178, 181)

This is the highest-yield safety item in the chapter. The chapter returns to it in six places — the key-term box, the adverse-effects section, the NMBD contraindications, the assessment section, the implementation section, and the key points — and builds critical thinking exercise 1 and review question 7 on it. Everything below is quoted or page-located; nothing is supplied from outside the chapter.

**What it is** (p. 168): "Malignant hyperthermia is an **uncommon but potentially fatal, genetically linked adverse metabolic reaction to general anesthesia.**" The key term (p. 165) calls it "a **genetically linked major adverse reaction** to general anesthesia." The NMBD reversal discussion calls it "a **dysmetabolic syndrome**" (p. 174). The assessment section calls it "a **rapid progression of hyperthermia that may be fatal if not promptly recognized and aggressively treated**" (p. 176).

**What triggers it** (p. 168): "It is **classically associated** with the use of **volatile inhalational anesthetics** as well as the **depolarizing NMBD succinylcholine.**"

**Who is at greater risk** (p. 168): "**children, adolescents, and individuals with muscular and/or skeletal abnormalities.**" And, from assessment (p. 176): "The tendency is **inherited,** so questions about related signs and symptoms in the **family and patient's medical histories** are important to document and report. A **familial history of malignant hyperthermia would put the patient at risk.**"

**Signs** — four lists in the chapter, of differing length:

| Where | Signs listed |
|---|---|
| Key term, p. 165 | rapid rise in body temperature, **tachycardia, tachypnea, sweating** |
| Adverse effects, p. 168 | rapid rise in body temperature, **tachycardia, tachypnea, muscular rigidity** |
| Assessment, p. 176 | rapid rise in body temperature, **tachycardia, tachypnea, muscle rigidity, cyanosis, irregular heartbeat, mottling of the skin, diaphoresis (profuse sweating), unstable blood pressure** |
| Key points, p. 181 | rapid rise in body temperature, **tachycardia (increased pulse rate), tachypnea (increased respiratory rate), muscle rigidity, unstable blood pressure** |

**The temperature number** (p. 178): "This sudden elevation in the patient's body temperature (e.g., **higher than 104° F [40° C]**)."

**Treatment** (p. 168): "Malignant hyperthermia is treated with **cardiorespiratory supportive care as needed to stabilize heart and lung function,** along with the **skeletal muscle relaxant dantrolene** (see Chapter 12). **In fact, by law, all health care institutions that provide general anesthesia must keep a certain amount of dantrolene on hand** in the event that a case of malignant hyperthermia should occur."

**Contraindication consequences.** Known susceptibility to malignant hyperthermia is a contraindication to **general anesthetics** generally (p. 167); succinylcholine "is contraindicated in patients with a **personal or familial history** of malignant hyperthermia" (p. 175); and contraindications to depolarizing NMBDs "may also include **previous history** of malignant hyperthermia" (p. 173). Malignant hyperthermia "can also occur with succinylcholine" is restated in the reversal-agent paragraph (p. 174).

**When and where it occurs** (p. 176): "Note that malignant hyperthermia occurs **during the anesthesia process and in the surgical suite;** nevertheless, **close observation after anesthesia is still important and much needed.**" Implementation (p. 178) adds that the sudden temperature elevation "not only requires **critical care during and immediately after anesthesia** but also calls for **close monitoring even during regular postoperative care.**"

> **Trap (priority question).** **The postoperative fever spike is malignant hyperthermia until proven otherwise, not an infection.** Critical thinking exercise 1 (p. 181) is built precisely on this: hip replacement 2 hours earlier, just arrived on the orthopedic unit from PACU, temperature **104.8° F (40.4° C)** against **98.9 °F (37.2° C)** on leaving PACU, and "**another nurse comments that the patient must be developing an infection from the hip replacement.** What is the nurse's **priority action** at this time?" The chapter's supporting facts: the tendency is inherited and the reaction is "**fatal if not promptly recognized and aggressively treated**" (p. 176); dantrolene is stocked by law (p. 168); and the exemplar temperature for malignant hyperthermia is "higher than 104° F" (p. 178). Cue: *a **surgical-site infection does not spike six degrees in two hours,** and the chapter explicitly extends malignant hyperthermia monitoring past the surgical suite into "regular postoperative care."* (Official answer is on Evolve; see GAPS item 3.)

> **Trap (SATA, five options mapped to categories).** **Review question 7 (p. 182) maps five options onto the sign list.** It asks what, "in addition to a rapid rise in body temperature," indicates the possible presence of malignant hyperthermia, offering **respiratory depression, tachypnea, tachycardia, seizure activity, muscle rigidity.** Held against the p. 176 assessment list, **tachypnea, tachycardia, and muscle rigidity** are all named there and in the key points; **respiratory depression** and **seizure activity** appear nowhere on any of the four sign lists — respiratory depression is a general-anesthetic and CNS-depressant effect elsewhere in the chapter (pp. 165, 180), which is what makes it plausible. (Official key is at the back of the book; see GAPS item 3.)

> **Trap (confusable pair).** **Dantrolene is this chapter's *treatment* for malignant hyperthermia and is also on Box 11.6 as a drug with *additive* effects on NMBDs.** Same drug, two opposite-feeling roles on adjacent pages. Cue: *for **malignant hyperthermia** it is the **antidote** (p. 168); for **neuromuscular blockade** it is on the **additive-effects** list, meaning it **deepens and prolongs** the paralysis (p. 174).* It is also **defined in Chapter 12,** not here — this chapter gives it a name, a class ("skeletal muscle relaxant"), and a cross-reference, but **no mechanism, dose, or route.** See GAPS item 4.

> **Trap (confusable pair).** **Malignant hyperthermia vs. neuroleptic malignant syndrome vs. serotonin syndrome.** Only **malignant hyperthermia** appears in this chapter; the other two hyperthermic syndromes are **not named anywhere in Chapter 11,** so nothing here distinguishes them. Cue: *if the stem's trigger is a **volatile inhaled anesthetic or succinylcholine,** this chapter's answer is malignant hyperthermia and its drug is **dantrolene.*** Do not import criteria for the other syndromes; see GAPS item 4.

> **Trap (level/stage swap).** **Rapid rise in temperature is the headline sign, but it is not necessarily the first thing charted.** The assessment section warns: "With any type of anesthesia, it is often **a very slight change in vital signs,** other vital parameters, and laboratory test results that may provide nursing and other health care providers with a **possible clue** to the patient's reaction to anesthesia" (p. 176). ## §4 Neuromuscular blocking drugs (pp. 172–175)

**What they do** (p. 172): NMBDs "**prevent nerve transmission in skeletal and smooth muscles, leading to paralysis.** They are often used as **adjuncts with general anesthetics** for surgical procedures."

**The safety fact that governs the whole class** (p. 172): "NMBDs also **paralyze the skeletal muscles required for breathing: the intercostal muscles and the diaphragm.** The patient is rendered **unable to breathe on his or her own, and mechanical ventilation is required to prevent brain damage or death from suffocation.**"

Clinical Pearl (p. 172): "**Never give a neuromuscular blocker to someone who is not on mechanical ventilation.**"

- **Why the chapter labours the point:** "Deaths have been reported when an NMBD is **accidentally mistaken for a different drug** and given to a patient who is **not mechanically ventilated.**" In such an error "the patient would experience a **horrendous death, because the mind is alert but the patient cannot speak or move.**"
- **Institutional controls:** "Most hospitals have taken extra precautions to **keep NMBDs separated from other drugs** or have **marked them with warning stickers.**"
- **Safety and Quality Improvement box** (p. 172): NMBDs "are considered **high-alert drugs**"; the **Institute for Safe Medication Practices** "has reported several cases of patient death or injury"; and errors have occurred "due to '**sound-alike**' drug names as well (e.g., **vancomycin and vecuronium**)." Institutional responses: "**restrict access** to these drugs, provide **warning labels and reminders,** and increase staff awareness."

**The nurse's two checks** (p. 172): "It is essential for the nurse to **ensure that the patient is ventilated before giving an NMBD** and to **double-check that an NMBD is not inadvertently given.**"

**History** (p. 172): "**Curare is considered the grandfather of modern NMBDs.** The first drug to be used medicinally was **d-tubocurarine,** which was introduced into anesthesia practice in **1940;** it has now been replaced by newer drugs."

### The two mechanisms

| | **Depolarizing** | **Nondepolarizing** |
|---|---|---|
| Relationship to ACh | "work much like the neurotransmitter **acetylcholine (ACh)**"; they **bind in place of ACh** at the motor endplates | "also bind to ACh receptors at the neuromuscular junction, but instead of **mimicking** ACh, they **block** its actions" |
| Receptor role | "**competitive agonists**" (see Ch. 2) | "**competitive antagonists**" (see Ch. 2) of ACh |
| What happens at the membrane | two phases (below) | "the nerve cell membrane is **not depolarized,** the muscle fibers are not stimulated, and **skeletal muscle contraction does not occur**" |
| Drugs | **succinylcholine is the only depolarizing NMBD** | **cisatracurium, rocuronium, vecuronium, pancuronium**; classified as **short-, intermediate-, and long-acting** |
| Reversal | **not reversed** by acetylcholinesterase inhibitors | **neostigmine, pyridostigmine**; **sugammadex (Bridion)** for rocuronium or vecuronium |

**The two phases of depolarizing block** (p. 173): "During **phase I (depolarizing phase), the muscles fasciculate (twitch).** Eventually, after continued depolarization has occurred, **muscles are no longer responsive to the ACh released;** muscle tone cannot be maintained, and the muscle becomes paralyzed. This is **phase II, or the desensitizing phase.**"

**Succinylcholine's duration and why** (p. 173): "The duration of action of succinylcholine after a single dose is only **5 to 9 minutes** because of its **rapid breakdown by cholinesterase,** the enzyme responsible for metabolizing succinylcholine."

**Cisatracurium's unique elimination** (p. 173): "Most drugs are biotransformed in the liver and eliminated by the kidneys. **Cisatracurium is broken down by Hoffman elimination, a process dependent on pH and temperature. This makes it the drug of choice for patients with end-stage renal disease.**"

### The order of paralysis

"The typical time course of NMBD-induced paralysis during a surgical procedure is as follows" (p. 173):

1. "The first sensation that the patient typically feels is **muscle weakness.**"
2. "This is usually followed by a **total flaccid paralysis.**"
3. "**Small, rapidly moving muscles such as those of the fingers and eyes are generally the first to be paralyzed.**"
4. "The next are those of the **limbs, neck, and trunk.**"
5. "Finally, the **intercostal muscles and the diaphragm** are paralyzed, which causes **respiratory arrest.**"

**Recovery** (p. 173): "Recovery of muscular activity after discontinuation of anesthesia usually occurs in the **reverse order of the paralysis;** thus **the diaphragm is ordinarily the first to regain function.**"

**The single most important thing NMBDs do NOT do** (p. 173): "It must be noted that **NMBDs, when used alone, do not cause sedation or relieve pain or anxiety.** Therefore the patient **must also receive appropriate medications to manage pain and/or anxiety.**"

**Indications** (p. 173): "maintaining **skeletal muscle paralysis to facilitate controlled ventilation** during surgical procedures. **Shorter-acting NMBDs** are often used to **facilitate intubation with an endotracheal tube**... for a variety of diagnostic procedures such as **laryngoscopy and bronchoscopy** or when the patient requires mechanical ventilation." They are "frequently combined with **anxiolytics, analgesics, and anesthetics.**" Beyond the operating room, "they are given in the **ICU to paralyze mechanically ventilated patients**" (p. 174).

**Contraindications to depolarizing NMBDs** (p. 173): "**known drug allergy** and may also include **previous history of malignant hyperthermia, penetrating eye injuries, narrow-angle glaucoma, burns, recent cerebrovascular accident, and crush injuries.**"

**Adverse effects** (p. 173):

- **Fasciculations:** paralysis from depolarizing NMBDs "is sometimes **preceded by muscle spasms, which may damage muscles.** These muscle spasms are termed **fasciculations** and are **most pronounced in the muscle groups of the hands, feet, and face.**"
- **The consequence:** "Injury to muscle cells may cause **postoperative muscle pain** and **release potassium into the circulation, resulting in hyperkalemia. Hyperkalemia is the primary concern for the anesthesia provider.** The nurse should make every effort to be aware of the patient's **potassium status.**"
- **The countermeasure:** "**Small doses of nondepolarizing NMBDs are sometimes administered with succinylcholine to minimize these muscle fasciculations.**"
- **Why succinylcholine survives its drawbacks:** "still popular because of its **rapid onset of action, depth of neuromuscular blockade, and short duration of action.** For these reasons, it is often **preferred to nondepolarizing NMBDs for rapid-sequence induction of anesthesia (e.g., for emergency intubation).**"
- **Succinylcholine's full adverse list:** "**hyperkalemia; dysrhythmias; fasciculations; muscle pain; myoglobinuria; increased intraocular, intragastric, and intracranial pressure; and malignant hyperthermia.**"
- **Histamine:** "Some NMBDs cause a **release of histamine,** which can result in **bronchospasm, hypotension, and excessive bronchial and salivary secretion.**"
- **GI:** "seldom affected. When it is affected, **decreased tone and motility** typically result, which can lead to **constipation or even ileus.**"

**The dose principle** (p. 173): "The key to limiting adverse effects with most NMBDs is to **use only enough of the drug to block the neuromuscular receptors. If too much is used, the risk that other ganglionic receptors will be affected is increased.**"

**Table 11.7 Effects of Ganglionic Blockade by Neuromuscular Blocking Drugs** (p. 173), reproduced:

| Site | Part of nervous system blocked | Physiologic effect |
|---|---|---|
| **Arterioles** | **Sympathetic** | "Vasodilation and **hypotension**" |
| **Veins** | **Sympathetic** | "Dilation" |
| **Heart** | **Parasympathetic** | "**Tachycardia**" |
| **Gastrointestinal tract** | **Parasympathetic** | "Reduced tone and motility; **constipation**" |
| **Urinary bladder** | **Parasympathetic** | "**Urinary retention**" |

**Box 11.4, Conditions That Predispose Patients to Toxic Effects** (p. 174) — these "**increase his or her sensitivity to NMBDs and prolong their effects**": "**Acidosis, hypocalcemia, hypokalemia, hypothermia, myasthenia gravis, neonatal status, paraplegia.**"

**Box 11.5, Conditions That Oppose the Effects** (p. 174) — these "make it **more difficult for NMBDs to work, thus requiring the use of higher doses**" and "**do not necessarily lead to toxicity or overdose**": "**Cirrhosis with ascites, clostridial infections, hemiplegia, hypercalcemia, hyperkalemia, peripheral nerve transection, peripheral neuropathies, thermal burns.**"

| Additive effects | Opposing effects |
|---|---|
| **Aminoglycosides**, calcium channel blockers, **clindamycin**, cyclophosphamide, cyclosporine, **dantrolene**, furosemide, **inhalation anesthetics**, **local anesthetics**, **magnesium**, quinidine | **carbamazepine**, **corticosteroids**, **phenytoin** |

The body text adds (p. 174): "**aminoglycoside antibiotics can have additive effects.** The **tetracycline** antibiotics can also produce neuromuscular blockade, **possibly by chelation of calcium,** and **calcium channel blockers** have been shown to enhance neuromuscular blockade."

**Toxicity** (p. 174): "The primary concern when NMBDs are overdosed is **prolonged paralysis requiring prolonged mechanical ventilation. Cardiovascular collapse** may be seen and is **thought to be the result of histamine release.**"

**Reversal, in the chapter's own mechanism** (p. 174): "**Anticholinesterase drugs such as neostigmine and pyridostigmine are antidotes for nondepolarizing NMBDs** such as vecuronium, rocuronium, and cisatracurium. Anticholinesterase drugs work by **preventing the enzyme cholinesterase from breaking down ACh. This causes ACh to build up at the motor endplate, where it eventually displaces the nondepolarizing NMBD molecule,** returning the nerve to its original state."

- **The exception:** "**However, succinylcholine is not reversed with acetylcholinesterase inhibitors because of its short duration of action and natural breakdown.**"
- **Sugammadex (Bridion)** is "a new **selective relaxant binding agent** used for the reversal of **rocuronium or vecuronium.**"

> **Trap (confusable pair).** **Depolarizing vs. nondepolarizing, and both bind the same receptor.** This is the chapter's central pair and it turns on one word. Cue: ***depolarizing = competitive AGONIST, mimics ACh, causes fasciculations first, and is succinylcholine ALONE; nondepolarizing = competitive ANTAGONIST, blocks ACh, no fasciculations, and is everything else.*** Ch 2's Table 2.8 supplies the underlying vocabulary: an agonist binds and produces a response, an antagonist binds and produces none.

> **Trap (priority question).** **Only nondepolarizing NMBDs are reversible with an anticholinesterase.** Cue: *neostigmine and pyridostigmine **raise ACh** so it can out-compete the blocker — which only works if the blocker is a **competitor to be displaced.*** Succinylcholine, being an agonist that is already producing the block, is **not reversed** that way; it simply wears off in 5 to 9 minutes. An option giving neostigmine to reverse succinylcholine applies the right drug to the wrong mechanism.

> **Trap (confusable pair).** **Box 11.4 vs. Box 11.5 point in opposite directions, and the electrolyte pairs are mirror images.** Cue: ***hypo**calcemia and **hypo**kalemia **increase** sensitivity (toxicity risk, Box 11.4); **hyper**calcemia and **hyper**kalemia **oppose** the block (higher doses needed, Box 11.5).* Likewise **paraplegia** predisposes to toxicity while **hemiplegia** opposes the effect — two neurologic conditions on opposite lists.

> **Trap (level/stage swap).** **The order of paralysis and the order of recovery are exact reverses, and the diaphragm sits at the far end of both.** Cue: ***paralysis: fingers and eyes → limbs, neck, trunk → intercostals and diaphragm LAST. Recovery: diaphragm FIRST.*** The muscle that fails last recovers first, which is why extubation decisions are not made on hand grip alone.

> **Trap (priority question).** **NMBDs do not sedate, relieve pain, or relieve anxiety.** A paralyzed patient may be fully awake and aware. Critical thinking exercise 2 (p. 181) is built on exactly this: a wife asks of her mechanically ventilated husband, "**Is he awake? Can he hear me?**" Cue: *paralysis is **not** unconsciousness, and the patient "**must also receive appropriate medications to manage pain and/or anxiety.**" (Official answer on Evolve; see GAPS item 3.)*

> **Trap (confusable pair).** **Aminoglycosides, clindamycin, tetracyclines, and magnesium all deepen neuromuscular blockade.** Three antibiotic classes plus an electrolyte on one list. Ch 39 gives the same fact from the antibiotic side: aminoglycosides "may **prolong the duration of action of the neuromuscular blockade**," and clindamycin has "neuromuscular blocking properties" that may require ventilatory support. Cue: *a ventilated surgical patient on an **aminoglycoside or clindamycin** risks **prolonged paralysis,** not antibiotic failure.*

> **Trap (plausible-but-adjacent).** **Hyperkalemia is the anesthesia provider's primary concern with succinylcholine, and it comes from the fasciculations, not from the drug directly.** Cue: *fasciculations **injure muscle cells → potassium leaks out → hyperkalemia.*** That chain is also why **small doses of a nondepolarizing NMBD are given first** to blunt the fasciculations. Links onward to Ch 29 (Fluids and Electrolytes) and Ch 28 (Diuretic Drugs), where potassium status recurs.

## §5 Local anesthetics (pp. 169–172)

**Definition and effect** (p. 171): local anesthetics "work by **rendering a specific portion of the body insensitive to pain by interfering with nerve transmission. Nerve conduction is blocked only in the area where the anesthetic is applied and there is no loss of consciousness.**"

**Mechanism** (p. 171): they "block **both the generation and conduction of impulses through all types of nerve fibers (autonomic, sensory, and motor)** by blocking the **movement of certain ions (sodium, potassium, and calcium)**... Some of these drugs are also described as **membrane-stabilizing** because they alter the cell membrane of the nerve so that the **free movement of ions is inhibited.**"

**The order of loss, the section's cleanest testable structure** (p. 171): "The membrane-stabilizing effects occur **first in the small fibers and then in the large fibers.** In terms of paralysis, usually **autonomic activity is affected first; then pain and other sensory functions are lost. Motor activity is the last to be lost.** When the effects of the local anesthetic wear off, **recovery occurs in reverse order: motor activity returns first, then sensory functions, and finally autonomic activity.**"

**Systemic effects** (p. 171): "Possible systemic effects... include effects on **circulatory and respiratory function**"; these "are **unlikely unless large quantities of a drug are injected.**" Also: "Local anesthetics **produce sympathetic blockade;** that is, they block the action of the two neurotransmitters of the sympathetic nervous system: **norepinephrine and epinephrine.**"

### The two chemical families

**Box 11.3, Chemical Groups of Local Anesthetics** (p. 171), reproduced:

| **Ester type** | **Amide type** |
|---|---|
| benzocaine, chloroprocaine, **cocaine**, **procaine**, proparacaine, propoxycaine, **tetracaine** | bupivacaine, dibucaine, etidocaine, **lidocaine**, mepivacaine, prilocaine |

Clinical Pearl (p. 172): "**To differentiate between esters and amide types of local anesthetics, remember amides have the letter 'i' before the suffix 'caine.'**"

The body text gives the same rule with its rationale (pp. 171–172): "amides all have the letter '**i**' in their name **before the suffix '-caine,' whereas esters do not.** Knowing the different classes... is important, because **patients who are allergic to an ester type may not be allergic to an amide type.**"

**Why the classes differ in allergy risk** (p. 172): "**Different enzymes are responsible for the breakdown of these two groups.** Anesthetics belonging to the **ester family are metabolized by cholinesterase in the plasma and liver. They are converted into a para-aminobenzoic acid (PABA) compound. This compound is responsible for the allergic reactions.** In contrast, the **amide type... is metabolized uneventfully to active and inactive metabolites in the liver by other enzymes.** Often when an individual has an adverse reaction to one of the local anesthetics, **using a drug from the alternate chemical class can avoid the problem.**"

**Box 11.2, Types of Local Anesthesia** (p. 170), reproduced by category:

| Type | Definition as given |
|---|---|
| **Spinal / neuraxial / central** | "Anesthetic drugs are **injected into the area near the spinal cord within the vertebral column**"; accomplished by one of two injection techniques, intrathecal and epidural |

- **Peripheral nerve catheter pumps** (p. 170): "designed to **infuse local anesthetic for several days postoperatively** around the nerves that innervate the surgical site. The catheter is **implanted during surgery and is normally taken out by the patient at home.** Common trade names include **Pain Buster and On-Q Pump.**"

**Spinal headache**, the chapter's named complication (p. 171):

- **Incidence:** "occurs in **up to 70% of patients** who either experience **inadvertent dural puncture during epidural anesthesia** or **undergo intrathecal anesthesia.**"

- **The blood patch:** "Severe cases... may be treated by the anesthetist by injecting a **small volume (roughly 15 mL) of the patient's own venous blood into the patient's epidural space. - Other uses: "**Parenteral lidocaine is also used to treat certain cardiac dysrhythmias** as well as in the **management of postoperative pain to decrease the use of systemic opioids.**"
- Pregnancy category **B**.
- **The allergy manoeuvre:** "Some patients may report that they have allergic or anaphylactic reactions to the '**caines**'... In these situations, **it may be wise to try a local anesthetic of the ester type.**"

The clinical payoff is the allergy: **esters metabolize to PABA, which is what causes the reactions,** so an ester-allergic patient can usually receive an amide, and vice versa.

> **Trap (level/stage swap).** **Autonomic goes first, motor goes last, and recovery reverses it.** Cue: ***loss: autonomic → sensory/pain → motor. Return: motor → sensory → autonomic.*** A patient who can move a limb has **not** necessarily regained sensation, and one reporting returning sensation still has autonomic blockade — which is why hypotension can persist after an epidural appears to be wearing off.

> **Trap (confusable pair).** **Intrathecal vs. epidural.** Both are neuraxial and both place a needle near the cord. Cue: ***intra**thecal goes **into the subarachnoid space** (through the dura), for major abdominal or limb surgery; **epi**dural goes **outside** the dura ("**without puncturing the dura**") via a catheter, and is the labor-and-delivery route.* The dura is the dividing line, which is also why **spinal headache** follows intrathecal anesthesia or an **accidental** dural puncture during an epidural.

> **Trap (confusable pair).** **Table 11.6's three columns do not rank together.** Cue: ***procaine is LOWEST potency and SHORTEST duration; tetracaine is HIGHEST potency and near-longest — but mepivacaine has the LONGEST duration (120–150 min) at only moderate potency.*** Onset splits differently again: **lidocaine and mepivacaine are immediate,** while the two potency extremes (procaine and tetracaine) both have a delay.

> **Trap (plausible-but-adjacent).** **Epinephrine is added to keep the anesthetic local, not to strengthen it.** Its two stated purposes are to "**maintain localized drug activity**" (preventing systemic absorption and toxicity) and to "**reduce local blood loss.**" Review question 1 (p. 182) opens with a physician requesting "**lidocaine with epinephrine**," testing whether you know why the combination exists. (Official key at the back of the book; see GAPS item 3.)

> **Trap (priority question).** **Spinal headache is common, self-limiting, and treated conservatively first.** Cue: *up to **70%** incidence; first-line is **bed rest, analgesics, and sometimes caffeine**; the **blood patch (~15 mL of the patient's own venous blood, >90% effective)** is for **severe** cases and is done by the anesthetist.* Going straight to a blood patch for a new, uncomplicated post-spinal headache skips the conservative tier.

## §6 Nursing process

**Who administers these drugs** (p. 175): anesthetics "are **not drugs that are typically given by the registered nurse** unless the nurse is licensed and certified as an anesthesia provider." The stated exceptions are **topical forms** — "oral swish-and-swallow solutions" and "**lidocaine patches** for pain relief."

**Before any anesthesia** (pp. 175–178):

- "resuscitative equipment and medications, **including opioid antidotes, must be readily available.**"
- Assess **swallowing ability and gag reflexes.**
- **Sensory assessment** focuses on defined anatomic areas with **comparison to baseline.**

**After anesthesia** (p. 178): "**check for the gag reflex, and expect to withhold food or drink until the patient's sensation and/or gag reflex has returned.**"

**With mechanical ventilation and NMBDs** (p. 176): the drugs are used "to **prevent the patient from fighting against the ventilation** provided by the machine, resisting the effects of the mechanical ventilatory assistance, and possibly causing him or her to **hypoventilate.**"

**Eye protection** (p. 178): loss of the blink reflex and "**loss of sensation to the eye**" makes eye care a named safety action.

**Discharge criteria after moderate sedation** (p. 171), reproduced because they are enumerable:

- "Patient is **alert and oriented** compared with the baseline neurologic assessment."
- "**Protective swallowing and gag reflexes are intact.**"
- "**Vital signs are stable and consistent with baseline values for at least 30 min after the last dosing.**" Institutions vary: "within **15%** of admission readings," or "blood pressure and pulse rate within normal limits or **within 20 points** of baseline, temperature **lower than 101° F (38.3° C); oxygen saturation is at least 95% on room air 30 min after the last dose.**"
- "Child must be **ambulatory without assistance** appropriate for his or her age."
- "Child is able to **ingest and retain oral fluids.**"
- "**An adult is present** to get the patient home and remain with the patient for **at least two half-lives** of the drugs used."

**Reversal agents to keep at the bedside** (p. 171): "**naloxone for opioids and flumazenil for benzodiazepines.**"

**Pediatric moderate sedation dosing** (p. 171): "**Medication dosing is calculated based on weight, but the response varies significantly from child to child.** Always consider **beginning with the lowest recommended dose, or even half that, and titrate as needed.**"

> **Trap (priority question).** **The gag reflex gates oral intake, not the patient's report of feeling fine.** Cue: *check the **gag reflex** and **withhold food and drink until sensation and/or the gag reflex has returned.*** This applies after general anesthesia and after topical anesthesia of the throat, where the anesthetic itself abolishes the reflex.

> **Trap (SATA, five options mapped to categories).** **The moderate-sedation discharge criteria span five systems and make a natural select-all:** **neurologic** (alert and oriented to baseline), **airway** (gag and swallow intact), **cardiovascular** (vital signs stable ≥30 min, within 15% or 20 points of baseline), **mobility** (ambulatory without assistance), and **GI** (able to ingest and retain fluids), plus the **social** requirement of an accompanying adult. An option about pain score or return to full pre-procedure activity is not on the chapter's list.

> **Trap (confusable pair).** **naloxone reverses opioids; flumazenil reverses benzodiazepines.** Both are named together in the moderate-sedation box, which is where they get swapped. Cue: *nal**ox**one → **o**pioids (Ch 10 develops it fully); flumazenil → benzodiazepines.* Neither reverses a general anesthetic or an NMBD; see GAPS item 4.
