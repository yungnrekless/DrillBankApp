> **Trap (confusable pair).** **Antiviral vs. antiretroviral.** *Antiviral is the **general** term; antiretroviral the **specific** one.* Every antiretroviral is an antiviral; only the HIV drugs are antiretrovirals. The chapter's structure and its key points (p. 649) use the same split.

> **Trap (confusable pair).** **Cell-mediated vs. humoral immunity.** Both definitions open with the identical words "One of two major parts of the immune system," which is what makes them swappable. Cue: ***cell-mediated = T cells and NONspecific; humoral = B cells, antibodies, specific.*** The printed discriminator is **nonspecific vs. specific**, not the cell type.

> **Trap (confusable pair).** **Nucleoside vs. nucleotide.** Cue: ***nucleoTide = phosphaTe.*** Stated twice: "A **nucleoside** is a single unit consisting of a **base and its attached sugar**... A **nucleotide** is a **nucleoside plus an attached phosphate**" (p. 634). Not cosmetic: **tenofovir alafenamide is a nucleoTide** RTI; **zidovudine, lamivudine, abacavir, emtricitabine, stavudine, didanosine are nucleoSide** RTIs.

> **Trap (confusable pair).** **DNA vs. RNA.** The key-term box cross-references each to the other ("Compare with..."), the book's own signal they will be swapped. Cue: ***DNA is primarily in the nuclei; RNA is in both nuclei and cytoplasm.*** A virion's genome is DNA **or** RNA, "**but not both**" (p. 632), and **retroviruses are RNA viruses** by definition.

> **Trap (confusable pair).** **Virion vs. virus.** A virion is "a mature virus particle." The mechanism sentences depend on it: **fusion** is what **virions** do to host cells, and **neuraminidase** is what lets **budding virions** escape.

> **Trap (plausible-but-adjacent).** **"The drug cures the infection" is always the wrong option here.** Teaching box: these drugs "**suppress but do not cure** the viral infection" (p. 648). What *is* true: "**herpetic lesions will crust over, and the frequency of recurrence will decrease**" (p. 648). Review question 3's key is the crusting statement; its distractors are eradication, preventing spread (abstinence and condoms do that), and treating the partner. Review question 4's distractor: "These drugs will eventually eliminate the virus from my body."

| **peramivir** (Rapivab) | **IV**, "**600-mg single IV dose**." |
| **baloxavir** (Xofluza) | "**not a neuraminidase inhibitor**; rather... an inhibitor of **cap-dependent endonuclease**," **single oral dose**. |
| **ribavirin** (Virazole) | Interferes with "**both RNA and DNA synthesis**"; spectrum "**broader than that of other available antiviral drugs**." **Inhaled = hospitalized infants with severe RSV; oral = hepatitis C.** |

> **Trap (confusable pair).** **Amantadine vs. rimantadine.** Cue: ***rimantadine = longer half-life, fewer CNS effects.*** Everything else is shared. The real asymmetry: the **12-month age floor and eczematous-rash contraindication are printed for amantadine only** (p. 635).

> **Trap (confusable pair).** **Influenza A only vs. A and B.** Cue: *the **-adines** are **A only**; the **-amivirs** (oseltamivir, zanamivir, peramivir) cover **A and B**.* Amantadine for influenza B is wrong by indication; amantadine for any influenza is wrong by current CDC guideline.

> **Trap (confusable pair).** **Oseltamivir vs. zanamivir.** Cue: ***oseltamivir = oral, prophylaxis AND treatment; zanamivir = inhaled Diskhaler, treatment of active illness ONLY*** (p. 638). Adverse effects split too: **oseltamivir = nausea and vomiting; zanamivir = diarrhea, nausea, sinusitis.**

> **Trap (confusable pair).** **Acyclovir vs. valacyclovir vs. famciclovir.** The chapter warns twice ("**Note the slight inconsistencies in the spelling**," p. 637; "**acyclovir may be mistaken for valacyclovir and famciclovir**," p. 647). Cue: ***acyclovir is the only one with IV and topical forms,*** so any infusion, finger cot, or ointment stem is acyclovir. **"Val-" means prodrug for oral use,** twice: valacyclovir → acyclovir, valganciclovir → ganciclovir.

> **Trap (confusable pair).** **Zovirax vs. Zostrix.** Its own safety box (p. 639), from a real reported error. Cue: ***Zostrix = capsaicin "derived from hot chili peppers" = arthritic pain; Zovirax 5% ointment = topical acyclovir = genital herpes.*** The box's stated fix is **using generic names.**

> **Trap (confusable pair).** **Acyclovir vs. ganciclovir: which virus.** Cue: ***acyclovir = HSV-1, HSV-2, VZV; ganciclovir = CMV.*** Review question 5 is exactly this, a post-transplant patient on ganciclovir with no viral infection; distractors offer HIV prophylaxis, influenza A prophylaxis, and antibiotic synergy.

> **Trap (confusable pair).** **Hepatitis B drugs vs. hepatitis C drugs.** Cue: ***B = entecavir, lamivudine, tenofovir (plus alfa-interferon, Ch 47); C = the "-buvir / -asvir / -previr" combinations headed by sofosbuvir.*** Review question 8 keys on it. Text discriminators (p. 635): "**Hepatitis B is generally milder than hepatitis C**"; "**Hepatitis B is preventable by administering a specific vaccine**"; "**Hepatitis C is the major cause of liver failure leading to liver transplantation.**"

> **Trap (setting vs. focus).** **Ribavirin's route names the disease and the population.** Cue: ***inhaled (Virazole via SPAG) = hospitalized infants with severe RSV; oral = hepatitis C.*** The axis is route, not drug name.

## §2 HIV and AIDS

**The three viral enzymes** (p. 639), which the class names are built from: **reverse transcriptase** "promotes the synthesis of **complementary (mirror image) DNA molecules from the viral RNA genome**"; **integrase** "promotes the **integration of this viral DNA into the host cell DNA.** This hybrid DNA complex is known as a **provirus**"; **protease** "serves to **chemically separate the new viral RNA from viral protein molecules.**" Reverse transcriptase "has a **high rate of errors**," which "allows **frequent genetic mutations**... and often results in **viral strains that are resistant to both medications and the patient's immune system**" and "**hamper[s] the development of an effective vaccine**" (p. 640).

**The antiretroviral classes** (pp. 640–641; Table 40.4, p. 642):

| Class | Mechanism, as stated | Drugs named in this chapter |
|---|---|---|
| **NRTIs** | block reverse transcriptase | zidovudine (AZT, ZDV; Retrovir), abacavir (Ziagen), didanosine, emtricitabine (Emtriva), lamivudine (Epivir), stavudine, tenofovir (Viread), tenofovir alafenamide (Vemlidy, a **nucleotide** RTI) |
| **NNRTIs** | block reverse transcriptase | nevirapine (Viramune), delavirdine, doravirine, efavirenz, etravirine, rilpivirine |
| **Protease inhibitors** | "**inhibiting the protease retroviral enzyme**" | indinavir (Crixivan), ritonavir (Norvir), nelfinavir, fosamprenavir, atazanavir, tipranavir, saquinavir, darunavir, lopinavir/ritonavir (Kaletra) |
| **Fusion inhibitor** | "**inhibiting viral fusion**" | **enfuvirtide (Fuzeon), the only drug in the class** |
| **Entry inhibitor / CCR5 antagonist** | "**selectively and reversibly binding to the type 5 chemokine coreceptors located on the CD4 cells**" | **maraviroc (Selzentry), the only drug in the class** |
| **Integrase strand transfer inhibitors** | "**inhibiting the catalytic activity of the enzyme integrase**" | raltegravir (Isentress), dolutegravir, cabotegravir |

**HAART** (pp. 640–641): "combinations of antiretroviral drugs ('**cocktails**')... **normally begun immediately on confirmation of HIV infection**," usually "**at least three medications**": two or three NRTIs; two NRTIs plus one or two protease inhibitors; or an NRTI plus an NNRTI with one or two protease inhibitors.

| Number | Meaning | p. |
|---|---|---|
| **CD4 below 200 cells/mm³** | when prophylaxis for opportunistic infections "is most frequently given" | 640 |
| **Viral load below 50 copies/mL** | "an **undetectable** viral load and... a **primary goal** of antiretroviral therapy" | 642 |
| **Falling CD4 and/or rising viral load** | "**Evidence of drug resistance**" | 642 |
| **At least 15 years** after infection | definition of **long-term survival** | 640 |
| **0.3%** | HIV transmission risk from a percutaneous (needlestick) injury | 639 |
| **Up to 90%** | PrEP effectiveness "if it is used as prescribed" | 639 |
| **Approximately 25%** | of US HIV patients also infected with hepatitis C | 643 |
| **At least two thirds** | reduction in infant infection from maternal prophylaxis; newborn prophylaxis "for the first **6 weeks** of life" | 643 |

**WHO four stages** (p. 640), quoted: **1** "asymptomatic infection"; **2** "early, general symptoms of disease"; **3** "moderate symptoms"; **4** "severe symptoms, often leading to death." Treated patients "**usually do not progress through all of these stages,** or at least such progression is **slowed considerably (by years)**."

**Transmission** (p. 639): "**sexual activity, intravenous drug use, and perinatal transfer from mother to child**." **Not** by hugging, kissing, coughing, sneezing, pools, shared food, water, utensils, toilets, or **insect bites**.

**PrEP vs. occupational post-exposure prophylaxis:** PrEP is "**tenofovir and emtricitabine (Truvada)**" daily, **before** exposure, for people at "**very high risk**" (p. 639). Occupational prophylaxis is "**Raltegravir + Truvada**" **after** exposure to "Blood; fluid containing visible blood or other potentially infectious fluid or tissue" (Table 40.5, p. 642).

> **Trap (confusable pair).** **NRTI vs. NNRTI.** One "N" apart, same enzyme, same HAART recipes. Cue: *both inhibit **reverse transcriptase**; the **N**RTIs are **nucleoside or nucleotide analogues**, the **NN**RTIs are **not**.* Membership is the reliable discriminator. Clinical use: "switching a patient **from a protease inhibitor to an NNRTI** may help reduce" lipodystrophy (p. 643).

> **Trap (confusable pair).** **Enfuvirtide vs. maraviroc.** Both block HIV before entry, both are the sole member of their class, both for treatment-experienced patients. Cue: ***enfuvirtide = FUSION inhibitor and the only injectable (SUBQ) antiretroviral here; maraviroc = ENTRY inhibitor / CCR5 antagonist, oral only, binds the CD4 cell's coreceptor.*** Second cue: *maraviroc needs an **FDA-approved medication guide** before dispensing and carries **hepatotoxicity with allergic-type features**.*

> **Trap (confusable pair).** **Reverse transcriptase vs. integrase vs. protease.** The chapter says the class names are the mnemonic: "**The name of each class... provides a reminder of its role**" (p. 641). Cue: ***reverse transcriptase makes DNA from RNA; integrase splices that DNA into the host chromosome (the provirus); protease cuts the finished protein strand at the end.*** Suffixes: **"-navir" = protease inhibitor; "-tegravir" = integrase inhibitor.**

> **Trap (SATA, five options mapped to categories).** **Why multiple drugs for HIV** (review question 6, p. 649). **True:** more effective against **resistant strains** ("combinations of drugs that work by different mechanisms... **reducing the likelihood of viral resistance**," p. 643) and the goal is **reducing viral load** (p. 642). **False, and each is the direction reversed:** fewer toxicities (they "carry **significant toxicities**," p. 640), reduced T-cell counts (treatment "**significantly increases T-cell counts**," p. 642), increased opportunistic infections (treatment brings "a significant **reduction in mortality and incidence of opportunistic infections**," p. 642). Check direction first.

> **Trap (level/stage swap).** **The WHO four stages describe UNTREATED infection.** Cue: *stage numbers rise only in the absence of effective therapy; the treated counterpart is **long-term survival, at least 15 years**.* Fig. 40.4's own title says "spectrum of **untreated**" HIV infection.

> **Trap (confusable pair).** **CD4 200 vs. viral load 50.** Cue: ***CD4 below 200 cells/mm³ → start prophylaxis for opportunistic infections; viral load below 50 copies/mL → undetectable, the therapy goal.*** One is a **floor you fall below and act on**, the other a **target you drive down to**. Resistance is the two moving in **opposite directions at once**.

> **Trap (confusable pair).** **PrEP vs. post-exposure prophylaxis.** Truvada is in **both** regimens, which is the trap. Cue: ***Truvada ALONE, daily, before exposure = PrEP; raltegravir PLUS Truvada, after a needlestick = occupational exposure prophylaxis.*** Review question 2 keys on it: the needlestick answer offered is "emtricitabine and tenofovir," i.e. Truvada; distractors are didanosine, "lamivudine and enfuvirtide," and acyclovir.

> **Trap (plausible-but-adjacent).** **"Not transmitted by casual contact" is a fact, not the nursing action.** The chapter says "**No solid evidence to date confirms** transmission of HIV by casual contact." The supported action is "**hand hygiene** and maintaining **Standard Precautions** to avoid contact with **all body fluids**," which "**dramatically reduces the risk for caregiver infection**" (p. 639).

**Three different time windows in one chapter:**

| Window | For what | p. |
|---|---|---|
| **within 2 days** of onset | oseltamivir and zanamivir for influenza | 638, 647 |
| **within 72 hours** of onset | acyclovir in herpes zoster; "may speed recovery, but this effect is usually **not dramatic**" | 634 |
| **within 24 to 48 hours** of onset | patient teaching, at "the **first sign**" of a **recurrent** genital herpes or zoster episode | 648 |

> **Trap (confusable pair).** **Ganciclovir vs. foscarnet and cidofovir: marrow vs. kidney.** The chapter works it as a decision: a **heart transplant recipient** on "**cyclosporine, which is nephrotoxic. Therefore using foscarnet in this patient may be more dangerous than using ganciclovir.** On the other hand, a patient... immunocompromised because of a **bone marrow transplant** might be better treated using **foscarnet**" (p. 638). Cue: ***match the drug to the organ the patient has NOT already compromised.***

> **Trap (confusable pair).** **Bone marrow suppression belongs to two drugs, one in each half of the chapter.** Cue: *stem about **CMV or a transplant** → **ganciclovir**; stem about **HIV or AZT** → **zidovudine**.* Review question 1 is a SATA on zidovudine's adverse effects; the true set is **nausea, headaches, bone marrow suppression** (Table 40.2, p. 636), and the two wrong options, **retinitis** and **Kaposi's sarcoma**, are **manifestations of HIV disease, not drug adverse effects.** That is the axis.

> **Trap (priority question).** **Antiviral plus pregnancy: teratogenicity outranks efficacy.** Critical thinking exercise 1 (p. 649) puts a post-bone-marrow-transplant CMV patient on antivirals and has her ask about pregnancy. Default: *teach the contraception and exposure rule first* (ribavirin is category X, black-boxed, covering **male partners** and **pregnant staff in the room**, with a **6-month tail**). Override: in **HIV** the direction reverses, because maternal prophylaxis "**reduce[s] infant infection by at least two thirds and is not normally harmful to either the mother or infant**" (p. 643), with **zidovudine given to pregnant patients and newborns.**

> **Trap (confusable pair).** **Indinavir vs. acyclovir: two different "push fluids," plus one fasting rule.** Cue: ***indinavir → at least 48 oz daily to prevent nephrolithiasis; IV acyclovir → hydrate during and for hours after the infusion to prevent crystalluria and renal tubular damage.*** Both renal, different events. Indinavir is also the fasting drug: "best dissolved and absorbed in an **acidic gastric environment**... administered in a **fasting state**" (p. 643), against the general rule to "take oral antivirals **with meals**" (p. 646). ***Indinavir and entecavir are the empty-stomach exceptions;*** zidovudine is explicitly neutral, "**not impeded** by taking the drug with food or milk" (p. 648).

> **Trap (setting vs. focus).** **2 days vs. 72 hours.** Cue: ***2 days = influenza (oseltamivir, zanamivir); 72 hours = zoster (acyclovir).*** The chapter repeats the influenza figure in both assessment and implementation, the sign it expects it back. The third figure, **24 to 48 hours**, appears only in patient teaching and is keyed to the patient noticing a **recurrence**, not to a clinician's clock.

> **Trap (priority question).** **Rash on an antiviral: the accompanying findings set the priority, not the rash.** Default: rash on nevirapine or zidovudine is a listed adverse effect and is monitored. Override: **blistering, fever, malaise, myalgias, oral lesions, swelling or edema, or conjunctivitis** turns it into "contact the prescriber immediately or... seek prompt **emergency medical attention**" (p. 648). Cue: *isolated rash = monitor; rash plus mucous membranes or systemic signs = emergency.*

## §4 Herpesviruses, hepatitis, coronavirus

**The eight human herpesviruses** (p. 634): **HSV-1**, "perioral blisters ('fever blisters' or 'cold sores')"; **HSV-2**, "genital herpes"; **HHV-3 (VZV)**, "**both chickenpox and shingles**"; **HHV-4**, "**Epstein-Barr virus**," mononucleosis and chronic fatigue syndrome; **HHV-5**, "**cytomegalovirus (CMV)**," cause of "**CMV retinitis**... and CMV disease"; **HHV-6 and HHV-7**, "not especially clinically significant"; **HHV-8**, "**Kaposi's sarcoma herpesvirus**... an **oncogenic (cancer-inducing)** virus." "**Types 3 through 7 normally do not cause diseases that require medication, except in the case of immunocompromised patients.**"

**VZV across the lifespan** (pp. 634–635): "**chickenpox (varicella) in childhood, remains dormant for many years, and can then reemerge in later adulthood** as painful **herpes zoster** lesions known as **shingles**," following nerve tracts "known as **dermatomes**." **Postherpetic neuralgia** follows "in **up to 50% of older adult patients**." Vaccines: varicella for healthy children "**older than 1 year of age** who have not had chickenpox"; **two** shingles vaccines at "**50 years of age or older** (see Chapter 49)." **Neonatal herpes** "is often a life-threatening infection. The **best strategy to prevent transmission**... is usually **delivery by cesarean section** for any mother with **active genital herpes lesions**" (p. 634).

**Hepatitis** (p. 635): "inflammation of the liver that can result in cell damage and liver dysfunction," from "**alcohol abuse, medications, and viruses.**" "**Acute hepatitis is generally treatable, whereas chronic hepatitis can lead to liver failure.**" Hepatitis **B** spreads "through **blood and body fluid** exposure" plus perinatally; hepatitis **C** "primarily from contact with **infected blood,** but can also occur from **sexual contact**."

**COVID-19** (p. 645): treatment "is **beyond the scope of this pharmacology textbook**." Named: **remdesivir**, "a **novel nucleotide analogue, originally developed for treatment of Ebola**," IV, "**loading dose of 200 mg** on day 1 and **100 mg/day for 5 days**," withheld "**if liver function tests are over five times the upper limit of normal or if creatinine clearance is less than 30 mL/min**"; monoclonal antibodies (bamlanivimab plus etesevimab, casirivimab plus imdevimab, sotrovimab); **tocilizumab (Actemra)** and **molnupiravir** ("a **ribonucleoside analog**") under EUA; **Paxlovid**, "an oral protease inhibitor given in conjunction with ritinovir, which is **awaiting EUA**." Vaccines: "The **Pfizer vaccine (Cormirnaty) is the only FDA-approved vaccine**," Moderna and Johnson & Johnson under EUA.

> **Trap (level/stage swap).** **Chickenpox vs. shingles is one virus at two life stages.** Cue: ***varicella = childhood, primary, spread by direct contact with weeping lesions; zoster = adult, reactivation from dormancy, follows dermatomes, brings postherpetic neuralgia.*** An option naming a "different herpesvirus" for shingles is wrong: both are **HHV-3**. Vaccine ages track the stage: **older than 1 year** vs. **50 or older**.

> **Trap (confusable pair).** **HSV-1 vs. HSV-2.** Cue: ***HSV-1 = perioral; HSV-2 = genital.*** But the chapter opens with the caution that kills the absolute version: "**Although there can be anatomic overlap between the two types**... HSV-1 infection is **most commonly** associated with perioral blisters." So "HSV-1 can never cause genital lesions" is a distractor, not a fact.

> **Trap (confusable pair).** **HHV-5 vs. HHV-8.** Both are herpesviruses in Box 40.1's AIDS indicator list. Cue: ***HHV-5 = CMV = retinitis, treated with ganciclovir; HHV-8 = Kaposi's sarcoma herpesvirus = oncogenic, treated with antineoplastics (Chs 45 and 46), radiation, and surgery, not with an antiviral.***

> **Trap (level/stage swap).** **Acute vs. chronic hepatitis.** Cue: ***acute is generally treatable; chronic leads to liver failure.*** The chapter names its own scope in the same paragraph: "**This chapter focuses on chronic hepatitis B and hepatitis C.**"

> **Trap (plausible-but-adjacent).** **An infection is "opportunistic" because of the HOST, not the organism.** "Infections caused by **any type of microorganism** that occur in an **immunocompromised host** but **normally would not occur in an immunocompetent host**" (p. 632). Box 40.1 sorts the indicator diseases into **protozoal, fungal, viral, bacterial, opportunistic neoplasias,** and **others** (p. 634), the category set a SATA item would draw five options from. Two belong to the antibiotic chapters: ***Pneumocystis jirovecii* pneumonia** (fungal) and ***Mycobacterium avium-intracellulare* complex** (bacterial).
