# Chapter 46 drills — Antineoplastic Drugs Part 2: Cell Cycle–Nonspecific and Miscellaneous Drugs

Authored from `sources/Ch46_Fact_Bank.md` (Lilley, Collins & Snyder, 10th ed.).

Blueprint weight is **1 question of 121 (0.8%), depth `light`** — the lowest in
the course. That earns **16 drills across 4 topics**, mid-band for `light`
(12–20). The fact bank is already a bare sheet, and even so it carries more
drillable material than 16 items can hold, so this set is named terms and
flagged traps only.

Kept, in the fact bank's own priority order: every confusable pair it flags
(specific vs nonspecific, bifunctional vs polyfunctional, doxorubicin vs
bleomycin, cisplatin vs carboplatin, cisplatin nephrotoxicity vs
cyclophosphamide cystitis, irritant vs vesicant), the two priority items
(first action on extravasation, the antibiotic class to avoid with cisplatin),
and the four key terms from the KEY TERMS box.

Deliberately left undrilled at this weight: the entire hormonal group and its
subcategories, the miscellaneous drugs as individual agents (bevacizumab,
imatinib, mitotane, octreotide), the supportive drugs amifostine and
allopurinol, the bleomycin/doxorubicin digoxin interaction, the safe-handling
and certification box, and `nadir` with its 10–28 day estimate. Each is one
fact connecting to nothing else on the sheet, which is what a `light` chapter
gives up first. Doses, routes, and lab thresholds were already cut upstream by
the fact bank. Nothing here comes from outside that sheet — in particular
mesna, tumor lysis syndrome, mitotic inhibitors, and topoisomerase inhibitors
as a class are absent from the chapter and so absent from these drills, except
where the last two appear as intended wrong answers.

Topic: cell cycle-nonspecific antineoplastic classification
Subtopic: specific versus nonspecific action

Q: A patient starting cyclophosphamide says her sister's chemotherapy was described as working only while the cancer cells were in one particular part of their growth cycle, and asks how her own drug differs. Which explanation is accurate?
A) It has antineoplastic activity regardless of the phase of the cell cycle
B) It is active only during specific phases of the cell growth cycle
C) It is effective against any type of cancer rather than against one tumor type
D) It damages cancer cells while sparing normal cells
Answer: A
Rationale: Chapter 45 describes the antineoplastic drugs effective against cancer cells during specific phases in the cell growth cycle; in contrast, this chapter focuses on drugs that have antineoplastic activity regardless of the phase of the cell cycle (p. 726). Cyclophosphamide is one of the classic alkylating drugs in that second group.
Trap: (C) and (D) are the axis swaps that make "nonspecific" sound like breadth or safety, and they pull because both are things a patient would want to hear. The word describes the phase of the cell cycle only - not the tumor type, and not any selectivity for cancer cells over normal cells.

Q: The same patient's sister is receiving a drug her oncologist called cell cycle-specific. What does that label establish about the sister's drug?
A) It acts only while cells are in particular phases of the cell growth cycle
B) It acts on cancer cells no matter which phase of the cycle they are in
C) It is specific to her tumor type and would not be used for a different cancer
D) It targets only cancer cells and leaves normal dividing cells untouched
Answer: A
Rationale: The specific/nonspecific split is what divides these two chapters: Chapter 45's drugs are effective during specific phases in the cell growth cycle, while this chapter's drugs work regardless of phase (p. 726).
Trap: (B) is the mirror error of the previous item, and it is easy to slide into right after "nonspecific" has just been explained. (C) repeats the tumor-type swap in the opposite direction.

Q: A student concludes that every drug discussed in the chapter on cell cycle-nonspecific drugs must itself be cell cycle-nonspecific. Which drug shows that conclusion to be wrong?
A) Bleomycin, which the chapter flags as actually being a cell cycle-specific drug
B) Doxorubicin, which acts by intercalating into DNA
C) Cisplatin, which is a bifunctional alkylating drug
D) Busulfan, which is grouped with the miscellaneous alkylating drugs
Answer: A
Rationale: The chapter names "bleomycin (which is actually a cell cycle-specific drug)" (p. 729), and hydroxyurea likewise works in the S and G1 phases and appears here only because it is included as a miscellaneous drug. Chapter membership is not classification.
Trap: (D) is the reflex pick, because "miscellaneous" reads as "the exception" - but busulfan is an alkylating drug and nonspecific like the rest of its group; miscellaneous is only a subcategory heading. The chapter marks its real exception in a parenthesis around bleomycin.

Q: A nurse is grouping the drug classes covered in the chapter on cell cycle-nonspecific antineoplastics. Which classes does the chapter name as the two broad classes of cell cycle-nonspecific cancer drugs? (Select all that apply.)
A) Alkylating drugs
B) Cytotoxic antibiotics
C) Antimetabolites
D) Mitotic inhibitors
E) Topoisomerase inhibitors
Answer: A, B
Rationale: "There are two broad classes of cell cycle-nonspecific cancer drugs: alkylating drugs and cytotoxic antibiotics" (p. 726). The chapter's two other groupings, miscellaneous and hormonal drugs, are not cell-cycle-based classifications at all.
Trap: (E) is the strongest pull, because cytotoxic antibiotics genuinely do inhibit topoisomerase II - but "topoisomerase inhibitors" is a class heading from the cell cycle-specific chapter and appears nowhere here. (C) and (D) are the same borrowing from the previous chapter.

Topic: alkylating and cytotoxic antibiotic mechanisms
Subtopic: how each class damages DNA

Q: A patient receiving mechlorethamine asks how the drug actually kills the cancer cells. Which description matches the mechanism of an alkylating drug?
A) It alters the chemical structure of the cell's DNA so that abnormal chemical bonds form between adjacent DNA strands
B) It is inserted between the two strands of the DNA molecule and blocks DNA synthesis
C) It inhibits topoisomerase II and generates free radicals
D) It is inactive until enzymes in the liver convert it to an active form
Answer: A
Rationale: Alkylating drugs alter the chemical structure of the cells' DNA, so abnormal chemical bonds form between adjacent DNA strands, yielding defective nucleic acids and cell death (p. 727); alkylation itself is a chemical reaction in which an alkyl group is transferred from one molecule to another.
Trap: (B) and (C) both describe the cytotoxic antibiotics, and they pull because the two classes are cell cycle-nonspecific and are presented side by side. (D) is the prodrug fact, which is true of cyclophosphamide but is not a mechanism of cell kill.

Q: The same patient is later switched to doxorubicin and asks whether the new drug works the same way. Which mechanism belongs to the cytotoxic antibiotics?
A) Intercalation, in which the drug is inserted between the two strands of a DNA molecule and ultimately blocks DNA synthesis
B) Alkylation, in which alkyl groups are transferred to DNA so that abnormal bonds form between adjacent strands
C) Transfer of two reactive alkyl groups to two separate sites on the DNA molecule
D) Inhibition of the bcr-abl tyrosine kinase
Answer: A
Rationale: Cytotoxic antibiotics work by intercalation, "inserted between the two strands of a DNA molecule, ultimately blocking DNA synthesis"; they also inhibit the enzyme topoisomerase II, and many generate free radicals (p. 729).
Trap: (B) is the mirror error of the previous item and (C) restates it in bifunctional terms. The word "antibiotic" is the other pull here: these are natural substances produced by the mold Streptomyces that kill cancer cells, not anti-infectives, and inhibiting topoisomerase II does not make them topoisomerase inhibitors.

Subtopic: bifunctional versus polyfunctional

Q: A drug reference describes mechlorethamine as a bifunctional alkylating drug. What does the nurse understand about the molecule?
A) It has two reactive alkyl groups and can therefore alkylate at two sites on the DNA molecule
B) It can engage in several alkylation reactions per single molecule of drug
C) It must be activated in the body before it can alkylate anything
D) It is active during two different phases of the cell cycle
Answer: A
Rationale: Bifunctional refers to alkylating drugs composed of molecules that have two reactive alkyl groups and that are therefore able to alkylate at two sites on the DNA molecule (p. 726). Mechlorethamine and cisplatin are the chapter's bifunctional examples.
Trap: (B) is the polyfunctional definition, one prefix away. (D) is the level swap that reads "functional" as a cell-cycle claim; neither bifunctional nor polyfunctional says anything about phase, and these drugs are nonspecific.

Q: Cyclophosphamide is ordered, and the reference describes it as a polyfunctional alkylating drug and a prodrug. Which statement reflects both of those descriptions?
A) It can engage in several alkylation reactions per single molecule of drug, and it requires in vivo activation before it acts
B) It has two reactive alkyl groups that alkylate at two sites on DNA, and it is fully active as given
C) It alkylates at several sites, and the liver inactivates most of the dose before it reaches the tumor
D) It alkylates at several sites, and it acts during several phases of the cell cycle
Answer: A
Rationale: Cyclophosphamide is "a polyfunctional alkylating drug and is a prodrug requiring in vivo activation" (pp. 728-729), and polyfunctional means several alkylation reactions with cancer cell DNA per single molecule of drug.
Trap: (B) is the mirror error, applying mechlorethamine's bifunctional definition to cyclophosphamide. (C) inverts the prodrug relationship, which is the reflex error whenever the liver is mentioned: the liver activates this drug rather than destroying it.

Topic: antineoplastic drug-specific toxicities
Subtopic: doxorubicin versus bleomycin

Q: A patient is starting doxorubicin. Which assessment does the nurse prioritize for this drug's characteristic severe toxicity?
A) Cardiac function, since severe cases of cardiomyopathy are associated with large cumulative doses
B) Respiratory function, since the drug causes pulmonary fibrosis and pneumonitis
C) Renal function and hearing, since the drug is nephrotoxic and ototoxic
D) Bone marrow suppression only, since that is the dose-limiting effect the whole class shares
Answer: A
Rationale: Table 46.5 lists liver and cardiovascular toxicities for doxorubicin and idarubicin, and severe cases of cardiomyopathy are associated with large cumulative doses, which is why total dose is capped and dexrazoxane is used for protection (pp. 729-730).
Trap: (C) is cisplatin's profile in disguise, and it pulls because nephrotoxicity and ototoxicity are the most memorable toxicities on the sheet. (B) belongs to bleomycin, the other half of this pair.

Q: Another patient on the unit is receiving bleomycin. Which assessment is the priority for this drug?
A) Respiratory function, since bleomycin causes pulmonary fibrosis and pneumonitis
B) Cardiac function, since bleomycin causes cardiomyopathy at large cumulative doses
C) Hearing and renal function, since bleomycin is ototoxic and nephrotoxic
D) Bone marrow suppression, which is the dose-limiting toxicity bleomycin shares with the rest of its class
Answer: A
Rationale: Table 46.5 gives pulmonary fibrosis and pneumonitis for bleomycin, and the chapter states that cytotoxic antibiotics generally cause bone marrow suppression, with "the one exception is bleomycin, which instead causes pulmonary toxicity" (pp. 729-730).
Trap: (B) is the mirror error of the previous item; the cue is that "doxo" hits the cardiac muscle while "bleo" blows out the lungs. (D) is the class rule that hides this drug - it is true of the cytotoxic antibiotics generally, and bleomycin is the stated exception to it.

Subtopic: platinum drugs and urinary toxicities

Q: An order is changed from cisplatin to carboplatin. According to Table 46.2 and its footnote, which statements about carboplatin compared with cisplatin are accurate? (Select all that apply.)
A) Carboplatin causes less nephrotoxicity
B) Carboplatin causes less neurotoxicity
C) Carboplatin causes more bone marrow suppression
D) Carboplatin is the one of the two listed as causing ototoxicity
E) Carboplatin is classified as a cytotoxic antibiotic rather than an alkylating drug
Answer: A, B, C
Rationale: Table 46.2 gives nephrotoxicity, peripheral neuropathy, and ototoxicity for cisplatin and nephrotoxicity, neurotoxicity, and bone marrow suppression for carboplatin, with the footnote that carboplatin has less nephrotoxicity and neurotoxicity but more bone marrow suppression; ototoxicity is listed for cisplatin only. Both are miscellaneous alkylating drugs, and the chapter warns that medication errors, resulting in deaths, have occurred when cisplatin was confused for carboplatin (p. 728).
Trap: (D) is the reflex miss, because the two platinum drugs get filed as one profile and ototoxicity is the most memorable item in it; hearing loss belongs to cisplatin alone. (E) pulls only if the platinum drugs have been sorted into the wrong class, which is worth catching now, since this is the pair the chapter says has killed patients.

Q: A patient receiving cyclophosphamide reports the sudden onset of hematuria with bladder pain and discomfort. Which adverse effect does the nurse suspect?
A) Hemorrhagic cystitis, cyclophosphamide's characteristic severe adverse effect
B) Nephrotoxicity, the severe adverse effect of the platinum alkylating drugs
C) Tissue injury from extravasation at the infusion site
D) Bone marrow suppression approaching its lowest point
Answer: A
Rationale: Table 46.2 lists hemorrhagic cystitis as cyclophosphamide's severe adverse effect, and the chapter describes it as the sudden onset of hematuria with bladder pain and discomfort (p. 735); forcing fluids is the stated prevention.
Trap: (B) is the confusion this pair produces, and it is a fair one: both drugs are alkylators, both declare themselves through the urinary tract, and hydration is the stated prevention for both. Cisplatin injures the kidney, which shows up as rising creatinine and falling urine output; cyclophosphamide injures the bladder wall, which shows up as blood and pain.

Subtopic: overlapping toxicity in combined therapy

Q: A patient receiving cisplatin develops pneumonia, and the prescriber asks which class of antibiotic should be avoided. Which class does the nurse identify?
A) Aminoglycosides
B) Penicillins
C) Sulfonamides
D) Fluoroquinolones
Answer: A
Rationale: The stated rule is to avoid administering an alkylating drug with any other drug capable of causing similar toxicities, and the chapter's worked example is cisplatin plus an aminoglycoside, which produces additive nephrotoxic effects and can lead to renal failure (p. 728).
Trap: (B) is the reflex pick for pneumonia because it is the first antibiotic most students reach for, and the question feels like an antibiotic-selection item. It is not: the rule turns on which antibiotic shares cisplatin's toxicity, and the shared toxicity that matters is renal.

Topic: chemotherapy extravasation and vesicant safety
Subtopic: irritant versus vesicant

Q: A drug reference describes doxorubicin as a strong vesicant. What does that tell the nurse about the consequence if the drug leaks into the tissue surrounding the vein?
A) It causes cell death with necrosis and ulcerative properties, so massive tissue injury may result
B) It irritates the vein, and leakage results in significantly less damage than a vesicant would cause
C) The risk disappears once the drug is given through a central venous access device
D) The risk applies only to peripheral sites, because infiltration cannot occur with a central catheter
Answer: A
Rationale: A vesicant causes cell death with extravasation and necrosis with ulcerative properties and may lead to massive tissue injury, whereas extravasation of an irritant results in significantly less damage (p. 728). Doxorubicin is the chapter's named strong vesicant, and central venous access is needed for administration of vesicants.
Trap: (B) is the irritant definition, and it gets picked because both words describe something unpleasant happening at the site; the cue is that an irritant irritates the vein while a vesicant kills the tissue. (C) and (D) are the central-line swap: Box 46.1 states that infiltration may occur with any intravenous catheter, and it is the specific drug and its characteristics that pose the concern.

Subtopic: responding to extravasation

Q: Midway through an infusion of a vesicant antineoplastic, the nurse finds swelling and no blood return at the site. What is the first action?
A) Stop the infusion immediately and contact the prescriber, leaving the intravenous catheter in place
B) Remove the catheter at once so that no further drug leaks into the tissue
C) Apply cold compresses and elevate the extremity per institutional policy
D) Aspirate any residual drug and blood from the catheter before doing anything else
Answer: A
Rationale: The protocol is to stop the infusion immediately and contact the prescriber, leaving the intravenous catheter in place; aspirating residual drug and blood follows, then institutional policy on antidote, compresses, and elevation (p. 728).
Trap: (B) is the reflex error, since pulling out the source of harm feels like the fastest way to stop it - but the catheter is the route by which an antidote reaches the extravasated drug, so removing it forfeits the treatment. (D) is a genuine step in the sequence, just not the first one, which is what makes it the harder distractor.

Q: Carmustine has extravasated at a peripheral site. Which antidote does Table 46.3 pair with this drug?
A) Sodium bicarbonate, along with cold compresses
B) Sodium thiosulfate, along with cold compresses
C) Dexrazoxane, which protects against anthracycline cardiotoxicity
D) Amifostine, which reduces the renal toxicity associated with cisplatin
Answer: A
Rationale: Table 46.3 pairs carmustine with sodium bicarbonate and mechlorethamine with sodium thiosulfate, with cold compresses used for both (p. 728).
Trap: (B) is the pair swap, and it is the whole difficulty of this item: the two sodium antidotes are listed adjacently, both are given with cold compresses, and only the drug each belongs to distinguishes them. (C) and (D) are protective drugs given systemically against cardiac and renal toxicity, not extravasation antidotes.
