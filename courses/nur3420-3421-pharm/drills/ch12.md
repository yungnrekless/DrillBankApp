# Chapter 12 drills — Central Nervous System Depressants and Muscle Relaxants

Authored from `sources/Ch12_Fact_Bank.md` (Lilley, Collins & Snyder, 10th ed.).

Blueprint weight is **7 questions of 121 (5.8%), depth `standard`** — 5 items on
Exam 1 and 2 on the Final, the fifth-heaviest chapter in the course and tied
with Ch 42. A `standard` chapter earns 30 to 45 drills; this file carries **44**,
in the upper part of the band because the chapter's testable surface is four
separate drug classes plus a muscle relaxant section, and each class carries its
own antidote rule, controlled-substance schedule, and adverse-effect signature
that is drilled here or nowhere.

Topics follow the chapter's own spine: the dose continuum that defines the
terms, then sleep architecture, then the classes in the order the chapter
presents them — benzodiazepines, the nonbenzodiazepine and novel-mechanism
hypnotics, the barbiturates — then the muscle relaxants, then the administration
and teaching rules that carry the safety weight.

Every distinction the chapter states in both directions is drilled from both
ends: sedative and hypnotic, REM interference and REM rebound, REM rebound and
rebound insomnia, enzyme inhibition and enzyme induction, flumazenil and
naloxone, centrally acting and direct acting, and the two opposite intravenous
rate rules one page apart. Inverting those is the reflex error this chapter
produces, and a one-directional item lets a learner pass on a rule they hold
backwards.

Topic: sedative and hypnotic terminology
Subtopic: dose decides the label

Q: A patient receives a small dose of a CNS depressant before a painful dressing change and becomes calm and less irritable but stays awake and conversational. That evening the same drug is given at a much larger dose and the patient sleeps through the night. Which statement best explains what the nurse observed?
A) The drug acted as a sedative at the lower dose and as a hypnotic at the higher dose
B) The drug is a hypnotic, and the smaller dose simply failed to reach the sleep-inducing threshold of its class
C) The patient developed tolerance between the two doses
D) The evening dose produced a paradoxical reaction
Answer: A
Rationale: Sedatives have an inhibitory effect on the CNS to the degree that they reduce nervousness, excitability, and irritability without causing sleep, whereas hypnotics cause sleep. The Overview states the rule directly: "a sedative can become a hypnotic if it is given in large enough doses," which is why a sedative-hypnotic is defined as a drug that can act in the body either as a sedative or as a hypnotic (p. 184).
Trap: (B) treats the label as a fixed property of the molecule. The chapter says a drug is one or the other depending on the degree to which it inhibits the transmission of nerve impulses to the CNS, not because of what the package insert calls it.

Q: A nursing student charts that a newly ordered drug "is a sedative and therefore cannot cause sleep at any dose." Which correction should the nurse make?
A) The statement is correct; a sedative never induces sleep
B) Sedative and hypnotic name effects at different points on a dose continuum, and a sedative given in large enough doses becomes a hypnotic
C) Only barbiturates cross from sedation into sleep induction; benzodiazepines do not
D) The distinction depends on the route of administration, not the dose
Answer: B
Rationale: The chapter states that "any of these drugs can function along a continuum as a sedative and/or hypnotic and/or anxiolytic, depending on the dosage and patient sensitivity," and that hypnotics cause sleep and have "a much more potent effect" on the CNS (p. 184).
Trap: (A) is the mirror error of the previous item — it freezes the label to the molecule instead of to the dose. (C) invents a class restriction; the continuum statement is made about the sedative-hypnotics generally, benzodiazepines included.

Subtopic: therapeutic index and GABA

Q: A patient's family asks why the prescriber chose a benzodiazepine rather than a barbiturate for short-term sedation. Which fact about the barbiturates most directly supports that choice?
A) Barbiturates have a low therapeutic index, so the difference between the therapeutic and the toxic concentration is small
B) Barbiturates have a high therapeutic index, so much larger doses are required to achieve an effect
C) Barbiturates have no effect on GABA and are therefore less predictable
D) Barbiturates are not controlled substances and are therefore more easily diverted
Answer: A
Rationale: Therapeutic index is the ratio between the toxic and therapeutic concentrations of a drug; if the index is low, the difference between the two is small and use of the drug is more hazardous (p. 183). The chapter applies this to the barbiturates by name — they "are physiologically habit forming and have a low therapeutic index" (p. 188) — and says they fell out of use because of "the favorable safety profile and proven efficacy of the benzodiazepines."
Trap: (B) inverts the index, and the inversion is intuitive because "high" sounds like high risk. A high index means a wide, safe gap. (D) is false twice over: the barbiturates are scheduled C-II, C-III, or C-IV depending on the drug.

Q: A drug ordered for a patient is described as potentiating the action of gamma-aminobutyric acid in the brain. Which effect should the nurse anticipate?
A) Increased CNS stimulation, because gamma-aminobutyric acid is excitatory
B) Increased CNS depression, because gamma-aminobutyric acid is the primary inhibitory neurotransmitter in the brain
C) No CNS effect, because gamma-aminobutyric acid acts only at the neuromuscular junction
D) Analgesia without any sedation
Answer: B
Rationale: GABA is the primary inhibitory neurotransmitter found in the brain and is the key compound affected by sedative, anxiolytic, psychotropic, and muscle-relaxing medications (p. 183). Barbiturates potentiate the action of GABA (p. 189), and benzodiazepines are thought to bind GABA receptors or adjacent receptors and, like GABA, inhibit stimulation of the brain (p. 185).
Trap: (A) inverts the neurotransmitter's role, and that single inversion unravels every mechanism statement in the chapter. GABA is inhibitory, so potentiating it deepens depression rather than producing stimulation.

Topic: sleep architecture and rem disruption
Subtopic: rem interference versus rem rebound

Q: A 68-year-old woman has taken alprazolam almost every night for a week to help her fall asleep. She tells the nurse, "I am sleeping very well, but I'm so tired during the day. I don't understand how I can get such good sleep and still feel tired." Which explanation should the nurse give?
A) The drug is reducing her cumulative REM sleep time, and REM sleep supplies part of the restfulness of sleep
B) She is experiencing REM rebound from the alprazolam
C) One week is too short for the drug to have taken effect
D) The daytime fatigue indicates that the alprazolam dose is too low
Answer: A
Rationale: "Prolonged sedative-hypnotic use may reduce the cumulative amount of REM sleep; this is known as REM interference. This can result in daytime fatigue because REM sleep provides a certain component of the 'restfulness' of sleep" (p. 184). Sleep quantity is preserved while sleep quality is degraded, which is exactly what she describes.
Trap: (B) is the reflex pick because rebound is the other REM word in the key-term box. Rebound happens after the drug is stopped; she is still taking it nightly, so the phenomenon in play is the one that happens while on the drug.

Q: A patient who has taken a sedative-hypnotic nightly for a month stops it abruptly and then reports several nights of unusually frequent, vivid dreams and nightmares. Which phenomenon is this?
A) REM interference
B) REM rebound
C) Paradoxical excitement
D) Rebound insomnia
Answer: B
Rationale: REM rebound is excessive REM sleep following discontinuation of a sleep-altering drug (p. 183). With the barbiturates the chapter is explicit that during rebound "the proportion of REM sleep is increased and nightmares often ensue" (p. 189).
Trap: (A) is the mirror error of the previous item. Interference happens while the patient is on the drug and yields less REM with daytime fatigue; rebound happens after stopping and yields more REM with vivid dreams. (D) is the neighboring withdrawal phenomenon, but it names too much wakefulness rather than too much dreaming.

Q: A patient stops a benzodiazepine abruptly and reports that she now lies awake far longer than she ever did before she started the drug. Which phenomenon should the nurse document, and what prevents it?
A) REM rebound, prevented by taking the drug earlier in the evening
B) Rebound insomnia, prevented by a gradual weaning-off period
C) Paradoxical excitement, prevented by increasing the dose
D) A hangover effect, prevented by reducing the evening dose
Answer: B
Rationale: Rebound insomnia is greater insomnia than pretreatment and occurs with abrupt discontinuation (p. 186). "Gradual weaning-off periods are recommended for benzodiazepines and all CNS depressants" (p. 194), and patient teaching instructs patients not to abruptly discontinue these drugs in order to avoid rebound insomnia.
Trap: (A) confuses the two rebound phenomena. Both follow withdrawal, which is why the words trade places so easily, but REM rebound is too much dreaming and rebound insomnia is too much wakefulness.

Subtopic: the stages of sleep

Q: A parent reports that their school-age child gets out of bed and walks around the house while still asleep and occasionally wets the bed. Which stage of sleep does Table 12.1 assign these events to?
A) Non-REM stage 1
B) Non-REM stage 2
C) Non-REM stage 4
D) REM sleep
Answer: C
Rationale: Table 12.1 assigns sleepwalking and bedwetting to non-REM stage 4, together with being very difficult to wake and being very groggy if awakened. Dreaming also occurs in stage 4, "especially about daily events."
Trap: (D) pulls because REM is the stage students associate with everything that happens during dreaming. In Table 12.1 the REM row owns rapid eye movements, vivid dreams, and irregular breathing — not sleepwalking or bedwetting.

Q: A nurse is teaching a patient about the characteristics of REM sleep. Which findings belong to REM sleep as this chapter describes it? Select all that apply.
A) Rapid movement of the eyes
B) Vivid dreams
C) Irregular breathing
D) Slow, side-to-side eye movements
E) The highest arousal threshold of any stage
Answer: A, B, C
Rationale: The key term defines REM sleep by three characteristics named together — rapid movement of the eyes, vivid dreams, and irregular breathing (p. 183) — and the REM row of Table 12.1 repeats all three. Naming only vivid dreams is half the term.
Trap: (D) is the stage 1 finding, where eye movement is slow and side to side; any mention of eye movement feels like REM, which is what makes it pull. (E) belongs to delta sleep, stages 3 and 4 combined, where the arousal threshold is highest.

Topic: benzodiazepine pharmacology and adverse effects
Subtopic: site of action and contraindications

Q: A patient asks how her newly prescribed benzodiazepine works in the brain. Which description matches the chapter?
A) It depresses the brainstem reticular formation, reducing nerve impulses traveling to the cerebral cortex
B) It depresses activity in the hypothalamic, thalamic, and limbic systems of the brain
C) It acts directly on the excitation-contraction coupling of muscle fibers
D) It agonizes melatonin receptors and regulates circadian rhythm
Answer: B
Rationale: Benzodiazepines act on the hypothalamic, thalamic, and limbic systems of the brain; their receptor sites are thought to be either GABA receptors or other adjacent receptors, and like GABA they inhibit stimulation of the brain (p. 185).
Trap: (A) is the barbiturate mechanism — the brainstem reticular formation, with reduced nerve impulses traveling to the cerebral cortex (p. 189). Both classes are CNS depressants that involve GABA, so the anatomic sites are the only thing separating them and they trade places readily.

Q: A prescriber is considering a benzodiazepine for a patient. Which findings in the history are stated contraindications in this chapter? Select all that apply.
A) Known drug allergy to the benzodiazepines
B) Narrow-angle glaucoma
C) Pregnancy
D) A history of low back strain with muscle spasm
E) Age older than 65 years
Answer: A, B, C
Rationale: The contraindications to benzodiazepines are known drug allergy, narrow-angle glaucoma, and pregnancy (p. 185).
Trap: (E) pulls because older adults get an entire caution paragraph — increased sensitivity, significant fall hazard, lowest effective dose — but caution is not contraindication. (D) names an indication rather than a contraindication, since skeletal muscle relaxation is on the benzodiazepine indication list.

Subtopic: adverse effects in the older adult

Q: A 79-year-old is started on a benzodiazepine for sleep. Which dosing principle does the chapter attach specifically to benzodiazepines in older adults?
A) Reduce the usual adult dose by half whenever possible
B) Use the lowest effective dose, because of the significant fall hazard
C) Give the full adult dose but restrict it to alternate nights
D) Keep the dose unchanged and double the interval between doses
Answer: B
Rationale: Benzodiazepines pose "a significant fall hazard in older adults, and the lowest effective dose must be used" (p. 185).
Trap: (A) is the barbiturate rule, where "the usual dose is reduced by half whenever possible" (p. 189). Two classes, two different instructions, and the half-dose figure belongs to the barbiturates only.

Q: An older adult is given a benzodiazepine at bedtime to help her sleep and instead stays awake most of the night, watching television and reading in bed. How should the nurse describe this response?
A) An allergic reaction
B) A teratogenic effect
C) A paradoxical reaction
D) An idiosyncratic response to the trade formulation
Answer: C
Rationale: The chapter lists "paradoxical excitement or nervousness" among the commonly reported benzodiazepine adverse effects (p. 185), and repeats the pattern as "paradoxical restlessness or excitement" for barbiturates (p. 189) and "paradoxical excitement in children" (p. 193). The drug produced the opposite of its intended effect.
Trap: (A) is the reflex pick because any unexpected drug response reads as allergy. Nothing here describes urticaria, angioedema, rash, or the other hypersensitivity findings the chapter names.

Q: A patient started on a benzodiazepine for insomnia complains of drowsiness the morning after each dose. What is this effect, and whom does the chapter say it affects most?
A) A hangover effect, more problematic in older adults and in patients with altered renal and hepatic function
B) REM rebound, which resolves once the drug reaches steady state
C) An early sign of overdose requiring flumazenil
D) Rebound insomnia, which requires an increased dose
Answer: A
Rationale: The chapter names a "hangover" effect and gives daytime sleepiness as its example (p. 186); patient teaching adds that hangover effects are "more problematic in older adults or in patients with altered renal and hepatic function" (p. 196). It also occurs less frequently with benzodiazepines and nonbenzodiazepines than with barbiturates.
Trap: (C) escalates a listed adverse effect into an emergency. Flumazenil is for oral overdose or excessive intravenous sedation, not for morning grogginess in a patient taking a prescribed dose.

Topic: benzodiazepine overdose and flumazenil reversal
Subtopic: matching the antidote to the class

Q: A patient is brought to the emergency department after taking an overdose of an oral benzodiazepine. He is drowsy but responsive, and his vital signs are stable. Which intervention should the nurse anticipate?
A) Hemodialysis
B) Administration of flumazenil
C) Administration of naloxone
D) Immediate intubation and mechanical ventilation
Answer: B
Rationale: Flumazenil is the benzodiazepine antidote and can be used to acutely reverse the sedative effects of benzodiazepines; it antagonizes their action on the CNS by directly competing with the benzodiazepine for binding at the receptors, and it is used in cases of oral overdose or excessive intravenous sedation (p. 186).
Trap: (D) tempts because respiratory failure is the feared outcome of any CNS depressant, but the chapter states that benzodiazepine overdose alone rarely results in hypotension and respiratory depression, and this patient is still responsive.

Q: The patient in the next bed took an overdose of an opioid analgesic and is unresponsive with a respiratory rate of 6 breaths/min. Which reversal drug applies to him?
A) Flumazenil
B) Naloxone
C) Activated charcoal
D) Procaine
Answer: B
Rationale: The chapter pairs the two antidotes deliberately: flumazenil reverses the benzodiazepines by competing for benzodiazepine receptor binding, while naloxone is the opioid antagonist carried in Chapter 10. Flumazenil has no action at opioid receptors.
Trap: (A) is the mirror error of the previous item, and it is the most common error in this chapter because both drugs are competitive antagonists given for CNS depressant overdose. Match the antidote to the drug class, not to the symptom.

Subtopic: the limits of reversal

Q: Flumazenil is given to a patient who overdosed on a long-acting benzodiazepine, and she wakes up and answers questions appropriately. What should the nurse do next?
A) Discontinue monitoring, since the antagonist has reversed the drug
B) Continue close monitoring, because flumazenil may wear off before the benzodiazepine does and the patient may become sedated again
C) Give naloxone as well, to cover the possibility of a co-ingestion
D) Withhold any further flumazenil permanently, since the maximum dose has been reached
Answer: B
Rationale: The note to Table 12.3 states that flumazenil has a relatively short half-life and a duration of effect of 1 to 4 hours, so if it is used to reverse a long-acting benzodiazepine, "the dose of the reversal drug may wear off and the patient may become sedated again, requiring more flumazenil."
Trap: (A) treats a successful reversal as the end of the intervention, which is exactly what the table's own note defeats. The antidote is shorter acting than the drug it is reversing.

Q: Flumazenil is ordered for two purposes on the same unit — reversal of moderate sedation in one patient and suspected benzodiazepine overdose in another. Which statement about the maximum cumulative doses in Table 12.3 is correct?
A) Both regimens stop at a total of 1 mg
B) Sedation reversal stops at 1 mg, and suspected overdose may go up to 3 mg
C) Sedation reversal stops at 3 mg, and suspected overdose stops at 1 mg
D) Neither regimen has a stated ceiling
Answer: B
Rationale: Table 12.3 gives 0.2 mg intravenously over 15 seconds for reversal of moderate sedation or general anesthesia, repeated at 60-second intervals up to four additional times, to a maximum total dose of 1 mg. For suspected benzodiazepine overdose the regimen starts at 0.2 mg over 30 seconds and escalates through 0.3 mg and then 0.5 mg doses at 1-minute intervals to a cumulative dose of 3 mg.
Trap: (C) swaps the two ceilings, and the swap is easy because both regimens open with the same 0.2 mg dose. The difference between the 1 mg and the 3 mg ceiling is the cleanest discriminator between the two indications.

Topic: sedative-hypnotic interactions and black box warnings
Subtopic: enzyme inhibition versus enzyme induction

Q: A patient taking a benzodiazepine mentions that she drinks a large glass of grapefruit juice every morning. What should the nurse anticipate?
A) Decreased benzodiazepine metabolism, with prolonged effect and possible toxicity
B) Increased benzodiazepine metabolism, with loss of therapeutic effect
C) No interaction, since grapefruit affects only the oral anticoagulants
D) An additive CNS depressant effect contributed by the juice itself
Answer: A
Rationale: Grapefruit and grapefruit juice alter drug metabolism by inhibition of the cytochrome P-450 system and can result in prolonged effect, increased effect, and toxicity (p. 186). Table 12.4 groups grapefruit juice with the azole antifungals, verapamil, diltiazem, protease inhibitors, and macrolide antibiotics under decreased benzodiazepine metabolism and prolonged benzodiazepine action.
Trap: (B) runs the interaction backwards. Inhibition slows metabolism, so more drug stays in the body longer; only induction produces the loss of effect described there.

Q: A patient stabilized on a benzodiazepine is started on rifampin for tuberculosis, and within days he reports that the benzodiazepine no longer seems to work. What explains this?
A) Rifampin inhibits benzodiazepine metabolism, causing accumulation and tolerance
B) Rifampin increases benzodiazepine metabolism, decreasing benzodiazepine effects
C) Rifampin displaces the benzodiazepine from its receptors, acting as an antagonist
D) Rifampin adds to CNS depression, masking the benzodiazepine effect
Answer: B
Rationale: Table 12.4 lists rifampin with increased metabolism and decreased benzodiazepine effects. Rifampin is the one row of that table that runs against all the others, which is why it is printed there; the chapter also names rifampin as reducing the efficacy of ramelteon by speeding its metabolism (p. 188).
Trap: (A) is the mirror error of the previous item — it applies the grapefruit and azole direction to an inducer. Inducers speed metabolism and cause treatment failure; inhibitors slow it and cause toxicity.

Q: A patient takes a benzodiazepine nightly. According to this chapter, which of the following would prolong or increase the benzodiazepine's effect? Select all that apply.
A) An azole antifungal
B) Grapefruit juice
C) Rifampin
D) An opioid analgesic
E) Verapamil
Answer: A, B, D, E
Rationale: Table 12.4 assigns decreased benzodiazepine metabolism and prolonged benzodiazepine action to the azole antifungals, verapamil, diltiazem, protease inhibitors, macrolide antibiotics, and grapefruit juice, and assigns additive effects with increased CNS depression to the CNS depressants, which the chapter exemplifies with alcohol, opioids, and muscle relaxants.
Trap: (C) is the single row in Table 12.4 that goes the other way — increased metabolism and decreased benzodiazepine effects. A learner who sorts the whole table into one bucket keeps it.

Subtopic: the three black box warnings

Q: A prescriber orders an opioid analgesic and a benzodiazepine together for a postoperative patient. Which statement reflects the 2016 FDA black box warning?
A) The combination is absolutely prohibited in every patient
B) The combination should be used only if no other alternatives are available, because of extreme sleepiness, respiratory depression, coma, and death
C) The warning applies only when the opioid is given intravenously
D) The warning concerns complex sleep behaviors such as sleepwalking and sleep-driving
Answer: B
Rationale: The 2016 warning covers all opioids and all benzodiazepines regarding the risk of combined use, and states that they should be used "only if no other alternatives are available," with risks including extreme sleepiness, respiratory depression, coma, and death (p. 186). The assessment section operationalizes it by watching for severe decreases in blood pressure, respiratory rate, reflexes, and level of consciousness.
Trap: (D) is the 2019 warning, which belongs to the nonbenzodiazepines. It is the most memorable of the three, so it gets attached to the wrong box; three warnings, three years, three different scopes.

Q: A patient asks which drugs carry the FDA warning about complex sleep behaviors, including sleepwalking, sleep-driving, and engaging in other activities while not fully awake. Which answer is correct?
A) The entire benzodiazepine class
B) The nonbenzodiazepines zolpidem, eszopiclone, and zaleplon
C) All barbiturates
D) The orexin receptor antagonists
Answer: B
Rationale: The 2019 warning names complex sleep behaviors for the nonbenzodiazepines zolpidem, eszopiclone, and zaleplon (p. 184). The late-2020 warning is the one covering the entire benzodiazepine class, and it concerns the risk for abuse, misuse, addiction, physical dependence, and withdrawal.
Trap: (A) crosses the years. It is the correct scope for the 2020 dependence warning and the wrong scope for the 2019 sleep-behavior warning, and the two collapse together because they were issued about a year apart.

Topic: nonbenzodiazepine and novel-mechanism hypnotics
Subtopic: which drugs are actually benzodiazepines

Q: Table 12.2 lists temazepam, triazolam, zolpidem, and eszopiclone together in one table of sedative-hypnotics, and a student concludes that all four are benzodiazepines. Which correction is accurate?
A) All four are benzodiazepines, since they appear in the same table and share schedule IV status
B) Zolpidem and eszopiclone are classified as miscellaneous hypnotic drugs that share many characteristics with the benzodiazepines but are chemically distinct from them
C) Only temazepam is a benzodiazepine; triazolam is a barbiturate
D) Classification depends on the duration column, not on the drug
Answer: B
Rationale: The footnote to Table 12.2 states that these drugs "share many characteristics with the benzodiazepines but are classified as miscellaneous hypnotic drugs," and the chapter describes zolpidem, eszopiclone, and zaleplon as functioning much like benzodiazepines but chemically distinct from them (pp. 184 and 185). The Clinical Pearl supplies the naming cue: many benzodiazepines end in "pam."
Trap: (A) is exactly the error the chapter's own Key Points make when they list eszopiclone, ramelteon, zaleplon, and zolpidem as short-acting benzodiazepines. The rows and the footnote have to be read together, and the shared schedule IV status makes the merge feel safe.

Q: A woman is discharged after a hysterectomy with a new prescription for zolpidem 10 mg at bedtime. What is the nurse's most appropriate action?
A) Review the adverse effects with her and reinforce the bedtime timing
B) Suggest a glass of wine at bedtime if the drug alone is not effective
C) Contact the prescriber to question the dose
D) Help her locate a pharmacy that stocks the drug
Answer: C
Rationale: The FDA now recommends doses of 5 mg as a maximum dose for women, or 5 to 10 mg for men, because of the risk for next-morning impairment (p. 188). A 10 mg dose for a female patient is twice the recommended maximum.
Trap: (A) is the safe-looking choice, and it is wrong precisely because teaching about a dose the FDA capped at half leaves the hazard in place. (B) fails on a separate rule: alcohol is an additive CNS depressant, and Box 12.1 says it fragments sleep rather than producing it.

Subtopic: melatonin agonists and orexin antagonists

Q: A patient with a history of alcohol and sedative misuse needs help falling asleep. Which drug in this chapter is described as an option for patients likely to abuse CNS depressants, and why?
A) Temazepam, because it is intermediate acting and therefore less reinforcing
B) Ramelteon, because it is not a controlled substance, is technically not a CNS depressant, and does not lead to withdrawal symptoms
C) Suvorexant, because the orexin receptor antagonists are not scheduled
D) Phenobarbital, because its long half-life smooths withdrawal
Answer: B
Rationale: Ramelteon is structurally similar to melatonin and works as an agonist at melatonin receptors in the CNS; it is "technically not a CNS depressant" and is "not classified as a controlled substance because of its lack of observed dependency risk" (p. 188). The nursing sections add that it does not carry the potential for abuse or dependence and does not lead to withdrawal symptoms, so it may be an option for patients likely to abuse CNS depressants.
Trap: (C) is the closest wrong answer, because suvorexant is also a newer non-benzodiazepine hypnotic with an unfamiliar mechanism. Suvorexant is a schedule IV drug; ramelteon is the only unscheduled hypnotic in this chapter.

Q: Suvorexant is ordered for a patient with insomnia. Which explanation of its mechanism is accurate?
A) It blocks orexin receptors, opposing hypothalamic neuropeptides that promote wakefulness
B) It agonizes melatonin receptors and resets the circadian rhythm
C) It potentiates GABA at the brainstem reticular formation
D) It decreases calcium release from the sarcoplasmic reticulum of muscle fibers
Answer: A
Rationale: Suvorexant is the first drug in a new class called the orexin receptor antagonists. Orexin A and orexin B are hypothalamic neuropeptides that play a key role in promoting wakefulness and regulating the sleep-wake cycle, and orexin antagonists compete with the physiologic effects of orexin (p. 188).
Trap: (B) is the ramelteon mechanism, and the two are the chapter's easiest swap because both are newer hypnotics that do not work through GABA. Suvorexant blocks a wake-promoting peptide; ramelteon agonizes a sleep-promoting receptor.

Q: A patient is starting suvorexant. Which statements should the nurse include in the teaching? Select all that apply.
A) Take the drug about 30 minutes before bedtime
B) Take the drug 2 hours before bedtime, because of its slow onset
C) Be cautious about daytime somnolence and unconscious nighttime behaviors
D) This drug is less likely than other hypnotics to interact with alcohol
E) The drug is available over the counter if a refill runs out
Answer: A, C
Rationale: Suvorexant's profile gives a 30-minute onset, and the chapter's dosing rule is to match the teaching time to the onset column, since a drug with an onset of 30 to 60 minutes is dosed 60 minutes before bedtime (p. 194). The safety concerns regarding daytime somnolence and unconscious nighttime behaviors are stated verbatim (pp. 188 and 196).
Trap: (E) is defeated by one phrase in the profile: suvorexant is a schedule IV drug, and a scheduled drug is never sold over the counter. (B) fabricates a pharmacokinetic that contradicts the 30-minute onset printed in that same profile.

Topic: barbiturate pharmacology and toxicity
Subtopic: site of action, duration groups, and schedules

Q: A patient asks how phenobarbital differs from a benzodiazepine in where it acts. Which description fits the barbiturates?
A) They act primarily on the brainstem in an area called the reticular formation, reducing nerve impulses traveling to the cerebral cortex
B) They act on the hypothalamic, thalamic, and limbic systems
C) They act at the neuromuscular junction
D) They antagonize orexin receptors in the hypothalamus
Answer: A
Rationale: Barbiturates are CNS depressants that act primarily on the brainstem in an area called the reticular formation, and they work by reducing the nerve impulses traveling to the cerebral cortex; they also potentiate the action of the inhibitory neurotransmitter GABA and raise the seizure threshold (p. 189).
Trap: (B) is the benzodiazepine site. Both classes are CNS depressants that involve GABA, so the anatomic site is the only thing separating them and it is the detail most often traded between them.

Q: A patient is scheduled for a short surgical procedure requiring anesthesia induction, and the neurosurgical team also wants intracranial pressure reduced. Which barbiturate duration group does Table 12.5 assign these purposes to?
A) Ultrashort acting, such as methohexital and thiopental
B) Short acting, such as pentobarbital and secobarbital
C) Intermediate acting, such as butabarbital
D) Long acting, such as phenobarbital and mephobarbital
Answer: A
Rationale: The ultrashort-acting barbiturates methohexital and thiopental are used for anesthesia for short surgical procedures, anesthesia induction, control of convulsions, and reduction of intracranial pressure in neurosurgical patients (p. 189). The long-acting group is used for epileptic seizure prophylaxis, and the short- and intermediate-acting groups carry the identical printed indication of sedation and control of convulsive conditions.
Trap: (D) picks the group whose drug names are most familiar, since phenobarbital is the prototype. Because the short and intermediate groups share word-for-word identical indications, any item separating the four tiers must be answered on the drug names rather than on the stated purpose.

Q: A nurse assumes that the shorter a barbiturate's duration of action, the more restrictive its controlled-substance schedule will be. Which fact from Table 12.7 defeats that assumption?
A) Phenobarbital is long acting and is schedule C-II
B) Thiopental is ultrashort acting but only schedule C-III, and methohexital is ultrashort acting but only schedule C-IV
C) Butabarbital is intermediate acting and is unscheduled
D) All barbiturates share a single schedule, the same one as the benzodiazepines
Answer: B
Rationale: Table 12.7 places pentobarbital and secobarbital, both short acting, at schedule C-II, the tightest in the chapter, while the two ultrashort-acting drugs sit lower — thiopental at C-III and methohexital at C-IV. Phenobarbital, the long-acting prototype, is C-IV, the same schedule as the benzodiazepines.
Trap: (A) inverts phenobarbital's schedule, which is C-IV rather than C-II. Tables 12.5 and 12.7 have to be read as independent lists, because the intuitive duration-tracks-schedule rule fails on two of the seven drugs.

Subtopic: enzyme induction and its victims

Q: A woman taking oral contraceptives is started on phenobarbital. What should the nurse teach her?
A) The contraceptive will be more effective, so its dose may be reduced
B) Phenobarbital accelerates the metabolism of the contraceptive, so she should use an additional method of contraception as a backup
C) Phenobarbital has no meaningful effect on oral contraceptives
D) She should stop the contraceptive entirely, since the combination causes clot formation
Answer: B
Rationale: Barbiturates as a class are notorious enzyme inducers: they increase the activity of hepatic microsomal or cytochrome P-450 enzymes, causing many drugs to be metabolized more quickly, which usually shortens their duration of action (pp. 189 and 190). With oral contraceptives the printed result is accelerated metabolism of the contraceptive drug and possible unintended pregnancy, and the chapter advises an additional method of contraception as a backup.
Trap: (D) borrows the clot-formation outcome, which belongs to the anticoagulant row, and attaches it to the wrong drug. Induction lowers the other drug's level, so the victim drug fails at its own job: the anticoagulant stops preventing clots and the contraceptive stops preventing pregnancy.

Q: A patient stabilized on a barbiturate is also taking a monoamine oxidase inhibitor. How does this interaction differ from the barbiturate's other listed interactions?
A) It causes the barbiturate itself to have prolonged effects, rather than causing the other drug to fail
B) It causes accelerated metabolism of the monoamine oxidase inhibitor
C) It produces purely additive CNS depression, exactly as alcohol does
D) It has no clinical significance and requires no monitoring
Answer: A
Rationale: The interaction table gives monoamine oxidase inhibitors the result of prolonged barbiturate effects. Every other listed interaction is either additive CNS depression, as with alcohol, antihistamines, benzodiazepines, opioids, and tranquilizers, or induction-driven failure of the other drug, as with the anticoagulants and the oral contraceptives (p. 190).
Trap: (B) is the mirror error of the previous item — it applies the induction direction, in which the other drug loses effect, to the one row where the arrow points back at the barbiturate.

Subtopic: barbiturate overdose

Q: A patient is admitted after a phenobarbital overdose. Which measures does this chapter identify as part of management? Select all that apply.
A) Maintenance of an adequate airway, assisted ventilation, and oxygen administration if needed
B) Alkalinization of the urine, raising the pH
C) Forced diuresis using a diuretic such as furosemide
D) Administration of flumazenil as the specific antidote
E) Activated charcoal, which recent clinical data support as improving outcome
Answer: A, B, C
Rationale: Barbiturate overdose treatment is mainly symptomatic and supportive, with the mainstays being maintenance of an adequate airway, assisted ventilation, and oxygen administration if needed, along with fluid and pressor support. Because phenobarbital and mephobarbital are relatively acidic, alkalinizing the urine keeps the drug in the urine and prevents it from being resorbed back into the circulation, and alkalinization along with forced diuresis using diuretics such as furosemide can hasten elimination (p. 189).
Trap: (D) offers the benzodiazepine antidote for a barbiturate, which trades on both drugs being CNS depressants; the barbiturates have no antidote. (E) is named and then dismissed in the same sentence of the chapter, which states that recent clinical data do not support charcoal because no improvement in clinical outcome has been shown.

Topic: skeletal muscle relaxant therapy
Subtopic: centrally acting versus direct acting

Q: A patient with multiple sclerosis is prescribed baclofen for spasticity, and a family member asks how the drug works on the muscles. Which explanation is accurate?
A) It acts directly on the muscle fiber by decreasing calcium release from the sarcoplasmic reticulum
B) Its beneficial effects are thought to come from its sedative effects rather than from direct muscle relaxation, because it acts in the CNS
C) It blocks transmission at the muscle-nerve junction
D) It increases the force of muscle contraction to overcome spasm
Answer: B
Rationale: Most muscle relaxants are centrally acting because their site of action is the CNS — the brainstem, thalamus, and basal ganglia, and also the spinal cord. Their beneficial effects are thought to come from their sedative effects rather than from direct muscle relaxation, and they have no direct effects on muscles, nerve conduction, or muscle-nerve junctions (p. 191).
Trap: (A) is dantrolene's mechanism, the one direct-acting drug in the chapter. Because every drug in the section is called a muscle relaxant, crediting a centrally acting agent with direct muscle action is the reflex error.

Q: A patient develops malignant hyperthermia during general anesthesia. Which muscle relaxant is indicated, and why is it the only one that fits?
A) Baclofen, because it can be delivered by an implantable pump
B) Cyclobenzaprine, because it is the most commonly used drug in the class
C) Dantrolene, because it acts directly on the excitation-contraction coupling of muscle fibers rather than at the level of the CNS
D) Diazepam, because it is a centrally acting skeletal muscle relaxant
Answer: C
Rationale: Only one compound, dantrolene, acts directly on skeletal muscle; it works on the excitation-contraction coupling of muscle fibers and not at the level of the CNS, by decreasing the amount of calcium released from storage sites in the sarcoplasmic reticula of muscle fibers. Intravenous dantrolene is used for the management of skeletal muscle spasms that accompany the crisis condition known as malignant hyperthermia (p. 191).
Trap: (A) is the mirror error of the previous item — it offers a centrally acting drug for a crisis occurring in the muscle fiber itself. Baclofen's pump and test dose are real facts attached to the wrong problem.

Subtopic: the two profiled relaxants and their adverse effects

Q: Two centrally acting muscle relaxants are on a unit's formulary. Which statement correctly distinguishes them?
A) Baclofen is available with an implantable pump requiring an initial test dose and relieves hiccups; cyclobenzaprine is related to the tricyclic antidepressants and commonly causes marked sedation
B) Cyclobenzaprine is the drug given by implantable pump; baclofen is the one related to the tricyclic antidepressants
C) Baclofen acts directly on muscle fibers, while cyclobenzaprine acts centrally
D) Only cyclobenzaprine is listed for spasticity
Answer: A
Rationale: Baclofen's injectable form is for use with an implantable baclofen pump device, and a test dose must be administered initially to test for a positive response; baclofen has also been shown to be effective in relieving hiccups (pp. 191 and 192). Cyclobenzaprine is structurally and pharmacologically related to the tricyclic antidepressants, is the most commonly used drug in this class to reduce spasms after musculoskeletal injuries, and very commonly produces marked sedation (p. 192).
Trap: (B) swaps the two drugs' signature facts, which is easy because both are centrally acting, both are dosed three times daily, and the dosages table lists spasticity for both.

Q: A patient receiving a skeletal muscle relaxant is being monitored for adverse effects. Which is the priority concern?
A) Hypertension
B) Peripheral edema
C) Blurred vision
D) CNS depression
Answer: D
Rationale: The adverse effects of the muscle relaxants are an extension of their effects on the CNS and skeletal muscles, and include generalized CNS depression manifested as sedation, somnolence, ataxia, and respiratory and cardiovascular depression (p. 191). Implementation escalates this: frequent monitoring of airway, breathing, and circulation is warranted, and early identification of toxicity is critical (p. 194).
Trap: (A) has the vital-sign direction backwards. The chapter lists hypotension among the less common adverse effects, not hypertension, so the option is inverted rather than merely unlisted.

Q: A patient takes the first dose of a newly prescribed muscle relaxant at home. Which teaching addresses the chapter's stated window of greatest risk?
A) The greatest risk for hypotension is usually within 1 hour of dosing, so change positions purposefully and slowly and ambulate cautiously
B) The greatest risk for hypotension occurs 8 to 12 hours after dosing, so avoid all morning activity
C) There is no hypotension risk with the centrally acting agents
D) Early dizziness and drowsiness mean the drug is failing and the dose should be doubled
Answer: A
Rationale: "The greatest risk for hypotension... is usually within 1 hour of dosing, so caution must be taken with activity" (p. 194); the patient is taught to change positions purposefully and slowly to prevent syncope or dizziness and to ambulate cautiously.
Trap: (D) misreads the chapter's reassurance about early effects. Euphoria, lightheadedness, dizziness, drowsiness, fatigue, confusion, and muscle weakness are common early in treatment and are generally short lived because patients grow tolerant to them — tolerance to the adverse effects, not loss of benefit, and never grounds for increasing the dose.

Topic: cns depressant administration and sleep hygiene teaching
Subtopic: the two intravenous rate rules

Q: A nurse prepares to give intravenous diazepam. Which action is correct?
A) Dilute the drug in 50 mL of normal saline and infuse it over 30 minutes
B) Inject it slowly, taking at least 1 minute for each 5 mg, through the infusion tubing as close as possible to the vein insertion site
C) Mix it in the same syringe with the ordered analgesic to reduce the number of injections
D) Choose a small vein in the hand so the site is easy to observe
Answer: B
Rationale: Intravenous diazepam must be injected slowly, at least 1 minute for each 5 mg given, through the infusion tubing as close as possible to the vein insertion site. It must not be mixed or diluted with other solutions or drugs in a syringe or infusion container, small veins are not to be used, and intraarterial administration and extravasation must be avoided (p. 194).
Trap: (A) applies the barbiturate rule to diazepam. Barbiturates require dilution with normal saline; diazepam must not be diluted at all, and swapping the two rules is the error the chapter's own page layout invites.

Q: A barbiturate is ordered intravenously. Which administration rule applies?
A) Give it undiluted by rapid intravenous push to reach a therapeutic level quickly
B) Dilute it with normal saline and give it no faster than 1 mg/kg/min
C) Give it no faster than 1 minute for each 5 mg
D) Add it to a running infusion of hydrocortisone
Answer: B
Rationale: Intravenous administration of a barbiturate requires dilution of the drug with normal saline, and most of these drugs are not to be given any faster than 1 mg/kg/min. Too rapid an infusion of a barbiturate may produce profound hypotension and marked respiratory depression, and the Key Points warn that too rapid an infusion may precipitate respiratory or cardiac arrest (pp. 194 and 196).
Trap: (C) is the mirror error of the previous item — it is diazepam's fixed time-per-dose rule, whereas the barbiturate rule is weight based. (D) names one of the chapter's stated incompatibilities, listed alongside amphotericin B and hydromorphone.

Subtopic: monitoring and nondrug measures

Q: A patient taking phenobarbital has a serum level of 46 mcg/mL and is found with cold, clammy skin and a respiratory rate of 8 breaths/min. How should the nurse interpret these findings?
A) The level is therapeutic, and the findings are unrelated to the drug
B) The level is above the therapeutic range, and the findings are the chapter's described signs of toxicity
C) The level is subtherapeutic, and the dose should be increased
D) The respiratory rate is within the acceptable range for this drug
Answer: B
Rationale: The therapeutic level of phenobarbital must range between 15 and 40 mcg/mL, and patients with serum levels greater than 40 mcg/mL may experience toxicity manifested by cold and clammy skin, a respiratory rate of less than 10 breaths/min, and other signs of severe CNS depression (p. 194).
Trap: (D) treats a rate of 8 as acceptable by rounding toward the printed threshold. The chapter's cutoff is a rate of less than 10, so a rate of 8 is already inside the toxic description rather than approaching it.

Q: A hospitalized patient tells the nurse she cannot fall asleep. Which action should the nurse take first?
A) Give the ordered sedative-hypnotic
B) Offer valerian tea
C) Encourage her to walk the halls for exercise
D) Provide a restful environment and decrease exposure to loud noises
Answer: D
Rationale: The chapter states in four separate places that nonpharmacologic measures come before drug therapy, and gives the reason directly: benzodiazepines and other drugs are used only when nonpharmacologic methods fail (p. 194). Decreasing exposure to loud noises is itself an item in Box 12.1.
Trap: (B) looks nonpharmacologic because it is herbal, but valerian is a drug with hepatotoxicity and CNS depressant interactions of its own. (C) is contradicted by Box 12.1's instruction to avoid exercise late in the evening.

Q: A nurse teaches a patient the nonpharmacologic measures in Box 12.1. Which instructions belong to that box? Select all that apply.
A) Keep fixed bed and wake times in order to reset your internal clock
B) Avoid daytime napping
C) Avoid caffeine within 6 hours of bedtime
D) Have a glass of wine in the evening to help you fall asleep
E) If you are still awake 20 minutes after going to bed, get up and do something relaxing until you feel drowsy
Answer: A, B, C, E
Rationale: Box 12.1 lists a set sleep pattern with fixed bed and wake times to reset the internal clock, avoiding daytime napping, avoiding stimulants such as caffeine, nicotine, and alcohol close to bedtime with no caffeine within 6 hours of bedtime, and getting up after 20 minutes awake to engage in a relaxing activity before returning to bed once drowsy (p. 195).
Trap: (D) is the trap the box itself sets up: alcohol is listed among the things to avoid in the evening "because rather than putting you to sleep, it actually results in fragmented sleep." Alcohol is never a correct sleep intervention in this chapter, in any form.
