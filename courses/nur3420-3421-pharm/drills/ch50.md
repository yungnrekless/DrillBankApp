# Chapter 50 drills — Acid-Controlling Drugs

Authored from `sources/Ch50_Fact_Bank.md` (Lilley, Collins & Snyder, 10th ed.,
printed pp. 788-800).

Blueprint weight is **6 questions, depth `standard`**, which earns 30 to 45
drills. This file carries **44** across 9 topics. The course blueprint document
labels this content "Chapter 52: Acid Controlling Drugs"; that label is wrong
and `blueprint.json` is already corrected. Acid-Controlling Drugs is Chapter 50;
Chapter 52 is Antiemetic and Antinausea Drugs.

The fact bank supplies far more drillable material than 44 questions can hold,
so the four blocks the chapter tests hardest got the space: the **four drug
classes and where each acts on the parietal cell**, the **antacid salt-to-adverse-
effect mapping**, the **antacid interaction and spacing thread**, and the
**administration rules**. Every confusable pair the fact bank flags is drilled
from both ends where the source states it in both directions.

Deliberately left undrilled, in the order the skill says to cut: all per-drug
pharmacokinetic rows (onset, peak, half-life, duration), which the fact bank
itself flags as internally implausible for omeprazole, lansoprazole, and
misoprostol; every dosage range except the sucralfate-versus-misoprostol units,
which are the discriminator for that pair; Box 50.2's brand-name product lists;
Table 50.2's full body-system adverse-effect grid; the pantoprazole IV rate
arithmetic; the H. pylori age-prevalence figures and the 1983 Campylobacter
renaming; the GERD and flatulence diet lists; and the baseline-lab assessment
split. The two FDA dates (2010 fractures, pneumonia, and C. difficile; 2011
magnesium depletion) were cut last and are the first thing to add if this
chapter's weight ever rises.

Where the chapter contradicts itself the fact bank reproduces the contradiction
rather than averaging it, and so does this file. The antacid-plus-H2-antagonist
interval is printed as four different figures; no question turns on the number,
only on the direction (the H2 antagonist goes first) and on the one thing never
defensible, which is giving them simultaneously.

Topic: gastric cells and secretions
Subtopic: the three cell types of the gastric glands

Q: A nursing student is reviewing which stomach cells the drugs in this chapter target. Which statements about the cells of the gastric glands are accurate? Select all that apply.
A) Parietal cells produce and secrete hydrochloric acid
B) Chief cells secrete pepsinogen, a precursor to pepsin
C) Mucous cells secrete mucus that serves as a protective coat and are also called surface epithelial cells
D) Endocrine and enterochromaffin cells are named among the cell types of the gastric glands
E) Chief cells are the primary site of action for many of the drugs used to treat acid-related disorders
Answer: A, B, C, D
Rationale: The key terms box defines parietal cells as the cells that produce and secrete HCl and names them the primary site of action for many of the drugs used to treat acid-related disorders; chief cells secrete the gastric enzyme pepsinogen, a precursor to pepsin; mucous cells secrete mucus that serves as a protective mucous coat against the digestive properties of HCl and are also called surface epithelial cells; and the gastric glands definition lists parietal, chief, mucous, endocrine, and enterochromaffin cells.
Trap: (E) moves the drug target from the parietal cell to the chief cell and is the only false option here. (D) reads as unfamiliar and gets dropped by students who read the three-cell paragraph but not the gland definition, since the chapter never returns to endocrine and enterochromaffin cells.

Subtopic: pepsinogen versus pepsin

Q: A patient asks why a drug that suppresses stomach acid would have any effect on the digestion of protein. Which explanation is correct?
A) Chief cells release pepsin directly, and acid suppression shuts down the chief cells
B) Chief cells release pepsinogen, an inactive proenzyme that becomes pepsin only after exposure to acid
C) Pepsinogen breaks down protein on its own, and acid only speeds the process up
D) Parietal cells release both acid and pepsin, so blocking one blocks the other
Answer: B
Rationale: Pepsinogen is defined as a proenzyme, or enzyme precursor, that becomes pepsin when activated by exposure to acid, and pepsin is the enzyme in the stomach that breaks down proteins. Removing the acid removes the activation step, which is why suppressing acid also suppresses proteolysis.
Trap: (A) collapses two stages into one by having the chief cell release the active enzyme. The order is the whole point: proenzyme from the chief cell first, acid second, active pepsin third.

Q: A patient with a duodenal ulcer is told the ulcer involves digestion of the lining of the GI tract. Which substance is doing that digesting, and where did it come from?
A) Pepsinogen, secreted in active form by the parietal cells
B) Pepsin, activated from chief cell pepsinogen by gastric acid
C) Hydrochloric acid alone, secreted by the chief cells
D) Intrinsic factor, secreted by the mucous cells
Answer: B
Rationale: Peptic ulcer disease is defined as a general term for gastric or duodenal ulcers that involve digestion of the GI mucosa by the enzyme pepsin, and the chapter states that ulceration is driven by the proteolytic actions of pepsin together with the caustic effects of hydrochloric acid.
Trap: (A) is the mirror error of the previous item, naming the inactive precursor as the active digester and relocating it to the wrong cell. (C) drops pepsin entirely, but the chapter calls these acid-peptic disorders precisely because both the enzyme and the acid are involved.

Topic: parietal cell receptors and sites of drug action
Subtopic: the three receptors and their second messengers

Q: A patient started on an H2 receptor antagonist asks how blocking a histamine receptor could possibly lower stomach acid. Which mechanism explains it?
A) Histamine occupation results in production of adenylate cyclase, which converts ATP to cyclic AMP, which provides the energy for the proton pump
B) Histamine occupation releases calcium ions that directly neutralize gastric acid in the lumen
C) Histamine occupation opens the proton pump itself, so blocking histamine inhibits the pump irreversibly
D) Histamine occupation stimulates the chief cells to release pepsinogen
Answer: A
Rationale: Occupation of the histamine receptor results in the production of adenylate cyclase, which converts ATP to cyclic adenosine monophosphate, which provides energy for the proton pump. This is also why prostaglandins are described as working by preventing histamine binding: without adenylate cyclase, no cAMP can be formed and no second messenger is available to activate the proton pump.
Trap: (B) borrows the second messenger belonging to the other two receptors, and it adds a lumen action that belongs to antacids. Calcium ions drive the acetylcholine and gastrin arms, never the histamine arm.

Q: The same patient asks what happens at the acetylcholine and gastrin receptors of the parietal cell. Which statement is accurate?
A) Both use cyclic AMP as their second messenger, exactly as histamine does
B) Both use calcium ions rather than cyclic AMP as the second messenger
C) Acetylcholine uses cyclic AMP and gastrin uses calcium ions
D) Neither has a second messenger, because both act directly on the pump
Answer: B
Rationale: The chapter states the contrast explicitly: for acetylcholine and for gastrin, the second messenger that drives the proton pump is not cAMP but is instead calcium ions. Only the histamine arm runs through adenylate cyclase and cAMP.
Trap: (A) is the mirror error of the previous item, generalizing the histamine pathway to all three receptors. (C) splits the pair, which is what students pick when they remember that something differs but not which arm.

Q: A nurse is asked which of the parietal cell's three stimulating receptors can be blocked by a currently marketed drug. Which answer reflects the chapter?
A) All three, by H2 antagonists, anticholinergics, and gastrin antagonists respectively
B) Histamine is blocked by drugs in current use; acetylcholine can be blocked by anticholinergics that have been superseded for this purpose; there is currently no drug that blocks gastrin
C) Only gastrin, because it is a hormone rather than a locally released mediator
D) None of them, which is why proton pump inhibitors had to be developed
Answer: B
Rationale: H2 receptor antagonists block the histamine receptor and remain in use; anticholinergics such as atropine block the ACh receptor, but the chapter states these drugs are no longer used for this purpose and have been superseded by other drug classes discussed in this chapter; and there is currently no drug to block the binding of the hormone gastrin to its corresponding receptor.
Trap: (A) invents a gastrin receptor antagonist, a drug class the chapter says does not exist, and simultaneously revives anticholinergics the chapter has explicitly retired for hyperacidity.

Subtopic: where each class acts along the pathway

Q: Over one admission a patient receives an antacid, an H2 receptor antagonist, and a proton pump inhibitor. Which statement correctly places the three along the acid-secretory pathway?
A) The H2 antagonist acts at a receptor, the proton pump inhibitor at the pump, and the antacid in the lumen on acid that has already been secreted
B) The antacid acts at a receptor, the H2 antagonist at the pump, and the proton pump inhibitor in the lumen
C) All three act on the parietal cell membrane before any acid is secreted
D) The proton pump inhibitor acts at the histamine receptor and the H2 antagonist acts at the pump
Answer: A
Rationale: H2 receptor antagonists competitively block the H2 receptor upstream; the hydrogen-potassium-ATPase pump is the final step in the acid-secretory process of the parietal cell and is where proton pump inhibitors act, which is why blocking it blocks all three receptor arms at once; and antacids work by neutralizing gastric acidity, since they do not prevent the overproduction of acid.
Trap: (C) is attractive because all three are filed mentally as acid drugs, but only the antacid works on acid that already exists. That single distinction is what makes antacids symptom relief rather than acid suppression.

Topic: acid-related pathophysiology and stress ulcer prophylaxis
Subtopic: ranking the acid-related conditions

Q: A nurse is asked which acid-related conditions the chapter names as the most harmful and which as the most common. Which pairing is correct?
A) Most harmful, peptic ulcer disease and esophageal cancer; most common, mild to moderate hyperacidity
B) Most harmful, mild to moderate hyperacidity; most common, peptic ulcer disease
C) Most harmful and most common are both gastroesophageal reflux disease
D) Most harmful, Barrett esophagus; most common, erosive esophagitis
Answer: A
Rationale: The chapter states that the most harmful of these conditions involve hypersecretion of acid and include peptic ulcer disease and esophageal cancer, but that the most common condition is mild to moderate hyperacidity. It also notes that many lay terms such as indigestion, sour stomach, heartburn, and acid stomach all describe that one common condition.
Trap: (B) swaps the two rankings, and the swap is invisible unless the reader notices which word the stem used. Most harmful and most common are different answers to different stems.

Q: A patient with long-standing gastroesophageal reflux disease asks what could happen if it goes untreated for years. Which chapter-supported answer is correct?
A) It can progress to erosive esophagitis and to Barrett esophagus, a precancerous condition
B) It resolves on its own once acid production declines with age
C) It progresses to Zollinger-Ellison syndrome
D) It causes hypersecretion of intrinsic factor and vitamin B12 overload
Answer: A
Rationale: GERD is described as the tendency of excessive and acidic stomach contents to back up, or reflux, into the lower and even upper esophagus, and over time this condition can lead to more serious disorders such as erosive esophagitis and Barrett esophagus, a precancerous condition.
Trap: (C) borrows a real condition from the H2 antagonist indications, where Zollinger-Ellison syndrome appears as a pathologic gastric hypersecretory condition and a form of hyperchlorhydria. It is not a sequela of reflux.

Subtopic: helicobacter pylori

Q: A patient with a duodenal ulcer tests positive for Helicobacter pylori and asks whether the organism fully explains the ulcer. Which response reflects the chapter?
A) Yes, H. pylori is the sole cause of peptic ulcer disease
B) The organism is found in about 90% of patients with duodenal ulcers, but it is also found in many patients without ulcer disease, and more than one factor is involved in ulceration
C) The organism is found in about 10% of patients with duodenal ulcers, so it is rarely the explanation
D) The organism causes gastric ulcers only, never duodenal ulcers
Answer: B
Rationale: H. pylori is found in 90% of patients with duodenal ulcers and 70% of those with gastric ulcers, but it is also found in many patients who do not have peptic ulcer disease, its presence is not associated with acute perforating ulcers, and the chapter concludes that more than one factor is involved in ulceration.
Trap: (A) states the claim more strongly than the chapter does, and it is the version most students carry into the item. (C) recycles the 10% figure, which belongs to prevalence in patients younger than 30 rather than to duodenal ulcers.

Q: A patient with an H. pylori-positive gastric ulcer is started on treatment. Which regimen matches the chapter's description of first-line therapy?
A) A 10- to 14-day course of a proton pump inhibitor plus clarithromycin and either amoxicillin or metronidazole
B) A 3-day course of an H2 receptor antagonist plus a single antibiotic
C) A 6-week course of an antacid alone
D) A 10- to 14-day course of sucralfate plus misoprostol
Answer: A
Rationale: First-line therapy as printed is a 10- to 14-day course of a proton pump inhibitor and the antibiotics clarithromycin and either amoxicillin or metronidazole, or a combination of a proton pump inhibitor, bismuth subsalicylate, and the antibiotics tetracycline and metronidazole. Many different combinations are used, but all incorporate those key drugs.
Trap: (B) is picked by students who retain "antibiotic plus acid drug" without the duration or the number of antibiotics. Patient teaching stresses taking each drug, including the antibiotics, exactly as prescribed and without fail, because the condition may recur if the protocol is not followed.

Subtopic: stress-related mucosal damage

Q: An intensive care nurse is identifying which patients are at highest risk for stress-related mucosal damage. Which findings does the chapter name as high-risk indicators? Select all that apply.
A) Coagulopathy
B) Sepsis
C) An intensive care unit stay longer than 1 week
D) Presence of a nasogastric tube
E) Occult bleeding
Answer: A, B, C, E
Rationale: The named high-risk indicators are coagulopathy, a history of peptic ulcer or GI bleed, sepsis, use of steroids, an ICU stay of longer than 1 week, and occult bleeding. The cause of stress-related mucosal damage is described as multifactorial and not fully understood, with decreased blood flow, mucosal ischemia, hypoperfusion, and reperfusion injury among the factors.
Trap: (D) comes from the adjacent sentence naming predisposing procedures, passing nasogastric tubes and placing patients on ventilators, which the chapter lists separately from the high-risk indicators. Category membership is the entire item here.

Q: A patient who received proton pump inhibitor prophylaxis throughout an intensive care unit stay is transferred to a medical-surgical unit and remains on the drug. Which action does the chapter support?
A) Continue the drug indefinitely, since no bleeding occurred in the intensive care unit
B) Question continuing the prophylaxis, because data do not support its continued use once the patient is moved out of the intensive care unit
C) Change to a scheduled antacid, the stepdown drug for prophylaxis
D) Increase the dose, because risk peaks after transfer
Answer: B
Rationale: The chapter states that data do not support the continued use of stress ulcer prophylaxis once the patient is moved out of the ICU. Prophylaxis is defined by the setting, and guidelines suggest only that all critically ill patients receive either a histamine receptor-blocking drug or a proton pump inhibitor while they are in that setting.
Trap: (A) is the reflex pick because the drug appears to have worked, which makes stopping it feel like withdrawing protection. This is the uncommon item whose correct answer is to stop an apparently effective drug.

Topic: antacid mechanism and safe self-use
Subtopic: what antacids actually do

Q: A patient says antacids are the best choice because they coat the stomach and protect the ulcer. Which correction is accurate?
A) Antacids neutralize acid that has already been secreted; forming a coating over an ulcer is sucralfate's mechanism
B) Antacids do coat the stomach lining, and that is their primary mechanism
C) Antacids act on the parietal cells to prevent the overproduction of acid
D) Antacids destroy pepsinogen before acid can activate it
Answer: A
Rationale: Antacids work primarily by neutralizing gastric acidity; they do not prevent the overproduction of acid but instead help neutralize acid secretions. Sucralfate is the drug that binds directly to the surface of an ulcer to form a protective barrier the chapter calls a liquid bandage. Antacids do promote mucosal defense, but indirectly and especially at lower dosages, by stimulating the stomach's own secretion of mucus, prostaglandins, and bicarbonate.
Trap: (C) is the other half of the same misconception and is the half the chapter states as an explicit negation. Antacids reduce symptoms such as pain and reflux; they do not reduce acid production.

Q: A 50-year-old who has self-treated heartburn with large amounts of antacids for years reports that they no longer help. Which nursing action is the priority?
A) Recommend a higher-potency antacid
B) Facilitate evaluation by a health care provider, because long-term self-medication with antacids may mask symptoms of serious underlying disease
C) Add a second antacid at bedtime
D) Reassure the patient that reduced response to antacids over time is expected and harmless
Answer: B
Rationale: Long-term self-medication with antacids may mask symptoms of serious underlying disease such as bleeding ulcer or malignancy, and patient teaching directs that a health care provider be contacted after 2 weeks of self-prescribed antacid use. The chapter's own case study follows exactly this patient to endoscopy, which finds GERD and gastritis.
Trap: (A) is the reflex pick, because escalating a drug that used to work reads as the obvious fix. Years of escalating self-treatment is a diagnostic red flag, not a dosing problem.

Subtopic: contraindications and administration of antacids

Q: A patient with a known small bowel obstruction requests an antacid. Which consideration applies?
A) Any antacid is acceptable, because the only usual contraindication is known allergy
B) Magnesium-containing antacids may be contraindicated in small bowel obstruction because of their laxative effect
C) Aluminum-containing antacids are contraindicated because they bind phosphate
D) Antacids are contraindicated only during pregnancy
Answer: B
Rationale: The only usual contraindication to antacids is known allergy, but other contraindications may include severe renal failure or electrolyte disturbances and GI obstruction; the chapter's own example is that magnesium antacids may be contraindicated for patients with small bowel obstructions because of the laxative effect.
Trap: (A) quotes the first clause of the contraindication sentence and stops before the exceptions, which is exactly how that sentence is built. (D) inverts the pregnancy statement, since antacids are considered safe in pregnancy if prolonged administration and high dosages are avoided.

Q: An antacid is ordered in a chewable tablet form. Which administration instruction does the chapter give?
A) Have the patient chew the tablet thoroughly and follow it with at least 8 oz of water, except for newer rapidly dissolving forms
B) Have the patient swallow the tablet whole with a small sip of water
C) Have the patient dissolve the tablet under the tongue
D) Withhold all fluids for 1 hour after the dose
Answer: A
Rationale: Implementation directs that chewable tablets be thoroughly chewed and that liquid forms be shaken thoroughly, and that antacids be given with at least 8 oz of water, except for newer dosage forms that are rapidly dissolving drugs.
Trap: (B) applies the rule that belongs to omeprazole capsules and pantoprazole tablets, which must be taken whole without crushing, opening, or chewing. The oral forms that must be altered and the ones that must not are on opposite sides of this chapter's line.

Topic: antacid salts and adverse effects
Subtopic: magnesium versus aluminum and calcium

Q: A patient taking a magnesium-only antacid several times a day reports loose stools. Which explanation and action are correct?
A) Magnesium antacids commonly have a laxative effect and can cause diarrhea, so a combination aluminum and magnesium product may be preferred
B) Magnesium antacids commonly constipate, so the diarrhea is unrelated to the drug
C) The patient should increase the dose of the magnesium-only product
D) Diarrhea from an antacid is the earliest sign of milk-alkali syndrome
Answer: A
Rationale: Magnesium-containing antacids, especially milk of magnesia, can cause diarrhea, and frequent administration of these antacids alone often cannot be tolerated. If constipation or diarrhea occurs with single-component drugs, a combination aluminum- and magnesium-based antacid may be preferred, and the planning outcome names avoidance of antacids containing only magnesium.
Trap: (B) reverses the flagship confusable pair of this chapter. Magnesium is the laxative salt; aluminum and calcium are the constipating ones.

Q: A patient taking an aluminum-only antacid reports no bowel movement in 3 days. Which explanation is correct?
A) Aluminum antacids cause diarrhea, so something other than the antacid is responsible
B) Aluminum and calcium antacids cause constipation, and magnesium is added to combination products specifically to counteract it
C) Aluminum causes constipation only in patients with renal failure
D) Constipation from an antacid always indicates a bowel obstruction
Answer: B
Rationale: Aluminum-containing and calcium-containing antacids cause constipation, and many aluminum- and calcium-based formulations include magnesium, which not only contributes to acid-neutralizing capacity but also counteracts the constipating effects of aluminum and calcium. The chapter describes the net effect of the combination as a balancing out of both adverse effects.
Trap: (A) is the mirror error of the previous item. The chapter's human need statements map constipation to aluminum-containing antacids and diarrhea to magnesium-containing antacids, one to one.

Q: A patient with chronic kidney disease asks which type of antacid is safest. Which choice does the chapter support, and why?
A) An aluminum-based antacid, because aluminum- and sodium-based antacids are more easily excreted
B) A magnesium-based antacid, because magnesium is cleared by the kidneys
C) A calcium-based antacid, because it doubles as a calcium supplement
D) No antacid can be used safely in kidney disease
Answer: A
Rationale: Aluminum- and sodium-based antacids are often recommended for patients with renal compromise because they are more easily excreted, whereas both calcium- and magnesium-based antacids are more likely to accumulate to toxic levels in patients with renal disease, and antacids containing magnesium must be avoided in patients with renal failure.
Trap: (B) is chosen by students who reason that a renally cleared salt suits a kidney patient, which is the opposite of what the chapter says. (C) picks up a true statement, that calcium antacids can serve as a source of calcium, and applies it to the one population where calcium accumulates.

Subtopic: the three complications that all sound like too much base

Q: A patient abruptly stops a high-potency antacid after weeks of heavy use and reports heartburn worse than before starting it. Which complication does this describe?
A) Milk-alkali syndrome
B) Acid rebound
C) Metabolic alkalosis
D) Hypophosphatemia
Answer: B
Rationale: Acid rebound is rebound hyperacidity in which the patient experiences hyperacidity when antacid use is discontinued. It occurs with the discontinuation of antacids that have high acid-neutralizing capacity and with overuse or misuse, because if acid neutralization is sudden and high, the immediate elevation in pH to alkalinity is followed by just as rapid a decline in pH to a more acidic state.
Trap: (A) and (C) are the reflex picks because all three complications sound like too much base. Discontinuation is what pins acid rebound, hypercalcemia pins milk-alkali syndrome, and sodium bicarbonate abuse pins metabolic alkalosis.

Q: A patient using large doses of a calcium carbonate antacid presents with headache, nausea, alkalosis, and hypercalcemia. Which complication does this describe?
A) Acid rebound
B) Milk-alkali syndrome
C) Systemic alkalosis from sodium bicarbonate
D) Hypophosphatemia from aluminum
Answer: B
Rationale: Chronic high-dose use of calcium-containing antacids, or their use in renal failure, can cause milk-alkali syndrome, characterized by headache, nausea, alkalosis, and hypercalcemia. Calcium antacids can also cause kidney stones, and acid rebound is more common with the calcium-containing products.
Trap: (C) is the plausible near-miss because alkalosis appears in both descriptions, but systemic or metabolic alkalosis is tied to sodium bicarbonate and carries no hypercalcemia. Hypercalcemia is the finding that pins this to the calcium salt.

Q: A patient on long-term calcium carbonate antacids is monitored for hypercalcemia. Which findings does Box 50.1 place in the early stage? Select all that apply.
A) Constipation
B) Increased thirst and dry mouth
C) A metallic taste in the mouth
D) Confusion and drowsiness
E) Irritability
Answer: A, B, C, E
Rationale: Box 50.1 lists early hypercalcemia as constipation, headache, increased thirst, dry mouth, decreased appetite, irritability, and a metallic taste in the mouth. The later findings are confusion, drowsiness, an increase in blood pressure, irregular heart rate, nausea, vomiting, and increased urination.
Trap: (D) belongs to the later stage, and it is the option that gets checked because confusion feels like the most serious finding on the list. Neurologic and cardiac findings mark the late stage; the metallic taste is the most distinctive early cue.

Topic: antacid drug interactions and dose spacing
Subtopic: the four interaction mechanisms

Q: A nurse is reviewing why antacids interfere with so many other oral drugs. Which mechanisms does the chapter name? Select all that apply.
A) Adsorption of other drugs to the antacid, which reduces the ability of the other drug to be absorbed
B) Chelation, the chemical inactivation of other drugs, producing insoluble complexes
C) Increased stomach pH, which changes the absorption of acidic and basic drugs
D) Increased urinary pH, which changes the excretion of acidic and basic drugs
E) Induction of the hepatic cytochrome P-450 enzyme system
Answer: A, B, C, D
Rationale: The chapter enumerates exactly four mechanisms: adsorption of other drugs to antacids, which reduces the ability of the other drug to be absorbed; chelation, which is the chemical inactivation of other drugs that produces insoluble complexes; increased stomach pH, which increases the absorption of basic drugs and decreases the absorption of acidic drugs; and increased urinary pH, which increases the excretion of acidic drugs and decreases the excretion of basic drugs. Most drugs are either weak acids or weak bases, so pH in both the GI and urinary tracts matters.
Trap: (E) is imported from the H2 antagonist section, where cimetidine binds enzymes of the hepatic cytochrome P-450 microsomal oxidase system. Antacids in this chapter act physically and by pH, never on hepatic enzymes.

Q: An antacid raises gastric pH. What is the effect on other oral drugs still in the stomach?
A) Absorption of basic drugs increases and absorption of acidic drugs decreases
B) Absorption of acidic drugs increases and absorption of basic drugs decreases
C) Absorption of all drugs increases
D) Absorption is unchanged; only excretion is affected
Answer: A
Rationale: Increased stomach pH increases the absorption of basic drugs and decreases the absorption of acidic drugs. Because most drugs are either weak acids or weak bases, pH conditions in the GI tract affect the extent to which drug molecules are absorbed.
Trap: (B) reverses the direction, and because the stomach and urinary statements are written to mirror each other, the reversed version reads exactly like the urinary rule. Anchor on the site: in the stomach, pH governs absorption.

Q: The same antacid also raises urinary pH. What is the effect on other drugs there?
A) Excretion of acidic drugs increases and excretion of basic drugs decreases
B) Excretion of basic drugs increases and excretion of acidic drugs decreases
C) Excretion of all drugs decreases
D) Excretion is unchanged; only stomach absorption is affected
Answer: A
Rationale: Increased urinary pH increases the excretion of acidic drugs and decreases the excretion of basic drugs. In the urine, pH governs excretion, whereas in the stomach the same pH change governs absorption.
Trap: (B) is the mirror error of the previous item. The two pH statements are written in deliberate parallel, so reversing one produces something that looks exactly like the other.

Subtopic: spacing antacids from other drugs

Q: A patient is due for oral ciprofloxacin and asks for an antacid at the same time. Which action is correct?
A) Give both together, because the interaction is minor
B) Give the antacid either 2 hours before or 2 hours after the quinolone dose
C) Hold the ciprofloxacin until the antacid has been discontinued
D) Give the antacid 15 minutes after the quinolone
Answer: B
Rationale: Significant patient harm may ensue when the quinolone antibiotics ciprofloxacin, levofloxacin, and moxifloxacin are given with antacids, because antacids can reduce their absorption by more than 50%. Antacids must therefore be given either 2 hours before or 2 hours after the dose of a quinolone antibiotic, or serious infections may go unsuccessfully treated because of altered absorption.
Trap: (C) is the over-correction distractor. Nothing in this chapter withholds a drug because of an antacid interaction; implementation states that this dosing schedule can be safely implemented without interrupting the safe dosing of other medications.

Q: A patient taking oral tetracycline asks for an antacid for heartburn. Which response matches this chapter?
A) Give the tetracycline, but delay the antacid for 1 to 2 hours
B) Explain that the antacid cannot be given while the patient is taking tetracycline
C) Give both at once with a full glass of water
D) Give the antacid first and the tetracycline 15 minutes later
Answer: A
Rationale: Tetracycline appears on this chapter's list of drugs whose effects are reduced through decreased GI absorption, and the general rule given here is to dose any interacting drug at least 1 to 2 hours before or after antacids are taken. Chapter 38 teaches a tighter tetracycline interval; answer from the chapter the item was written for.
Trap: (B) is the over-correction distractor again, and it is the intuitive one because tetracycline losses feel dangerous. In this chapter the antacid interaction answer is almost always to separate the doses, not to stop the drug.

Topic: h2 receptor antagonists
Subtopic: class mechanism and roster

Q: A patient started on famotidine for GERD asks how it works. Which explanation is accurate?
A) It competitively blocks the H2 receptor, which also makes the parietal cell less responsive to acetylcholine and gastrin, reducing but not completely abolishing acid secretion
B) It irreversibly inhibits the hydrogen-potassium-ATPase pump, abolishing all gastric acid secretion
C) It neutralizes the acid already present in the stomach
D) It binds to the ulcer surface and forms a protective barrier
Answer: A
Rationale: H2 receptor antagonists competitively block the H2 receptor of acid-producing parietal cells, which makes the parietal cell less responsive not only to histamine but also to the stimulation of ACh and gastrin. Up to 90% inhibition of vagal- and gastrin-stimulated acid secretion occurs when histamine is blocked, but complete inhibition has not been shown, and these drugs reduce but do not completely abolish acid secretion.
Trap: (B) describes the proton pump inhibitors, and one word separates the classes: H2 antagonists block competitively and reversibly, proton pump inhibitors bind irreversibly. A surmountable receptor block cannot produce achlorhydria.

Q: Which statement about the currently available H2 receptor antagonists is accurate?
A) Cimetidine, famotidine, and nizatidine are available, and ranitidine was pulled from the market in 2020
B) Ranitidine remains the prototype and the most widely used drug in the class
C) Cimetidine is the newest drug in the class and has the fewest drug interactions
D) The drugs differ substantially in efficacy, so one cannot be substituted for another
Answer: A
Rationale: The roster given is cimetidine, famotidine, and nizatidine, with ranitidine (Zantac) pulled from the market in 2020. There is little difference among the three available H2 receptor antagonists from the standpoint of efficacy, and drugs in this class are available over the counter.
Trap: (B) tests currency rather than pharmacology, and ranitidine is the most recognizable name in the class and still appears in older question banks. (C) reverses the timeline: cimetidine was the first drug in this class, released in 1977, and famotidine was the last introduced.

Subtopic: cimetidine versus famotidine

Q: A male patient on long-term cimetidine reports breast enlargement and impotence and is afraid the changes are permanent. Which response is accurate?
A) These effects are known with cimetidine and are reversible
B) These effects belong to famotidine, so cimetidine is not the cause
C) These effects are permanent, and the drug must be stopped at once
D) These effects indicate developing thrombocytopenia
Answer: A
Rationale: Cimetidine may induce impotence and gynecomastia, the result of cimetidine's inhibition of estradiol metabolism and displacement of dihydrotestosterone from peripheral androgen-binding sites, and patient teaching states that gynecomastia and impotence may occur with cimetidine and are reversible. The class may also increase secretion of prolactin from the anterior pituitary gland.
Trap: (B) attributes the effect to the wrong member of the pair. Famotidine is the drug with reported thrombocytopenia, which is the adverse effect option (D) is reaching for.

Q: A patient taking cimetidine is also prescribed warfarin. Why does this combination warrant attention?
A) Cimetidine binds hepatic cytochrome P-450 enzymes and may raise blood concentrations of drugs with a narrow therapeutic range, such as theophylline, warfarin, lidocaine, and phenytoin
B) Cimetidine induces hepatic enzymes and lowers warfarin blood levels
C) Warfarin blocks the absorption of cimetidine
D) The interaction is clinically significant with essentially every drug given alongside cimetidine
Answer: A
Rationale: Cimetidine carries a higher risk for drug interactions than the other drugs in the class, especially in older adults, because it binds enzymes of the hepatic cytochrome P-450 microsomal oxidase system and may raise the blood concentrations of certain drugs, whereas nizatidine and famotidine have essentially no effect. The chapter adds that the interaction has little clinical significance for most drugs and names the exceptions by shared property, a narrow therapeutic range.
Trap: (B) converts inhibition into induction, which flips the direction of the level change. (D) overstates the chapter, which explicitly limits clinical significance to the narrow-therapeutic-range group.

Subtopic: older adults on h2 antagonists

Q: An 80-year-old admitted 2 days ago and started on an H2 receptor antagonist becomes newly confused and disoriented. Which nursing action is the priority?
A) Document the confusion as an expected age-related change
B) Assess mental status and consider the H2 receptor antagonist as the cause, because these effects are seen in older adults and matter most when they are new to the patient
C) Reassure the family, because central nervous system effects occur in less than 1% of patients and are therefore not clinically relevant
D) Request that an antacid be given at the same time as the next dose
Answer: B
Rationale: Central nervous system effects occur in less than 1% of patients but are sometimes seen in older adults and include confusion and disorientation, and the chapter directs the nurse to be alert for mental status changes, especially if they are new to the patient. Assessment repeats that older adult patients are known to react to these drugs with more disorientation and confusion.
Trap: (C) uses the low incidence figure as grounds for dismissing the finding, which is precisely why the chapter attaches these effects to older adults twice. A low base rate does not make a new deficit expected. (D) also violates the rule that an antacid and an H2 antagonist are never given simultaneously.

Topic: proton pump inhibitors
Subtopic: irreversible pump inhibition

Q: A patient asks why a proton pump inhibitor keeps working for days after a dose. Which explanation is correct?
A) The drug binds directly to the hydrogen-potassium-ATPase pump and irreversibly inhibits it, so recovery requires the parietal cell to synthesize new pump enzyme
B) The drug has an unusually long half-life, which keeps blood levels high for days
C) The drug is stored in the gastric mucus layer and slowly released
D) The drug competitively blocks the histamine receptor, and the block outlasts the drug
Answer: A
Rationale: Proton pump inhibitors bind directly to the hydrogen-potassium-ATPase pump mechanism and irreversibly inhibit the action of this enzyme, resulting in a total blockage of hydrogen ion secretion from the parietal cells, and recovery requires that the parietal cell synthesize new hydrogen-potassium-ATPase. That is why the duration of effect outlasts the drug's half-life.
Trap: (D) borrows the H2 antagonist mechanism, which is competitive and therefore surmountable and ends with the drug. Irreversible binding, not persistence in the blood, is what produces the prolonged effect.

Q: A patient on long-term proton pump inhibitor therapy is described as temporarily achlorhydric. Which consequence does the chapter attribute to that state?
A) Malabsorption of food, which is why supplements are required
B) Bacterial overgrowth, intestinal metaplasia, and hip fracture, while food absorption is not affected
C) Loss of intrinsic factor secretion, with immediate vitamin deficiency
D) Complete loss of pepsinogen secretion by the chief cells
Answer: B
Rationale: Proton pump inhibitors stop more than 90% of acid secretion over 24 hours, which makes most patients temporarily achlorhydric, and the complications named are bacterial overgrowth, intestinal metaplasia, and hip fracture. The chapter then rules out the obvious distractor in the next sentence: food absorption is not affected.
Trap: (A) is the intuitive inference, since no acid ought to mean no digestion, and the chapter closes it off explicitly. The related concern it does raise is that reduced acid-mediated antimicrobial protection may predispose patients to GI tract infections.

Subtopic: administering proton pump inhibitors

Q: A patient is being taught how to take an oral proton pump inhibitor. Which instructions are supported by the chapter? Select all that apply.
A) Take the drug on an empty stomach
B) Take the drug 30 to 60 minutes before meals
C) Do not crush or chew the capsule
D) Open the capsule and chew the contents for a faster effect
E) Take the drug only when heartburn occurs
Answer: A, B, C
Rationale: Orally administered proton pump inhibitors work best when taken 30 to 60 minutes before meals, food may decrease their absorption so they are recommended on an empty stomach, and the capsule must be taken whole and not crushed, opened, or chewed. Every proton pump inhibitor indication in this chapter is a scheduled course rather than an as-needed dose.
Trap: (D) is checked by students who remember that lansoprazole granules may be sprinkled on applesauce; those granules must be swallowed immediately, never chewed. (E) turns a scheduled acid-suppressing course into a symptom-triggered antacid.

Q: A patient with a nasogastric tube is ordered pantoprazole granules. Which consideration applies?
A) The granules require a tube larger than 16 French and may clog smaller tubes
B) The granules must be dissolved in 50 mL of water, which is the rule for every proton pump inhibitor
C) The granules must be mixed with apple juice, never water
D) Tube administration is identical for all proton pump inhibitors, so any product may be substituted
Answer: A
Rationale: Pantoprazole granules are to be used with nasogastric tubes larger than 16 French and may clog the tube if used with smaller tubes. Tube administration is drug-specific: esomeprazole granules are dissolved in 50 mL of water, lansoprazole capsules are opened and mixed but not crushed with apple juice or given as a SoluTab dissolved in water, and omeprazole is supplied as a powder for oral suspension.
Trap: (D) is the assumption that makes the whole item fail, and the chapter counters it with a standing instruction to consult the drug packaging for drug-specific instructions and to be aware of the particle size and the tube size being used. The French-size rule belongs to exactly one drug.

Subtopic: proton pump inhibitor indications and interactions

Q: A patient with GERD that has responded poorly to an H2 receptor antagonist is switched to a proton pump inhibitor. Which statement supports this change?
A) Proton pump inhibitors are first-line therapy for erosive esophagitis and are indicated for symptomatic GERD that is poorly responsive to treatment such as H2 receptor antagonists
B) Proton pump inhibitors and H2 receptor antagonists are interchangeable, so the change is arbitrary
C) Proton pump inhibitors are indicated only for Zollinger-Ellison syndrome
D) Proton pump inhibitors are used only when an H. pylori infection has been confirmed
Answer: A
Rationale: Proton pump inhibitors are first-line therapy for erosive esophagitis and for symptomatic GERD that is poorly responsive to other medical treatment such as therapy with H2 receptor antagonists, and they are also used for active duodenal and benign gastric ulcers, gastric hypersecretory conditions, NSAID-induced ulcers, and stress ulcer prophylaxis.
Trap: (D) picks up a true statement, that all of the proton pump inhibitors can be used with antibiotics to treat H. pylori infection, and narrows the whole class to that one use. (C) does the same with the hypersecretory indication.

Q: A patient taking clopidogrel after a coronary stent needs acid suppression. Which proton pump inhibitor consideration applies?
A) Omeprazole is a strong CYP2C19 inhibitor and may reduce clopidogrel's effectiveness, so another proton pump inhibitor is generally the better alternative
B) Omeprazole raises clopidogrel levels and increases bleeding risk
C) All proton pump inhibitors affect clopidogrel identically
D) Clopidogrel prevents the absorption of all proton pump inhibitors
Answer: A
Rationale: Clopidogrel depends on conversion to an active metabolite by the CYP-450 enzyme system, specifically CYP2C19; omeprazole is a strong inhibitor of CYP2C19, so there is concern for a decrease in clopidogrel's effectiveness. Other proton pump inhibitors are less strong CYP2C19 inhibitors and are generally considered better alternatives.
Trap: (B) inverts the direction of the interaction, which is the reflex error because the cimetidine interactions in this same chapter all raise the other drug's level. Here the interaction weakens the other drug, because clopidogrel is a prodrug that needs the enzyme.

Topic: mucosal protectants and antiflatulents
Subtopic: sucralfate

Q: A patient with an active stress ulceration is started on sucralfate and asks how it differs from an antacid. Which explanation is correct?
A) It acts locally, binding to positively charged tissue proteins at the base of the ulcer to form a protective barrier described as a liquid bandage, and it also limits the access of pepsin
B) It neutralizes gastric acid throughout the stomach, exactly as an antacid does
C) It blocks the proton pump of the parietal cell
D) It is absorbed systemically and suppresses gastrin release
Answer: A
Rationale: Sucralfate is a mucosal protectant that acts locally, not systemically, binding directly to the surface of an ulcer; once in contact with stomach acid it dissociates into aluminum hydroxide and sulfate anions, and the sulfated sucrose molecules bind to positively charged tissue proteins at the bases of ulcers and erosions to form a protective barrier the chapter compares to a liquid bandage, which also limits the access of pepsin. Only minimal systemic absorption occurs and the drug is virtually inert.
Trap: (B) picks up a true component and mislabels the whole, since sucralfate does dissociate into aluminum hydroxide, which the chapter calls an antacid. Its mechanism is the barrier, not neutralization, and its adverse effects carry aluminum's signature of nausea, constipation, and dry mouth.

Q: A patient is receiving sucralfate along with several other oral medications and an antacid. Which schedule reflects the chapter?
A) Give other drugs at least 2 hours ahead of sucralfate, avoid antacids for 30 minutes before or after sucralfate, and give sucralfate 1 hour before meals and at bedtime
B) Give other drugs 30 minutes ahead of sucralfate and antacids 2 hours before or after
C) Give sucralfate with meals and give all other drugs at the same time
D) Give sucralfate once daily at bedtime only, with fluids restricted
Answer: A
Rationale: Sucralfate causes physical interference with the absorption of other drugs, which can be alleviated by taking other drugs at least 2 hours ahead of sucralfate; antacids are to be avoided for 30 minutes before or after sucralfate; and sucralfate is best given 1 hour before meals and at bedtime, at a usual dose of 1 g orally four times daily because its effects are transient.
Trap: (B) swaps the two intervals, applying the antacid window to the other drugs and the other-drug window to the antacid. Three different clocks attach to this one drug, and swapping any two of them is the entire item.

Subtopic: misoprostol

Q: A patient on long-term NSAID therapy is prescribed misoprostol. Which statement describes its role?
A) It is a prostaglandin E analogue given to reduce the incidence of gastric ulcers in patients taking NSAIDs, for the duration of NSAID therapy
B) It is the preferred drug for healing an existing duodenal ulcer
C) It neutralizes acid in the same way as a calcium-based antacid
D) It is an antiflatulent that reduces gas pain during NSAID therapy
Answer: A
Rationale: Misoprostol is a prostaglandin E analogue shown to effectively reduce the incidence of gastric ulcers in patients taking NSAIDs, dosed at 200 mcg four times daily with meals for the duration of NSAID therapy in patients at high risk for ulceration. Prostaglandins inhibit gastric acid secretion and protect the gastric mucosa from injury, a cytoprotective function.
Trap: (B) is the plausible-but-adjacent option, because the chapter concedes that prostaglandin analogues promote the healing of duodenal ulcers, then rules them out on tolerability: the required dosages usually produce disturbing adverse effects such as abdominal cramps and diarrhea, so they are not thought to be as effective as H2 receptor antagonists and proton pump inhibitors for that indication.

Q: A woman of childbearing age is about to receive a first dose of misoprostol. Which finding is the contraindication the chapter names?
A) Hypothyroidism
B) Pregnancy
C) Type 2 diabetes
D) Hypertension
Answer: B
Rationale: The chapter names exactly two contraindications for misoprostol, known drug allergy and pregnant women, and gives the reason: misoprostol is also used for its abortifacient properties and is classified as a pregnancy category X drug. Its adverse effects are headache, GI distress, and vaginal bleeding.
Trap: (A), (C), and (D) appear nowhere in the misoprostol profile and are chosen only by elimination. The pair to keep straight is sucralfate, pregnancy category B and dosed 1 g four times daily, against misoprostol, pregnancy category X and dosed 200 mcg four times daily; both are four times daily, so frequency does not discriminate and the units and the category do.

Subtopic: simethicone

Q: A postoperative patient with gaseous distention is given simethicone and asks how it will help. Which explanation is correct?
A) It neutralizes gastric pH
B) It alters the elasticity of mucus-coated gas bubbles, causing them to break into smaller ones, which reduces gas pain and facilitates expulsion of gas
C) It decreases gastric acid secretion at the parietal cell
D) It buffers the protein-digesting effects of pepsin
Answer: B
Rationale: Simethicone is classified as an antiflatulent and works by altering the elasticity of mucus-coated gas bubbles, which causes them to break into smaller ones, reducing gas pain and facilitating the expulsion of gas by mouth or rectum. The chapter lists no adverse effects, no drug interactions, and no pharmacokinetic parameters for it, and it is given after meals and at bedtime.
Trap: (A), (C), and (D) are the mechanisms of antacids and of the acid-suppressing classes transplanted onto the wrong drug, and they are the reflex picks because simethicone sits in a chapter about acid. Simethicone changes bubble elasticity and has no acid effect at all.
