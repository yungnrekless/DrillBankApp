# Chapter 28 drills — Diuretic Drugs

Authored from `sources/Ch28_Fact_Bank.md` (Lilley, Collins & Snyder, 10th ed.).

Blueprint weight is **2 questions of 121 (1.7%), depth `focused`** — near the
bottom of the course. `focused` earns **20 to 30 drills**, and this file carries
**29 across 7 topics.** The chapter hands over far more drillable material than
that, so the count is set by exam weight, not by chapter length.

The two structures everything is built around are the ones the fact bank names:
the class-by-class grid of **nephron site of action** against **signature
potassium effect**, and the **potassium-wasting versus potassium-sparing** split
that drives the teaching item, the digoxin item, and the lab item the chapter's
own review questions ask. Every confusable pair the fact bank flags is drilled
from both ends — loop versus thiazide by segment, wasting versus sparing,
calcium out versus calcium up, mannitol versus furosemide by where the fluid is,
spironolactone versus triamterene, and the renal-function thresholds.

Deliberately left out, and recoverable from the fact bank if this chapter's
weight ever rises: the seven pharmacokinetic tables and all dosage ranges except
the two where the number is the fact (the HCTZ 50 mg ceiling is drilled, the
equipotent loop doses are not), the lab reference ranges, the lifespan boxes,
the nephron anatomy vocabulary that is not attached to a drug decision
(afferent/efferent, glomerulus/capsule), the sequential-nephron-blockade timing,
the postural-hypotension number, and the nursing-process phase sorting. Those
are cuts by weight, not judgments that they are untestable.

Topic: diuretic classes and nephron sites of action
Subtopic: site of action by class

Q: A patient who takes hydrochlorothiazide at home is switched to intravenous furosemide for acute decompensated heart failure and produces far more urine than she ever did on the tablet. The difference follows from where furosemide acts. Which segment does it block?
A) The thick ascending limb of the loop of Henle, blocking chloride and, secondarily, sodium resorption
B) The distal convoluted tubule, inhibiting the resorption of sodium, potassium, and chloride
C) The proximal tubule, by blocking carbonic anhydrase
D) The collecting duct, by interfering with sodium-potassium exchange
Answer: A
Rationale: Loop diuretics act primarily along the thick ascending limb of the loop of Henle, blocking chloride and, secondarily, sodium resorption (p. 448). That segment returns another 20% to 25% of filtered sodium, against the distal tubule's remaining 5% to 10%, which is why the loop drugs are the most potent class.
Trap: (B) is the reflex pick for anyone who holds the loop/thiazide pair backwards, and it is the chapter's most common site swap. The class is named after its segment — the book's own key point is that if a loop diuretic is given, its site of action is the loop of Henle — which leaves the distal convoluted tubule to the thiazides.

Q: The same patient's home hydrochlorothiazide is restarted at discharge. Which nephron segment does it act on?
A) The thick ascending limb of the loop of Henle
B) The distal convoluted tubule, where it inhibits the resorption of sodium, potassium, and chloride
C) The glomerular capsule, where filtrate is first received
D) The proximal tubule and descending limb of the loop of Henle
Answer: B
Rationale: The primary site of action of the thiazides and thiazide-like drugs is the distal convoluted tubule, where they inhibit the resorption of sodium, potassium, and chloride (p. 452). That segment handles only the remaining 5% to 10% of filtered sodium, which is why thiazides are less potent than loop diuretics.
Trap: (A) is the mirror error of the previous item. (D) is mannitol's major site of action, offered here because both drugs are described as acting on more than one structure.

Q: A patient with severe fluid volume overload has not responded to hydrochlorothiazide, and the prescriber asks for the most potent class available. Which class fits, and on what basis?
A) Loop diuretics, because potency is a function of where a drug works in the nephron and they block the segment that still holds the largest usable share of sodium
B) Potassium-sparing diuretics, because they act at the most distal point in the nephron
C) Carbonic anhydrase inhibitors, because the proximal tubule returns 60% to 70% of filtered sodium
D) Thiazide diuretics, because they are among the first-line drugs for hypertension
Answer: A
Rationale: The printed potency order is loop diuretics, then mannitol, metolazone, the thiazides, and the potassium-sparing diuretics (p. 446). The stated reason is that potency is a function of where the drugs work in the nephron: the more sodium and water they inhibit from resorption, the greater the amount of diuresis.
Trap: (C) pulls hardest because 60% to 70% is the biggest number on the page, but the chapter calls the carbonic anhydrase inhibitors much less potent than loop diuretics or thiazides. (B) confuses most distal with most powerful — the potassium-sparing drugs sit last on the potency list, not first.

Q: A nursing student is asked to pull every loop diuretic off the unit formulary list. Select all that are loop diuretics.
A) bumetanide
B) ethacrynic acid
C) furosemide
D) torsemide
E) metolazone
F) acetazolamide
Answer: A, B, C, D
Rationale: Table 28.1 lists bumetanide, ethacrynic acid, furosemide, and torsemide as the loop diuretics (p. 447). Metolazone is a thiazide-like drug and acetazolamide is the only carbonic anhydrase inhibitor in the table.
Trap: (B) is the one students leave out, because ethacrynic acid is the only loop diuretic whose name gives no hint of its class. (E) is wrongly added, because metolazone is routinely combined with a loop diuretic for sequential nephron blockade, and sharing an order set is mistaken for sharing a class.

Topic: potassium-wasting versus potassium-sparing diuretics
Subtopic: dietary teaching in both directions

Q: A patient is being discharged on hydrochlorothiazide for hypertension. Which dietary instruction belongs in the teaching?
A) Include bananas, oranges, potatoes, and legumes in the daily diet
B) Avoid bananas, oranges, potatoes, and legumes
C) Take the dose in the late afternoon or evening so the urinating happens overnight
D) Expect vomiting and diarrhea as the earliest sign that the potassium level has fallen
Answer: A
Rationale: Hydrochlorothiazide is a potassium-wasting diuretic, and the chapter's teaching is to encourage potassium-rich foods except when contraindicated or when potassium-sparing diuretics are used (p. 455). The printed food list includes bananas, oranges, apricots, dates, raisins, broccoli, green beans, potatoes, tomatoes, meats, fish, wheat bread, and legumes.
Trap: (B) is the potassium-sparing instruction misapplied to a potassium-wasting drug, which is the chapter's most-swapped fact. (C) inverts the timing rule: doses taken late in the afternoon or evening cause nocturia and loss of sleep, so diuretics are usually scheduled in the morning.

Q: The same patient is later switched from hydrochlorothiazide to spironolactone, and her daughter asks whether she should keep buying bananas and orange juice. Which response is correct?
A) Yes, keep them up, because every diuretic causes potassium to be lost in the urine
B) No, because spironolactone keeps potassium in the body and the risk with it is a high potassium level
C) Yes, because spironolactone blocks aldosterone and aldosterone conserves potassium
D) It makes no difference, because dietary potassium does not affect serum levels
Answer: B
Rationale: The potassium-sparing diuretics spare potassium rather than wasting it, so the problem that may occur is hyperkalemia rather than hypokalemia (p. 455), and the teaching to encourage potassium-rich foods is explicitly suspended when potassium-sparing diuretics are used.
Trap: (A) is the mirror error of the previous item, and it is the classic reversal — "eat foods high in potassium" offered to a spironolactone patient. (C) reasons from aldosterone in the wrong direction: aldosterone regulates sodium and water balance by trading sodium for potassium, so blocking its receptors holds potassium in, not out.

Subtopic: recognizing the electrolyte problem each side produces

Q: A patient has been taking furosemide for three weeks and the serum potassium is now 3.0 mEq/L. Select all the findings consistent with that result.
A) Muscle weakness
B) Mental confusion
C) Lethargy
D) Hypotension
E) Vomiting
F) Diarrhea
Answer: A, B, C, D
Rationale: The hypokalemia set is anorexia, nausea, lethargy, muscle weakness, mental confusion, and hypotension (p. 455). Normal serum potassium is 3.5 to 5 mEq/L, so 3.0 is below range.
Trap: (E) and (F) are drawn from the hyperkalemia column — nausea, vomiting, and diarrhea, with cardiac rhythm abnormalities at toxic levels. Nausea is the only symptom the two lists share, so it discriminates nothing; weakness and confusion point low, while vomiting and diarrhea point high.

Q: A prescriber writes spironolactone 50 mg daily for a patient who is already taking potassium chloride 20 mEq daily. The morning serum potassium is 4.0 mEq/L. What should the nurse do?
A) Hold the spironolactone and clarify the order with the prescriber, because a potassium-sparing diuretic plus a potassium supplement risks hyperkalemia
B) Give both drugs and recheck the potassium level in one week, since the current level is normal
C) Give the spironolactone and teach the patient to add potassium-rich foods to offset the diuresis
D) Discontinue the potassium chloride, give the spironolactone, and document the change
Answer: A
Rationale: Potassium supplements are named among the conditions that contraindicate potassium-sparing diuretics, along with hyperkalemia and concurrent ACE inhibitors or other potassium-sparing drugs (pp. 451, 454). The combination drives potassium up from two directions at once.
Trap: (B) pulls because the potassium level is normal right now, which makes give-and-monitor feel defensible — but the level is normal *before* the interacting drug is added, so it says nothing about where the patient will be tomorrow. (D) has the nurse discontinuing a prescribed drug, which is outside the nurse's authority even when the reasoning is right.

Subtopic: the digoxin chain and its one exclusion

Q: A patient taking digoxin and furosemide reports nausea, blurred vision with yellow halos, and confusion; the apical pulse is 56 beats/min. Which laboratory value should the nurse check first?
A) Serum sodium
B) Serum potassium
C) Serum uric acid
D) Serum calcium
Answer: B
Rationale: Both interaction tables give the mechanism as hypokalemia and the result as increased digoxin toxicity (Tables 28.3, 28.6), and the chapter states that with any diuretic-induced hypokalemia, excluding the potassium-sparing diuretics, digoxin toxicity may occur (p. 453). Potassium is checked first because potassium is the mechanism. A pulse rate of 60 beats/min or lower may indicate digoxin toxicity, and the printed toxicity signs are headache, dizziness, confusion, nausea, visual disturbances, and bradycardia.
Trap: (A) is the reflex pick because diuretics are filed mentally under sodium, and sodium is genuinely monitored during therapy — but it is not what links the diuretic to the digoxin. (C) and (D) are real thiazide effects attached to the wrong drug and the wrong problem.

Q: A student states that a patient taking spironolactone with digoxin should be watched for digoxin toxicity caused by a low potassium level. Which response by the nurse is correct?
A) The student is right, because every diuretic lowers potassium and therefore raises digoxin toxicity risk
B) The chain from hypokalemia to digoxin toxicity explicitly excludes the potassium-sparing diuretics, and spironolactone raises potassium rather than lowering it
C) The student is right, but the effect takes several weeks to appear
D) Spironolactone has no clinically relevant effect on serum potassium
Answer: B
Rationale: The clause "excluding the potassium-sparing diuretics" is load-bearing (p. 453): the hypokalemia-to-digoxin-toxicity chain does not apply to spironolactone, triamterene, or amiloride, because those drugs produce hyperkalemia rather than hypokalemia.
Trap: (A) is the mirror error of the previous item, generalizing a rule that the chapter states with an exclusion built into it. (D) overcorrects in the other direction — spironolactone very much affects potassium, just in the opposite direction from what the student assumed.

Topic: loop diuretic therapy and furosemide safety
Subtopic: diuresis when kidney function is failing

Q: A patient with heart failure has a creatinine clearance of 20 mL/min, against a normal of 125 mL/min, and still needs diuresis. Which drug fits, and why?
A) furosemide, because loop diuretics continue to work even when creatinine clearance falls below 25 mL/min
B) hydrochlorothiazide, because thiazides are unaffected by declining kidney function
C) spironolactone, because aldosterone blockade does not depend on filtration
D) acetazolamide, because it acts at the proximal tubule before filtration declines
Answer: A
Rationale: Loop diuretics have a distinct advantage over thiazide diuretics in that their diuretic action continues even when creatinine clearance decreases below 25 mL/min; even when kidney function diminishes, loop diuretics can still work (p. 448).
Trap: (B) is the trap the number 25 exists to catch. Loops and thiazides share hypokalemia, so students treat them as interchangeable, but renal threshold is one of the two discriminators between them — the thiazides fail here, and this patient would simply not respond.

Q: A prescriber wants a thiazide-like diuretic for a patient whose creatinine clearance is 15 mL/min. Which drug is appropriate?
A) hydrochlorothiazide
B) chlorothiazide
C) metolazone, which remains effective to a creatinine clearance as low as 10 mL/min
D) indapamide
Answer: C
Rationale: Metolazone appears to be more potent than the thiazide diuretics and remains effective to a creatinine clearance as low as 10 mL/min (p. 453), which is why it is the thiazide-like drug combined with a loop diuretic in moderate to severe heart failure. The plain thiazides should not be used once clearance falls below the 30 to 50 mL/min range.
Trap: (A) is the mirror error of the previous item, reaching for the prototypical thiazide because it is the one everybody knows. (B) and (D) are the same error wearing different names — every drug on this list except metolazone loses effect well above a clearance of 15.

Subtopic: furosemide safety

Q: Before giving the first dose of furosemide, the nurse reviews its black box warning. What does it concern?
A) Fluid and electrolyte loss
B) Permanent ototoxicity at any dose
C) Stevens-Johnson syndrome
D) Increased mortality in heart failure
Answer: A
Rationale: Furosemide has a black box warning regarding fluid and electrolyte loss (pp. 449, 456). Its toxicity is electrolyte loss and dehydration, which can result in circulatory failure, and it is treated with electrolyte and fluid replacement.
Trap: (B) pulls because ototoxicity is the toxicity everyone files under furosemide, but the chapter describes it as a rare consequence of prolonged administration of high dosages, not as the boxed warning. (C) is torsemide's skin reaction; the skin reaction printed for furosemide is exfoliative dermatitis.

Subtopic: what each class does to calcium

Q: A patient with malignancy-related hypercalcemia needs a diuretic that will help lower the serum calcium. Which drug does the chapter name for that purpose?
A) furosemide, which increases the renal excretion of calcium in patients with hypercalcemia
B) hydrochlorothiazide, which pulls calcium into the urine
C) spironolactone, which blocks aldosterone-driven calcium retention
D) mannitol, which drags calcium out osmotically
Answer: A
Rationale: Loop diuretics are indicated to increase the renal excretion of calcium in patients with hypercalcemia (p. 448) — calcium goes out with the urine.
Trap: (B) is exactly backwards and is the harder half of the pair to keep straight, because thiazides raise serum calcium rather than lowering it. Giving a thiazide for hypercalcemia would worsen the very problem being treated.

Topic: thiazide and thiazide-like diuretic therapy
Subtopic: what each class does to calcium

Q: A patient with recurrent calcium stones from idiopathic hypercalciuria is started on hydrochlorothiazide. Which statement explains the choice?
A) Thiazides are associated with elevated calcium levels, and idiopathic hypercalciuria is one of their listed indications
B) Thiazides increase the renal excretion of calcium, which clears the stone-forming load
C) Thiazides chelate calcium in the renal tubule
D) Thiazides lower serum calcium by blocking aldosterone
Answer: A
Rationale: The thiazide metabolic signature includes elevated levels of calcium, lipids, glucose, and uric acid (p. 452), and idiopathic hypercalciuria sits in the thiazide indication list alongside hypertension, edema, and diabetes insipidus.
Trap: (B) is the mirror error of the previous item — it states the loop diuretic's calcium effect and attaches it to the thiazide. Both statements are printed in this chapter, five pages apart, which is exactly what makes the pair swappable.

Subtopic: dose ceiling and metabolic effects

Q: A patient's blood pressure remains elevated on hydrochlorothiazide 50 mg/day, and the prescriber asks whether doubling the dose to 100 mg/day would help. What is the nurse's understanding?
A) Dosages above 50 mg/day rarely produce additional clinical results and may only increase drug toxicity
B) Doubling the dose doubles the diuresis, since thiazide response is linear
C) The drug's effect will diminish in 2 to 4 days regardless of dose, because of the metabolic acidosis it induces
D) The dose should instead be split and given twice daily, at bedtime and on waking
Answer: A
Rationale: Dosages of hydrochlorothiazide exceeding 50 mg/day rarely produce additional clinical results and may only increase drug toxicity; this property is known as a ceiling effect (p. 453).
Trap: (C) describes the carbonic anhydrase inhibitors, whose effectiveness diminishes in 2 to 4 days because of the metabolic acidosis they induce. That is a tolerance phenomenon over time; the ceiling effect is a dose concept — more drug stops producing more result and adds only toxicity. (D) also puts half the dose at bedtime, which invites nocturia.

Q: A patient has been on hydrochlorothiazide for six months. Select all the laboratory changes that fit the thiazide metabolic profile.
A) Decreased potassium
B) Decreased sodium
C) Increased calcium
D) Increased glucose
E) Increased uric acid
F) Increased potassium
Answer: A, B, C, D, E
Rationale: The thiazide metabolic signature is mainly reduced potassium and sodium levels and elevated levels of calcium, lipids, glucose, and uric acid (p. 452); the mechanism section names hypokalemia and hypercalcemia specifically, plus hyperlipidemia, hyperglycemia, and hyperuricemia.
Trap: (F) is taken from the other side of the potassium split — it is what a potassium-sparing diuretic would produce. It is tempting here because the option list already contains four values moving up, so the pattern of the question pulls toward adding one more.

Q: A patient with type 2 diabetes is started on hydrochlorothiazide for hypertension. Which instruction is the priority?
A) Monitor blood glucose more closely than usual and report sustained increases
B) Avoid foods high in potassium
C) Take the dose at bedtime to limit daytime urination
D) Expect the oral antidiabetic drug to become more effective
Answer: A
Rationale: Thiazides cause hyperglycemia, and Table 28.6 gives the interaction with antidiabetic drugs as antagonism, producing a reduced therapeutic hypoglycemic effect, meaning increased blood glucose levels.
Trap: (B) is the potassium-sparing teaching misapplied — this patient is on a potassium-wasting drug and should be encouraged toward potassium-rich foods, not away from them. (C) contradicts the morning-dosing rule, since evening doses cause nocturia and loss of sleep. (D) states the interaction backwards.

Topic: potassium-sparing diuretic drug profiles
Subtopic: one class, two mechanisms

Q: A patient taking spironolactone asks how her drug differs from the triamterene her husband takes, since both were called potassium-sparing. Which response is accurate?
A) Spironolactone competitively binds to aldosterone receptors, while triamterene and amiloride do not bind those receptors at all
B) All three drugs work by competitively binding aldosterone receptors, and the difference is only in duration
C) Triamterene binds aldosterone receptors and spironolactone does not
D) The drugs differ only in cost, not in mechanism
Answer: A
Rationale: Spironolactone competitively binds to aldosterone receptors, found primarily in the distal tubule. Amiloride and triamterene do not bind to aldosterone receptors; they inhibit both aldosterone-induced and basal sodium resorption, working in both the distal tubule and collecting ducts (p. 450).
Trap: (B) assumes one class means one mechanism, which is the reasonable default and is wrong here. Everything else about these drugs follows from this split: spironolactone has the greatest antihypertensive activity of the three, while triamterene has little or no antihypertensive effect.

Subtopic: adverse effects belong to specific drugs

Q: A man taking a potassium-sparing diuretic reports breast enlargement and tenderness. Which drug is the likely cause, and why?
A) spironolactone, because it is a synthetic steroid that blocks aldosterone receptors
B) triamterene, because it reduces folic acid levels
C) amiloride, because it is most effective in metabolic alkalosis
D) Any of the three, since gynecomastia is a class effect
Answer: A
Rationale: Spironolactone is a synthetic steroid that blocks aldosterone receptors, and its printed adverse effects are gynecomastia, amenorrhea, irregular menses, and postmenopausal bleeding (p. 451). The endocrine profile follows directly from the drug being a steroid.
Trap: (D) is the reflex answer for a student who has learned these drugs as a block rather than individually. (B) attaches triamterene's real effect to the wrong presentation — folic acid reduction is genuinely printed for triamterene, which is what makes these two profiles swappable.

Q: A patient on triamterene develops flank pain, and urinalysis shows urinary casts. Which additional finding fits this drug's own adverse-effect profile?
A) Gynecomastia
B) Reduced folic acid levels, with the possibility of megaloblastic anemia
C) Postmenopausal bleeding
D) Amenorrhea
Answer: B
Rationale: Triamterene may reduce folic acid levels and cause the formation of kidney stones and urinary casts, and may also precipitate megaloblastic anemia, though adverse effects from triamterene are rare (p. 451).
Trap: (A), (C), and (D) are all spironolactone's endocrine effects, and this item is the mirror of the previous one: both drug profiles are printed on the same page, both are real, and the error is assigning the steroid effects to the non-steroid drug.

Subtopic: onset decides the acute case

Q: A patient in acute pulmonary edema needs diuresis now, and the potassium level is 3.2 mEq/L. A new nurse suggests spironolactone because it would protect the potassium. Which reasoning corrects that?
A) Spironolactone's onset is 1 to 3 days, while intravenous furosemide begins working in about 5 minutes
B) Spironolactone is contraindicated in heart failure
C) Spironolactone would raise the potassium too quickly to be safe in this setting
D) Spironolactone works only in the proximal tubule and cannot relieve pulmonary congestion
Answer: A
Rationale: Spironolactone's onset of action is 1 to 3 days, against intravenous furosemide's 5-minute onset and 6-hour duration. A stem that needs rapid diuresis is never answered with a potassium-sparing diuretic, however sound the electrolyte reasoning behind it.
Trap: (B) overstates the case in the opposite direction — spironolactone has actually been shown to reduce morbidity and mortality in patients with severe heart failure, and it is the drug most commonly prescribed for children with heart failure. The error being corrected here is timing, not indication. (D) misstates the site, which is the distal tubule.

Topic: mannitol and acetazolamide therapy
Subtopic: matching the drug to where the fluid or pressure is

Q: A patient with heart failure is in acute pulmonary edema, and a new nurse suggests acetazolamide because edema appears in its indication list. Which statement is correct?
A) Acetazolamide is much less potent than loop diuretics or thiazides, and its headline uses are open-angle glaucoma and high-altitude sickness; the edema use is reserved for edema that has become resistant to other diuretics
B) Acetazolamide is a good choice because it acts in the proximal tubule, where 60% to 70% of filtered sodium is returned
C) Acetazolamide is a good choice because its diuretic effect builds over the first 2 to 4 days of therapy
D) Acetazolamide is contraindicated in any patient who has edema
Answer: A
Rationale: The carbonic anhydrase inhibitors are much less potent than loop diuretics or thiazides, and acetazolamide's indications are glaucoma, edema, and high-altitude sickness, with the edema use specified as heart-failure edema that has become resistant to other diuretics (p. 447).
Trap: (B) reaches for the largest sodium figure in the chapter, but blocking carbonic anhydrase does not translate that figure into potency, which is why the chapter ranks these drugs last on effect. (C) inverts the printed fact: because of the metabolic acidosis they induce, their effectiveness diminishes in 2 to 4 days.

Q: The same patient's pulmonary edema is not improving, and mannitol is suggested. What does the nurse recognize?
A) Pulmonary edema is a contraindication to mannitol, and loop diuretics are used instead
B) Mannitol is appropriate because it works along the entire nephron
C) Mannitol is appropriate because it produces only a slight loss of electrolytes
D) Mannitol may be given if the patient's potassium is normal
Answer: A
Rationale: Mannitol's contraindications are known drug allergy, severe renal disease, pulmonary edema — with loop diuretics used instead — and active intracranial bleeding (p. 450). Its own adverse effects include pulmonary congestion, so it can worsen the presenting problem.
Trap: (C) is true of mannitol and irrelevant here, which is what makes it attractive to a student reasoning from the low-potassium worry in the previous scenario. Electrolyte sparing does not override a printed contraindication.

Q: A patient with a traumatic head injury has a rising intracranial pressure. Which drug fits, and on what mechanism?
A) mannitol, a nonabsorbable solute that increases osmotic pressure in the glomerular filtrate and pulls fluid, primarily water, into the renal tubules
B) furosemide, because loop diuretics are the most potent class available
C) mannitol, because it promotes sufficient sodium excretion to drain peripheral and cerebral fluid alike
D) acetazolamide, because it lowers pressure in the eye and therefore in the skull
Answer: A
Rationale: Mannitol is indicated to reduce intracranial pressure and treat cerebral edema, along with the early oliguric phase of acute renal failure, excretion of toxic substances, and intraocular pressure unresponsive to other drugs (pp. 449 to 450).
Trap: (B) is the mirror error of the previous item — fluid in the lungs is the furosemide answer, and pressure in the head or the eye is the mannitol answer. (C) attaches a true drug to a false reason: mannitol is explicitly not indicated for peripheral edema, because it does not promote sufficient sodium excretion.

Subtopic: administering mannitol

Q: A nurse takes a 100-mL vial of 20% mannitol from the warmer to give to a patient with raised intracranial pressure. Which action is required?
A) Inspect the container for precipitants and administer it intravenously through a filter
B) Give it orally with a full glass of water to increase the osmotic load
C) Discard the vial, because mannitol should never be stored warm
D) Give it by rapid intravenous push without a filter, since the concentration is below the 15% crystallization threshold
Answer: A
Rationale: Mannitol may crystallize when exposed to low temperatures, which is more likely when concentrations exceed 15%. Because of this, mannitol is always administered intravenously through a filter, vials are often stored in a warmer, and the container is visually inspected for precipitants before administration (p. 450).
Trap: (D) is the reasoning trap built into the numbers — 15% is the concentration above which crystallization is more likely, so a student infers that a lower concentration exempts the filter, and then misreads 20% as being under the threshold besides. The filter rule is unconditional. (C) inverts the storage fact: warming is the precaution, not the hazard.

Topic: diuretic drug interactions
Subtopic: which toxicity the combination produces

Q: A patient receiving intravenous furosemide is started on gentamicin for a gram-negative infection. Which risk does this combination specifically increase?
A) Ototoxicity, from an additive effect on neurotoxicity
B) Nephrotoxicity, from additive renal tubular injury
C) Hepatotoxicity, from competing metabolic pathways
D) Bone marrow suppression
Answer: A
Rationale: Table 28.3 gives aminoglycosides and vancomycin as an additive effect with loop diuretics, producing increased neurotoxicity, especially ototoxicity (p. 449). Chapter 39 states the same interaction from the antibiotic's side.
Trap: (B) is the plausible-but-adjacent pick, and it pulls because nephrotoxicity is the aminoglycoside's own signature toxicity — but it is not what the interaction adds. The distinction matters clinically: Chapter 39 notes that ototoxicity is likely to be permanent, whereas nephrotoxicity is usually reversible once the offending drug is withdrawn.

Q: A patient maintained on lithium is started on hydrochlorothiazide for hypertension. What should the nurse anticipate?
A) The lithium level will rise, and lithium toxicity may occur
B) The lithium level will fall, and the dose may need to be increased
C) The lithium will blunt the thiazide's diuretic effect
D) No interaction, since the drugs are cleared by different routes
Answer: A
Rationale: Thiazides decrease lithium clearance and loop diuretics decrease its renal excretion, both producing increased lithium toxicity (Tables 28.3, 28.6); with potassium-sparing diuretics, lithium toxicity may occur because of the hyperkalemia (p. 454). No diuretic in this chapter lowers lithium.
Trap: (B) is the direction reversal, and it is tempting because a drug that increases urine output sounds as though it should flush lithium out. The printed mechanisms run the other way — the diuretic reduces how much lithium leaves.

Q: A patient stable on furosemide begins taking ibuprofen daily for arthritis, and urine output drops. Which explanation fits?
A) NSAIDs inhibit renal prostaglandins, which loop diuretics activate, so diuretic activity decreases
B) The diuretic blunts the analgesic effect of the NSAID
C) The NSAID displaces furosemide from plasma proteins, shortening its duration
D) The two drugs have additive effects on prostaglandin activity, causing excessive diuresis
Answer: A
Rationale: Both interaction tables give the NSAID mechanism as inhibition of renal prostaglandins and the result as decreased diuretic activity. The chapter adds that NSAIDs may diminish the reduction in vascular resistance induced by loop diuretics because the two drug classes have opposite effects on prostaglandin activity (p. 449).
Trap: (B) reverses the direction of a real interaction — the tables state the loss on the diuretic's side, not the analgesic's. (D) gets the pharmacology exactly backwards by calling opposite effects additive; prostaglandins are what the diuretic recruits and what the NSAID blocks, so the NSAID wins and the diuresis fails.
