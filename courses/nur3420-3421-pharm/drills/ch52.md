# Chapter 52 drills — Antiemetic and Antinausea Drugs

Authored from `sources/Ch52_Fact_Bank.md` (Lilley, Collins & Snyder, 10th ed.).

Blueprint weight is **2 questions of 121 (1.7%), depth `focused`** — the
smallest weight in the course, tied with Chapters 28 and 45. A `focused`
chapter earns 20 to 30 drills, and this file carries **30**, at the top of the
band because the chapter is seven drug classes wide even though the exam only
takes two items from it.

Note on the chapter number: the blueprint source document labels its
6-question **Acid-Controlling Drugs** row "Chapter 52." That row is
**Chapter 50**. This file is the real Chapter 52, worth **2**, which is what
`blueprint.json` carries.

The organizing spine is the chapter's own grid — class, receptor blocked, site,
signature adverse effect — because that grid is where the distractors come
from: swap the receptor, swap the site, or swap the signature adverse effect
and three wrong options fall out of one right one.

Every distinction the chapter states in both directions is drilled from both
ends: CTZ signalling and vomiting center initiating, anticholinergic and
antihistamine mechanism, prokinetic and antidopaminergic indication, scopolamine
and meclizine, ondansetron first line and dronabinol second line, before the
start of chemotherapy and before the end of surgery, promethazine's permitted
intravenous route and hydroxyzine's forbidden one.

**Deliberately left undrilled at this weight**, and where to find it if it ever
matters: all pharmacokinetics tables (onset, peak, half-life, duration); the
full usual-adult-dosage column, including the promethazine 12.5 mg from
25 mg/mL volume calculation; the obsolete printed pregnancy categories; the
ginger herbal box; amisulpride, Akynzeo, and rolapitant, which the chapter names
once each with almost no detail; the syrup of ipecac pediatric box, which is
about an emetic rather than an antiemetic; and the nursing-process assessment,
planning, and evaluation narrative except where it produced a trap. Two items of
exam weight cannot carry a seven-class chapter in full, and the cut fell on
single-drug numbers rather than on any confusable pair or safety rule.

Topic: nausea pathway and antiemetic terminology
Subtopic: chemoreceptor trigger zone versus vomiting center

Q: A nursing student asks how a chemotherapy drug circulating in the bloodstream ends up producing vomiting. Which sequence should the nurse describe?
A) The chemoreceptor trigger zone detects the drug and sends neurotransmitter signals to the vomiting center
B) The vomiting center detects the drug and sends neurotransmitter signals to the chemoreceptor trigger zone
C) The drug acts directly on the vomiting center, with no involvement of the chemoreceptor trigger zone
D) The drug acts on the vestibular apparatus, which then signals the chemoreceptor trigger zone
Answer: A
Rationale: The chapter states that neurotransmitter signals are sent to the vomiting center from the chemoreceptor trigger zone. The CTZ is the area of the brain involved in the sensation of nausea and the action of vomiting, and Fig. 52.1 shows the labyrinth, GI tract, and cerebral cortex feeding the CTZ, with the CTZ feeding the vomiting center in the medulla.
Trap: (B) reverses the direction of the signal, and it is the reflex pick because the key terms box defines both structures with the same opening words, "the area of the brain that is involved in." The signal runs CTZ to VC, never the reverse.

Q: A patient asks which part of the brain actually sets off the retching and vomiting once the signal has arrived. Which structure should the nurse name?
A) The chemoreceptor trigger zone, because it senses emetogenic substances in the blood
B) The vomiting center in the medulla, because it initiates the physiologic events that lead to nausea and vomiting
C) The vestibular nuclei, because they receive input from the inner ear
D) The reticular formation, because it is where the tetrahydrocannabinoids act
Answer: B
Rationale: The vomiting center is defined as the area of the brain involved in stimulating the physiologic events that lead to nausea and vomiting, and the body text says it is responsible for initiating those events. Fig. 52.1 places it in the medulla.
Trap: (A) is the mirror error of the previous item. The CTZ senses and signals; the VC initiates. Because both key-term definitions begin identically, the discriminator has to come from the verbs.

Subtopic: nausea versus emesis

Q: A postoperative patient reports feeling queasy and says the urge to vomit keeps coming and going, but nothing has come up. How should the nurse document this?
A) Emesis, because the urge to vomit is itself the defining feature of emesis
B) Nausea, because nausea is the sensation that often leads to the urge to vomit
C) Neither term applies until gastric contents are actually expelled
D) Either term is correct, because the chapter uses nausea and emesis interchangeably
Answer: B
Rationale: Nausea is the sensation often leading to the urge to vomit, described elsewhere in the chapter as an unpleasant feeling that often precedes vomiting. Emesis is the forcible emptying or expulsion of gastric and occasionally intestinal contents through the mouth, which has not occurred here.
Trap: (A) is the reflex pick for students who treat the two words as interchangeable. Nausea is the feeling; emesis is the event, and only emesis requires forcible expulsion. An antiemetic is defined against both, as a drug given to relieve nausea and vomiting, not vomiting alone.

Subtopic: neurotransmitters in the vomiting pathway

Q: A nurse is reviewing the neurotransmitters involved in nausea and vomiting alongside the drug classes that act on them. Which of these neurotransmitters listed by the chapter are blocked by one of the seven antiemetic categories? Select all that apply.
A) Acetylcholine
B) Dopamine
C) Histamine
D) Prostaglandins
E) Serotonin
F) Substance P
Answer: A, B, C, E, F
Rationale: Table 52.1 lists six neurotransmitters in the vomiting pathway, but only five map to a blocking class in Tables 52.2 and 52.3: acetylcholine to the anticholinergics, histamine to the antihistamines, dopamine to the antidopaminergics and the prokinetics, serotonin to the serotonin blockers, and substance P or neurokinin 1 to the neurokinin antagonists.
Trap: (D) is the option the whole item turns on. Prostaglandins act in the GI tract and are printed right in Table 52.1, so they look as though they belong, but no antiemetic category in this chapter blocks them. The tetrahydrocannabinoids are the opposite case: one of the seven categories, but described only by anatomic site, with no receptor in Table 52.1 at all.

Topic: antiemetic classes and receptor targets
Subtopic: anticholinergics versus antihistamines

Q: A patient taking scopolamine for motion sickness and a patient taking meclizine for motion sickness both report dry mouth and blurred vision. How should the nurse explain the difference in mechanism?
A) Scopolamine blocks acetylcholine receptors directly, while meclizine blocks histamine 1 receptors and thereby prevents acetylcholine from binding
B) Scopolamine blocks histamine 1 receptors, while meclizine blocks acetylcholine receptors directly
C) Scopolamine acts at the chemoreceptor trigger zone, while meclizine acts at the vestibular nuclei
D) Only scopolamine has anticholinergic activity; meclizine's effects have nothing to do with acetylcholine
Answer: A
Rationale: Table 52.3 says anticholinergics block ACh receptors in the vestibular nuclei and reticular formation, while antihistamines block histamine 1 receptors, thereby preventing ACh from binding to receptors in the vestibular nuclei. The chapter adds that antihistamines also have potent anticholinergic activity, which is why the dry mouth and blurred vision set appears under both classes in Table 52.4.
Trap: (D) is the reflex pick because the class name antihistamine says nothing about acetylcholine. Table 52.1 gives acetylcholine and histamine 1 the identical site entry and the shared adverse effect profile cannot discriminate these two classes, so only the receptor actually bound can.

Q: A patient is started on dimenhydrinate and the nurse anticipates dry mouth and urinary retention. Which explanation is correct?
A) Dimenhydrinate blocks acetylcholine receptors directly in the vestibular nuclei
B) Dimenhydrinate blocks histamine 1 receptors and also has potent anticholinergic activity
C) Dimenhydrinate blocks dopamine in the chemoreceptor trigger zone
D) Dimenhydrinate blocks serotonin receptors in the GI tract
Answer: B
Rationale: Dimenhydrinate is one of the antihistamine antiemetics along with meclizine and diphenhydramine. Table 52.3 says antihistamines block histamine 1 receptors, thereby preventing ACh from binding in the vestibular nuclei, and these drugs also have potent anticholinergic activity. Table 52.4 gives this class dizziness, drowsiness, and confusion plus blurred vision, dilated pupils, dry mouth, and urinary retention.
Trap: (A) is the mirror error of the previous item, handing the anticholinergic's direct receptor blockade to the antihistamine. Same destination, different door: anticholinergics block acetylcholine directly, antihistamines get there through histamine 1.

Q: A patient says a friend told her that "a histamine blocker" will prevent her motion sickness, and she already has a histamine 2 receptor blocker at home for gastric acid control. What should the nurse explain?
A) The histamine 2 blocker will work, because all histamine blockers act on the vestibular pathways
B) Only histamine 1 blockers are antiemetics; histamine 2 blockers act at the parietal cell for gastric acid control and are a different drug group
C) The histamine 2 blocker will work, but only if it is taken 1 hour before travel
D) Neither histamine 1 nor histamine 2 blockers are used for motion sickness
Answer: B
Rationale: The chapter warns directly that the antihistamine antiemetics are not to be confused with histamine 2 receptor blockers, used for gastric acid control, which are covered in Chapter 50. Histamine 1 blockers act on the vestibular and labyrinthine pathways of the inner ear; histamine 2 blockers control gastric acid.
Trap: (A) is the reflex pick because both drug groups are called histamine blockers in ordinary speech. The subscript is the entire distinction, and this is a confusion the chapter flags itself rather than one a test writer invented.

Subtopic: antidopaminergics versus prokinetics

Q: A patient with delayed gastric emptying reports nausea after every meal. Which antiemetic class is aimed at that problem?
A) Antidopaminergics, because they block dopamine in the chemoreceptor trigger zone
B) Prokinetics, because along with blocking dopamine in the chemoreceptor trigger zone their primary action is to stimulate peristalsis and empty stomach contents into the duodenum
C) Serotonin blockers, because they act at the GI tract, chemoreceptor trigger zone, and vomiting center
D) Anticholinergics, because they reduce gastrointestinal secretions
Answer: B
Rationale: Both antidopaminergics and prokinetics block dopamine in the chemoreceptor trigger zone, and the key points say so outright. What separates the prokinetics is that their primary action is to stimulate peristalsis in the GI tract, promoting emptying of stomach contents into the duodenum. Table 52.2 gives prokinetics the indications delayed gastric emptying, gastroesophageal reflux, and nausea and vomiting.
Trap: (A) is the reflex pick precisely because it is true as far as it goes; both classes do block dopamine at the CTZ, so a partially correct mechanism feels like the answer. The prokinetic row of Table 52.3 is the only line in the chapter where a class stimulates a receptor rather than blocking one, and motility is the discriminator.

Q: A hospitalized patient has intractable hiccups and also needs an injectable antiemetic. Which class and drug fit both needs?
A) Prokinetics, such as metoclopramide, because they empty the stomach
B) Antidopaminergics, such as prochlorperazine, because the class also treats psychotic disorders and intractable hiccups
C) Serotonin blockers, such as ondansetron, because the class has very few adverse effects
D) Neurokinin antagonists, such as aprepitant, because the class covers acute and delayed vomiting
Answer: B
Rationale: Table 52.2 gives the antidopaminergics the indications psychotic disorders including mania, schizophrenia, and anxiety, intractable hiccups, and nausea and vomiting. Prochlorperazine, especially in the injectable form, is used frequently in the hospital setting.
Trap: (A) is the mirror error of the previous item. Since both classes block dopamine in the chemoreceptor trigger zone, the stem's cue decides: gastric emptying, reflux, or motility points to the prokinetic, while psychosis, hiccups, or an injectable hospital antiemetic points to the antidopaminergic.

Subtopic: sites of action across the pathway

Q: A prescriber wants an antiemetic that acts at every level of the vomiting pathway for a patient starting chemotherapy. Which class does the chapter describe as acting at all three sites?
A) Anticholinergics
B) Antihistamines
C) Serotonin blockers
D) Neurokinin antagonists
Answer: C
Rationale: Table 52.3 says serotonin blockers block serotonin receptors in the GI tract, the chemoreceptor trigger zone, and the vomiting center, and Fig. 52.2 places only the serotonin blockers at the vomiting center. Prokinetics reach two sites; every other class reaches one.
Trap: (A) pulls because Table 52.1 lists acetylcholine as acting at the vomiting center in the brain. That entry names where the neurotransmitter acts, not where the drug was placed, and Fig. 52.2 does not put anticholinergics at the vomiting center. (D) reaches the brain stem only.

Q: A prescriber asks the nurse which conditions the prokinetic class is indicated for. Which does the chapter list? Select all that apply.
A) Motion sickness
B) Vertigo
C) Gastroesophageal reflux
D) Delayed gastric emptying
E) Gastrointestinal obstruction
F) Nausea and vomiting
Answer: C, D, F
Rationale: Table 52.2 gives the prokinetics exactly three indications: delayed gastric emptying, gastroesophageal reflux, and nausea and vomiting. Motion sickness and vertigo belong to the anticholinergics and the antihistamines, and metoclopramide is contraindicated in gastrointestinal obstruction.
Trap: (E) is the sharpest distractor in the set because it inverts a contraindication into an indication, which is the most damaging form this tactic takes: stimulating peristalsis against a mechanical blockage is exactly what the contraindication forbids. (A) and (B) borrow the indications of the two motion-sickness classes.

Topic: antiemetic drug selection by indication
Subtopic: motion sickness versus vertigo

Q: A patient is leaving on a 5-day cruise and wants something for motion sickness that will not have to be taken over and over. Which drug fits?
A) meclizine, taken 1 hour before departure and repeated daily during travel
B) scopolamine transdermal patch, applied behind one ear every 3 days
C) prochlorperazine by injection each morning
D) ondansetron 30 minutes before each meal
Answer: B
Rationale: Scopolamine is the primary anticholinergic drug used as an antiemetic, and its transdermal patch is applied to a hairless area behind one ear every 3 days, releasing a total of 1 mg over 72 hours. That 72-hour duration is what makes it the travel option.
Trap: (A) is a genuine motion-sickness option and not a throwaway, which is what makes it pull: meclizine is dosed 25 to 50 mg 1 hour before travel and repeated daily during travel. The cue in the stem is the patch and the multi-day duration, and that points to scopolamine.

Q: A patient with dizziness and vertigo asks for a drug that treats vertigo specifically. Which drug carries that separate indication?
A) scopolamine, because it corrects an imbalance between acetylcholine and norepinephrine
B) meclizine, because it treats the dizziness, vertigo, and nausea and vomiting associated with motion sickness
C) metoclopramide, because it stimulates peristalsis in the GI tract
D) dronabinol, because it acts on the reticular formation and cerebral cortex
Answer: B
Rationale: The meclizine profile states that it treats the dizziness, vertigo, and nausea and vomiting associated with motion sickness, and it is the only drug in the chapter's dosage table carrying a separate treatment-of-vertigo indication.
Trap: (A) is the mirror error of the previous item. Both drugs treat motion sickness, both are contraindicated in narrow-angle glaucoma, and both sedate, so the stem's cue decides: a patch or travel points to scopolamine, while the word vertigo points to meclizine.

Q: A patient with narrow-angle glaucoma asks for something for motion sickness before a long car trip. What should the nurse anticipate?
A) A scopolamine patch will be ordered, because the transdermal route avoids systemic anticholinergic effects
B) Meclizine will be ordered, because the glaucoma concern applies only to scopolamine
C) Another antiemetic or antinausea drug must be prescribed, because the glaucoma concern applies to both scopolamine and the antihistamines
D) Either drug may be used as long as intraocular pressure is checked afterward
Answer: C
Rationale: Scopolamine is contraindicated in patients with glaucoma, and the nursing process section extends the point explicitly: the same concern regarding use in patients with narrow-angle glaucoma applies to antihistamines such as meclizine. The taught action is that another antiemetic or antinausea drug must be prescribed.
Trap: (B) is the reflex pick because the contraindication is printed inside the scopolamine profile and not inside the meclizine profile. Glaucoma rules out both of the motion-sickness options, which makes this stem a dead end rather than a choice between two drugs.

Subtopic: first-line versus second-line chemotherapy antiemetics

Q: A patient is admitted for a first cycle of highly emetogenic chemotherapy and has orders for both ondansetron and dronabinol. Which should the nurse expect to give?
A) dronabinol, because it also stimulates appetite in patients with cancer
B) ondansetron, because dronabinol is generally used as a second-line drug after treatment with other antiemetics has failed
C) dronabinol, because tetrahydrocannabinoids act on the cortex where anticipatory nausea begins
D) Neither, until the patient actually reports nausea
Answer: B
Rationale: Dronabinol is generally used as a second-line drug after treatment with other antiemetics has failed, and the chapter's case study models exactly that sequence, adding dronabinol only after 2 weeks of failed ondansetron. The key points name granisetron and ondansetron as most commonly used for the prevention of chemotherapy-induced nausea and vomiting.
Trap: (D) fails the chapter's core principle, since antiemetics for chemotherapy are prophylactic and given 30 to 60 minutes before the drug rather than held for symptoms. (A) attaches a true fact about dronabinol to a decision that fact does not govern; a first round of chemotherapy points to the serotonin blocker.

Q: A patient has had nausea and vomiting through 2 weeks of chemotherapy despite scheduled ondansetron. Which drug does the chapter support adding, and on what grounds?
A) dronabinol, because it is generally used as a second-line drug after treatment with other antiemetics has failed
B) dronabinol, because it is the first-line drug for chemotherapy-induced nausea and vomiting
C) phosphorated carbohydrate solution, because it acts directly on the walls of the GI tract
D) meclizine, because it treats dizziness and vertigo
Answer: A
Rationale: Dronabinol is the only commercially available tetrahydrocannabinoid, a synthetic derivative approved in 1985, and is generally used as a second-line drug after treatment with other antiemetics has failed. It also stimulates appetite and promotes weight gain in patients with AIDS and cancer.
Trap: (B) is the mirror error of the previous item, and it is unusually seductive because it names the same drug with only the sequence inverted. (C) names the phosphorated carbohydrate solution, which is explicitly not sufficient for nausea as severe as that associated with cancer chemotherapy.

Q: A patient asks whether the over-the-counter phosphorated carbohydrate solution in her medicine cabinet can be used for her chemotherapy-related vomiting. What is the nurse's best response?
A) It is appropriate, because it acts directly on the walls of the GI tract and reduces cramping
B) It is not sufficient for treatment of nausea as severe as that associated with cancer chemotherapy
C) It may be used, but only after other antiemetics have not worked
D) It should be reserved for severe chemotherapy-induced nausea
Answer: B
Rationale: Phosphorated carbohydrate solution has a direct local action on the walls of the GI tract, where it reduces cramping, but the chapter states twice that it is not sufficient for treatment of more severe nausea symptoms such as those associated with cancer chemotherapy. Its unlabeled use is for morning sickness.
Trap: (C) describes dronabinol, the second-line drug, and is the answer a student reaches for after drilling that sequence. (D) inverts the fact outright. This drug's local GI action with no central receptor blockade at all is what confines it to mild nausea.

Subtopic: dronabinol profile

Q: A nurse is reviewing dronabinol before administering it. Which statements about this drug are accurate? Select all that apply.
A) It is indicated for nausea and vomiting associated with chemotherapy
B) It is the drug named for treatment of hyperemesis gravidarum
C) It stimulates appetite and promotes weight gain in patients with AIDS and cancer
D) It can cause extrapyramidal symptoms
E) It can cause drowsiness, dizziness, and euphoria
Answer: A, C, E
Rationale: Dronabinol is a synthetic tetrahydrocannabinoid indicated for chemotherapy-induced nausea and vomiting and for anorexia associated with weight loss in patients with AIDS and cancer. Table 52.4 gives its central nervous system effects as drowsiness, dizziness, anxiety, confusion, and euphoria, along with visual disturbances and dry mouth.
Trap: (B) borrows ondansetron's profile, which is where hyperemesis gravidarum appears. (D) borrows the antidopaminergic and prokinetic rows of Table 52.4. Both false options are lifted from a neighboring drug's profile rather than invented, which is what makes an all-or-nothing item hard.

Topic: antiemetic prophylaxis and timing
Subtopic: postoperative versus chemotherapy anchors

Q: A serotonin blocker is ordered to prevent postoperative nausea and vomiting. When should it be given?
A) Approximately 30 minutes before the beginning of the surgical procedure
B) Approximately 30 minutes before the end of the surgical procedure
C) 30 to 60 minutes after the patient arrives in the recovery area
D) When the patient first reports nausea after surgery
Answer: B
Rationale: For postoperative nausea and vomiting the chapter directs that serotonin blockers be given approximately 30 minutes before the end of the surgical procedure, so the drug is already on board as the patient wakes.
Trap: (A) is the distractor form this fact always takes: same class, same 30 minutes, opposite anchor, and the two phrases differ by one word. Surgical nausea is prevented at the back end of the procedure; chemotherapy nausea is prevented at the front end.

Q: The same serotonin blocker is ordered for a patient about to receive chemotherapy. When should it be given?
A) 30 to 60 minutes before the start of chemotherapy
B) 30 minutes before the end of the chemotherapy infusion
C) 4 hours before the start of chemotherapy
D) At the first sign of nausea
Answer: A
Rationale: For chemotherapy the chapter gives 30 to 60 minutes before the start of chemotherapy, and states more generally that antiemetics are given 30 to 60 minutes before a chemotherapy drug is administered and may also be given during the treatment.
Trap: (B) is the mirror error of the previous item, dragging the surgical anchor onto a chemotherapy stem. (D) fails the chapter's default of prophylaxis rather than rescue.

Subtopic: prophylaxis versus rescue

Q: A patient receiving a highly emetogenic chemotherapy drug says he would rather wait and take the antiemetic only if he actually gets sick. What is the nurse's best response?
A) That is reasonable, because antiemetics work equally well once nausea has started
B) Antiemetics for chemotherapy are given beforehand so the trigger zone is blocked before it fires, though a dose may also be given during the treatment
C) Antiemetics must never be given once nausea has already begun
D) He should take it 4 hours before the infusion instead
Answer: B
Rationale: The chapter's default is prophylaxis rather than rescue: antiemetics are given 30 to 60 minutes before a chemotherapy drug is administered, and they may also be given during the chemotherapy treatment. Chapter 45 calls premedication 30 to 60 minutes before the preferred approach.
Trap: (A) is the reflex answer because it matches how patients use antiemetics at home, but by the time nausea is reported the chemoreceptor trigger zone has already fired. (C) overcorrects into an absolute the chapter does not make, since a breakthrough dose during treatment is supported.

Subtopic: acute versus delayed emesis

Q: A patient's chemotherapy regimen produces vomiting both on the day of treatment and for several days afterward. Which antiemetic class does the chapter describe as inhibiting both the acute and the delayed phases?
A) Anticholinergics
B) Antihistamines
C) Neurokinin antagonists
D) Tetrahydrocannabinoids
Answer: C
Rationale: Table 52.2 gives the neurokinin antagonists the indication acute and delayed vomiting associated with chemotherapy, and the body text says these drugs inhibit the acute and delayed phases of chemotherapy-induced emesis. Aprepitant, fosaprepitant, and rolapitant are the drugs in this class.
Trap: (D) pulls because dronabinol is also a chemotherapy antiemetic and students sort chemotherapy stems by drug rather than by phase. The acute-and-delayed phrasing is printed only for the neurokinin antagonists, so when a stem names the delayed phase that class is in the answer.

Topic: antiemetic route safety and administration
Subtopic: promethazine and hydroxyzine route rules

Q: A nurse is preparing to give intravenous promethazine to a patient with a peripheral line. Which action is correct?
A) Give it undiluted as a rapid intravenous push through the port closest to the patient's vein
B) Dilute it in at least 10 mL of fluid and give it in a running intravenous line at the port farthest from the patient's vein, using a large-bore vein
C) Give it subcutaneously if intravenous access cannot be obtained
D) Give it into a hand or wrist vein so that any reaction is easy to see
Answer: B
Rationale: Promethazine is the chapter's only high-alert drug. The intravenous route is not the preferred route but is commonly used, and when it is used the drug is best diluted in at least 10 mL of fluid, the more dilute the better, and given in a running intravenous line at the port farthest from the patient's vein or through a large-bore vein, not a hand or wrist vein. Therapy must be discontinued immediately if burning or pain occurs.
Trap: (D) names the exact vein the chapter forbids, and it pulls because a small visible vein feels easier to monitor. (C) is forbidden outright: promethazine is not to be given subcutaneously. If promethazine is inadvertently given intraarterially instead of intravenously, severe tissue damage often requiring amputation can occur.

Q: A patient has an order for intramuscular hydroxyzine for nausea, and the intramuscular site is bruised. The nurse considers giving the dose intravenously instead. What is correct?
A) Hydroxyzine may be given intravenously if it is diluted in at least 10 mL of fluid
B) Hydroxyzine must never be given by the intravenous route, and intravenous, intraarterial, or subcutaneous administration can cause tissue damage, thrombosis, and gangrene
C) Hydroxyzine may be given intravenously but never intraarterially
D) Hydroxyzine may be given subcutaneously as the alternative route
Answer: B
Rationale: The Safety and Quality Improvement box titled Right Route Is Essential states that hydroxyzine is given orally or intramuscularly only, and that intravenous, intraarterial, or subcutaneous administration of hydroxyzine can result in significant tissue damage, thrombosis, and gangrene.
Trap: (A) is the mirror error of the previous item, carrying promethazine's dilution rule across to hydroxyzine. These two drugs are the safety box's two examples and they sit one word apart: for promethazine the intravenous route is allowed but not preferred, while for hydroxyzine it is never allowed.

Subtopic: transdermal and long-term administration

Q: A patient is being taught to use a scopolamine patch before travel. Which instruction is correct?
A) Apply the patch to the shoulder at least 4 hours before travel and leave it in place for 72 hours
B) Apply the patch to a hairless, cleansed, dried, nonirritated area behind one ear at least 4 hours before travel, rotating sites and washing hands before and after
C) Apply the patch to the temple the day before travel and replace it every day
D) If the patch becomes dislodged, press it back into place right away
Answer: B
Rationale: The dosage table directs applying 1 patch to a hairless area behind 1 ear every 3 days, starting at least 4 hours before travel, and the patient teaching section adds cleansed, dried, nonirritated skin, rotation of sites, and handwashing before and after. If the patch is dislodged, the residual drug must be washed off and a fresh patch put in place.
Trap: (D) is the reflex action a patient would take on their own and it is wrong twice over, since the residual drug has to be washed off and a fresh patch applied. (A) and (C) move the site off the area behind the ear, which is the only site the chapter teaches.

Q: A patient has been taking metoclopramide for several months. Which finding reflects the problem named in the FDA advisory that specifically concerns long-term use of this drug?
A) Acute dystonia within hours of the first dose
B) Tardive dyskinesia, an involuntary neurologic movement
C) Orthostatic hypotension on standing
D) Prolonged QT interval on the electrocardiogram
Answer: B
Rationale: In 2009 the FDA issued a public health advisory regarding the potential for development of tardive dyskinesia with long-term use of metoclopramide. Tardive dyskinesia is described as an involuntary neurologic movement, and it is the long-term-use problem, whereas extrapyramidal symptoms are what the nurse assesses at baseline, monitors throughout therapy, and reports immediately.
Trap: (A) names dystonia, which Table 52.4 genuinely does list for the prokinetics, so it is a real adverse effect placed in the wrong time frame. Extrapyramidal symptoms and tardive dyskinesia sit in the same cell of that table, which is what makes the pair invertible. (D) belongs to the serotonin blockers and to droperidol.

Topic: antiemetic interactions and contraindications
Subtopic: aprepitant as the interaction-heavy drug

Q: A patient is starting aprepitant with a chemotherapy regimen. Which of the patient's current drugs or drug groups does this chapter identify as interacting with aprepitant? Select all that apply.
A) digoxin
B) warfarin
C) oral contraceptives
D) nonsteroidal antiinflammatory drugs
E) corticosteroids
Answer: B, C, E
Rationale: Neurokinin antagonists may induce the metabolism of warfarin, so the INR must be checked before each cycle, and they may reduce the effectiveness of oral contraceptives. Aprepitant may also increase the bioavailability of corticosteroids including dexamethasone and methylprednisolone, whose dosages may need to be adjusted by 25% to 50%. The chapter names these three separately on three different pages.
Trap: (A) and (D) appear nowhere in this chapter's interaction text, and in an all-or-nothing item the two absent options are the whole question. Aprepitant is a major inhibitor of cytochrome P-450, so the real caution list is CYP3A4 substrates such as azole antifungals, clarithromycin, diltiazem, nicardipine, protease inhibitors, and verapamil.

Q: A prescriber asks whether aprepitant can simply replace ondansetron for a patient on highly emetogenic chemotherapy. What should the nurse know about aprepitant?
A) It blocks the same serotonin receptors as ondansetron, so it is a direct substitute
B) In contrast to other antiemetics it has little affinity for serotonin and dopamine receptors, and it is used in conjunction with serotonin blockers and glucocorticoids to augment them
C) It blocks dopamine in the chemoreceptor trigger zone, so it also treats intractable hiccups
D) It has no significant drug interactions, so it is the safer of the two drugs
Answer: B
Rationale: The aprepitant profile states that in contrast to other antiemetics this drug has little affinity for serotonin and dopamine receptors; it inhibits the substance P and neurokinin receptors in the brain stem. Neurokinin blockers are used in conjunction with serotonin blockers and glucocorticoids, and their use augments those drugs, because combining categories blocks more than one pathway.
Trap: (D) is exactly backwards, and it is the reflex pick because the two drugs are given together and their profiles blur. Aprepitant is the most interaction-heavy drug in the chapter; the serotonin blockers are the class with no significant drug interactions. Aprepitant adds a pathway rather than replacing one.

Subtopic: the classes that stay clean, and one that loses effect

Q: A patient taking ondansetron is worried about interactions because he takes several other medications. What does the chapter say about this class?
A) Serotonin blockers are major inhibitors of cytochrome P-450 and interact with many drugs
B) Serotonin blockers have no significant known drug interactions, and the tetrahydrocannabinoids are the only other class the chapter describes the same way
C) Serotonin blockers may induce the metabolism of warfarin and reduce the effectiveness of oral contraceptives
D) Serotonin blockers must not be combined with any other antiemetic class
Answer: B
Rationale: The chapter states that serotonin blockers and tetrahydrocannabinoids have no significant drug interactions, and the ondansetron profile repeats it: very specific actions, very few adverse effects, and no significant drug interactions known to occur. Ondansetron's only listed contraindication is known drug allergy.
Trap: (A) and (C) both borrow aprepitant's paragraph, which sits directly beside this one on the page. The class that revolutionized chemotherapy antiemesis is the cleanest; the class that augments it is the dirtiest. (D) contradicts the chapter's own point that combining categories works because more than one pathway becomes blocked.

Q: A postoperative patient receiving opioids has metoclopramide ordered for delayed gastric emptying, and the nurse notes that an anticholinergic is also on the medication list. What effect should the nurse anticipate?
A) Additive central nervous system depression
B) The motility effects of metoclopramide will be blocked, decreasing its action
C) An increased risk of extrapyramidal reactions
D) Prolongation of the QT interval
Answer: B
Rationale: The chapter states that anticholinergics and analgesics can block the motility effects of metoclopramide, and that its action is decreased when it is taken with anticholinergics or opiates. This is the only interaction in the chapter that reduces an antiemetic's effect rather than compounding a toxicity, and it is clinically likely, because opioid patients are exactly the ones receiving antiemetics.
Trap: (A) substitutes the far more common interaction type, and it pulls because metoclopramide with alcohol genuinely does produce additive central nervous system depression. (C) names a real metoclopramide danger with the wrong partner: it is the phenothiazines, prochlorperazine and promethazine, that would lead to exacerbation of extrapyramidal reactions.
