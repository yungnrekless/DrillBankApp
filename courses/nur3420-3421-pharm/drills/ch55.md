# Chapter 55 drills — Nutritional Supplements

Authored from `sources/Ch55_Fact_Bank.md` (Lilley, Collins & Snyder, 10th ed.).

Blueprint weight is **2 questions of 121 (1.7%), depth `focused`** — one of the
lightest lines on the blueprint. That earns **28 drills** across 6 topics, near
the middle of the 20 to 30 band a `focused` chapter carries.

The chapter is far richer than 28 questions can hold, so the set is compressed
onto the four comparison structures the distractors actually live in — enteral
versus parenteral, naso- versus -ostomy routes, elemental versus polymeric
formulas, peripheral versus central parenteral nutrition — plus the amino acid
classification, the glucose complications, and the two-hour interaction rules.
Every confusable pair the fact bank flags is drilled from both ends.

**Deliberately left undrilled**, because the exam weight does not justify the
space: brand rosters beyond the one product each topic needs, caloric densities
per gram, the trace element list and multivitamin infusion, amino acid dosing in
grams per kilogram, flush volumes, tube French sizes, the specific parenteral
monitoring intervals, and the fluid-overload sign list. All of it is in the
source file if a later exam turns out to reach for it.

The chapter defers its electrolyte content to Chapter 29 by name, and that
chapter has no fact bank yet, so nothing electrolyte-specific is drilled here.
Refeeding syndrome is not in this chapter at all and is not invented into it.

Topic: enteral versus parenteral nutritional support
Subtopic: does the gastrointestinal tract work

Q: A patient three days after an ischemic stroke has an intact and fully functioning gastrointestinal tract but fails a swallow screen and cannot safely take food by mouth. Which form of nutritional support does the chapter identify as preferred for this patient?
A) Enteral nutrition delivered through a feeding tube
B) Peripheral parenteral nutrition, because it avoids the risk of aspiration
C) Central total parenteral nutrition through a subclavian catheter
D) No nutritional support until the swallow study is repeated
Answer: A
Rationale: Enteral nutrition is "the preferred route of administration of nutritional supplements" and is used "when the patient has a functioning gastrointestinal tract"; tube delivery exists precisely for patients with depressed consciousness, altered anatomy, or abnormal peristalsis who cannot eat. A working gut plus an inability to eat is the definition case for tube feeding.
Trap: (B) pulls because the stem names a swallowing problem and a vein seems to route around it, but parenteral nutrition is reserved for when oral or enteral support is impossible or when absorptive capacity is insufficient. Neither is true here.

Q: A patient with short bowel syndrome has been on an elemental formula for two weeks and continues to lose weight, and the team documents that the remaining absorptive capacity is not sufficient to meet nutrient needs. Which form of support is now indicated?
A) Add a modular protein product to the elemental formula
B) Change to a polymeric formula to reduce osmolarity
C) Parenteral nutrition, which bypasses the gastrointestinal system entirely
D) Continue enteral feeding, because enteral is always preferred
Answer: C
Rationale: Parenteral nutrition is used "only when oral or enteral support is impossible or when the gastrointestinal absorptive or functional capacity is not sufficient," and it "bypasses the entire gastrointestinal system, which eliminates the need for absorption, metabolism, and excretion." Documented insufficient absorptive capacity is the stated trigger.
Trap: (D) is the mirror error of the previous item — the enteral preference is conditional on a gut that can absorb, not absolute, and treating it as absolute is what keeps a malabsorbing patient undernourished.

Subtopic: naming and scope of support

Q: A nursing student charts that a patient on total parenteral nutrition "developed hyperalimentation" after two days of therapy. Which correction should the nurse make?
A) Hyperalimentation is an older term for parenteral nutrition itself, not a complication of it
B) Hyperalimentation means overfeeding and should be documented as a caloric excess
C) Hyperalimentation refers to enteral feeding delivered faster than the ordered rate
D) Hyperalimentation is the correct term for the metabolic response to a high dextrose load
Answer: A
Rationale: The key terms box defines hyperalimentation as "an older term for parenteral nutrition; its use is now discouraged because it may be misinterpreted to mean overfeeding," and states that the process is now referred to as total parenteral nutrition.
Trap: (B) is the reflex pick because the prefix hyper- reads as excess, which is exactly the misreading the book says caused the term to be retired. The word never named a complication; it named the therapy.

Q: A patient who eats poorly is started on an oral polymeric supplement between meals, and a student concludes that the patient is now on enteral nutrition and therefore no longer needs to be assessed for oral intake. Which statement corrects the student?
A) Oral supplements are not enteral nutrition, because no feeding tube is involved
B) Enteral nutrition may supplement an oral diet or be used alone to meet all nutrient needs, so how much of the need it covers is a separate question from the route
C) Any enteral formulation by definition supplies the patient's total nutrient requirement
D) Once a supplement is ordered, oral intake no longer contributes to the nutritional plan
Answer: B
Rationale: Enteral nutrition can "supplement an oral diet ... or be used alone to meet all of the patient's nutrient needs," and the oral route is itself one of the six enteral routes and the most common and least invasive. Route and completeness are independent axes.
Trap: (C) is the swap distractors are built on — students trade where the nutrition goes for how much of the need it covers. The same swap runs through Table 55.2, where peripheral parenteral nutrition is supplemental and central is total, even though both are parenteral.

Q: A nurse is reviewing the complications the chapter attributes to enteral feeding. Which of the following belong to enteral nutrition rather than to parenteral nutrition? Select all that apply.
A) Gastrointestinal intolerance manifesting as diarrhea
B) Aspiration
C) Air embolism
D) Dumping syndrome
E) Rebound hypoglycemia
Answer: A, B, D
Rationale: The key points list the enteral complications as "hyperglycemia, dumping syndrome, and aspiration," and diarrhea is named as the most common adverse effect of enteral feeding. Air embolism and rebound hypoglycemia appear on the parenteral list.
Trap: (E) is the option that trips people, because hypoglycemia sounds like a feeding problem in general; the chapter attaches it specifically to abrupt discontinuation of parenteral nutrition. (C) is the giveaway parenteral option, since an air embolism requires a vascular line.

Topic: enteral tube routes and formula selection
Subtopic: nasal routes versus surgical ostomies

Q: An order reads "insert nasogastric tube for feeding." A new nurse asks whether the surgical team needs to be notified. Which description of the tube is accurate?
A) A feeding tube placed from the nose to the stomach, requiring no surgery
B) A feeding tube surgically inserted directly into the stomach
C) A feeding tube placed from the nose to the duodenum
D) A feeding tube surgically inserted into the esophagus
Answer: A
Rationale: Table 55.1 defines the nasogastric route as a "feeding tube placed from the nose to the stomach," and a feeding tube is used in five of the six enteral routes. Only the ostomy routes are surgically created.
Trap: (B) is the reflex pick because both names end in the same organ and the word "gastric" carries all the attention; the prefix is what decides whether a surgeon is involved. Naso- means through the nose with no surgery, and -ostomy means a surgically created opening.

Q: A patient with a history of gastric outlet obstruction is scheduled for placement of a feeding tube that will require an operative procedure and will deliver formula directly into the jejunum. Which route is being described?
A) Nasojejunal
B) Jejunostomy
C) Nasoduodenal
D) Esophagostomy
Answer: B
Rationale: Table 55.1 defines jejunostomy as a "feeding tube surgically inserted into the jejunum," while the nasojejunal route reaches the same organ from the nose without surgery. The second half of each name gives the destination organ and the first half gives the method.
Trap: (A) is the mirror error of the previous item — it names the right organ by the wrong method. Reading only the organ half of these words is what makes all four cells of Table 55.1 interchangeable to a student.

Subtopic: choosing among the formula groups

Q: A postoperative patient with a fully functional gastrointestinal tract and no specialized nutrient requirements needs tube feeding for a few weeks. Which formulation does the chapter state is preferred, and why?
A) An elemental formula, because predigested nutrients are gentler on the gut
B) A polymeric formula, because it most closely resembles normal dietary intake and is less hyperosmolar than elemental formulas
C) A modular formula, because single nutrients can be titrated individually
D) An altered amino acid formula, because surgery raises amino acid requirements
Answer: B
Rationale: Polymeric formulations contain complex nutrients — proteins, carbohydrates, and fat — and are "preferred over elemental formulations for patients with a fully functional gastrointestinal tract and few specialized nutrient requirements" because they most closely resemble normal dietary intake and are "less hyperosmolar than elemental formulations and therefore cause fewer gastrointestinal problems."
Trap: (A) is the intuitive inversion: predigested sounds gentler, so students reach for elemental whenever the gut is mentioned at all. The chapter puts elemental where digestion is impaired and polymeric where it is intact, and the hyperosmolarity runs the other way from the intuition.

Q: A patient with radiation enteritis and documented malabsorption cannot tolerate a standard tube feeding. Which formulation matches this patient's problem?
A) Polymeric, containing complex proteins, carbohydrates, and fat
B) Impaired glucose tolerance formula, containing protein, carbohydrate, fat, sodium, and potassium
C) Elemental, containing dipeptides, tripeptides, or crystalline amino acids and requiring minimal digestion with minimal residue
D) Infant formulation used as sole or supplemental intake
Answer: C
Rationale: Box 55.1 lists elemental formulations as requiring "minimal digestion" and leaving "minimal residue," indicated for malabsorption, partial bowel obstruction, irritable bowel disease, radiation enteritis, bowel fistula, and short bowel syndrome.
Trap: (A) is the mirror error of the previous item — polymeric is correct only when digestion is intact, and a stem naming malabsorption has already ruled that out. Matching the formula to the gut's remaining capacity is the whole decision.

Q: A family member asks whether the patient could be maintained entirely on the protein supplement listed on the plan of care as a modular product. Which response is accurate?
A) Modular products are single-nutrient formulas added to an elemental or polymeric formulation, not a complete feeding
B) Modular products contain all required macronutrients and can serve as sole nutrition
C) Modular products are indicated only for genetic metabolic enzyme deficiencies
D) Modular products must be given intravenously to be complete
Answer: A
Rationale: Box 55.1 describes modular formulations as "single-nutrient formulas" of protein, carbohydrate, or fat that are "added to a monomeric or polymeric formulation to provide a more individualized nutrient formulation." Carbohydrate, fat, and protein modulars are all named as additives.
Trap: (B) is a true statement about complete formulations attached to the wrong product. A modular is real nutrition, which is what makes the option feel defensible, but by itself it supplies one macronutrient and leaves the rest of the requirement unmet.

Topic: peripheral versus central parenteral nutrition
Subtopic: matching the patient to the vein

Q: A patient is expected to need parenteral support for about 10 days after abdominal surgery, has modest nutritional needs of roughly 1600 kcal per day, and tolerates large fluid volumes without difficulty. Which form of parenteral nutrition fits the chapter's criteria?
A) Peripheral parenteral nutrition, using a dextrose concentration below 10%
B) Central total parenteral nutrition, using a dextrose concentration of 25%
C) Peripheral parenteral nutrition, using a dextrose concentration of 20%
D) Central total parenteral nutrition, because any therapy beyond 7 days must be central
Answer: A
Rationale: Table 55.2 sets peripheral parenteral nutrition at fewer than 14 days, dextrose less than 10%, and fewer than 2000 kcal per day, and requires that fluid tolerance "must be high." The chapter also describes it as "a temporary means (less than 2 weeks)" for patients without large nutritional needs.
Trap: (C) pairs the right vein with a central-line dextrose concentration, and it pulls because the stem's duration and calories both fit peripherally, so students stop reading before the concentration. Dextrose above 10% in a peripheral vein scleroses the vein and is the setup for phlebitis.

Q: A patient with severe burns requires about 3000 kcal per day, a dextrose concentration of 25%, and support expected to last several weeks, possibly continuing at home. Which route is indicated?
A) Peripheral parenteral nutrition, because the therapy can be started immediately
B) Central total parenteral nutrition through a large central vein such as the subclavian or internal jugular
C) Peripheral parenteral nutrition, because burns raise fluid tolerance
D) Enteral feeding by nasogastric tube regardless of gastrointestinal status
Answer: B
Rationale: Central total parenteral nutrition uses "a large central vein ... usually the subclavian or internal jugular" for a "prolonged period, usually longer than 7 to 10 days," carries dextrose of 10% to 35% and more than 2000 kcal per day, and can be continued at home. Large nutritional requirements from metabolic stress or hypermetabolism are a stated indication.
Trap: (A) is the mirror error of the previous item — every one of the three numbers in this stem violates a peripheral limit, and picking the peripheral route because it is quicker to start is the reflex when a patient looks unstable.

Q: A student states that the way to tell peripheral parenteral nutrition from central total parenteral nutrition is that only the central solution is hyperosmolar. Which correction is accurate?
A) Only the peripheral solution is hyperosmolar, which is why it causes phlebitis
B) Both are hyperosmolar at 600 to 900 mOsm/L, so osmolarity does not distinguish them
C) Neither solution is hyperosmolar once lipids are added
D) Osmolarity is not reported for parenteral solutions
Answer: B
Rationale: Table 55.2 gives the identical osmolarity range, hyperosmolar at 600 to 900 mOsm/L, in both columns. The chapter also notes that the components of the two solutions "are the same. The difference lies in the concentrations and amounts." The reason central access is used is "the hyperosmolarity of the substances used and the need for dilution provided by a larger-diameter vein."
Trap: (A) inverts a row that is not a difference at all, and it pulls because phlebitis really is a peripheral problem, so students reason backwards from the complication to a difference the table does not state. Osmolarity is the one row of Table 55.2 that cannot discriminate.

Q: A patient with heart failure who cannot tolerate large fluid volumes needs parenteral nutrition. A new nurse suggests the peripheral route because it is less invasive. Which reasoning should the nurse apply instead?
A) Peripheral is correct, because peripheral solutions are given in smaller volumes
B) Peripheral is correct, because a central line would worsen fluid overload
C) Central is correct, because peripheral dextrose is capped below 10% to avoid sclerosing the vein, so large fluid volumes are required to deliver the calories
D) Either route is acceptable, because fluid volume is determined only by the prescriber's order
Answer: C
Rationale: Table 55.2 states that peripheral fluid tolerance "must be high" while central "can be fluid-restricted," and the chapter explains why: peripheral dextrose is "generally less than 10%, to avoid sclerosing the vein. Thus large volumes are needed," and patients "with renal failure or heart failure, cannot tolerate large fluid volumes."
Trap: (A) is the classic miss, because the smaller vein suggests smaller volumes and the fluid rule actually runs the opposite way. Heart failure and renal failure push a patient toward central access, not away from it.

Q: A patient receiving peripheral parenteral nutrition reports increasing pain at the infusion site. The site looks unremarkable, vital signs are stable, and the hanging bag reads 20% dextrose. What is the nurse's priority action?
A) Stop the infusion
B) Apply a warm compress and continue the infusion at a slower rate
C) Document the finding and reassess at the end of the shift
D) Draw a blood glucose level before taking any other action
Answer: A
Rationale: The chapter states that "the most devastating adverse effect of PPN is phlebitis," which "can lead to the loss of a limb, although this is rare," and peripheral dextrose is held below 10% precisely to prevent it. A 20% concentration is a central-line strength running in a peripheral vein, so the solution is causing the injury and comes off first.
Trap: (B) pulls because a stable patient with a normal-looking site does not feel like an emergency, and slowing an infusion feels like a reasonable middle step. When the infusing solution is itself the cause, slowing it continues the injury; the only situation that outranks stopping the line is hemodynamic instability, which this stem rules out.

Topic: parenteral nutrition components and amino acid classes
Subtopic: essential, semiessential, and nonessential

Q: A parenteral formulation for an infant is being reviewed, and the pharmacist notes that arginine and histidine must be supplied even though an adult would not require them from the diet. How should the nurse classify these two amino acids?
A) Essential, because the body cannot manufacture them at all
B) Semiessential, because the body produces them but not in sufficient amounts during rapid growth in infancy and childhood
C) Nonessential, because the body produces them without dietary help at every age
D) Conditionally toxic, because they accumulate in infants
Answer: B
Rationale: Box 55.3 lists arginine and histidine as the two semiessential amino acids, and the chapter states they "are not manufactured by the body in large enough quantities during rapid growth periods such as infancy or childhood." Semiessential amino acids are defined as those the body can produce but that infants and children cannot produce in sufficient amounts.
Trap: (A) is the promotion error, and it pulls because the stem says the infant must be supplied with them, which sounds like the definition of essential. The distinction is that the body makes these two at all, just not fast enough during growth.

Q: A nurse is teaching about the eight amino acids listed as essential, including leucine and tryptophan. Which statement correctly explains what makes them essential?
A) They cannot be manufactured by the body and must come from intake
B) They are produced by the body but not in sufficient amounts during infancy
C) They are the amino acids the body produces without extracting them from dietary intake
D) They are the only amino acids that participate in protein synthesis
Answer: A
Rationale: Essential amino acids are defined as "those amino acids that cannot be manufactured by the body," and Box 55.3 lists eight of them: isoleucine, leucine, lysine, methionine, phenylalanine, threonine, tryptophan, and valine. The body is able to manufacture "all but eight."
Trap: (C) is the mirror error of the previous item, stating the nonessential definition under the essential label; the two definitions differ only in whether the body can make the compound. Nonessential also does not mean unnecessary, only that it is not dietarily required.

Subtopic: what the components are for

Q: A patient with severe trauma is receiving amino acids as part of total parenteral nutrition. Which statement describes the intended metabolic effect?
A) They promote catabolism, liberating energy by breaking down complex substances
B) They promote anabolism, supporting protein synthesis and reducing the breakdown of proteins
C) They replace dextrose as the greatest source of calories in the solution
D) They eliminate the need for lipid emulsion in the solution
Answer: B
Rationale: The chapter states that the primary role of amino acids is "protein synthesis, or anabolism," and that they "reduce the breakdown of proteins (catabolism)." Anabolism is defined as the conversion of simple substances into more complex compounds, or tissue building.
Trap: (A) inverts the pair, and it pulls because an "accelerated catabolic status" is one of the chapter's own indications for supplementation, so the word appears in the same context. Catabolism is the problem being treated, not the goal of the therapy; ana- builds up and cata- tears down.

Q: A nurse is explaining the purpose of the intravenous fat emulsion hanging alongside a patient's total parenteral nutrition. Which functions does the chapter assign to lipid emulsions? Select all that apply.
A) Supplying essential fatty acids the body cannot synthesize
B) Serving as a source of energy or calories
C) Supplying the amino acids needed for protein synthesis
D) Supplying trace minerals such as zinc and selenium
E) Supplying immunoglobulins for infection resistance
Answer: A, B
Rationale: Lipid emulsions "serve two functions: they supply essential fatty acids and are a source of energy or calories." Linoleic acid cannot be synthesized by the body and is needed to produce linolenic and arachidonic acid. Covering part of the caloric load with fat prevents hyperglycemia, hyperinsulinemia, and hyperosmolarity that occur when the entire caloric need is met by dextrose alone.
Trap: (C) and (D) are each true of a different component of the same bag — amino acid solutions and trace element additives — which is what makes them feel correct when the question is read as "what is in parenteral nutrition" instead of "what do the lipids do." (E) is not a component of parenteral nutrition at all.

Topic: glucose complications of parenteral nutrition
Subtopic: hyperglycemia versus rebound hypoglycemia

Q: A total parenteral nutrition infusion has fallen behind schedule, and a nurse considers increasing the rate to deliver the ordered volume by the end of the shift. What is the concern with this action?
A) Accelerating the rate risks hyperglycemia, and the chapter directs that these infusion rates never be accelerated
B) Accelerating the rate risks rebound hypoglycemia from pancreatic overshoot
C) Accelerating the rate risks air embolism from turbulent flow
D) There is no concern, because the total daily volume is what matters
Answer: A
Rationale: The chapter instructs, "Never accelerate these infusion rates to increase plasma volume," and lists a high dextrose load and an accelerated rate as the causes of hyperglycemia. Because dextrose is a sugar, supplemental insulin may be given simultaneously with the infusion.
Trap: (B) names the right chapter and the wrong direction, and it pulls because both glucose complications belong to parenteral nutrition and students memorize the pair without the causes. Sort by cause first: too much sugar too fast raises glucose, and stopping abruptly drops it.

Q: A patient's total parenteral nutrition bag runs dry and the replacement bag will not arrive from pharmacy for two hours. What is the nurse's priority concern and action?
A) Hyperglycemia; hold all glucose sources until the new bag arrives
B) Rebound hypoglycemia; check the blood glucose and infuse 5% to 10% glucose in the interim
C) Fluid volume overload; restrict oral intake until the infusion resumes
D) Phlebitis; discontinue the line and restart it at a new site
Answer: B
Rationale: If parenteral nutrition "is discontinued abruptly, rebound hypoglycemia may result." The chapter's prevention rules are a gradual reduction of the intravenous feeding rate to allow the pancreas time to adapt, and, when the feeding must stop immediately, "providing infusions of 5% to 10% glucose."
Trap: (A) is the mirror error of the previous item — a bag that has stopped is the opposite problem from a bag running too fast, yet hyperglycemia is the complication students associate with the therapy. Hanging plain saline or simply waiting for pharmacy is the same error in a different form: neither bridges the glucose.

Subtopic: sorting the signs

Q: Shortly after a total parenteral nutrition infusion is interrupted, a patient becomes symptomatic. Which findings does the chapter attribute to hypoglycemia? Select all that apply.
A) Cold clammy skin
B) Dizziness
C) Tachycardia
D) Polyuria
E) Polydipsia
Answer: A, B, C
Rationale: The chapter lists hypoglycemia as "cold clammy skin, dizziness, tachycardia, and tingling of the extremities," with the patient teaching section adding restlessness, nervousness, and fainting. Polyuria and polydipsia appear on the hyperglycemia list.
Trap: (D) and (E) are the directional inversions this pair generates — both are glucose symptoms in this chapter, just from the opposite complication, and the shared "poly-" prefix makes them read as generic glucose findings rather than as hyperglycemia findings.

Q: A patient receiving total parenteral nutrition with a high dextrose concentration reports headache and excessive thirst, is voiding large volumes, and appears dehydrated. Which complication do these findings indicate?
A) Rebound hypoglycemia from a slowed infusion
B) Hyperglycemia from the dextrose load
C) Fluid volume overload from the infusion volume
D) Essential fatty acid deficiency
Answer: B
Rationale: Hyperglycemia in parenteral nutrition presents as "polydipsia, polyuria, polyphagia, headache, dehydration, nausea, vomiting, and weakness," and its causes in this chapter are a high dextrose concentration and an accelerated infusion rate.
Trap: (C) pulls because a large-volume infusion is running and the patient's fluid status is abnormal, but fluid overload presents as weak pulse, tachycardia, confusion, decreased urine output, and pitting edema. Urine output is the discriminator: it rises with hyperglycemia and falls with overload.

Topic: tube feeding administration and nutrient-drug interactions
Subtopic: verifying placement and residual

Q: Before administering a scheduled tube feeding, a nurse aspirates gastric contents and tests the pH, which reads 5.0. What does this finding indicate?
A) The tube is correctly positioned within the stomach
B) The tube has migrated into the respiratory tract
C) The tube tip has advanced into the jejunum
D) The reading is inconclusive and the feeding may proceed regardless
Answer: A
Rationale: The chapter directs the nurse to test the pH of the aspirate: "With a pH of 5.5 or lower, the tube is in the correct position (within the stomach)." Placement may also be confirmed by x-ray. Newer small-bore tubes of 5 to 10 French make checking for gastric aspiration more difficult.
Trap: (D) pulls when a student remembers only that x-ray is the definitive check and treats every bedside method as unusable. The chapter gives an explicit numeric threshold, and a reading below it is an actionable confirmation, not a guess.

Q: A patient is receiving continuous tube feedings at 50 mL per hour. The nurse checks the gastric residual and obtains 140 mL. What is the correct action?
A) Discard the aspirate and continue the feeding at the ordered rate
B) Return the aspirate, hold the feeding, contact the prescriber, and keep the head of the bed elevated
C) Return the aspirate and increase the rate to compensate for the delay
D) Discard the aspirate and lower the head of the bed to promote gastric emptying
Answer: B
Rationale: For continuous feedings, a residual of "more than the volume delivered over the previous 2 hours" calls for returning the aspirate, holding the feeding, contacting the prescriber, and keeping the head of the bed elevated at least 30 to 45 degrees. At 50 mL per hour the two-hour volume is 100 mL, so 140 mL exceeds the threshold. For intermittent bolus feedings the threshold is instead more than 50% of the volume previously infused.
Trap: (D) inverts the positioning rule, and it pulls because lying flat sounds like it would help a stomach empty; head-of-bed elevation is what protects against aspiration and it matters more with nasogastric than with gastrostomy feedings. Discarding the aspirate also discards the patient's own electrolytes and feeding.

Subtopic: separating drugs from feedings

Q: A patient on continuous nasogastric feedings of a polymeric formula is ordered phenytoin. Which action reflects the chapter's interaction rule?
A) Hold the tube feeding for at least 2 hours before and after the phenytoin dose
B) Give the phenytoin 2 hours before or after the feeding, but do not interrupt the feeding
C) Mix the phenytoin directly into the feeding bag for even delivery
D) No separation is needed, because feedings affect only antibiotic absorption
Answer: A
Rationale: Tube feedings "can also reduce the absorption of phenytoin ... which may result in seizures," and the chapter recommends that "tube feedings be held for at least 2 hours before and after" the dose. The chapter also notes that the interaction is considered "more theoretical than actual" by some, so some institutions ignore it while others continue to hold the feedings, and intravenous phenytoin is often required when continuous feedings are necessary.
Trap: (B) uses the right interval on the wrong object. Two separation rules in this chapter share the same two hours, and the thing being moved is what differs: for phenytoin, the feeding is held around the dose.

Q: A patient receiving intermittent tube feedings is ordered oral ciprofloxacin and doxycycline. How should these doses be timed relative to the feedings?
A) Hold the feeding for 2 hours before and after each dose, as with phenytoin
B) Give each drug at least 2 hours before or after the tube feedings
C) Give each drug with the feeding to reduce gastric irritation
D) No separation is needed, because chemical inactivation affects only intravenous drugs
Answer: B
Rationale: The absorption of "tetracyclines and quinolones is decreased, as a result of chemical inactivation," and these drugs "must be given at least 2 hours before or after tube feedings." The Clinical Pearl states that enteral feedings interact with quinolone and tetracycline antibiotics and phenytoin and need to be separated by 2 hours.
Trap: (A) is the mirror error of the previous item — same interval, wrong object moved. For these antibiotics the drug is scheduled around the feeding, while for phenytoin the feeding is held around the drug.

Subtopic: delivery problems

Q: A patient receiving intermittent bolus gastric feedings develops nausea, sweating, palpitations, and diarrhea shortly after a bolus is delivered quickly. Which complication does this presentation indicate, and what change is needed?
A) Aspiration; raise the head of the bed and suction the airway
B) Dumping syndrome; slow the rate of delivery
C) Hypoglycemia; infuse 5% to 10% glucose
D) Fluid volume overload; restrict fluids and notify the prescriber
Answer: B
Rationale: Dumping syndrome is "a complex reaction to the rapid entry of concentrated nutrients into the jejunum," producing "nausea, weakness, sweating, palpitations, syncope, sensations of warmth, and diarrhea," and it occurs with enteral feedings administered too rapidly as well as after partial gastrectomy. The chapter states that rapid feeding or bolus doses can result in dumping syndrome.
Trap: (A) pulls because both complications come from how the feeding is delivered rather than what is in it, so students pick the more feared one. Sort by the trigger: too fast produces dumping, while a high residual, a flat bed, or a compromised gag reflex produces aspiration.
