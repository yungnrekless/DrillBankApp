# Chapter 29 drills — Fluids and Electrolytes

Authored from `sources/Ch29_Fact_Bank.md` (Lilley, Collins & Snyder, 10th ed.).

Blueprint weight is **1 question of 121 (0.8%), depth `light`** — the smallest
line in the blueprint. A `light` chapter earns 12 to 20 drills across 3 to 5
topics, and this file lands at **18 across 5 topics**.

The fact bank carries about twenty flagged traps, which is more than this band
can hold, so the set was cut to the confusable pairs and the safety items and
nothing else. **Deliberately left undrilled**, and why:

- **Every dose and volume number.** Table 29.3's plasma-expansion ratios
  (5 to 6 L of saline per 1 L of plasma volume, and so on) and Table 29.6's
  blood-loss bands are numeric minutiae for a chapter worth one exam item.
  The single volume fact kept is the potassium infusion rate, because it is a
  safety limit rather than a dosing table.
- **Kayexalate, patiromer, and sodium zirconium.** The fact bank itself reduced
  these to one-liners; the emergency-versus-resin cue is worth a question in a
  heavier chapter, not this one.
- **Hyponatremia subtypes and the vaptans**, the acid-base pH block, and the
  albumin profile, all of which the chapter treats in a sentence or two.
- **Objective 2's missing serum ranges.** The chapter names calcium, magnesium,
  phosphorus, chloride, and bicarbonate but prints ranges only for potassium and
  sodium, so only those two are drilled. Nothing was filled from memory.

Both directions are drilled for every distinction the chapter states twice:
serum and plasma, hypotonic and hypertonic as an intravenous fluid, isotonic and
hypertonic as a dehydration, hypokalemia and hyperkalemia, hyponatremia and
hypernatremia. The two select-all items come from the two lists in the fact bank
that genuinely sort in two directions — the compartment map and the
product-to-reason table — and no others were manufactured.

Topic: body fluid compartments and blood fluid terms
Subtopic: how total body water is distributed

Q: A nursing student is building a diagram of body fluid compartments before a fluid-replacement clinical. Which statements about the distribution of total body water are accurate? Select all that apply.
A) Water makes up roughly 60% of an average adult's body weight
B) Two thirds of total body water is intracellular fluid
C) Extracellular fluid comprises the interstitial fluid and the intravascular fluid
D) Interstitial fluid is a subdivision of the intracellular fluid
E) Transcellular fluid, such as cerebrospinal, pleural, and synovial fluid, lies within the interstitial fluid
F) Total body water accounts for a smaller share of body weight in an infant than in an adult
Answer: A, B, C, E
Rationale: Total body water is about 60% of adult body weight and splits two thirds intracellular to one third extracellular; the extracellular third is itself the interstitial fluid plus the intravascular fluid, and the transcellular fluid of the specialized cavities sits inside the interstitial portion rather than beside it as a fourth peer compartment. Death often occurs when 20% to 25% of total body water is lost.
Trap: (D) is the reflex error because "interstitial" and "intracellular" differ by three letters and both start with "int" — interstitial fluid fills the spaces *between* cells and is extracellular by definition. (F) inverts the age rule: total body water is a *higher* proportion of body weight in infants and a lower one in older adults.

Subtopic: serum versus plasma

Q: A chemistry panel is drawn into a clot-activator tube and the result returns as "serum potassium 3.2 mEq/L." Which description matches the fluid the value was actually measured in?
A) The clear, cell-free portion of the blood from which fibrinogen has been separated during the clotting process
B) The watery, straw-colored liquid component of blood in which the erythrocytes, leukocytes, and platelets are suspended
C) The extracellular fluid drawn from the spaces between the cells of the body
D) Whole blood, meaning the liquid component together with all three of its major solid components
Answer: A
Rationale: Serum is defined as the clear, cell-free portion of the blood from which fibrinogen has been separated during clotting, as typically carried out with a laboratory sample. That is why every reference value in this chapter is written as a *serum* potassium or a *serum* sodium.
Trap: (B) is the reflex pick because it is the richer, more textbook-sounding definition — but it defines plasma, which still holds the cells and still contains fibrinogen. The cue is that serum is the lab-tube word.

Q: The same patient is now to receive two units of fresh frozen plasma. Which statement about plasma is accurate?
A) It is the cell-free fraction that remains after fibrinogen has been removed by clotting
B) It is the watery, straw-colored liquid component of lymph and blood in which the leukocytes, erythrocytes, and platelets are suspended
C) It is the fluid contained within specialized body compartments such as the pleural and synovial cavities
D) It is the portion of the extracellular fluid that fills the spaces between most of the cells of the body
Answer: B
Rationale: Plasma is the watery, straw-colored fluid component of lymph and blood in which the leukocytes, erythrocytes, and platelets are suspended, and it retains fibrinogen — which is exactly why fresh frozen plasma is the product given to replace clotting factors.
Trap: (A) is the mirror error of the previous item, swapping the serum definition onto plasma. (C) defines transcellular fluid and (D) defines interstitial fluid, both of which are compartment words rather than blood-component words.

Topic: iv fluid tonicity and types of dehydration
Subtopic: osmolality versus tonicity

Q: A patient's chart records a serum osmolality of 302 mOsm/kg, and the pharmacy label on a hanging bag of 0.45% sodium chloride reads "hypotonic." A student asks whether the two terms mean the same thing. What is the accurate response?
A) Osmolality is used in reference to body fluids and tonicity is used in reference to intravenous fluids
B) Osmolality is used in reference to intravenous fluids and tonicity is used in reference to body fluids
C) Osmolality applies only to electrolyte solutions and tonicity applies only to protein solutions
D) They are interchangeable, so either word may be used for either measurement
Answer: A
Rationale: Osmolality is used in reference to body fluids, with a normal range of 290 to 310 mOsm/kg, while tonicity is used in reference to intravenous fluids. The axis is what is being measured: osmolality describes the patient, tonicity describes the bag.
Trap: (D) is the reflex pick precisely because the textbook calls these "similar terms that are often used interchangeably" — that sentence is the book flagging a common error, not endorsing it. (B) is the direct inversion and is the one students who half-remember the rule choose.

Subtopic: where water moves with each tonicity

Q: A patient is receiving 0.45% sodium chloride. What fluid movement should the nurse expect, and what is the associated risk?
A) Fluid moves out of the vein and into the tissues and cells, and the cells may swell and burst
B) Fluid is pulled from the interstitial space into the veins, and the cells shrink as water leaves them
C) No net fluid movement occurs, because the pressures inside and outside the cell are the same
D) No fluid shift occurs, but red blood cells are destroyed on contact because the solution contains no sodium at all
Answer: A
Rationale: A hypotonic solution has a lower concentration of solutes outside the cell than inside it, so fluid moves out of the vein and into the tissues and cells; the chapter warns that cells can burst as a result. The most dilute member of this group, 0.25% sodium chloride, can cause hemolysis of the red blood cells.
Trap: (D) is a plausible-but-adjacent distractor built from a real fact attached to the wrong solution and the wrong reason — hemolysis belongs to 0.25% sodium chloride, and 0.45% is dilute, not sodium-free. (C) describes an isotonic fluid.

Q: The same patient is later switched to 3% sodium chloride. What fluid movement should the nurse now expect?
A) Fluid moves out of the vein into the cells, which swell and may burst
B) Fluid moves from the interstitial space into the veins, and the cells shrink as water leaves them
C) No net fluid movement occurs in either direction
D) Fluid moves out of the veins into the interstitial space, producing peripheral edema
Answer: B
Rationale: A hypertonic solution has a higher concentration of solutes outside the cell, so fluid is drawn from the interstitial fluid into the veins and the cells shrink. This is why 3% and 5% sodium chloride are classed as hypertonic and flagged high-alert.
Trap: (A) is the mirror error of the previous item — it is the hypotonic answer attached to the hypertonic solution. The reliable cue for a *fluid* is to name where the water goes, and hypertonic pulls water toward the vein.

Subtopic: the same three words as a type of dehydration

Q: A patient is admitted after two days of vomiting and diarrhea. Which type of dehydration does this presentation represent?
A) Isotonic dehydration, because sodium and water are lost together and the volume of extracellular fluid falls
B) Hypotonic dehydration, because sodium loss is greater than water loss
C) Hypertonic dehydration, because water loss is greater than sodium loss
D) No dehydration is present, because gastrointestinal losses replace themselves from the intracellular compartment
Answer: A
Rationale: Isotonic dehydration is the loss of both sodium and water, producing a decrease in the volume of extracellular fluid, and the chapter's own example is diarrhea and vomiting. The chapter's critical thinking exercise uses this same case.
Trap: (B) and (C) pull because "isotonic" sounds like a normal, balanced state and therefore not like a diagnosis at all, so students reach for a word that sounds abnormal. For a *dehydration*, name the loss that is greater; when neither is greater, it is isotonic.

Q: A different patient has spiked a fever with profuse perspiration over the past 12 hours. Which type of dehydration does this represent?
A) Isotonic dehydration, because sodium and water are lost in equal proportion
B) Hypotonic dehydration, because sodium loss is greater than water loss
C) Hypertonic dehydration, because water loss is greater than sodium loss, which dehydrates the cells
D) Hypertonic dehydration, because sodium loss is greater than water loss, which swells the cells
Answer: C
Rationale: Hypertonic dehydration is water loss greater than sodium loss, thus dehydrating the cells, and the chapter's example is fever with perspiration. Hypotonic dehydration is the opposite case — sodium loss greater than water loss, with fluid pulled from outside the cells into the cells — and its examples are renal insufficiency and inadequate aldosterone.
Trap: (A) is the mirror error of the previous item, carrying the vomiting-and-diarrhea answer onto a case where only water is being lost. (D) is the same word as the key with the definition inverted, which is the reason the tonicity words must be read as a rule about which loss is greater rather than recognized on sight.

Topic: crystalloid colloid and blood product selection
Subtopic: what distinguishes the three categories

Q: A patient in shock needs plasma volume expansion, and the team is weighing 5% albumin against 0.9% sodium chloride. Which statement correctly distinguishes the two categories?
A) Crystalloids are substances in solution that diffuse through a semipermeable membrane, whereas colloids are protein substances that raise colloid oncotic pressure and move fluid from the interstitial compartment into the plasma compartment
B) Crystalloids are protein substances that raise colloid oncotic pressure, whereas colloids are small solutes that diffuse freely across the membrane
C) Both categories contain protein, and they differ only in cost and shelf life
D) Colloids supply water and sodium but contain no protein, which is why they are the maintenance fluid of choice
Answer: A
Rationale: Crystalloids are defined as substances in a solution that diffuse through a semipermeable membrane, and they supply water and sodium with no proteins; colloids are protein substances that increase the colloid oncotic pressure and move fluid from the interstitial compartment to the plasma compartment. Albumin generates approximately 70% of the colloid oncotic pressure.
Trap: (B) is the straight inversion and is the one chosen by anyone who anchors on "crystal" sounding large and structured. The discriminator to hold is that crystalloids diffuse through the membrane and colloids cannot.

Q: A trauma patient in the emergency department needs immediate plasma volume expansion. A student argues for a colloid on the ground that colloids last longer. What is the accurate response?
A) Colloids do hold plasma volume longer, but crystalloids are better than colloids for emergency short-term plasma volume expansion
B) Colloids are the better choice in every time frame, both immediate and sustained
C) Crystalloids hold plasma volume longer than colloids, so the premise is wrong
D) Neither category expands plasma volume; only blood products do
Answer: A
Rationale: Both statements appear on the same page: colloids hold plasma volume longer and are less likely than crystalloids to cause edema, yet crystalloids are better than colloids for emergency short-term plasma volume expansion. Crystalloids are also the cheapest option, carry no risk for viral transmission or anaphylaxis, and do not alter the coagulation profile.
Trap: (B) is the reflex pick because the word "longer" reads as "better," and the sentence containing "better" is about a different time frame entirely. Read the time frame before the adjective.

Subtopic: matching a blood product to its reason

Q: A nurse is reviewing why each blood product is selected. Which statements correctly match a product to its reason for use? Select all that apply.
A) Packed red blood cells are given to increase oxygen-carrying capacity
B) Fresh frozen plasma is given to replace clotting factors
C) Whole blood is reserved for extreme loss, over 25% of blood volume, because it adds plasma proteins, the chief osmotic component
D) Cryoprecipitate and plasma protein fraction are used for acute bleeding
E) Packed red blood cells are given chiefly to supply clotting factors
F) Hetastarch is given as a source of clotting factors when plasma is unavailable
Answer: A, B, C, D
Rationale: The blood-product table matches each agent to a reason rather than to a severity: packed red blood cells for oxygen-carrying capacity, fresh frozen plasma for clotting factors, whole blood for extreme loss because it adds plasma proteins, and cryoprecipitate and plasma protein fraction for acute bleeding. Blood products are the only category that carries oxygen, and they hold oncotic pressure for several hours to days.
Trap: (E) swaps two products that sit adjacent in the same table, which is the single most common inversion here. (F) is drawn from the wrong category entirely: colloids such as hetastarch have no oxygen-carrying ability and no clotting factors, and are in fact more likely than crystalloids to promote bleeding.

Subtopic: transfusion administration priorities

Q: A nurse prepares to hang a unit of packed red blood cells and finds that the tubing has been primed with 5% dextrose in water. Thirty minutes into a correctly hung unit, a different patient develops a new fever and hematuria. Which statement reflects correct practice for both situations?
A) Blood products can be administered with normal saline only, so the dextrose tubing must be replaced before hanging; new fever or hematuria during a transfusion is a reaction requiring immediate attention
B) Dextrose in water is an acceptable primer for blood products; new fever or hematuria is a reaction requiring immediate attention
C) Blood products can be administered with normal saline only, so the dextrose tubing must be replaced; new fever or hematuria is an expected response to transfused cells and may be monitored
D) Either fluid may be used for priming, and fever or hematuria is an expected response to transfused cells
Answer: A
Rationale: Blood products can be administered with normal saline only, because dextrose solutions hemolyze red cells, and fever or hematuria appearing during a transfusion is a reaction that requires immediate attention rather than an expected finding. Blood products are also the one category with no absolute contraindications, so the safety work is in administration rather than in screening.
Trap: The dextrose flush in (B) and (D) is the standing distractor for anyone who reaches for the most familiar intravenous solution on the unit. (C) and (D) pull through the word "expected," which quietly converts a transfusion reaction into routine monitoring — the single most costly wrong answer in this chapter.

Topic: potassium imbalance and iv potassium safety
Subtopic: recognizing high versus low potassium

Q: A patient taking furosemide and digoxin has a serum potassium of 3.0 mEq/L. Which cluster of findings fits this value?
A) Hypotension, lethargy, mental confusion, muscle weakness, and nausea, progressing to cardiac irregularities and paralytic ileus
B) Generalized fatigue, paresthesia, and palpitations, progressing to paralysis
C) Flushed skin, dry sticky mucous membranes, thirst, and decreased urination
D) Muscle cramps, headache, and seizures caused by cellular dehydration
Answer: A
Rationale: Hypokalemia is a serum potassium below 3.5 mEq/L; early findings are hypotension, lethargy, mental confusion, muscle weakness, and nausea, and late findings are cardiac irregularities, neuropathies, and paralytic ileus. A low potassium also raises the risk of digoxin toxicity, which matters directly for this patient.
Trap: Muscle weakness appears at both ends of the potassium range, so weakness decides nothing and cannot be used to pick between the first two options — hypotension, lethargy, confusion, nausea, and ileus are what point low. (C) and (D) are sodium presentations rather than potassium ones.

Q: A patient with chronic kidney disease taking an ACE inhibitor has a serum potassium of 6.2 mEq/L. Which cluster of findings fits this value, and what is the danger if it climbs further?
A) Hypotension, lethargy, mental confusion, nausea, and paralytic ileus, with hypertension expected above 7 mEq/L
B) Generalized fatigue, weakness, paresthesia, palpitations, and paralysis, with ventricular fibrillation and cardiac arrest above 7 mEq/L
C) Flushed skin, dry sticky mucous membranes, and thirst, with seizures above 7 mEq/L
D) No findings at all, because potassium excess is asymptomatic until dialysis is required
Answer: B
Rationale: Hyperkalemia is generally a serum potassium above 5.5 mEq/L and its effects are generally related to the heart: generalized fatigue, weakness, paresthesia, palpitations, and paralysis, with ventricular fibrillation and cardiac arrest once the level exceeds 7 mEq/L. Defective renal excretion is the most common cause, and potassium-sparing diuretics and ACE inhibitors are named contributors.
Trap: (A) is the mirror error of the previous item, attaching the hypokalemia cluster to a high value. The chapter is also internally inconsistent on the cutoff — one page gives greater than 5.5 and the safety box gives greater than 5 — so carry both: above 5 is reportable and above 5.5 is toxic and dangerous.

Subtopic: administering intravenous potassium

Q: An order reads potassium chloride 40 mEq intravenously for a patient on a general medical floor with no cardiac monitoring. Which administration plan is correct?
A) Dilute the dose and infuse no faster than 10 mEq/hr, never by intravenous push or bolus, at a peripheral concentration no greater than 20 to 40 mEq/L
B) Give the dose as a slow intravenous push over 5 minutes to shorten the patient's infusion time
C) Infuse at 20 mEq/hr or faster, since that rate is standard for adult replacement on any unit
D) Dilute the dose and infuse at any rate, since the rate limit applies only to central lines
Answer: A
Rationale: Intravenous potassium is given no faster than 10 mEq/hr in unmonitored patients, with 20 mEq/hr or more reserved for critically ill patients on cardiac monitors, and maximum concentrations of 20 to 40 mEq/L peripherally and 60 mEq/L centrally. It is never given by intravenous push or bolus, because cardiac arrest may occur.
Trap: (C) is the reflex pick because 20 mEq/hr is a rate students genuinely see written — it belongs to the monitored critical care patient, and this stem specifies no cardiac monitoring, so the rate band is the whole question. (B) is the error the chapter warns is fatal.

Topic: sodium imbalance and hypertonic saline safety
Subtopic: recognizing high versus low sodium

Q: A patient admitted after several days of excessive water intake has a serum sodium of 128 mEq/L. Which findings should the nurse anticipate?
A) Lethargy, hypotension, stomach cramps, vomiting, diarrhea, and seizures, along with anorexia, confusion, agitation, and headache
B) Flushed skin, dry sticky mucous membranes, thirst, temperature elevation, and decreased urination
C) Palpitations, paresthesia, and generalized fatigue progressing to paralysis
D) Muscle cramps and headache caused by cellular dehydration, with a risk of intracranial hemorrhage
Answer: A
Rationale: Hyponatremia is a serum sodium below 135 mEq/L, caused by inadequate excretion of water or by excessive water intake, and presents with lethargy, hypotension, stomach cramps, vomiting, diarrhea, and seizures, as well as anorexia, confusion, agitation, and headache. The normal serum sodium range is 135 to 145 mEq/L.
Trap: (B) and (D) are the hypernatremia presentations, and D pulls hardest because it names a dramatic complication that feels like it should belong to the more acute-sounding problem. (C) is a potassium excess cluster.

Q: A patient's serum sodium returns at 152 mEq/L, and the nurse assumes the patient must be fluid-overloaded. Which statement corrects that assumption?
A) A sodium above 145 mEq/L always indicates excess fluid volume, so the assumption is correct and no further assessment is needed
B) Hypernatremia is associated with symptoms of water retention but can also occur with normal or even low fluid volume, so volume status cannot be inferred from the sodium value alone
C) Hypernatremia causes cellular swelling, so the patient's cells are overhydrated even if the vascular volume is low
D) A sodium above 145 mEq/L always indicates dehydration, so the patient must be volume-depleted
Answer: B
Rationale: Hypernatremia is associated with symptoms of water retention but can be associated with normal fluid or even low fluid volume, so the number does not settle volume status. It causes cellular dehydration, presenting with flushed skin, dry sticky mucous membranes, thirst, temperature elevation, decreased urination, muscle cramps, headache, lethargy, seizures, coma, and possible intracranial hemorrhage.
Trap: (A) and (D) are the two opposite over-readings of the same number, and both are wrong for the same reason — a serum concentration is a ratio, so it cannot tell you the size of either the sodium pool or the water pool. (C) inverts the cellular effect: high extracellular sodium pulls water out of cells and dehydrates them.

Subtopic: correcting hyponatremia safely

Q: A patient with hyponatremia present for several weeks is ordered 3% sodium chloride. Which statement reflects safe administration?
A) It is a high-alert drug and should be infused no faster than 100 mL/hr with frequent serum monitoring, because too-rapid correction causes osmotic demyelination syndrome, which is potentially fatal and worst when chronic hyponatremia is corrected quickly
B) It should be run wide open until the serum sodium returns to normal, since the fastest correction carries the lowest risk
C) It is a high-alert drug, but the infusion rate matters only in acute hyponatremia; chronic hyponatremia may be corrected at any rate
D) It is an isotonic maintenance fluid and requires no special monitoring beyond routine intake and output
Answer: A
Rationale: Hypertonic saline is a high-alert drug and should not be given faster than 100 mL/hr, with frequent serum monitoring. Infusing it too rapidly produces osmotic demyelination syndrome, formerly called central pontine myelinolysis, which is potentially fatal and is most associated with the rapid correction of chronic hyponatremia.
Trap: (C) is the plausible-but-adjacent error because it sounds like a nuance and gets the modifier exactly backward — chronic hyponatremia is the higher-risk case, not the exempt one. (D) misclassifies 3% sodium chloride as isotonic, which is the mistake that would let it be hung without a pump limit at all.
