# Chapter 7 drills — Over-the-Counter Drugs and Herbal and Dietary Supplements

Authored from `sources/Ch7_Fact_Bank.md` (Lilley, Collins & Snyder, 10th ed.).

Blueprint weight is **4 questions of 121 (3.3%), depth `standard`**, which earns
30 to 45 drills. This file carries **43** across 8 topics, averaging about 5 per
topic.

Chapter 7 is a rules-and-definitions chapter, not a drug chapter: it profiles no
drugs and names exactly one dose. The three things it can actually be tested on
got the weight — the legal line between OTC drugs and dietary supplements and
what DSHEA does *not* require, the Box 7.1 criteria for Rx-to-OTC
reclassification, and Table 7.3's herb-drug interactions.

Every distinction the chapter states in both directions is drilled from both
ends: alternative and complementary use, high and narrow therapeutic index,
acetaminophen's liver and the NSAIDs' four organs, patient out-of-pocket cost
and system cost, hawthorn and ginger root on cardiac drugs, cranberry's
elimination and grapefruit's metabolism, kava and valerian, kava and ephedra,
topical dermatitis and systemic nephritis.

What was deliberately left out, and why. **NCCAM's five categories** are named
in the chapter but never populated with examples (fact bank GAPS item 4), so a
question about them would have to be answered from memory. **Table 7.2's nine
plant-derived conventional medicines** are a list with no clinical decision
attached. The **cultural safety box's** acetylation, CYP2D6, warfarin-dose, and
beta blocker findings belong to the ethnopharmacology topics already banked from
Chapter 4; only ginseng's metabolism effect is drilled here, because the chapter
attaches it to supplement use. The **seven human need statements and their
planning outcomes** are generic and untestable as written. Individual
**evening primrose and saw palmetto** rows lost their own items to keep Table
7.3 at seven questions; both survive as named distractors. Cutting was the
intended outcome at this weight, not a shortfall.

Topic: otc, legend, and complementary medicine terms
Subtopic: legend drugs versus otc drugs versus dietary supplements

Q: A patient empties a bag onto the bedside table during a medication history: acetaminophen (Tylenol), warfarin (Coumadin), ginkgo biloba, morphine sulfate, and diphenhydramine (Benadryl). Which of these are legend drugs? Select all that apply.
A) acetaminophen (Tylenol)
B) warfarin (Coumadin)
C) ginkgo biloba
D) morphine sulfate
E) diphenhydramine (Benadryl)
Answer: B, D
Rationale: Legend drugs are "medications that are not legally available without a prescription from a prescriber; also called prescription drugs." Acetaminophen and diphenhydramine both appear in Table 7.1 as OTC products, and ginkgo biloba is a dietary supplement — a third legal category that is not a drug at all.
Trap: (C) pulls because an herb is the least ordinary item in the bag and feels like it needs special classification, but "legend" means prescription-only and has nothing to do with folklore or herbal tradition; ginkgo is sold freely under DSHEA. (A) and (E) pull because both are real drugs with real toxicity, and students slide from how dangerous a product is to how it is legally obtained.

Q: A patient tells the nurse, "The pharmacist keeps my pseudoephedrine (Sudafed) behind the counter now, so it must have become a prescription drug." Which response is accurate?
A) Correct — it was reclassified as a legend drug because of its abuse potential
B) It is still an OTC drug; it is kept behind the counter because it can be used to manufacture methamphetamine
C) It is now classified as a dietary supplement, so DSHEA governs where it is shelved
D) It became prescription-only in 2009, when the FDA began requiring special labeling
Answer: B
Rationale: Table 7.1 still lists pseudoephedrine (Sudafed) among the OTC antihistamines and decongestants. The chapter explains that pseudoephedrine is "used to manufacture the widely abused street drug methamphetamine" and that "because of the potential for abuse, products containing pseudoephedrine must be sold from behind the pharmacy counter."
Trap: (A) confuses where a drug is stored with what it legally is; counter placement is an abuse-diversion control, not reclassification to legend status. (D) borrows a real 2009 event — the FDA labeling requirement for acetaminophen, aspirin, and NSAIDs — and attaches it to the wrong drug.

Subtopic: alternative, complementary, and integrative use

Q: A patient receiving scheduled chemotherapy also attends weekly acupuncture and massage sessions and intends to complete the full chemotherapy course. Which term names what this patient is doing?
A) Alternative medicine
B) Complementary medicine
C) Conventional medicine
D) Iatrogenic therapy
Answer: B
Rationale: Complementary medicine is "alternative medicine when used simultaneously with, rather than instead of, standard Western medicine," and the chapter adds that this practice "is also referred to as integrative medicine."
Trap: (A) is the reflex pick because acupuncture and massage are themselves alternative therapies, and the option matches the therapy rather than the pattern. The axis this term names is not what the therapy is but what it is used with, and this patient is still receiving chemotherapy.

Q: A second patient declines the recommended chemotherapy entirely and treats the diagnosis with herbal preparations alone. Which term names what this patient is doing?
A) Complementary medicine
B) Integrative medicine
C) Alternative medicine
D) Conventional medicine
Answer: C
Rationale: Alternative medicine is "herbal medicine, chiropractic, acupuncture, massage, reflexology, and any other therapies traditionally not emphasized in Western medical schools." It becomes complementary only when used simultaneously with, rather than instead of, standard Western medicine.
Trap: (A) is the mirror error of the previous item — this patient stopped the conventional therapy, so nothing is being complemented. (B) fails a second way: integrative is simply the chapter's other name for complementary, so whenever both appear as options the discriminator lies elsewhere in the stem.

Subtopic: herbs, phytochemicals, and iatrogenic effects

Q: A patient holding a ginkgo capsule asks the nurse what part of it actually produces an effect in the body. Which answer is accurate?
A) The herbs — the bark, root, leaf, seed, flower, and fruit components of the plant
B) The phytochemicals — the pharmacologically active ingredients in the remedy
C) The herbal medicine — the practice of using herbs to heal
D) The standardized legend ingredients the FDA verified before marketing
Answer: B
Rationale: Phytochemicals are "the pharmacologically active ingredients in herbal remedies," and the chapter describes herbal products as containing "a wide variety of active phytochemicals (plant compounds)." Herbs are the plant parts themselves, and herbal medicine is the practice of using them.
Trap: (A) names the level immediately below the answer and pulls hard because the capsule literally contains plant material; the stem asks what produces the effect, not what the capsule is made of. (C) swaps a practice for a substance, which is the same nesting error one step further out.

Q: A nursing student asks why interest in "natural health" surged in the United States beginning in the 1960s. Which answer reflects the chapter?
A) Clinical trials had begun to show that herbs outperform conventional drugs
B) Concern over the iatrogenic effects of conventional medicine, together with a desire for more self-reliance
C) The FDA had begun approving herbal products as drugs, which reassured consumers
D) Herbal products had become substantially cheaper than prescription drugs
Answer: B
Rationale: The chapter states that "in the 1960s, concerns were expressed over the iatrogenic effects of conventional medicine. These concerns, along with a desire for more self-reliance, led to a renewed interest in 'natural health.'" Iatrogenic effects are "unintentional adverse effects that are caused by the actions of a prescriber or other health care professional, or by a specific treatment."
Trap: (A) pulls because evidence of benefit feels like the natural driver of a health trend, but the chapter attributes the shift to distrust of conventional care — and it says elsewhere that "there are few published scientific data regarding the safety of dietary supplements." (C) inverts the regulatory picture entirely.

Topic: dietary supplement regulation and dshea
Subtopic: what dshea does and does not require

Q: A patient asks what the FDA actually requires of the company that makes her supplement. Which requirements currently apply to dietary supplement manufacturers? Select all that apply.
A) Comply with quality-control standards set by the DSHEA of 1994
B) Prove the product's efficacy before it is sold
C) Identify the active ingredients on the label
D) Obtain FDA approval before the product is marketed
E) Demonstrate that the product is free from contaminants such as microbes, pesticides, and heavy metals
Answer: C, E
Rationale: "The DSHEA requires no proof of efficacy and sets no standards for quality control for supplements," and "dietary supplements do not need approval from the FDA before they are marketed." Since June 2007, however, manufacturers "must provide data that demonstrate product identity, composition, quality, purity, and strength of active ingredients" and "must also demonstrate that products are free from contaminants such as microbes, pesticides, and heavy metals."
Trap: (A), (B), and (D) are each a genuine FDA requirement moved to the wrong category — all three apply to legend drugs, for which "the FDA has specific and stringent requirements." (D) is the strongest pull, because a product sitting on a pharmacy shelf looks approved.

Q: A patient states flatly that the FDA has no authority whatsoever over herbal products. Which response is accurate?
A) That is correct; when DSHEA passed in 1994 the FDA lost all jurisdiction over supplements
B) The FDA banned ephedra, posts warnings on its website, and has set manufacturing standards since 2007, but it does not approve supplements before they are marketed
C) The FDA approves each supplement for safety, though not for effectiveness
D) The FDA reviews every supplement label for accuracy before the product reaches the shelf
Answer: B
Rationale: The chapter's operative rule is that these products "are not regulated by the FDA unless there is sufficient data to support a recall of the product. Companies are not required to provide evidence of safety and/or effectiveness." The FDA nonetheless banned ephedra in April 2004, sent a consumer-warning letter about kava in 2002, "posts warnings on herbal products on its website," and imposed good manufacturing practice requirements in June 2007.
Trap: (A) overshoots in one direction while (C) and (D) undershoot in the other, and the chapter's position is the modest middle one. (C) pulls hardest because "approved for safety but not effectiveness" sounds like a sensible regulatory compromise and describes no real category here.

Q: A patient asks how dietary supplements came to have a law of their own. Which sequence is accurate?
A) The FDA threatened to remove supplements from the market in 1993, the public mounted a letter-writing campaign, and the 103rd Congress passed the DSHEA in 1994
B) Congress passed the DSHEA in 1972 as part of the FDA's OTC Drug Review
C) The German Commission E monographs of 1978 were adopted into United States law as the DSHEA
D) The US Pharmacopeia drafted the DSHEA in 2002 as part of its verification program
Answer: A
Rationale: "In 1993, the FDA threatened to remove dietary supplements from the market. The American public reacted with a massive letter-writing campaign to Congress, and the 103rd Congress responded by passing the Dietary Supplement and Health Education Act (DSHEA) of 1994." Before that, "neither federal legislation nor the FDA provided any safeguards," and "manufacturers were responsible only for ensuring product safety."
Trap: (B) moves the 1972 date, which belongs to the FDA's OTC Drug Review, onto the supplement law. (C) and (D) move the act to the wrong actor entirely: the 1978 Commission E monographs are German, and the 2002 US Pharmacopeia program is independent certification, not legislation.

Q: A patient wants some assurance that a supplement bottle actually contains what its label claims. Which resource does the chapter offer?
A) The FDA preapproval seal, required on every supplement since 1994
B) The US Pharmacopeia's Dietary Supplement Verification Program, which certifies products it has independently tested
C) The Commission E monographs, which certify United States supplement manufacturers
D) The DSHEA registry of verified supplement ingredients
Answer: B
Rationale: In 2002 the US Pharmacopeia, "an independent organization that is the government's official standard-setting authority for dietary supplements, began certifying products that it had independently tested as part of its Dietary Supplement Verification Program."
Trap: (C) pulls because Commission E is the one herbal-quality body most students can name, but it is the German equivalent of the FDA, and its 1978 monographs "focus on herbs whose effectiveness for specific indications is supported by the research literature" rather than certifying United States products. (A) describes a seal that does not exist.

Q: A supplement label reads "supports joint comfort," and the patient asks whether the manufacturer is allowed to print that. Which statement is accurate?
A) Manufacturers may claim an effect but cannot promise a specific cure on the product label
B) Manufacturers may make any claim, including a cure, because DSHEA removed all labeling limits
C) Manufacturers may print only the ingredient list; any statement of effect is prohibited
D) Manufacturers may promise a cure if the ingredient appears in the Commission E monographs
Answer: A
Rationale: Dietary supplement manufacturers "may currently claim an effect but cannot promise a specific cure on the product label." The chapter adds that "herbal products are not FDA-approved drugs, and therefore their labeling cannot be relied on to provide consumers and patients with adequate instructions for use or even information about warnings."
Trap: (B) and (C) are the two intuitive extremes — total freedom or total silence — and the real rule sits between them. (B) pulls hardest for a student who has learned "supplements are unregulated" and generalizes that into a permission the law does not actually grant.

Topic: rx-to-otc reclassification criteria
Subtopic: box 7.1 criteria

Q: A manufacturer asks a nurse educator which features the FDA weighs when considering a prescription drug for OTC status. Which of these are criteria listed in Box 7.1? Select all that apply.
A) The condition must be diagnosed by a health care provider
B) Benefits of correct usage outweigh the risks
C) Limited interaction with other drugs
D) The drug is easy to use
E) The company will sell the OTC version at a lower price
Answer: B, C, D
Rationale: Box 7.1 groups its criteria under three headings — indication for use, safety profile, and practicality for over-the-counter use. "Benefits of correct usage must outweigh risks" is an indication bullet, "limited interaction with other drugs" a safety bullet, and "easy to use" a practicality bullet.
Trap: (A) is the exact inversion of the box's first bullet: the consumer "must be able to easily diagnose condition," so a condition that requires a provider to diagnose would disqualify the drug. (E) pulls because reclassification is loosely associated with saving money, but the box says nothing about price, and reclassification "may increase out-of-pocket costs."

Q: A drug under review for OTC status has a wide gap between the dose that produces toxicity and the dose that produces benefit. How does that bear on its candidacy?
A) It favors reclassification, because Box 7.1 requires a high therapeutic index
B) It argues against reclassification, because a wide gap makes the dose hard to titrate
C) It is irrelevant, because therapeutic index applies only to intravenous drugs
D) It favors reclassification only if the drug also requires blood level monitoring
Answer: A
Rationale: Box 7.1 lists "high therapeutic index" under safety profile, and the box's own footnote defines the index as the "ratio of toxic to therapeutic dosage." A high ratio means the toxic dose sits far above the therapeutic dose, which is the wide safety margin a self-treating consumer needs.
Trap: (B) inverts the direction, and it is the common inversion because "narrow" sounds precise and precision sounds safe. (D) pulls because monitoring sounds like extra protection, but a drug needing serum levels drawn fails the box's "easy to monitor" practicality criterion and stays prescription-only.

Q: A manufacturer proposes OTC status for a drug whose toxic concentrations lie just above therapeutic ones, so that periodic serum levels are drawn. Which response is accurate?
A) It qualifies, because the monitoring requirement proves the drug has been well studied
B) It qualifies, because a narrow therapeutic index makes a drug safer for self-treatment
C) It does not qualify, because Box 7.1 requires a high therapeutic index and a drug that is easy to monitor
D) It does not qualify, because Box 7.1 bars reclassification of any drug that ever required a prescription
Answer: C
Rationale: Box 7.1's safety profile requires a "high therapeutic index," the ratio of toxic to therapeutic dosage, and its practicality heading requires drugs that are "easy to use" and "easy to monitor." A narrow margin requiring serum levels fails both headings at once.
Trap: (B) is the mirror error of the previous item, reading a narrow margin as tight control rather than as no room for error. (D) overshoots in the opposite direction: "more than 40 medications that were previously available by prescription only have been reclassified to OTC status," so prior prescription status is the starting point of the process rather than a disqualifier.

Q: A nurse is asked what evidence the FDA relies on when deciding whether to reclassify a drug to OTC status. Which answer is accurate?
A) Clinical trials and postmarketing safety surveillance data submitted by the manufacturer
B) Consumer satisfaction surveys collected by retail pharmacies
C) The Commission E monographs
D) The volume of the drug sold during the preceding year
Answer: A
Rationale: Reclassification data come "from clinical trials and postmarketing safety surveillance data, which are submitted to the FDA by the manufacturer." The procedure has been "criticized as overly time-consuming," but it is structured to ensure that reclassified products are "safe and effective when used by the average consumer."
Trap: (B) pulls because every Box 7.1 criterion is written from the consumer's side of the counter, which makes a consumer survey sound on-topic. The box asks whether the average consumer can diagnose, monitor, and safely use the drug — not whether consumers were pleased with it.

Subtopic: reclassified products in box 7.2

Q: A patient picking up famotidine (Pepcid AC) says, "This is an antihistamine, so it should help my hay fever too." Which response is accurate?
A) Correct — all histamine blockers relieve allergy symptoms
B) Famotidine blocks histamine-2 receptors and reduces stomach acid; the allergy antihistamines are the histamine-1 blockers, such as loratadine and cetirizine
C) Famotidine is a nasal steroid, so it treats allergy but not heartburn
D) Famotidine is a proton pump inhibitor and has no effect on histamine at all
Answer: B
Rationale: Box 7.2 splits reclassified histamine blockers into histamine-1 receptor agents — chlorpheniramine, diphenhydramine, fexofenadine, loratadine, cetirizine — and histamine-2 receptor agents — cimetidine, famotidine, nizatidine. Table 7.1 reinforces the split by sending acid-controlling drugs to Chapter 50 and antihistamines to Chapter 36.
Trap: (A) has the right word family and the wrong receptor, which is exactly the confusion the split heading in Box 7.2 creates. (D) pulls because famotidine sits in Table 7.1's acid-controlling row beside the proton pump inhibitors, but the proton pump inhibitors in that row are esomeprazole, lansoprazole, and omeprazole.

Topic: otc drug hazards and self-medication toxicity
Subtopic: acetaminophen versus nsaid toxicity

Q: A patient with chronic back pain reports taking two 325-mg acetaminophen tablets eight times a day and adds, "It's only Tylenol." What is the nurse's concern?
A) The daily total is 5.2 g, above the 3 to 4 g/day ceiling, and excessive doses cause liver toxicity
B) The daily total is 2.6 g, which is within limits, so no concern exists
C) The daily total exceeds the ceiling, and the resulting risk is gastrointestinal ulceration
D) The dose is safe, because a drug sold OTC cannot reach a toxic amount
Answer: A
Rationale: Two 325-mg tablets is 650 mg per dose, and eight doses is 5200 mg, or 5.2 g/day. The chapter states that acetaminophen doses are "not to exceed a total of 3 to 4 g/day" and that "liver toxicity is associated with excessive doses ... and is a leading cause of liver failure."
Trap: (C) gets the arithmetic right and the organ wrong; gastrointestinal ulceration belongs to the NSAIDs, and the chapter never assigns it to acetaminophen. (D) is the chapter's central fallacy written as a distractor: "patients often assume that if a drug is sold OTC, it is completely safe ... This is not true."

Q: A patient taking generic OTC ibuprofen, three or four tablets three times a day, asks what is at risk. Which answer reflects the chapter?
A) Liver failure, the same risk acetaminophen carries
B) Gastrointestinal ulceration, kidney dysfunction, myocardial infarction, and stroke
C) Reye syndrome
D) Rebound congestion and dependency
Answer: B
Rationale: The chapter associates NSAIDs with "gastrointestinal ulceration, kidney dysfunction, myocardial infarction, and stroke." In 2009 the FDA began requiring specific labeling for acetaminophen, aspirin, and NSAIDs, which is the regulatory response to this cluster of risks.
Trap: (A) is the mirror error of the previous item — liver failure is the acetaminophen profile, and the chapter never assigns it to NSAIDs. (C) is a third, separate profile: aspirin alone is tied to Reye syndrome, and only in children.

Subtopic: the otc hazards catalog

Q: A parent asks the nurse to review OTC safety before a family trip. Which statements reflect the chapter's warnings? Select all that apply.
A) OTC cough and cold products are not to be used in children younger than 2 years of age
B) Aspirin is not to be used in children, because it can cause Reye syndrome
C) Long-term use of antacids can result in constipation or impaction
D) Long-term use of antacids can result in liver failure
E) OTC cough and cold products are especially effective in small children
Answer: A, B, C
Rationale: In 2008 the FDA recommended that OTC cough and cold products "not be used in children younger than 2 years of age," after reports of "oversedation, seizures, tachycardia, and even death"; such products are also "simply not efficacious in small children." "Aspirin is not to be used in children because it can cause a rare condition called Reye syndrome," and "long-term use of antacids can result in constipation or impaction."
Trap: (E) inverts the efficacy statement, and it pulls because a parent's experience of a quiet, sedated child reads as the medicine working — which is the oversedation the FDA was reacting to. (D) moves acetaminophen's organ onto the antacids.

Q: A patient has used an OTC nasal decongestant spray several times a day for three weeks and reports that it now relieves the stuffiness for only about an hour. What explains this?
A) The patient has developed rebound congestion and dependency on the spray
B) The spray has expired and lost potency
C) The patient has developed Reye syndrome
D) Nasal sprays are absorbed too slowly to remain effective beyond one week
Answer: A
Rationale: The chapter warns that patients "become addicted to OTC nasal sprays because they can cause rebound congestion and dependency." It also states that OTC medications are normally "used only for short-term treatment of common minor illnesses."
Trap: (B) is the explanation a patient reaches for first, and it pulls because it keeps the fault in the bottle rather than in the pattern of use; the chapter locates the problem squarely in continued use. (D) invents a pharmacokinetic claim the chapter never makes.

Q: A school nurse is told that students are abusing an OTC cough product. Which ingredient does the chapter name, and what is the practice called?
A) Guaifenesin, an expectorant, in a practice called Robotripping
B) Dextromethorphan, a cough suppressant, in a practice called Robotripping
C) Pseudoephedrine, a decongestant, in a practice called Robotripping
D) Diphenhydramine, an antihistamine, in a practice called Robotripping
Answer: B
Rationale: The chapter identifies dextromethorphan as "a cough suppressant" that is "commonly abused ... known by the brand name of Robitussin, and abusing it is called Robotripping."
Trap: (C) pulls because pseudoephedrine is the other abused OTC ingredient the chapter names, but its abuse route is entirely different — it is diverted to manufacture methamphetamine, which is why it sits behind the pharmacy counter. (A) pulls because guaifenesin shares the same brand shelf, and Table 7.1 lists guaifenesin (Robitussin) among the antihistamines and decongestants, but it is not the agent named as abused.

Q: A patient with type 1 diabetes and hypertension asks about taking an OTC sympathomimetic decongestant for a head cold. Which response is accurate?
A) Sympathomimetics are safe in any chronic illness, because they are available without a prescription
B) Sympathomimetics can cause problems in patients with type 1 diabetes and in patients with hypertension or angina, so the patient should consult the provider first
C) Sympathomimetics are a concern only in glaucoma
D) Sympathomimetics are legend drugs and require a prescription
Answer: B
Rationale: The chapter states that sympathomimetics "can cause problems in patients with type 1 diabetes and patients with hypertension or angina," and that OTC drug use can be "hazardous for patients with various chronic illnesses, including diabetes, hypertension, cardiovascular disease, and glaucoma."
Trap: (C) names a condition that really is on the chapter's hazardous-illness list, which makes the option feel sourced, but narrowing the warning to glaucoma drops the two conditions this patient actually has. (A) reasons from availability to safety, the fallacy the chapter calls out by name.

Topic: advantages and drawbacks of otc self-treatment
Subtopic: who pays when a drug is reclassified

Q: A drug the patient has taken for years is reclassified from prescription to OTC, and the patient asks what that will do to the cost. Which answer is accurate?
A) Out-of-pocket cost will likely rise, because third-party health insurance payers usually do not cover OTC products
B) Out-of-pocket cost will fall, because OTC products are always cheaper than prescriptions
C) Insurance coverage transfers automatically to the OTC version
D) Cost will be unchanged, because the drug and the dose are the same
Answer: A
Rationale: The chapter states that reclassification "may increase out-of-pocket costs for many patients because third-party health insurance payers usually do not cover OTC products."
Trap: (B) has the right fact on the wrong payer: overall health care costs do tend to decrease with reclassification, but that saving accrues to the system, not to this patient's wallet. (C) states the opposite of what the chapter says about third-party coverage.

Q: A hospital committee asks how a wave of Rx-to-OTC reclassifications affects overall health care spending. Which answer reflects the chapter?
A) Overall costs rise, because patients buy more products once they are freely available
B) Overall costs decrease, through a direct reduction in drug costs, elimination of prescriber office visits, and avoidance of pharmacy dispensing fees
C) Overall costs are unaffected, because the same drugs are consumed either way
D) Overall costs decrease, because insurers begin covering the OTC versions
Answer: B
Rationale: The chapter states that "overall health care costs tend to decrease ... due to a direct reduction in drug costs, elimination of prescriber office visits, and avoidance of pharmacy dispensing fees."
Trap: (D) is the mirror error of the previous item: third-party payers usually do not cover OTC products at all, and that is precisely why the patient's own cost rises while the system's falls. (A) confuses volume of purchases with total cost.

Subtopic: symptom relief versus addressing the cause

Q: A patient has been self-treating "acid stomach" with an OTC proton pump inhibitor for about a year. What is the nurse's primary concern?
A) The product interacts with several of the herbal supplements listed in Table 7.3
B) OTC drugs are meant for short-term treatment of minor illnesses, and a chronic condition warrants an appropriate medical evaluation
C) The product will cause rebound congestion
D) The product is a legend drug that was obtained improperly
Answer: B
Rationale: "Normally OTC medications are used only for short-term treatment of common minor illnesses. An appropriate medical evaluation is recommended for all chronic health conditions." The chapter also warns that OTC drugs "may relieve symptoms without necessarily addressing the cause of the disorder; this can cause delay in the effective management of chronic disease states."
Trap: (A) pulls because a year on any product invites a hunt for an interaction, but nothing in Table 7.3 touches proton pump inhibitors; this is a duration problem, not an interaction problem. (D) is wrong on the facts, since omeprazole (Prilosec-OTC) appears in Box 7.2 as a reclassified OTC product.

Q: A nurse is planning OTC teaching for a group of older adults. Which concern does the chapter raise about the products' labels?
A) Small print on OTC package labels complicates interpretation, especially for older adults, and inexperience in reading medication labels results in misuse
B) OTC labels are written at a fifth-grade reading level, so misuse is rare
C) OTC labels omit dosage instructions, which must be obtained from a pharmacist
D) OTC labels list only inactive ingredients
Answer: A
Rationale: "Many patients are inexperienced in the interpretation of medication labels, which results in misuse of the products. Small print on OTC package labels often complicates the situation, especially for older adults." The Institute for Safe Medication Practices reported that parents gave children incorrect doses of OTC fever medications more than 50% of the time.
Trap: (C) and (D) both misstate the required "drug facts" label, which must carry purpose and uses, specific warnings, when to consult a doctor or pharmacist, side effects, substances or activities to avoid, dosage instructions, active ingredients, storage information, and inactive ingredients. (B) invents a reading-level standard the chapter never claims and would make the whole teaching problem disappear.

Topic: herb and supplement drug interactions
Subtopic: bleeding risk with anticoagulants and antiplatelets

Q: A patient taking warfarin (Coumadin) lists the supplements kept in the kitchen cabinet. Which of them may interact with anticoagulants and alter bleeding? Select all that apply.
A) Chamomile
B) Ginkgo
C) Echinacea
D) Kava
E) Garlic
Answer: A, B, E
Rationale: Table 7.3 gives chamomile "increased risk for bleeding with anticoagulants," gingko "may increase risk for bleeding with anticoagulants (warfarin, heparin) and antiplatelets (aspirin, clopidogrel)," and garlic "possible interference with hypoglycemic therapy and the anticoagulant warfarin (Coumadin)."
Trap: (D) is the seductive miss, because kava's famous hazard is liver toxicity, which reads as adjacent to a coagulopathy; kava's actual Table 7.3 row names barbiturates and alcohol, not anticoagulants. (C) has a row naming immunosuppressants and antivirals, an entirely different drug class.

Subtopic: cardiac and metabolic interactions

Q: A patient taking digoxin also takes an herbal product daily. Which supplement in Table 7.3 may lead to toxic levels of cardiac glycosides?
A) Hawthorn
B) Ginger root
C) Valerian
D) Cranberry
Answer: A
Rationale: Table 7.3 gives hawthorn "may lead to toxic levels of cardiac glycosides (e.g., digitalis)," and that is hawthorn's only listed interaction. The chapter separately warns that herbal products can "potentiate digoxin toxicity."
Trap: (B) pulls because ginger root's row does name cardiac drugs, but it reads "at high dosages, possible interference with cardiac, antidiabetic, or anticoagulant drugs" — interference across three classes, conditioned on dose, rather than a specific push toward glycoside toxicity.

Q: A patient asks what the textbook says about ginger root and prescription drugs. Which statement matches Table 7.3?
A) It raises cardiac glycoside levels toward toxicity at any dose
B) At high dosages it may interfere with cardiac, antidiabetic, or anticoagulant drugs
C) It increases central nervous system depression when taken with sedatives
D) It decreases the elimination of renally excreted drugs
Answer: B
Rationale: Table 7.3's ginger root row reads "at high dosages, possible interference with cardiac, antidiabetic, or anticoagulant drugs." No other row in the table is dose-conditional, so the qualifier belongs to ginger alone.
Trap: (A) is the mirror error of the previous item, crossing hawthorn's glycoside effect onto ginger and stripping off the dose qualifier that distinguishes them. (C) is valerian's row and (D) is cranberry's.

Q: A patient taking a renally excreted drug begins drinking large amounts of cranberry juice daily. What does Table 7.3 predict?
A) Decreased elimination of the drug, so drug levels rise
B) Decreased metabolism of the drug by hepatic enzymes
C) Increased renal elimination, so drug levels fall
D) No change, because juices do not affect drug handling
Answer: A
Rationale: Table 7.3 gives cranberry "decreased elimination of many drugs that are renally excreted." Less elimination leaves more drug in the body.
Trap: (B) is grapefruit's row, not cranberry's; both raise drug levels, so the result is never the discriminator — the organ is, with cranberry acting on renal elimination and grapefruit on metabolism. (C) inverts the direction because cranberry and the urinary tract are linked in most students' minds.

Q: A patient taking an HMG-CoA reductase inhibitor asks whether the morning grapefruit juice matters. Which response reflects Table 7.3?
A) Grapefruit increases metabolism, so the statin will be less effective
B) Grapefruit decreases metabolism and increases the risk for toxicity of immunosuppressants, HMG-CoA reductase inhibitors, and some psychotherapeutic drugs
C) Grapefruit decreases the renal elimination of the statin
D) Grapefruit has no listed interaction in Table 7.3
Answer: B
Rationale: Table 7.3's grapefruit row states that it "decreases metabolism of drugs used for erectile dysfunction, estrogens, and some psychotherapeutic drugs" and "increases risk for toxicity of immunosuppressants, HMG-CoA reductase inhibitors, and some psychotherapeutic drugs." The same row notes that grapefruit "increases intensity and duration of effects of caffeine."
Trap: (A) is the reflex inversion, because the word "decreases" sitting next to "juice" fools people into predicting a weaker drug effect; decreased metabolism means more drug and a stronger effect. (C) is the mirror error of the previous item, handing grapefruit cranberry's renal mechanism.

Subtopic: central nervous system and serotonergic interactions

Q: A patient taking a selective serotonin reuptake inhibitor for depression begins St. John's wort on a friend's recommendation. Which risk does Table 7.3 name?
A) Serotonin syndrome
B) Rebound congestion
C) Reye syndrome
D) Hypoglycemia
Answer: A
Rationale: Table 7.3 states that St. John's wort "may lead to serotonin syndrome if used with other serotonergic drugs (e.g., selective serotonin reuptake inhibitors [see Chapter 16])." Its row also names antidepressants, antihistamines, digoxin, immunosuppressants, theophylline, and warfarin — the widest interaction profile in the chapter.
Trap: (D) pulls because a glucose effect is the second-most-remembered herbal hazard, but hypoglycemic therapy belongs to garlic's row and to ginger root at high dosages. Neither of the remaining options appears anywhere in Table 7.3.

Q: A patient who takes a benzodiazepine sedative at bedtime asks about adding an herbal sleep product containing valerian. Which interaction does Table 7.3 name?
A) Valerian increases central nervous system depression if used with sedatives
B) Valerian increases the effect of barbiturates and alcohol
C) Valerian interferes with immunosuppressants and antivirals
D) Valerian may change the effects of hormonal contraceptives
Answer: A
Rationale: Table 7.3 gives valerian "increases central nervous system depression if used with sedatives." Valerian is also the ingredient in the case study's sleep product, taken "at night if needed."
Trap: (B) is kava's row, and it is the hardest pull because kava and valerian are the table's two sedating herbs; the discriminator is the partner drug — kava with barbiturates and alcohol, valerian with sedatives. (C) is echinacea's row and (D) is saw palmetto's.

Topic: herbal product safety and adverse effects
Subtopic: kava versus ephedra

Q: A patient who has taken kava for several months to manage stress now has a yellow tinge to the skin and sclera. Which concern is the priority?
A) Possible liver toxicity, the risk behind the FDA's 2002 consumer-warning letter
B) Possible cardiovascular and stroke risk, which is why the FDA banned kava in April 2004
C) Serotonin syndrome, because kava potentiates serotonergic drugs
D) Nephritis, because all systemically taken herbals damage the kidney
Answer: A
Rationale: The chapter pairs kava with "possible liver toxicity" and a "2002 FDA consumer-warning letter," and notes that "kava remains on the market." Yellowing of the skin and sclera is the liver sign.
Trap: (B) belongs to ephedra — cardiovascular and stroke risk, banned in April 2004 — and it is the standing confusion because both herbs appear in the same safety paragraph. Kava was warned about and never banned; ephedra was banned and never merely warned about.

Q: A patient asks why an ephedra-containing weight-loss product she used years ago is no longer on the shelf. Which response is accurate?
A) It was withdrawn voluntarily after reports of liver failure
B) The FDA officially banned it in April 2004 because of possible cardiovascular and stroke risks
C) It received only a consumer-warning letter and is still available under another name
D) DSHEA prohibited all weight-loss supplements in 1994
Answer: B
Rationale: The chapter states that ephedra carries "possible cardiovascular and stroke risks" and was "officially banned by the FDA in April 2004." That ban is one of the chapter's demonstrations that the FDA is not powerless over supplements.
Trap: (C) is the mirror error of the previous item — a warning letter that left the product on the market is kava's outcome, not ephedra's. (A) moves kava's organ onto ephedra while keeping ephedra's removal from the market.

Subtopic: the natural equals safe fallacy

Q: A patient explains that she did not mention her herbal products during the history because "they're natural, so there's nothing to report." Which response reflects the chapter?
A) That is reasonable — natural products have no reportable effects
B) Herbal products are often perceived as natural and therefore harmless, but allergic reactions, toxic reactions, adverse effects, possible mutagenic effects, and drug interactions have all been documented
C) Only herbal products taken together with prescription drugs need to be reported
D) The FDA tests every herbal product for allergens before it is marketed
Answer: B
Rationale: "Dietary supplements, and especially herbal medicines, are often perceived as being natural and therefore harmless; however, this is not the case. Many examples exist of allergic reactions, toxic reactions, and adverse effects caused by herbs. Some herbs have been shown to have possible mutagenic effects and to interact with drugs." More than 40% of patients using dietary supplements do not disclose this to their health care providers.
Trap: (A) is the "false sense of security" the chapter names outright, and it is the belief the chapter exists to correct. (C) narrows the disclosure rule until it excuses exactly the patients least likely to report, since a patient who has not disclosed the herb cannot know whether it interacts.

Q: A patient develops jaundice while taking an herbal product, and the nurse notifies the provider. What else should the nurse do?
A) Report the adverse effect to the toll-free number 800-332-1088
B) Report the event to the state pharmaceutical board
C) Contact the product's manufacturer, which is responsible for investigating
D) Take no further action, since supplements are not FDA-approved and cannot be reported
Answer: A
Rationale: "The toll-free number for reporting adverse effects of prescription drugs, OTC drugs, herbals, and dietary supplements is 800-332-1088. Nurses may report adverse events anonymously and without consequence via telephone." Reporting is also available inside medical reference applications such as Epocrates or Medscape.
Trap: (D) is the classic under-response, and it pulls because it reasons from a true premise — supplements are not FDA-approved — to a false conclusion, when the reporting line explicitly covers all four product categories. (C) sends the report to the least interested party.

Subtopic: route determines the organ at risk

Q: A patient with chronic kidney disease asks about starting an oral herbal supplement. Which caution from the chapter applies?
A) Herbals taken systemically may be associated with kidney disorders such as nephritis, so this patient must seek medical advice before using certain herbals
B) Herbals taken systemically cause dermatitis, so the concern is a skin reaction
C) Kidney disease is a concern only with topical herbal preparations
D) No caution applies, because oral herbals are poorly absorbed
Answer: A
Rationale: The chapter states that some herbals "may cause dermatitis when used topically, whereas some taken systemically may be associated with kidney disorders such as nephritis," and therefore that "patients with existing skin problems or kidney dysfunction must seek medical advice before using certain herbals."
Trap: (B) and (C) both swap the axis: dermatitis pairs with topical use and pre-existing skin problems, nephritis with systemic use and pre-existing kidney dysfunction. (C) is the more tempting swap, because a topical product feels like the milder exposure and therefore like the one that would carry the milder warning.

Topic: otc and herbal medication history and teaching
Subtopic: what belongs in the medication history

Q: A nurse is taking a medication history from a newly admitted patient. Which items belong in that history? Select all that apply.
A) Prescription drugs
B) Over-the-counter drugs
C) Herbal products
D) Vitamins and minerals
E) Only the products the patient personally considers to be medicines
Answer: A, B, C, D
Rationale: The medication history must include "all medications and substances used by the patient, including OTC drugs, prescription drugs, herbal products, vitamins, and minerals," and the nurse should "also note use of alcohol, tobacco, and caffeine," along with allergies to any of the ingredients.
Trap: (E) is the boundary the patient draws and a rushed nurse accepts, and it is why more than 40% of supplement users never disclose their use. The chapter's own case study opens with a patient announcing "I don't have any prescribed medicines" while taking seven products.

Q: During a drug history a patient did not mention taking daily aspirin, explaining, "It's not a prescription." Which response by the nurse is best?
A) "Over-the-counter drugs are generally not harmful, so it's fine that you left it out."
B) "Aspirin is one of the safest drugs out there."
C) "Although aspirin is over the counter, it's still important to know why you take it, how much you take, and how often."
D) "We need you to be honest about the drugs you are taking."
Answer: C
Rationale: The chapter's rule is that "OTC drugs, including herbal products, are still medications," and what the nurse supplies is "choice of an appropriate product, correct dosing, common adverse effects, and drug interactions." Safety with these products "begins with education."
Trap: (A) and (B) validate the exact misconception the chapter exists to correct — that OTC availability implies safety. (D) fails a different way: it treats an information gap as a character problem, and it shuts down the disclosure the nurse still needs.

Subtopic: hidden duplicate ingredients

Q: A patient who takes acetaminophen (Tylenol) daily for arthritis adds an OTC cold and flu product for a head cold. What should the nurse teach?
A) The two may be taken together freely, since both are available without a prescription
B) Read the ingredient lists of both products, because the cold and flu product may also contain acetaminophen and push the daily total past the 3 to 4 g ceiling
C) Take the two products at least one hour apart to avoid an absorption interaction
D) Stop the arthritis medication, because cold products replace analgesics
Answer: B
Rationale: The patient teaching box instructs patients to read the ingredients of every product when using more than one, "because the ingredient/chemical may occur in both products," and gives products containing acetaminophen plus a cold and flu product as one of its two examples. The hazards section adds that patients must be "careful not to give two products that contain the same active ingredient(s)."
Trap: (C) treats the situation as an interaction between two different drugs when it is duplication of one drug; separating the doses in time does nothing to the daily total, which is what crosses the ceiling. (A) applies the availability-equals-safety fallacy to two products at once.

Q: A patient taking an herbal stress product containing ginseng also takes a daily multivitamin supplement. Which teaching point applies?
A) Multivitamin supplements contain no herbal ingredients, so no ingredient review is needed
B) A multivitamin supplement may itself contain ginseng, so the ingredient lists of both products should be reviewed
C) Ginseng always accelerates drug metabolism, so every other drug the patient takes will be less effective
D) Ginseng is FDA-approved and standardized, so duplication is harmless
Answer: B
Rationale: The patient teaching box gives "a multivitamin supplement may contain ginseng" as one of its two hidden-ingredient examples. The chapter's only ginseng pharmacology comes from the cultural safety box, where ginseng "may inhibit or accelerate the metabolism of a specific medication" and can "significantly affect the drug's absorption or elimination."
Trap: (C) commits to one direction when the source is deliberately bidirectional — inhibit or accelerate — so an option naming only one of the two overstates what the chapter actually says. (D) invents an approval status that dietary supplements do not have.

Subtopic: measuring otc liquids for children

Q: A parent asks how to measure a dose of an OTC liquid fever medicine for a toddler. Which instruction is correct?
A) Use a household teaspoon and select the dose according to the child's age
B) Use only the device that came with that medicine, measure at eye level on a flat surface, and dose according to the child's current weight
C) Use the dosing cup from any of the family's OTC liquids, since the calibrations are standardized
D) Measure quickly in a dimly lit room so the child is not fully woken
Answer: B
Rationale: The Preventing Medication Errors box states "never use household measuring devices," directs the use of "only the device that comes with the OTC medicine," which is "calibrated to match the specific product labeling," instructs reading "at eye level" while measuring "on a flat surface," and states that for children it is "best to dose according to weight, not age."
Trap: (A) combines both shortcuts the box rejects: the household spoon, which it calls inaccurate, and the age-based dose, which uses the axis the box explicitly sets aside in favor of the child's current weight. (C) pulls because dosing cups look interchangeable, and the box says never to use a device supplied with one medicine for a different medicine.
