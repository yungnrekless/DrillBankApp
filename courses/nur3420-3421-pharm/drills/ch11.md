# Chapter 11 drills — General and Local Anesthetics

Authored from `sources/Ch11_Fact_Bank.md` (Lilley, Collins & Snyder, 10th ed.,
printed pp. 165–182).

Blueprint weight is **6 questions of 121 (5.0%), depth `standard`** — split 4 on
Exam 1 and 2 on the Final. That earns **44 drills** across 8 topics, near the top
of the 30-to-45 band a `standard` chapter carries. The count sits high inside the
band for one reason the fact bank states outright: this chapter is unusually
definition-heavy, and its 6 blueprint items are far more likely to land on
which-word-means-what than on any drug's dose. Twelve key terms plus the
ester/amide and depolarizing/nondepolarizing splits do not compress below this.

Topics follow the chapter's own order — the vocabulary map, then general
anesthetics, then malignant hyperthermia, then the neuromuscular blockers, then
the local anesthetics, then the nursing process — because the exam asks these by
category and a stem usually turns on which category a drug or finding belongs to.

Every distinction the chapter states in both directions is drilled from both
ends: general and local, spinal-feels-central-but-is-local against
sedation-feels-local-but-is-general, lipid and water solubility, tachycardia and
vagal bradycardia, agonist and antagonist, paralysis order and recovery order,
Box 11.4's potentiating conditions and Box 11.5's opposing ones, ester and amide,
intrathecal and epidural, naloxone and flumazenil. Inverting those is the reflex
error this chapter produces.

Topic: anesthesia terminology and scope
Subtopic: general versus local anesthesia

Q: A patient scheduled for an abdominal hysterectomy under general anesthesia asks why she will need a breathing tube and a ventilator when her neighbor had a hand laceration repaired without one. Which explanation is accurate?
A) General anesthesia alters CNS nerve impulses throughout the entire body and depresses the respiratory drive, so ventilatory support is required
B) General anesthesia blocks only the nerves that innervate the surgical site, but the tube keeps secretions out of the airway
C) The breathing tube is required because all general anesthetics are gases and there is no other way to deliver them
D) General anesthesia preserves the respiratory drive, but the ventilator is used to speed emergence at the end of the case
Answer: A
Rationale: General anesthesia is a drug-induced state in which the CNS nerve impulses are altered to reduce pain and other sensations throughout the entire body, involving complete loss of consciousness and depression of respiratory drive. The overview states the consequence flatly: loss of normal respiratory function requires mechanical or manual ventilatory support to avoid brain damage and suffocation.
Trap: (B) borrows the local anesthesia definition, which limits the effect to the tissues innervated by the anesthetized nerves. The two key-term definitions are built on the same sentence frame and differ in exactly two slots, which is what makes them swappable.

Q: The neighbor is having a forearm laceration sutured after infiltration with lidocaine and asks whether she too could stop breathing. Which response is accurate?
A) Local anesthesia does not involve paralysis of respiratory function; it eliminates pain sensation only in the tissues innervated by the anesthetized nerves
B) Local anesthesia depresses the CNS enough that a ventilator must be at the bedside for every procedure
C) Local anesthesia produces complete loss of consciousness but spares the respiratory drive
D) Local anesthesia alters CNS nerve impulses throughout the entire body, but at a dose too small to matter
Answer: A
Rationale: Local anesthesia is a drug-induced state in which peripheral or spinal nerve impulses are altered to reduce or eliminate pain and other sensations in tissues innervated by those nerves. The chapter contrasts it directly with general anesthesia: local anesthesia does not involve paralysis of respiratory function but only elimination of pain sensation in the tissues innervated by anesthetized nerves.
Trap: (C) is the mirror error of the previous item and also smuggles in the moderate sedation definition, which is the one that pairs altered consciousness with a preserved respiratory drive. Local anesthesia leaves consciousness intact as well.

Subtopic: where the sedation terms sit

Q: A nurse preparing a patient for a cesarean birth under spinal anesthesia is asked by a student how spinal anesthesia is classified. Which response is correct?
A) It is a form of general anesthesia, because the drug is placed near the spinal cord and reaches the CNS
B) It is a form of local anesthesia, because it does not depress the CNS at a level that causes loss of consciousness
C) It is a form of moderate sedation, because consciousness is only partly reduced
D) It is monitored anesthesia care, because sedation and analgesia are combined
Answer: B
Rationale: The key term for spinal anesthesia opens with the words "Local anesthesia induced by injection of an anesthetic drug near the spinal cord," and the key points repeat it: spinal anesthesia, or regional anesthesia, is a form of local anesthesia. The body text explains that because spinal anesthesia does not depress the CNS at a level that causes loss of consciousness, it can be thought of as a large-scale type of local rather than general anesthesia.
Trap: (A) is the reflex pick because a needle near the spinal cord feels central, and the word "spinal" pulls toward the CNS. The cue is that a needle near the spinal cord still means local, and the block runs distal to the injection site.

Q: A patient scheduled for a colonoscopy with moderate sedation asks whether that counts as being "put under." How should the nurse classify moderate sedation?
A) As a form of local anesthesia, because the patient stays awake and breathes on his own
B) As a milder form of general anesthesia that causes partial or complete loss of consciousness but does not generally reduce the normal respiratory drive
C) As monitored anesthesia care, because a provider stays at the bedside
D) As a form of spinal anesthesia delivered intravenously
Answer: B
Rationale: Both the key term and the key points file moderate sedation under the general heading: it is a milder form of general anesthesia resulting in partial or complete loss of consciousness but without reducing normal respiratory drive. Moderate sedation, conscious sedation, and procedural sedation are synonymous terms.
Trap: (A) pulls because everything about moderate sedation's clinical use reads local — outpatient, awake, self-ventilating. Pair it against the previous item to hold both: spinal anesthesia feels central and is classified local; moderate sedation feels local and is classified general.

Q: A procedure is documented as monitored anesthesia care. Which description matches what the patient will receive?
A) Local anesthesia along with sedation and analgesia, with the patient able to answer questions appropriately and protect the airway
B) General anesthesia with an inhaled agent, titrated to keep the respiratory drive intact
C) Continuous monitoring of a mechanically ventilated patient receiving a neuromuscular blocking drug
D) A spinal injection with the patient monitored until sensation returns
Answer: A
Rationale: Monitored anesthesia care is a planned procedure in which the patient undergoes local anesthesia along with sedation and analgesia. Its three fundamental elements are safe sedation, control of anxiety, and pain control; patients are able to answer questions appropriately and protect their airways, and can be discharged soon after the procedure.
Trap: (B) confuses monitored anesthesia care with moderate sedation, and the two are separated by a different axis than depth. Monitored anesthesia care is defined by what is combined; moderate sedation is defined by how deep.

Subtopic: adjuncts versus balanced anesthesia

Q: An anesthesia provider plans to combine several drug classes rather than use a single agent. Select all the accurate statements about adjunct anesthetics and balanced anesthesia.
A) Balanced anesthesia is the practice of using combinations of different drug classes rather than a single drug
B) Adjunct anesthetics are used to control the adverse effects of anesthetics
C) Adjunct anesthetics help maintain the anesthetic state in the patient
D) With general anesthetics, adjuncts are used for induction, sedation, reduction of anxiety, and amnesia
E) An adjunct is a practice rather than a drug, and balanced anesthesia names the helper drugs themselves
Answer: A, B, C, D
Rationale: An adjunct is any drug that enhances clinical therapy when used simultaneously with another drug, and adjuncts can be thought of as "helper drugs." The chapter's own sentence joins the two terms: the simultaneous use of both general anesthetics and adjuncts is called balanced anesthesia. Adjuncts have two stated jobs, and both are on this list.
Trap: (E) inverts the pair, and it is the intuitive inversion because the key term for adjuncts sends the reader straight to balanced anesthesia. An adjunct is a drug; balanced anesthesia is the practice of combining them. Distractors also like to drop one of the adjunct's two jobs and keep only the other.

Topic: general anesthetic classes and adjunct drugs
Subtopic: inhalational versus parenteral

Q: A student is asked to sort the general anesthetics by route. Select all the drugs on the chapter's parenteral list.
A) etomidate
B) ketamine
C) sevoflurane
D) propofol
E) thiopental
F) nitrous oxide
Answer: A, B, D, E
Rationale: Table 11.1 gives the parenteral general anesthetics as etomidate, ketamine, methohexital, propofol, and thiopental, all given intravenously for induction and/or maintenance of general anesthesia, induction of amnesia, and as adjuncts to inhalation-type anesthetics. Table 11.2 holds the inhalational agents.
Trap: (C) is the reflex inclusion because sevoflurane is one of the most familiar anesthetic names and the two tables sit on the same page. Apply the ending rule: if it ends in -flurane or is a gas, it is inhalational. Watch ketamine, which stays on the parenteral list even though it is the chapter's most-routed drug, given intravenously, intramuscularly, and subcutaneously.

Q: Which statement about the inhalational general anesthetics is accurate?
A) Nitrous oxide is the only inhaled gas; desflurane, isoflurane, and sevoflurane are volatile liquids
B) All four inhaled agents are gases stored under pressure and mixed with oxygen
C) The volatile liquids are vaporized and then injected intravenously
D) Nitrous oxide is the volatile liquid and the -flurane drugs are the gases
Answer: A
Rationale: The inhalational anesthetics are volatile liquids or gases that are vaporized or mixed with oxygen or medical air to induce anesthesia. The chapter lists nitrous oxide, or laughing gas, as the inhaled gas, and desflurane, isoflurane, and sevoflurane as the inhaled volatile liquids.
Trap: (D) is the mirror error of the correct option — it keeps the two categories and swaps their members. Nitrous oxide is the odd one out on the inhaled list, and it is the gas rather than a liquid.

Subtopic: the Overton-Meyer theory

Q: An instructor asks why a highly lipid-soluble anesthetic is more potent than a water-soluble one. Which response reflects the Overton-Meyer theory?
A) Potency varies directly with lipid solubility, because nerve cell membranes, the brain, the spinal cord, and the blood-brain barrier all have high lipid content
B) Potency varies directly with water solubility, because the drug must dissolve in plasma before it can reach the brain
C) Potency varies directly with molecular weight, because larger molecules occupy more receptors
D) Potency varies inversely with lipid solubility, because fat stores the drug away from the brain
Answer: A
Rationale: The Overton-Meyer theory describes the relationship between the lipid solubility of anesthetic drugs and their potency. Nerve cell membranes have high lipid content, as do the brain, spinal cord, and blood-brain barrier, so lipid-soluble anesthetics can easily cross the blood-brain barrier to concentrate in nerve cell membranes.
Trap: (D) is chosen by students who remember from Chapter 2 that fat sequesters drugs, then apply that to potency. Overton-Meyer runs the other way: fat-soluble drugs are stronger anesthetics than water-soluble drugs.

Q: Two anesthetics are compared and the more water-soluble one turns out to be substantially less potent. How should the nurse interpret that finding?
A) It is expected, because potency varies directly with lipid solubility, so the more water-soluble drug is the weaker anesthetic
B) It is unexpected, because water solubility speeds delivery to the brain and should raise potency
C) It confirms that the Overton-Meyer theory explains every property of the anesthetic drugs
D) It reflects protein binding rather than solubility, which is what actually determines anesthetic potency
Answer: A
Rationale: Because potency varies directly with lipid solubility, the water-soluble member of a pair is predicted to be the weaker anesthetic. It is the lipid-soluble drug that crosses the blood-brain barrier readily to concentrate in the high-lipid nerve cell membranes.
Trap: (B) is the mirror error of the previous item, inverting the same axis from the other end. (C) overreaches in a way the chapter itself refuses: many theories have been proposed, the drugs vary widely in chemical structure, and Overton-Meyer has been used to explain only some of the properties of anesthetic drugs.

Subtopic: adjunctive anesthetic drugs and their indications

Q: Table 11.3 lists both fentanyl and morphine as adjunctive anesthetic drugs, and both are labeled opioid analgesics. Which pairing of drug to stated indication is correct?
A) fentanyl for anesthesia induction; morphine for pain prevention and pain relief
B) fentanyl for pain prevention and pain relief; morphine for anesthesia induction
C) Both are listed for drying up excessive secretions
D) Both are listed for amnesia and anxiety reduction
Answer: A
Rationale: Table 11.3 has two opioid rows with different jobs. The short-acting synthetics alfentanil, fentanyl, and sufentanil are listed for anesthesia induction, while meperidine and morphine are listed for pain prevention and pain relief.
Trap: (B) crosses the two opioid rows, which is easy to do because both carry the same pharmacologic class label. (C) takes the anticholinergic row's purpose: atropine and glycopyrrolate are the only entry whose job is drying secretions rather than sedation, amnesia, anxiety, or pain, so an option giving atropine "for sedation" has also borrowed a neighboring row.

Topic: general anesthesia adverse effects and risk factors
Subtopic: cardiovascular and organ system effects

Q: A patient's blood pressure falls steadily during a lengthy procedure under general anesthesia. How should the nurse interpret this?
A) It is the one major complication of general anesthesia the chapter names, and it threatens perfusion of the heart, peripheral circulation, liver, kidneys, and respiratory tract
B) It indicates an allergic reaction and the anesthetic should be presumed contraindicated
C) It is an unexpected finding, since general anesthetics reliably raise blood pressure
D) It is the earliest and most specific sign of malignant hyperthermia
Answer: A
Rationale: Adverse effects of general anesthetics are dose dependent and vary with the individual drug; the sites primarily affected are the heart, peripheral circulation, liver, kidneys, and respiratory tract. One major complication of general anesthesia is hypotension, affecting perfusion of those organs.
Trap: (D) is tempting because malignant hyperthermia is the chapter's headline emergency, but the blood pressure sign it lists is *unstable* blood pressure rather than a predictable fall, and its defining sign is a rapid rise in body temperature.

Q: During general anesthesia a patient shows tachycardia with a falling blood pressure, and later the heart rate drops sharply during vagal stimulation. Which statement matches Table 11.4?
A) Depressed myocardium, hypotension, and tachycardia are the listed cardiovascular effects; bradycardia is listed specifically in response to vagal stimulation
B) Bradycardia is the routine cardiovascular effect, and tachycardia occurs only in response to vagal stimulation
C) Only bradycardia is listed, so the tachycardia must have another cause entirely
D) Cardiovascular effects are limited to the peripheral circulation, so neither rate change is drug related
Answer: A
Rationale: Table 11.4 puts both rate changes in the cardiovascular row but attaches a trigger to only one of them: depressed myocardium, hypotension and tachycardia, bradycardia in response to vagal stimulation.
Trap: (B) reverses the pair and keeps the trigger clause on the wrong half. An option that offers bradycardia as a routine general-anesthetic effect has dropped the trigger clause altogether.

Q: Select all the organ system effects of inhaled and intravenous general anesthetics that Table 11.4 lists.
A) Increased intracranial pressure
B) Reduced hepatic blood flow and reduced hepatic clearance
C) Decreased glomerular filtration
D) Cutaneous vasodilation
E) Increased glomerular filtration with diuresis
F) Skeletal muscle relaxation
Answer: A, B, C, D, F
Rationale: Table 11.4 gives increased intracranial pressure for the cerebrovascular system, reduced hepatic blood flow and thus reduced hepatic clearance for the gastrointestinal system, decreased glomerular filtration for the renal system, vasodilation for the cutaneous circulation, and skeletal muscle relaxation for skeletal muscles.
Trap: (E) is the reflex inclusion for anyone who remembers that anesthesia affects the kidney but not which direction it moves. Both the renal and the hepatic rows point down, and holding them as a pair is easier than holding either alone.

Subtopic: postoperative nausea and vomiting and the older adult

Q: A child is still in the PACU three hours after a tonsillectomy because of persistent nausea and vomiting, and rates pain 8 out of 10. Which nursing action does the chapter's own statement support?
A) Treat the patient's pain adequately
B) Restrict all oral intake for the next 24 hours
C) Reposition the patient onto the left side and dim the lights
D) Explain that the exact cause of postoperative nausea is the inhaled agent and it will resolve as the drug is exhaled
Answer: A
Rationale: Postoperative nausea and vomiting remains one of the most common reasons children and adults have extended stays in the postanesthesia care unit. The chapter states there is no exact cause and that it is thought to be due to multiple factors, then names one causal link: pain is associated with postoperative nausea and vomiting, so adequate treatment of pain frequently decreases nausea.
Trap: (D) asserts a single cause, which the chapter explicitly denies. Because the source gives no exact cause, the only intervention it supports on causal grounds is treating the pain — not the antiemetic reflex most students reach for first.

Q: A 79-year-old with declining liver and kidney function is scheduled for general anesthesia. Which explanation of the increased risk matches the chapter's lifespan box?
A) Declining liver function decreases drug metabolism and declining renal function decreases drug excretion, either of which can lead to drug toxicity, unsafe levels, or overdose
B) Declining liver function increases absorption while declining renal function increases distribution into tissue
C) Older adults are less sensitive to CNS drug effects, which offsets the slower clearance
D) The risk is unchanged as long as the patient is taking fewer than five medications
Answer: A
Rationale: The older adult patient is affected more adversely by anesthesia than the young or middle-aged adult. A decline in liver function results in decreased metabolism of drugs and a decline in renal function leads to decreased drug excretion; if both organs are impaired the risk is even greater. Older adults are also more sensitive to the effects of drugs affecting the central nervous system.
Trap: (B) names two pharmacokinetic steps the box never mentions. The two steps it does name are metabolism, which is hepatic, and excretion, which is renal — the same phases Chapter 2 defines. (D) contradicts the box's polypharmacy warning that the more drugs a patient takes, the higher the risk for adverse reactions and drug-drug interactions.

Topic: malignant hyperthermia and dantrolene
Subtopic: triggers and risk

Q: A preoperative patient reports that a cousin nearly died of a very high fever during surgery. Which two agents are classically associated with malignant hyperthermia?
A) Volatile inhalational anesthetics and the depolarizing neuromuscular blocker succinylcholine
B) Nitrous oxide and the nondepolarizing neuromuscular blocker rocuronium
C) Propofol and neostigmine
D) Lidocaine and epinephrine
Answer: A
Rationale: Malignant hyperthermia is an uncommon but potentially fatal, genetically linked adverse metabolic reaction to general anesthesia, and it is classically associated with the use of volatile inhalational anesthetics as well as the depolarizing NMBD succinylcholine. Those at greater risk are children, adolescents, and individuals with muscular and/or skeletal abnormalities.
Trap: (B) keeps both right categories but names the wrong member of each. Nitrous oxide is the inhaled gas rather than a volatile liquid, and rocuronium is nondepolarizing. Succinylcholine is the only depolarizing NMBD in the chapter, and it is the one on the trigger list.

Q: A patient with a family history of malignant hyperthermia states that he personally has had anesthesia twice with no trouble, and succinylcholine is on the plan. What should the nurse conclude?
A) Succinylcholine is contraindicated, because its drug profile names a personal or familial history of malignant hyperthermia
B) Succinylcholine is safe, because the patient himself has no history of the reaction
C) Succinylcholine is safe, because only a documented episode under the same anesthetic agent disqualifies a patient
D) The family history is relevant only if the relative is a first-degree relative on the maternal side
Answer: A
Rationale: Succinylcholine is contraindicated in patients with a personal or familial history of malignant hyperthermia. The assessment section adds that the tendency is inherited, so questions about related signs and symptoms in the family and the patient's medical history are important to document and report, and a familial history would put the patient at risk.
Trap: (B) reads the narrowest of the chapter's three phrasings. General anesthetics list "known susceptibility," depolarizing NMBDs list a "previous history," and succinylcholine's own profile lists "a personal or familial history" — the widest of the three. A relative's episode is enough.

Subtopic: recognition and treatment

Q: A patient arrived on the orthopedic unit from the PACU 2 hours after a hip replacement with a temperature of 98.9 degrees F. It is now 104.8 degrees F. Another nurse comments that the patient must be developing an infection from the hip replacement. What is the nurse's priority action?
A) Treat this as possible malignant hyperthermia, notify the anesthesia provider and surgeon immediately, and anticipate dantrolene
B) Obtain wound and blood cultures and wait for the results before notifying anyone
C) Administer an antipyretic and recheck the temperature in one hour
D) Document the reading as an expected postoperative inflammatory response
Answer: A
Rationale: The chapter's exemplar temperature for malignant hyperthermia is higher than 104 degrees F, it describes the reaction as a rapid progression of hyperthermia that may be fatal if not promptly recognized and aggressively treated, and it extends monitoring past the surgical suite into regular postoperative care. Dantrolene is stocked by law at every institution that provides general anesthesia.
Trap: (B) is the reflex pick because a colleague has already named infection, and cultures feel like the diligent answer. A surgical-site infection does not raise a temperature nearly six degrees in 2 hours, and waiting on cultures spends the time the reaction is fatal without.

Q: In addition to a rapid rise in body temperature, which findings indicate the possible presence of malignant hyperthermia? Select all that apply.
A) Tachypnea
B) Tachycardia
C) Muscle rigidity
D) Respiratory depression
E) Seizure activity
F) Diaphoresis
Answer: A, B, C, F
Rationale: The assessment section gives the fullest sign list: rapid rise in body temperature, tachycardia, tachypnea, muscle rigidity, cyanosis, irregular heartbeat, mottling of the skin, diaphoresis or profuse sweating, and an unstable blood pressure. Tachycardia and tachypnea appear on all four versions of the list in this chapter.
Trap: (D) is the strongest distractor because respiratory depression is a genuine effect of general anesthetics and CNS depressants elsewhere in this chapter — but it is on none of the four malignant hyperthermia sign lists. (E) appears nowhere either, and neither do bradycardia, bradypnea, or hypothermia.

Q: Malignant hyperthermia is confirmed intraoperatively. Which statement about treatment is accurate?
A) Cardiorespiratory supportive care is given as needed to stabilize heart and lung function, along with the skeletal muscle relaxant dantrolene, which by law every institution providing general anesthesia must keep on hand
B) Flumazenil is given, because it reverses the CNS depression that drives the temperature rise
C) Naloxone is given, because the reaction is an opioid effect of the balanced anesthesia regimen
D) The anesthetic is simply discontinued, because general anesthetics have a specific reversal agent that ends the reaction
Answer: A
Rationale: Malignant hyperthermia is treated with cardiorespiratory supportive care as needed to stabilize heart and lung function, along with the skeletal muscle relaxant dantrolene. By law, all health care institutions that provide general anesthesia must keep a certain amount of dantrolene on hand.
Trap: (D) assumes an antidote for the anesthetic itself. This chapter names no reversal agent for general anesthetics; the reversibility it claims comes from rapid metabolism plus a controlled environment. Its reversal agents belong to opioids, to benzodiazepines, and to the nondepolarizing neuromuscular blockers, and dantrolene treats the reaction rather than the drug.

Q: A mechanically ventilated ICU patient receiving a nondepolarizing neuromuscular blocker is also receiving dantrolene. What should the nurse anticipate?
A) Deepened and prolonged neuromuscular blockade, because dantrolene sits on Box 11.6's additive-effects list
B) Reversal of the neuromuscular blockade, because dantrolene is the antidote used in malignant hyperthermia
C) No interaction, because dantrolene acts on skeletal muscle and the blocker acts at the neuromuscular junction
D) Shortened paralysis requiring escalating doses of the blocker
Answer: A
Rationale: Box 11.6 lists dantrolene among the drugs with additive effects on neuromuscular blocking drugs, alongside aminoglycosides, clindamycin, calcium channel blockers, inhalation anesthetics, local anesthetics, and magnesium.
Trap: (B) is the mirror error of the previous item and comes from the same drug's other role. Dantrolene has two opposite-feeling jobs on adjacent pages: for malignant hyperthermia it is the treatment, but for neuromuscular blockade it deepens and prolongs the paralysis. Note also that dantrolene's mechanism, dose, and route are all deferred to Chapter 12; this chapter gives only its name and class.

Topic: neuromuscular blocker mechanisms and reversal
Subtopic: depolarizing versus nondepolarizing

Q: Succinylcholine is given and the nurse observes brief twitching across the patient's hands and face before paralysis sets in. Which mechanism explains it?
A) Succinylcholine is a competitive agonist that binds in place of acetylcholine and depolarizes the membrane, so during the depolarizing phase the muscles fasciculate before the desensitizing phase produces paralysis
B) Succinylcholine is a competitive antagonist that blocks acetylcholine, and the twitching is the last stored acetylcholine being consumed
C) The twitching is a histamine release reaction rather than a receptor effect
D) The twitching is an early sign that reversal has begun
Answer: A
Rationale: Depolarizing NMBDs work much like the neurotransmitter acetylcholine and are competitive agonists that bind in place of it at the motor endplates. During phase I, the depolarizing phase, the muscles fasciculate or twitch; after continued depolarization the muscles are no longer responsive to the acetylcholine released, and the muscle becomes paralyzed in phase II, the desensitizing phase.
Trap: (B) swaps the one word the whole class distinction turns on. Depolarizing agents are competitive agonists that mimic acetylcholine; nondepolarizing agents are competitive antagonists that block it. Only the agonist mechanism produces fasciculations, which are most pronounced in the muscle groups of the hands, feet, and face.

Q: A patient receives rocuronium for intubation and no fasciculations occur. What accounts for this?
A) Rocuronium is a nondepolarizing blocker, a competitive antagonist of acetylcholine, so the nerve cell membrane is not depolarized and skeletal muscle contraction does not occur
B) Rocuronium is a depolarizing blocker given slowly enough to avoid the twitching phase
C) The fasciculations were suppressed by the anticholinesterase drug given at induction
D) Rocuronium mimics acetylcholine, but at a dose too low to depolarize the membrane
Answer: A
Rationale: Nondepolarizing NMBDs also bind to acetylcholine receptors at the neuromuscular junction, but instead of mimicking acetylcholine they block its actions. The nerve cell membrane is not depolarized, the muscle fibers are not stimulated, and skeletal muscle contraction does not occur. Cisatracurium, rocuronium, vecuronium, and pancuronium are all nondepolarizing.
Trap: (D) is the mirror error of the previous item, keeping the agonist language for the antagonist drug. Both classes bind the same receptor, which is what makes the pair confusable, and succinylcholine is the only depolarizing NMBD in the chapter.

Subtopic: reversal agents

Q: At the end of a procedure the anesthesia provider gives neostigmine to reverse vecuronium. How does that work?
A) It prevents the enzyme cholinesterase from breaking down acetylcholine, so acetylcholine builds up at the motor endplate and displaces the nondepolarizing blocker molecule
B) It binds the blocker molecule directly and carries it out of the circulation
C) It depolarizes the membrane so the muscle can contract through the block
D) It relaxes skeletal muscle, which restores the normal resting tone
Answer: A
Rationale: Anticholinesterase drugs such as neostigmine and pyridostigmine are antidotes for nondepolarizing NMBDs such as vecuronium, rocuronium, and cisatracurium. They work by preventing cholinesterase from breaking down acetylcholine, which causes acetylcholine to build up at the motor endplate, where it eventually displaces the nondepolarizing molecule and returns the nerve to its original state.
Trap: (B) describes sugammadex, a selective relaxant binding agent used for the reversal of rocuronium or vecuronium — a different mechanism for the same two drugs. Anticholinesterase reversal works only because raised acetylcholine can out-compete a blocker that is a competitor to begin with.

Q: A nurse asks why no anticholinesterase was given to reverse the succinylcholine at the end of a short case. Which response is correct?
A) Succinylcholine is not reversed with acetylcholinesterase inhibitors because of its short duration of action and natural breakdown; a single dose lasts only 5 to 9 minutes
B) It was reversed, but with pyridostigmine rather than neostigmine
C) Sugammadex is the reversal agent used for succinylcholine
D) Reversal is withheld only in patients with a personal history of malignant hyperthermia
Answer: A
Rationale: The chapter states the exception directly: succinylcholine is not reversed with acetylcholinesterase inhibitors because of its short duration of action and natural breakdown. Its duration after a single dose is only 5 to 9 minutes because of rapid breakdown by cholinesterase, the enzyme responsible for metabolizing it.
Trap: (C) is the mirror error of the previous item — sugammadex is the selective relaxant binding agent for rocuronium or vecuronium, both nondepolarizing. Giving an anticholinesterase for succinylcholine applies the right drug to the wrong mechanism, since succinylcholine is an agonist already producing the block rather than a competitor waiting to be displaced.

Q: A patient with end-stage renal disease requires neuromuscular blockade for a long procedure. Which drug does the chapter name as the drug of choice, and on what grounds?
A) cisatracurium, because it is broken down by Hoffman elimination, a process dependent on pH and temperature rather than on renal excretion
B) succinylcholine, because it is metabolized by plasma cholinesterase
C) rocuronium, because sugammadex is available to reverse it
D) pancuronium, because long-acting drugs need fewer redosings in renal failure
Answer: A
Rationale: Most drugs are biotransformed in the liver and eliminated by the kidneys. Cisatracurium is broken down by Hoffman elimination, a process dependent on pH and temperature, which makes it the drug of choice for patients with end-stage renal disease.
Trap: (B) sounds defensible because succinylcholine's plasma breakdown also bypasses the kidney, but the chapter attaches the end-stage renal disease recommendation to cisatracurium alone. Succinylcholine is also the drug whose fasciculations raise potassium, which is the last thing a renal patient needs.

Topic: neuromuscular blocker safety and adverse effects
Subtopic: the ventilation rule and what these drugs do not do

Q: A nurse notices that vecuronium and vancomycin are stored beside each other, and is asked to give the antibiotic to a patient who is breathing on his own. Which principle governs this situation?
A) Never give a neuromuscular blocker to someone who is not on mechanical ventilation; the nurse must ensure the patient is ventilated before giving one and double-check that a blocker is not inadvertently given
B) A neuromuscular blocker may be given to a spontaneously breathing patient as long as oxygen is applied by mask
C) The risk applies only to continuous infusions, not to single doses
D) An accidental dose would be tolerated, because these drugs cause sedation and the patient would simply sleep
Answer: A
Rationale: Neuromuscular blocking drugs paralyze the skeletal muscles required for breathing — the intercostal muscles and the diaphragm — leaving the patient unable to breathe independently, so mechanical ventilation is required to prevent brain damage or death from suffocation. The Clinical Pearl states it as a rule: never give a neuromuscular blocker to someone who is not on mechanical ventilation.
Trap: (D) is the belief that makes this error feel survivable, and it is precisely what the chapter denies. In such an error the patient would experience a horrendous death, because the mind is alert but the patient cannot speak or move. Vancomycin and vecuronium are the chapter's own named sound-alike pair, and these are high-alert drugs for that reason.

Q: The wife of a mechanically ventilated patient receiving a continuous neuromuscular blocker asks, "Is he awake? Can he hear me?" Which response is accurate?
A) Neuromuscular blockers used alone do not cause sedation or relieve pain or anxiety, so he must also receive medication to manage pain and anxiety
B) The paralytic keeps him unconscious, so he cannot hear you
C) The drug produces amnesia, so anything he hears will not be remembered
D) He is unconscious, because the drug blocks acetylcholine in the brain as well as at the muscle
Answer: A
Rationale: The chapter is explicit that NMBDs, when used alone, do not cause sedation or relieve pain or anxiety, and therefore the patient must also receive appropriate medications to manage pain and/or anxiety. In the ICU these drugs are used to prevent the patient from fighting against the ventilator.
Trap: (B) is the reflex reassurance and is exactly why this error is dangerous: paralysis is not unconsciousness. These drugs act at the neuromuscular junction rather than the cortex, so a paralyzed patient may be fully awake and aware.

Subtopic: order of paralysis and recovery

Q: A neuromuscular blocker is started during a surgical procedure. In what order will the muscles be affected?
A) The small, rapidly moving muscles of the fingers and eyes first, then the limbs, neck, and trunk, and finally the intercostal muscles and the diaphragm
B) The diaphragm and intercostal muscles first, then the trunk and limbs, and finally the fingers and eyes
C) The trunk and neck first, then the diaphragm, then the small muscles of the hands
D) All skeletal muscle groups simultaneously, since the block is systemic
Answer: A
Rationale: The typical time course begins with muscle weakness, followed by total flaccid paralysis. Small, rapidly moving muscles such as those of the fingers and eyes are generally the first to be paralyzed, next the limbs, neck, and trunk, and finally the intercostal muscles and the diaphragm, which causes respiratory arrest.
Trap: (B) inverts the sequence, and it pulls because respiratory arrest is the outcome students associate most strongly with these drugs, so the diaphragm feels like the headline. The diaphragm is last, which is why respiratory arrest marks a completed block rather than an early one.

Q: A ventilated patient recovering from neuromuscular blockade has a weak but present hand grip, and the team is weighing extubation. What should the nurse understand about recovery?
A) Recovery occurs in the reverse order of paralysis, so the diaphragm is ordinarily the first to regain function and the small muscles of the hands are among the last
B) Recovery follows the same order as paralysis, so hand grip returns first and the diaphragm last
C) Recovery of the diaphragm always lags behind every other muscle group, so the hand grip is the reassuring sign
D) Recovery is simultaneous across all muscle groups once the drug is stopped
Answer: A
Rationale: Recovery of muscular activity after discontinuation of anesthesia usually occurs in the reverse order of the paralysis; thus the diaphragm is ordinarily the first to regain function.
Trap: (B) is the mirror error of the previous item, applying the paralysis order to recovery. The muscle that fails last recovers first, which is precisely why extubation decisions are not made on hand grip alone.

Subtopic: adverse effects and conditions that alter the block

Q: A patient's serum potassium rises after succinylcholine. Which chain explains it, and which countermeasure does the chapter name?
A) Fasciculations injure muscle cells, releasing potassium into the circulation; small doses of a nondepolarizing blocker are sometimes given with succinylcholine to minimize the fasciculations
B) Succinylcholine directly blocks renal potassium excretion; a loop diuretic is added
C) Histamine release shifts potassium out of cells; an antihistamine is given first
D) The anticholinesterase reversal agent raises potassium; the reversal is simply omitted
Answer: A
Rationale: Paralysis from a depolarizing blocker is sometimes preceded by muscle spasms called fasciculations. Injury to muscle cells may cause postoperative muscle pain and release potassium into the circulation, resulting in hyperkalemia, which is the primary concern for the anesthesia provider; the nurse should make every effort to be aware of the patient's potassium status. Small doses of nondepolarizing NMBDs are sometimes administered with succinylcholine to minimize these fasciculations.
Trap: (B) invents a renal mechanism, which is plausible only if the muscle chain is not held. The route here is muscular, and the same muscle injury explains the postoperative muscle pain patients report after succinylcholine.

Q: Select all the conditions that increase a patient's sensitivity to neuromuscular blocking drugs and prolong their effects.
A) Hypocalcemia
B) Hypokalemia
C) Hypothermia
D) Myasthenia gravis
E) Hyperkalemia
F) Thermal burns
Answer: A, B, C, D
Rationale: Box 11.4 lists the conditions that predispose patients to toxic effects by increasing sensitivity to NMBDs and prolonging their effects: acidosis, hypocalcemia, hypokalemia, hypothermia, myasthenia gravis, neonatal status, and paraplegia.
Trap: (E) is the mirror-image entry. Hyperkalemia and hypercalcemia sit on Box 11.5, the list of conditions that oppose the effects and require higher doses without necessarily causing toxicity. (F) is on that same opposing list. The electrolyte pairs are exact mirrors, and so is the neurologic pair: paraplegia predisposes to toxicity while hemiplegia opposes the effect.

Topic: local anesthetic classes and injection techniques
Subtopic: ester versus amide

Q: A patient reports a severe rash and facial swelling after dental work done with procaine, and a local anesthetic is now needed to suture a laceration. Which choice and rationale are correct?
A) lidocaine, because it is an amide and patients allergic to an ester type may not be allergic to an amide type
B) tetracaine, because it is the most potent of the parenteral local anesthetics
C) benzocaine, because topical formulations do not provoke allergic reactions
D) chloroprocaine, because it is a different drug from the one that caused the reaction
Answer: A
Rationale: Amides all have the letter "i" in their name before the suffix "-caine," whereas esters do not. Knowing the classes matters because patients who are allergic to an ester type may not be allergic to an amide type, and often when an individual has an adverse reaction to one local anesthetic, using a drug from the alternate chemical class can avoid the problem.
Trap: (D) is the reflex pick because the name is unfamiliar enough to look like a fresh class, but chloroprocaine is an ester, in the same family as procaine. (B) picks on potency rather than chemistry, and tetracaine is also an ester.

Q: Select all the local anesthetics that belong to the amide group.
A) bupivacaine
B) lidocaine
C) mepivacaine
D) prilocaine
E) tetracaine
F) procaine
Answer: A, B, C, D
Rationale: Box 11.3 gives the amide type as bupivacaine, dibucaine, etidocaine, lidocaine, mepivacaine, and prilocaine, and the ester type as benzocaine, chloroprocaine, cocaine, procaine, proparacaine, propoxycaine, and tetracaine. The Clinical Pearl supplies the sorting rule: amides have the letter "i" before the suffix "-caine."
Trap: (E) is the reflex inclusion because tetracaine is a familiar injectable and sounds like the amides, but there is no "i" in the syllable before its "-caine." Say the name and look for the letter rather than trusting familiarity.

Q: A patient reports an anaphylactic reaction to "the caines" after a procedure in which lidocaine was used. What does the chapter suggest may be tried?
A) A local anesthetic of the ester type, because using a drug from the alternate chemical class can avoid the problem
B) A larger dose of lidocaine given together with an antihistamine
C) A different amide from another manufacturer, since the reaction was likely to a preservative
D) No local anesthetic at all, since a reaction to one implies a reaction to all
Answer: A
Rationale: The lidocaine drug profile addresses this directly: some patients report allergic or anaphylactic reactions to the "caines," and in those situations it may be wise to try a local anesthetic of the ester type. Lidocaine is an amide, so the alternate class is the ester family.
Trap: (C) is the mirror error of the earlier ester-to-amide item — it changes the product but not the chemical class, which is the only variable that matters here. The switch has to cross the ester-amide line in whichever direction the reaction came from.

Q: Why are true allergic reactions to local anesthetics generally limited to the ester type?
A) Esters are metabolized by cholinesterase in the plasma and liver and are converted into a para-aminobenzoic acid compound, and that compound is responsible for the allergic reactions
B) Esters are metabolized in the liver by the same enzymes as the amides, only more slowly, so they accumulate
C) Esters are more potent, and higher potency carries a higher allergy risk
D) Amides are not metabolized at all and are excreted unchanged, so they never form an antigen
Answer: A
Rationale: Different enzymes are responsible for the breakdown of the two groups. Esters are metabolized by cholinesterase in the plasma and liver and are converted into a para-aminobenzoic acid compound, which is responsible for the allergic reactions; the amide type is metabolized uneventfully to active and inactive metabolites in the liver by other enzymes.
Trap: (C) confuses potency with immunogenicity, which is a tempting substitution when the metabolic detail has not been held. Note that true allergic reactions to local anesthetics are rare overall, ranging from skin rash, urticaria, and edema to anaphylactic shock, and are generally limited to the ester class.

Subtopic: neuraxial techniques and epinephrine

Q: A laboring patient requests pain relief and a small catheter is threaded so that anesthetic can be delivered without puncturing the dura. Which technique is this, and how does it differ from an intrathecal injection?
A) Epidural — the anesthetic goes into the epidural space without puncturing the dura, whereas an intrathecal injection crosses the dura into the subarachnoid space
B) Intrathecal — the catheter delivers drug into the subarachnoid space, whereas an epidural injection is placed outside the vertebral column
C) Nerve block — the catheter sits adjacent to a major nerve trunk rather than near the cord
D) Infiltration — small amounts of anesthetic are placed in the tissue surrounding the operative site
Answer: A
Rationale: Box 11.2 defines the epidural technique as injection of anesthetic via a small catheter into the epidural space without puncturing the dura, commonly used to reduce maternal discomfort during labor and delivery and to manage postoperative acute pain. The intrathecal technique is injection into the subarachnoid space, commonly used for major abdominal or limb surgery when the risks of general anesthesia are too high.
Trap: (B) inverts the pair, and the dura is the entire dividing line. It is also why spinal headache follows intrathecal anesthesia or an inadvertent dural puncture during an epidural, in up to 70% of such patients.

Q: A physician requests lidocaine with epinephrine for a laceration repair, and the patient asks why two drugs are needed. Which explanation is correct?
A) Epinephrine is a vasoconstrictor added to maintain localized drug activity, keeping the anesthetic from being absorbed systemically, and it also reduces local blood loss during the procedure
B) Epinephrine makes the lidocaine more potent, so a smaller total dose can be used
C) Epinephrine speeds the return of sensation once the procedure is finished
D) Epinephrine is added to prevent an allergic reaction to the lidocaine
Answer: A
Rationale: Systemic reactions are possible if sufficiently large quantities of a local anesthetic are absorbed into the circulation, so a vasoconstrictor such as epinephrine is often co-administered to maintain localized drug activity. This property also serves to reduce local blood loss during minor surgical procedures.
Trap: (B) is the intuitive reading of any two-drug combination, but the stated purpose is containment rather than potentiation. Note also that local anesthetics given with epinephrine or with enflurane can lead to dysrhythmias, which is one of the few clinically significant interactions this class has.

Topic: local anesthesia and sedation nursing care
Subtopic: recovery from local and spinal anesthesia

Q: Two hours after an epidural, a postpartum patient can move both legs and asks to walk to the bathroom. Her blood pressure is 88/54. What should the nurse understand?
A) Recovery from local anesthesia occurs in reverse order — motor activity returns first, then sensory function, and autonomic function last — so autonomic blockade and hypotension can persist after movement has returned
B) The return of movement means the block has fully resolved, so the low blood pressure has an unrelated cause
C) Autonomic function returns first, so hypotension at this point is unexpected and suggests hemorrhage
D) Sensory function returns first, then motor function, then autonomic function
Answer: A
Rationale: Autonomic activity is affected first, then pain and other sensory functions are lost, and motor activity is the last to be lost. When the effects wear off, recovery occurs in reverse order: motor activity returns first, then sensory functions, and finally autonomic activity. Local anesthetics also produce sympathetic blockade, blocking the actions of norepinephrine and epinephrine.
Trap: (B) is the reflex conclusion because movement is the most visible sign of recovery. A patient who can move a limb has not necessarily regained sensation, and one reporting returning sensation still has autonomic blockade — which is why hypotension can persist after an epidural appears to be wearing off.

Q: A patient develops a headache the day after intrathecal anesthesia. Which management does the chapter support first?
A) Bed rest and conventional analgesics, with oral or intravenous caffeine sometimes added, because the headache is most often self-limiting
B) An immediate epidural blood patch, since it is effective in over 90% of cases
C) Broad-spectrum antibiotics, since a post-dural headache signals meningitis until proven otherwise
D) Strict fluid restriction to lower cerebrospinal fluid pressure
Answer: A
Rationale: Spinal headache occurs in up to 70% of patients who experience inadvertent dural puncture during epidural anesthesia or undergo intrathecal anesthesia. It is most often self-limiting and is treated with bed rest and conventional analgesic medications; oral or intravenous forms of the CNS stimulant caffeine are also sometimes used.
Trap: (B) states a true fact about the blood patch and attaches it to the wrong tier of care. Injecting roughly 15 mL of the patient's own venous blood into the epidural space is done by the anesthetist for severe cases, not for a new uncomplicated post-spinal headache.

Subtopic: airway, reversal agents, and scope of practice

Q: A patient who had a throat procedure under topical anesthesia says he feels fine and asks for a drink of water. What should the nurse do?
A) Check for the return of the gag reflex and withhold food and fluids until sensation and the gag reflex have returned
B) Provide clear liquids, since the patient is alert and requesting them
C) Provide thickened liquids only, since these are safe with an absent gag reflex
D) Withhold all intake for a full 24 hours regardless of the gag reflex
Answer: A
Rationale: After anesthesia the nurse should check for the gag reflex and expect to withhold food or drink until the patient's sensation and/or gag reflex has returned. Assessment of swallowing ability and gag reflexes is a named step before and after anesthesia.
Trap: (B) substitutes the patient's report for the assessment, which is the exact substitution this item is built on. The gag reflex gates oral intake, not the patient's sense of feeling fine — and topical throat anesthesia abolishes that reflex directly.

Q: A nurse is setting up for a procedure under moderate sedation. Which reversal agents does the chapter direct be kept available, and for what?
A) naloxone for opioids and flumazenil for benzodiazepines
B) naloxone for benzodiazepines and flumazenil for opioids
C) neostigmine for opioids and dantrolene for benzodiazepines
D) sugammadex, which reverses both drug classes used in moderate sedation
Answer: A
Rationale: The moderate sedation box directs that reversal agents be kept at the bedside: naloxone for opioids and flumazenil for benzodiazepines. Resuscitative equipment and medications, including opioid antidotes, must be readily available for any anesthesia.
Trap: (B) simply swaps the pair, which is the error the chapter invites by naming both in one sentence. Neither drug reverses a general anesthetic or a neuromuscular blocker; the anticholinesterases and sugammadex belong to the nondepolarizing blockers, and dantrolene belongs to malignant hyperthermia.

Q: A staff nurse on a medical unit asks which anesthetics fall within her scope of practice. Which statement is accurate?
A) Anesthetics are not typically given by the registered nurse unless the nurse is licensed and certified as an anesthesia provider; the stated exceptions are topical forms such as oral swish-and-swallow solutions and lidocaine patches
B) Any registered nurse may give moderate sedation drugs as long as a provider is somewhere on the unit
C) Registered nurses may administer propofol in every state as part of a moderate sedation protocol
D) Registered nurses may administer inhaled anesthetics whenever the patient is on continuous monitoring
Answer: A
Rationale: The assessment section states that anesthetics are not drugs that are typically given by the registered nurse unless the nurse is licensed and certified as an anesthesia provider, that is, a nurse anesthetist. The exceptions are orders for topical forms, such as oral swish-and-swallow solutions used during chemotherapy and lidocaine patches for pain relief. Both general anesthetics and the drugs for moderate sedation are given only under controlled conditions by anesthesia providers.
Trap: (C) overstates a point the chapter deliberately leaves contested. Some states, under the state's nurse practice act, allow nurses to administer propofol as part of a moderate sedation protocol, but many states prohibit administration by nurses, so the defensible answer is that it depends on the state nurse practice act rather than a flat yes.
