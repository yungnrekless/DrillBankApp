# Chapter 42 drills — Antifungal Drugs

Authored from `sources/Ch42_Fact_Bank.md` (Lilley, Collins & Snyder, 10th ed.,
printed pp. 662–670).

Blueprint weight is **7 questions of 121 (5.8%), depth `standard`** — 6 items on
Exam 1 and 1 on the Final, tied with Ch 12 for fifth heaviest overall and
front-loaded onto the first exam.

That earns **45 drills**, the top of the `standard` band (30 to 45). The chapter
sits high in its band for a structural reason rather than a weight reason: it
carries only six profiled drugs but four separate blocks that invert cleanly —
membrane versus wall, bind versus block, inhibitor versus inducer, prevention
versus response to an infusion reaction — and each of those needs both
directions drilled or a learner passes on a rule held backward.

Topics follow the chapter's own progression: what a mycosis is, which organism
causes which one, how the drug classes are sorted, what each class attacks, what
each drug treats, what each drug does to the patient, what the drugs do to each
other, and then the two blocks the chapter builds its case study and review
questions around — the amphotericin B infusion and the administration and
teaching rules.

Every invertible distinction is written from both ends: yeast and mold, oral and
vaginal candidiasis, imidazole and the azole umbrella, polyene binding and azole
blocking, enzyme inhibition and enzyme induction, prevention of an infusion
reaction and response to one in progress, normal saline and dextrose diluents.

Topic: fungal infections and mycosis types
Subtopic: yeasts versus molds

Q: A microbiology report on a wound describes the organism as a single-celled fungus that reproduces by budding, with a daughter cell pouching out of and breaking off from a mother cell. Which term names this organism?
A) A yeast
B) A mold
C) A dermatophyte
D) A mycelium
Answer: A
Rationale: Yeasts are single-celled fungi that reproduce by budding, a process in which "a daughter cell forms by pouching out of and breaking off from a mother cell." Fungi as a group are a very large, diverse group of eukaryotic microorganisms consisting of yeasts and molds.
Trap: (B) pulls because the two words are learned as a pair and both name fungi. Molds are multicellular, built of long branching filaments called hyphae that entwine into a mycelium — the structural opposite of a single cell that buds.

Q: A second report describes a fungus made of long, branching filaments that entwine to form a complex branched structure. What is that structure called, and what type of fungus is this?
A) A mycelium, and the fungus is a mold
B) A mycelium, and the fungus is a yeast
C) A hypha, and the fungus is a yeast
D) A sterol, and the fungus is a mold
Answer: A
Rationale: Molds are multicellular fungi characterized by long, branching filaments called hyphae, which entwine to form a complex branched structure known as a mycelium. The key points restate the yeast and mold contrast verbatim and add that yeasts may be harmful, causing infections, or helpful, aiding in baking or brewing beer.
Trap: (B) is the mirror error of the previous item, keeping the right structure but attaching it to the single-celled fungus. (C) swaps the individual filament for the entwined mass; both hypha and mycelium are defined inside the Molds key-term entry rather than as their own terms, which is why they get skimmed past.

Subtopic: mycosis, dermatophyte, and the four types

Q: A patient with athlete's foot asks the nurse what the word "mycosis" on his chart means. Which explanation is accurate?
A) It is the general term for any fungal infection
B) It is the name of the fungus that infects skin, nails, or hair
C) It refers only to fungal infections that have become systemic
D) It is the main sterol in fungal cell membranes
Answer: A
Rationale: Mycosis is "the general term for any fungal infection," and the body text states the same thing as "an infection caused by a fungus." Athlete's foot is the chapter's own example of the mild end of a range that runs from annoying symptoms to life-threatening systemic mycoses.
Trap: (B) names the dermatophyte, which is the organism rather than the infection, and the reflex is to fuse the two. The chapter states the chain outright: fungi that cause integumentary infections are dermatophytes, and the infections they cause are dermatomycoses. (D) is the definition of ergosterol.

Q: A nurse is reviewing the four general types of mycotic infection. Which type is NOT an infection of a layer of the integumentary system?
A) Systemic
B) Cutaneous
C) Subcutaneous
D) Superficial
Answer: A
Rationale: There are four general types of mycotic infection — systemic, cutaneous, subcutaneous, and superficial — and "the latter three are infections of various layers of the integumentary system (skin, hair, or nails)." The treatment split follows the same line: systemic mycoses and some cutaneous or subcutaneous mycoses get oral or parenteral drugs, while superficial infections are treated topically.
Trap: (D) pulls because "superficial" sounds like the least skin-specific of the four names, so students reach for it as the odd one out. It is squarely one of the three integumentary types; systemic is the single name that is not.

Subtopic: who develops severe fungal infections

Q: A nurse is identifying patients at increased risk for a severe systemic mycosis. Select all that apply.
A) A patient taking immunosuppressive drugs after a kidney transplant
B) A healthy adult with athlete's foot
C) A patient who is immunocompromised as a result of cancer chemotherapy
D) A patient with acquired immunodeficiency syndrome
E) A patient receiving a prolonged course of antibiotics
Answer: A, C, D, E
Rationale: The most severe systemic fungal infections generally affect people whose host immune defenses are compromised — organ transplant recipients taking immunosuppressive drug therapy, cancer patients immunocompromised by chemotherapy, and patients with AIDS. The chapter adds that antibiotics, antineoplastics, or immunosuppressants such as corticosteroids may result in colonization of Candida albicans followed by systemic infection.
Trap: (B) is the chapter's example of the mild end of the range, not a risk factor. Athlete's foot in an immunocompetent adult is exactly the "annoying symptoms" case the chapter contrasts with life-threatening systemic mycoses, and its presence in an option list makes the list look like it is about fungal infection generally rather than about compromised host defenses.

Topic: candidiasis and other mycotic infections
Subtopic: oral versus vaginal candidiasis

Q: A newborn in the nursery has whitish patches on the oral mucosa, and the nurse recognizes oral candidiasis. Which population does the chapter identify as commonly affected by this form?
A) Newborns and immunocompromised patients
B) Pregnant women and women with diabetes mellitus
C) Patients with a history of inhaled soil exposure
D) Adults living in the southwestern United States
Answer: A
Rationale: "When the infection affects the mouth, it is referred to as oral candidiasis, or thrush, and is common in newborns and immunocompromised patients." The key points call the same thing an opportunistic fungal infection caused by C. albicans.
Trap: (B) is the vaginal candidiasis risk list moved onto the oral form. The two lists overlap only in antibiotic use, so offering diabetes or pregnancy as the classic risk for thrush in a newborn has crossed them.

Q: A woman taking an oral contraceptive during a course of antibiotics develops a vaginal yeast infection. Which risk factors does the chapter list for vaginal candidiasis?
A) Newborn status and immunocompromise
B) Pregnancy, diabetes mellitus, antibiotics, and oral contraceptives
C) Inhalation of airborne fungal spores
D) Organ transplantation and chemotherapy only
Answer: B
Rationale: "Vaginal candidiasis, commonly called a yeast infection, often affects pregnant women, women with diabetes mellitus, women taking antibiotics, and women taking oral contraceptives."
Trap: (A) is the mirror error of the previous item, handing the thrush list to the vaginal form. Antibiotic use is the only item on both lists, which is precisely why antibiotic use alone cannot discriminate between the two.

Subtopic: reservoir, transmission, and tissue affected

Q: A blood culture grows Candida glabrata, and the chart documents involvement of the blood and lungs. Candidiasis is the one mycosis appearing in both halves of Table 42.1. What separates the systemic row from the superficial row?
A) The reservoir, since the systemic form is soil-borne and the superficial form is human
B) The transmission route, since only the systemic form is acquired by inhalation
C) The tissue affected, since the systemic row lists blood and lungs while the superficial row lists mucous membrane and skin
D) The mode of acquisition, since only the superficial form results from antibiotic overgrowth
Answer: C
Rationale: Table 42.1 lists candidiasis twice, and the axis separating the rows is where the organism lands. The systemic row gives blood and lungs and widens the organism list to five Candida species; the superficial row gives mucous membrane and skin for C. albicans alone, with its own hedge that the infection may be disseminated.
Trap: (A) and (B) both fail on the same fact: the two rows carry an identical reservoir, humans, and an identical transmission clause, direct contact plus overgrowth in response to treatment with an antibiotic to which the organism is nonsusceptible. Neither column can tell the rows apart, which is why reaching for reservoir or transmission feels productive and is not.

Q: A patient who recently moved away from the southwestern United States is diagnosed with a soil-borne mycosis acquired by inhalation. Which mycosis carries that endemic location in Table 42.1?
A) Blastomycosis
B) Coccidioidomycosis
C) Histoplasmosis
D) Aspergillosis
Answer: B
Rationale: Coccidioidomycosis, caused by Coccidioides immitis, is listed with an endemic location of the southwestern United States, a reservoir of soil and dust, transmission by inhalation, and the lungs as primary tissue affected.
Trap: (A) is the near-miss, and it is the only other row in the table naming a place: blastomycosis is endemic to North America, not the southwestern United States. Every other row simply says universal, so any geographic cue in a stem points at one of these two, and location is the only usable discriminator between them.

Q: A patient with a systemic mycosis develops signs of meningeal involvement. All six systemic mycoses in Table 42.1 affect the lungs. Which one also lists the meninges of the brain?
A) Cryptococcosis
B) Candidiasis
C) Histoplasmosis
D) Blastomycosis
Answer: A
Rationale: Cryptococcosis, caused by Cryptococcus neoformans with a reservoir of soil and bird and chicken droppings, is the only row listing "lungs, meninges of brain." That is the anatomic basis for cryptococcal meningitis, which fluconazole treats.
Trap: (B) pulls because candidiasis is the other systemic row with a second tissue listed, so it feels like the answer to a question about a second site. Its second tissue is blood, not brain.

Topic: antifungal drug classes
Subtopic: imidazoles, triazoles, and the azole umbrella

Q: A nursing student lists fluconazole as an imidazole antifungal. Which correction should the instructor make?
A) Fluconazole is a triazole; ketoconazole is the imidazole the chapter lists
B) Fluconazole is an echinocandin and not an azole at all
C) Fluconazole is not classified according to chemical structure
D) Both imidazole and triazole are wrong, since fluconazole is a polyene
Answer: A
Rationale: The chapter's four chemical classes are polyenes (amphotericin B and nystatin), imidazoles (ketoconazole), triazoles (fluconazole, itraconazole, voriconazole, posaconazole, and isavuconazonium), and echinocandins (caspofungin, micafungin, and anidulafungin).
Trap: (D) is chosen by students who overcorrect once told that "imidazole" is wrong and assume the whole azole label must be wrong too. The error is one level down: fluconazole is an azole, just the triazole kind.

Q: A drug reference files a warfarin interaction under the heading "Azole Antifungals." Which drugs does that heading cover?
A) Only the triazoles — fluconazole, itraconazole, voriconazole, posaconazole, and isavuconazonium
B) Only ketoconazole, the chapter's single imidazole
C) The imidazoles and the triazoles together
D) The azoles and the echinocandins together
Answer: C
Rationale: "The imidazoles and triazoles are often referred to by the more general term azole antifungals." Table 42.3 files its whole interaction block under that heading precisely so it covers all six of those drugs at once.
Trap: (A) is the mirror error of the previous item: having learned that ketoconazole is the odd one out of the triazole list, students push it outside the umbrella term as well. Being the only imidazole does not make ketoconazole a non-azole.

Subtopic: the unclassified drugs and the small classes

Q: A patient is started on flucytosine, and a student asks which chemical class it belongs to. Which response is accurate?
A) It is a polyene, like amphotericin B and nystatin
B) It is an echinocandin
C) It is one of two antifungals not specifically classified according to chemical structure
D) It is an allylamine, like terbinafine
Answer: C
Rationale: "Two antifungal drugs, flucytosine and griseofulvin, are not specifically classified according to their chemical structures." They are the chapter's two oldest drugs, largely replaced by the newer antifungal classes, though both remain available in the US market.
Trap: (A) pulls because flucytosine is given together with amphotericin B for Candida and cryptococcal infections, and students file drugs used together into the same class. Assigning flucytosine to the polyenes invents a class the chapter explicitly denies it.

Q: A nurse compares caspofungin and terbinafine. Which statement correctly describes each drug's position within its class?
A) Caspofungin was the first echinocandin, and terbinafine is currently the only allylamine
B) Caspofungin is the only echinocandin, and terbinafine was the first allylamine
C) Both are currently the only drugs in their respective classes
D) Both were the first drugs in their respective classes
Answer: A
Rationale: "Caspofungin (Cancidas) was the first echinocandin antifungal drug," with micafungin and anidulafungin following it. Terbinafine "is classified as an allylamine antifungal drug and is currently the only drug in its class."
Trap: (B) is the direct inversion, and it is easy to hold backward because terbinafine's class label is absent from the classification paragraph that names the four chemical classes — the allylamine label arrives four pages later, in the drug profile and the dosage table.

Q: A patient asks about ibrexafungerp, which she saw advertised for a vaginal yeast infection. Which statement about it is accurate?
A) It is an echinocandin given intravenously
B) It is a triterpenoid taken orally for vulvovaginal candidiasis
C) It is a polyene applied topically
D) It is a triazole used for invasive aspergillosis
Answer: B
Rationale: "Ibrexafungerp is a new drug in the class of triterpenoids and is used orally for vulvovaginal candidiasis." Its single mention sits at the end of the fluconazole profile.
Trap: (A) is chosen on the drug name alone. The suffix that marks the echinocandins is "-fungin" — caspofungin, micafungin, anidulafungin — and ibrexafungerp is a deliberate near-miss on that stem. It is neither an echinocandin nor an injectable.

Topic: antifungal mechanisms of action
Subtopic: cell membrane versus cell wall

Q: A nurse explains why caspofungin does not damage human cells. Which fungal structure does the echinocandin class attack?
A) Ergosterol in the fungal cell membrane
B) Glucans in the fungal cell wall
C) Fungal DNA synthesis
D) Microtubules within the fungal cell
Answer: B
Rationale: The echinocandins act by "preventing the synthesis of glucans, essential components of fungal cell walls that are not present in human cells." That absence from human cells is the chapter's stated basis for selective toxicity.
Trap: (A) is the reflex pick because four of the six profiled drugs work on the membrane through ergosterol, which makes the cell-wall answer look like the outlier when it is the correct one. Caspofungin is this chapter's single cell-wall drug.

Q: A nurse reviews amphotericin B's mechanism before the first dose. Which statement describes how the polyenes kill fungal cells?
A) They bind sterols in the fungal cell membrane, forming a channel that lets potassium and magnesium leak out
B) They inhibit fungal cytochrome P-450 enzymes so that ergosterol is never produced
C) They prevent glucan synthesis in the fungal cell wall
D) They are deaminated to 5-fluorouracil, which blocks fungal DNA synthesis
Answer: A
Rationale: The polyenes, amphotericin B and nystatin, bind to sterols in the cell membranes of fungi; once the drug binds ergosterol, a channel forms that allows potassium and magnesium ions to leak out, altering fungal cellular metabolism and killing the cell.
Trap: (B) is the azole mechanism and is the mirror error of this pair. The chapter flags the resemblance itself, noting that inhibiting ergosterol production produces "a defect similar to that caused by the polyene antifungals." The polyene binds the finished sterol and punches a channel; the azole shuts down production so the sterol is never made.

Q: A patient on an azole antifungal takes several other drugs metabolized by the liver. Which feature of the azole mechanism explains why this class carries such a large human interaction list?
A) The azoles bind ergosterol directly, and humans produce ergosterol as well
B) The azoles work by inhibiting cytochrome P-450 enzymes, and human drug metabolism runs through the same enzyme system
C) The azoles block glucan synthesis, and glucans are shared with human cells
D) The azoles are converted to 5-fluorouracil in the liver
Answer: B
Rationale: The imidazoles and triazoles "work by inhibiting fungal cell cytochrome P-450 enzymes that are needed to produce ergosterol." Only the azole mechanism runs through cytochrome P-450, which is why the interaction section names that enzyme system as "a common underlying source of the problem."
Trap: (C) inverts the chapter's own selective-toxicity statement. Glucans are specifically "not present in human cells," which is exactly why the echinocandins do not carry this interaction burden.

Subtopic: the two unclassified mechanisms

Q: Two older antifungals sit outside the chemical classes and are easily swapped. Which pairing of drug to mechanism is correct?
A) Flucytosine binds microtubules and blocks mitosis; griseofulvin becomes 5-FU and blocks DNA synthesis
B) Flucytosine is deaminated to 5-fluorouracil and interferes with fungal DNA synthesis; griseofulvin binds microtubules and inhibits fungal mitosis
C) Both act as antimetabolites against fungal DNA
D) Both inhibit ergosterol production through fungal cytochrome P-450
Answer: B
Rationale: Flucytosine is an antimetabolite; inside a susceptible fungal cell the enzyme cytosine deaminase converts it to 5-FU, which interferes with fungal DNA synthesis, and human cells are unharmed because they lack that enzyme. Griseofulvin enters the fungal cell through an energy-dependent transport system and inhibits fungal mitosis by binding microtubules.
Trap: (A) is the direct swap, and it is tempting because the two drugs share every surface feature: both old, both outside the chemical classes, both largely replaced by newer drugs. One extra discriminator is that flucytosine is the drug the chapter says acts "in much the same way as the antiviral drugs"; griseofulvin is compared to nothing.

Q: A student states flatly that the azole antifungals are fungicidal drugs. Which correction is accurate?
A) They are strictly fungistatic
B) They act as either fungistatic or fungicidal depending on their concentration in the fungus
C) Only the polyenes can be either, depending on the dose
D) The distinction applies to antibacterials only and is not used for antifungals
Answer: B
Rationale: The imidazoles and triazoles "act as either fungistatic or fungicidal drugs, depending on their concentration in the fungus," and they are most effective in combating rapidly growing fungi.
Trap: (A) pulls because students carry over the fixed, per-class bactericidal and bacteriostatic labeling from the antibiotic chapters. Here the property belongs to the concentration rather than to the class, so any option that flatly labels the azoles one or the other is more certain than the chapter is.

Topic: antifungal indications by drug
Subtopic: matching drug to infection

Q: A patient's medication history includes oral terbinafine. The nurse concludes the patient is most likely being treated for which infection?
A) Vaginal candidiasis
B) Cryptococcal meningitis
C) Invasive aspergillosis
D) Onychomycosis of the fingernails or toenails
Answer: D
Rationale: A tablet form of terbinafine is available for systemic use and "is used primarily to treat onychomycoses of the fingernails or toenails." Its topical cream, gel, and spray treat tinea pedis, tinea cruris, and tinea corporis.
Trap: (A) and (B) are both fluconazole's indications and (C) is voriconazole's, which makes this a four-way drug-to-indication match rather than a recall item. The distractors are the flagship indications of the other profiled systemic drugs, so any of them looks correct in isolation.

Q: A patient is to receive fluconazole. Which infections does the chapter list among its indications? Select all that apply.
A) Esophageal and oropharyngeal candidiasis
B) Urinary tract candidal infection
C) Invasive aspergillosis
D) Cryptococcal meningitis
E) Blastomycosis
Answer: A, B, D, E
Rationale: Fluconazole is used for "esophageal, oropharyngeal, peritoneal, urinary tract, vaginal, and systemic candidal infections, blastomycosis, and cryptococcal meningitis," and it may be used prophylactically. It owns the longest indication list in the chapter and is the only drug whose list includes the urinary tract.
Trap: (C) is voriconazole's flagship indication and caspofungin's salvage one. Fluconazole and voriconazole are two letters apart, share a class and a suffix, and the indication and the pregnancy status are the only things that reliably separate them.

Q: A patient with severe systemic candidiasis is to receive a polyene antifungal intravenously. Which order should the nurse question?
A) Amphotericin B intravenously
B) Nystatin intravenously
C) Amphotericin B by bladder irrigation for candidal cystitis
D) Nystatin oral suspension for thrush
Answer: B
Rationale: Nystatin "is not available in a parenteral form" but does come in several oral and topical formulations. Its activity is similar to amphotericin B's, but its usefulness is limited by toxic effects at the doses that would be required to match it, so nystatin is confined to the mouth and the skin while amphotericin B keeps the systemic job.
Trap: (C) looks like an error because a non-intravenous route for amphotericin B sounds wrong on its face, but bladder irrigation for candidal cystitis is a real use, as are intrapleural and intraperitoneal administration for infections in those cavities. The discriminator is whether the infection is local to a cavity, not whether the route is unusual.

Q: Amphotericin B is ordered together with flucytosine for a patient with a cryptococcal infection. What is the rationale for the combination?
A) Flucytosine reduces amphotericin B's infusion reactions
B) The two drugs are synergistic against Candida and cryptococcal infections
C) Flucytosine protects the kidneys from amphotericin B
D) The combination shortens amphotericin B's long half-life
Answer: B
Rationale: Amphotericin B "is sometimes given with flucytosine in the treatment of Candida and cryptococcal infections because of the synergy of the two drugs." From flucytosine's side the indication is narrower: endocarditis and cryptococcal meningoencephalitis, in conjunction with amphotericin B.
Trap: (C) borrows the drug's best-known toxicity and invents a protective role for the partner drug. Nothing in the chapter gives flucytosine a renal-protective effect, and the chapter's actual renal instruction runs the other way — avoid concurrent administration of nephrotoxic drugs if at all possible.

Q: A severely immunocompromised patient with no current fungal infection is started on posaconazole. What is the indication?
A) First-line treatment of invasive aspergillosis
B) Salvage therapy after other antifungals have failed
C) Prophylaxis of invasive aspergillus and candidal infections
D) Treatment of invasive mucormycosis
Answer: C
Rationale: Posaconazole is indicated for "the prophylaxis of invasive aspergillus and candidal infections in severely immunocompromised patients." Among the drugs covering aspergillosis, the discriminator is treatment tier: voriconazole treats invasive aspergillosis, caspofungin is reserved for patients intolerant of or refractory to other drugs, and isavuconazonium covers invasive aspergillosis and mucormycosis.
Trap: (B) is caspofungin's slot, and it pulls because both drugs read as second-line agents. Posaconazole never appears as rescue treatment in this chapter — it prevents an infection rather than treating an established one, and the stem's patient has no infection yet.

Topic: antifungal contraindications and adverse effects
Subtopic: which condition rules a drug out

Q: A nurse reviews a patient's history before starting antifungal therapy. Which preexisting condition is of most concern for this drug class as a whole?
A) Endocrine disease
B) Hepatic disease
C) Cardiac disease
D) Pulmonary disease
Answer: B
Rationale: The adverse effects section opens with the governing sentence, "Drug interactions and hepatotoxicity are the primary concerns in patients receiving antifungal drugs." Three findings reinforce it: caspofungin doses are adjusted for hepatic dysfunction, fluconazole and terbinafine carry liver cautions in Table 42.2, and terbinafine is outright contraindicated in chronic or active liver disease.
Trap: (C) is the seductive distractor because two drugs in the chapter carry cardiac language — itraconazole's onychomycosis restriction and voriconazole's dysrhythmia risk with quinidine. Those are drug-specific restrictions, not the class default, and the class default is the liver.

Q: A nurse reviews the drug-specific contraindications the chapter names. Which pairing is correct?
A) Terbinafine and porphyria
B) Griseofulvin and chronic or active liver disease
C) Itraconazole and severe cardiac problems when the drug is used for onychomycosis
D) Voriconazole and severe bone marrow suppression
Answer: C
Rationale: "Itraconazole should not be used to treat onychomycoses in patients with severe cardiac problems." The other three drug-specific contraindications are chronic or active liver disease for terbinafine, porphyria for griseofulvin, and fetal harm for voriconazole in pregnancy. Bone marrow suppression belongs to amphotericin B.
Trap: (A) and (B) are the same two contraindications with the drugs exchanged, which is the cheapest distractor this material offers: each contraindication appears exactly once, in one clause, so a rotated pairing reads as familiar as the correct one. Note also that the cardiac restriction is indication-limited — it applies to itraconazole used for onychomycosis, not to itraconazole generally.

Q: A pregnant patient needs treatment for a fungal infection. Which antifungal does the chapter identify as contraindicated in pregnancy?
A) Fluconazole
B) Voriconazole
C) Caspofungin
D) Nystatin
Answer: B
Rationale: Voriconazole "can cause fetal harm in pregnant women," and the drug profile escalates that to "It is also the only antifungal drug contraindicated in pregnancy." Women of childbearing age taking it are taught to use effective contraception because of drug-related teratogenic effects.
Trap: (A) pulls because fluconazole also carries a contraception instruction in the teaching box, so students remember a pregnancy-adjacent warning attached to it. Fluconazole's bullet is about needing an alternative method of contraception, an interaction problem; voriconazole's is a fetal-harm problem, and the chapter says "only."

Subtopic: which adverse effect belongs to which drug

Q: A patient is receiving caspofungin. Which adverse effects does Table 42.2 attribute to this drug? Select all that apply.
A) Hypotension
B) Hepatotoxicity
C) Decreased hemoglobin and hematocrit, leukopenia, and anemia
D) Facial edema
E) Pulmonary infiltrates
F) Tinnitus
Answer: A, B, C, D
Rationale: Table 42.2 gives caspofungin hypotension, peripheral edema, and tachycardia under cardiovascular; hepatotoxicity under gastrointestinal; decreased hemoglobin and hematocrit, leukopenia, and anemia under hematologic; and rash, facial edema, and itching under integumentary. Note the table's own filing quirk — caspofungin's hepatotoxicity sits under a gastrointestinal heading.
Trap: (E) and (F) belong to amphotericin B and appear nowhere else in Table 42.2; caspofungin has no pulmonary row at all. This is a borrow-from-the-neighboring-block distractor, and the amphotericin B block sits directly above caspofungin's on the same printed page, which is what makes the borrowed items feel like they were just read.

Q: A patient taking voriconazole reports seeing things that are not there and says bright light hurts his eyes. Which drug's adverse effect profile matches these findings?
A) Voriconazole, which causes hallucinations and photophobia
B) Amphotericin B, which causes convulsions and visual disturbances
C) Terbinafine, which causes alopecia and fatigue
D) Nystatin, which causes local oral irritation
Answer: A
Rationale: Table 42.2 lists hallucinations for voriconazole under central nervous effects and photophobia under other effects. The teaching box adds that the patient should avoid driving at night because of drug-related vision changes, and visual acuity is monitored during therapy, especially beyond 28 days.
Trap: (B) pulls because both drugs have a central nervous row and both rows contain a vision problem — which is exactly the overlap. The discriminator is which vision problem: voriconazole's is light sensitivity, while amphotericin B's is blurring or visual disturbance, alongside tinnitus, paresthesias, and convulsions.

Topic: antifungal drug interactions and cytochrome p-450
Subtopic: enzyme competition as the common cause

Q: A patient taking several medications is started on an azole antifungal. Which patient factor accounts for most antifungal drug interactions?
A) The patient's cardiac history
B) A history of gallbladder surgery
C) The patient's ethnic background
D) The patient's cytochrome P-450 enzyme system
Answer: D
Rationale: "A common underlying source of the problem is that many of the antifungal drugs, as well as other drugs, are metabolized by the cytochrome P-450 enzyme system." Two such drugs "compete for the limited amount of enzymes, and one of the drugs ends up accumulating."
Trap: (A) pulls because the most alarming consequence named in Table 42.3 is cardiac — quinidine plus an azole prolonging the QT interval on the electrocardiogram. That is a result of an interaction, not its cause. The cause is enzyme competition, and the answer is a metabolic system rather than an organ history or a demographic.

Subtopic: inhibition versus induction

Q: A patient stabilized on warfarin is started on fluconazole. What should the nurse anticipate?
A) Increased effects of the anticoagulant
B) Decreased effects of the anticoagulant
C) Decreased fluconazole levels
D) No interaction, since anticoagulants are not metabolized hepatically
Answer: A
Rationale: Table 42.3 lists oral anticoagulants under the azole antifungals with the possible effect "increased effects of anticoagulants." The azoles behave as enzyme inhibitors: metabolism of the target drug slows and its level rises. The same direction governs the table's cyclosporine, sirolimus, tacrolimus, calcium channel blocker, benzodiazepine, oral hypoglycemic, and statin rows.
Trap: (C) is the right effect attached to the wrong row. Table 42.3's final azole row runs in the opposite direction from the four above it, and conflating the two directions is the single most common error this table produces.

Q: A patient taking rifampin and phenytoin is started on an azole antifungal. What effect should the nurse anticipate?
A) The rifampin and phenytoin will accumulate to toxic levels
B) Azole antifungal levels will fall, so the antifungal may fail to work
C) Both the azole and the anticonvulsant levels will rise
D) The azole will prolong the QT interval on the electrocardiogram
Answer: B
Rationale: Table 42.3's final azole row lists phenytoin, rifampin, phenobarbital, and carbamazepine with the effect "decreased levels of azole antifungals." Those four are enzyme inducers, which speed metabolism and lower the level of the affected drug — here the antifungal itself.
Trap: (A) is the mirror error of the previous item, applying the inhibitor direction to inducers. The four named drugs do not become toxic; they make the antifungal stop working, which is a treatment failure rather than a toxicity, and that is a much quieter problem to detect.

Q: A patient receiving amphotericin B also takes digoxin. Which mechanism explains the interaction?
A) Amphotericin B competes with digoxin for cytochrome P-450 enzymes
B) Hypokalemia induced by amphotericin B increases the potential for digitalis toxicity
C) Amphotericin B inhibits gastric P-glycoprotein and raises digoxin absorption
D) Amphotericin B induces digoxin metabolism and lowers its level
Answer: B
Rationale: Table 42.3 pairs digitalis glycosides with amphotericin B and gives the effect as amphotericin B-induced hypokalemia increasing the potential for digitalis toxicity. Potassium loss is confirmed from two directions — the polyene mechanism lets potassium and magnesium leak from the fungal cell, and Table 42.2 lists potassium loss and hypomagnesemia as renal adverse effects.
Trap: (A) borrows the azole mechanism. None of amphotericin B's three interaction rows is a P-450 interaction; all three run through potassium or the kidney. (C) misattributes P-glycoprotein inhibition, which the chapter assigns only to itraconazole and posaconazole, and which is a transporter rather than an enzyme.

Topic: amphotericin b infusion management
Subtopic: preventing the infusion reaction

Q: A patient is to begin an intravenous amphotericin B infusion. Which measure reduces the likelihood of an infusion-related reaction?
A) Infuse the dose rapidly to shorten the patient's exposure
B) Infuse the dose over a longer-than-average time of 2 to 6 hours
C) Stop the infusion for 2 hours partway through and then resume
D) Force fluids throughout the infusion
Answer: B
Rationale: The likelihood of infusion-related reactions "can be reduced by using longer-than-average drug infusion times (i.e., 2 to 6 hours)." Premedications — antiemetics, antihistamines, antipyretics, and corticosteroids — are often given for the same purpose.
Trap: (A) is the reflex inversion, chosen on the intuition that briefer exposure means a smaller reaction. When an intravenous antiinfective in this book produces infusion reactions, the answer is to slow it down, which is the same logic used for vancomycin and red man syndrome.

Q: Before the first dose, the prescriber orders 1 mg of amphotericin B to be given over 20 to 30 minutes. What is the purpose of this order?
A) To premedicate the patient against fever and chills
B) To determine whether the patient will tolerate the drug
C) To saturate protein binding sites before the full dose
D) To confirm patency of the intravenous line
Answer: B
Rationale: "Often a 1-mg test dose is given over 20 to 30 minutes to see if the patient will tolerate the drug."
Trap: (A) fuses the test dose with premedication, which is a different intervention doing a different job. Three separate measures address the same problem and are not substitutes for one another: the test dose tells you whether the patient tolerates the drug, premedication blunts the reaction, and a 2- to 6-hour infusion reduces its likelihood.

Q: A patient tolerates conventional amphotericin B poorly overnight, and the next morning the prescriber changes the order to liposomal amphotericin B. Which statement explains why the liposomal form was not used first?
A) Liposomal amphotericin B is less effective than the conventional form
B) The lipid preparations cost much more, so they are reserved for patients intolerant of or refractory to the nonlipid drug
C) Liposomal amphotericin B cannot be given intravenously
D) The lipid preparations carry a higher incidence of adverse effects
Answer: B
Rationale: The three lipid preparations — lipid complex, cholesteryl complex, and liposomal — were developed to decrease adverse effects and increase efficacy, but "these lipid dosage forms have a much higher cost... and for this reason are often used only when patients are intolerant of or have an infection refractory to nonlipid amphotericin B."
Trap: (D) inverts the reason the lipid forms exist at all — fewer adverse effects is precisely their advantage. The gate on them is cost, not safety, and reaching for a lipid form first skips a gate the chapter states twice.

Subtopic: responding to and monitoring for toxicity

Q: Thirty minutes into an amphotericin B infusion, a patient's vital signs decline and he develops difficulty breathing along with worsening chills and nausea. What is the nurse's priority action?
A) Slow the infusion rate and continue
B) Discontinue the infusion while continuing to monitor the patient closely, then contact the prescriber immediately
C) Give the ordered premedications now and continue the infusion
D) Complete the infusion and document the reaction
Answer: B
Rationale: "If a severe reaction occurs (e.g., exacerbation of adverse effects, a decline in vital signs, difficulty breathing), discontinue the infusion while continuing to closely monitor the patient. Contact the prescriber immediately." Vital signs are monitored every 15 minutes during the infusion for exactly this reason.
Trap: (A) applies the prevention rule after the fact. A longer infusion time prevents reactions; an established severe reaction gets the infusion stopped. The two rules live in different sections of the chapter and merge easily, and Chapter 39's vancomycin protocol, which slows rather than stops, reinforces the wrong instinct here.

Q: A patient is receiving intravenous amphotericin B. Which findings should the nurse report? Select all that apply.
A) Urinary output less than 240 mL in 8 hours
B) A weight gain of 2 lb in a 24-hour period
C) A serum potassium level at the upper limit of normal
D) A weight gain of 5 lb in 1 week
E) Urinary output less than 0.5 mL/kg/hr
Answer: A, B, D, E
Rationale: The implementation section directs the nurse to "monitor intake and output and report decreasing urinary output of less than 240 mL/8 hr or less than 0.5 mL/kg/hr output," and states that "a gain of 2 lb or more in a 24-hour period or 5 lb or more in 1 week may indicate possible medication-induced renal damage and the need for prompt medical attention."
Trap: (C) runs the wrong direction. Amphotericin B causes potassium loss and hypomagnesemia, and it is the resulting hypokalemia that raises digitalis toxicity risk, so a high-normal potassium is not this drug's problem. Note also that the two reportable thresholds measure opposite things — urine out as a rate, weight as a gain — so swapping the 2 and the 5, or the 24 hours and the 1 week, produces numbers that sound entirely plausible.

Topic: antifungal administration and patient teaching
Subtopic: oral nystatin forms

Q: A patient with oral candidiasis is prescribed nystatin lozenges. Which instruction is correct?
A) Chew the lozenge thoroughly before swallowing it
B) Swallow the lozenge whole with a full glass of water
C) Allow the lozenge to dissolve slowly and completely in the mouth
D) Dissolve the lozenge in warm water and drink the solution
Answer: C
Rationale: Nystatin may be given orally as lozenges or troches: "These dosage forms must be slowly and completely dissolved in the mouth for optimal effects. They are not to be chewed or swallowed whole." Both oral nystatin forms work by local contact with the oral mucosa. Lozenges are generally not used in children younger than 5 years of age.
Trap: (A) and (B) are the two actions the chapter explicitly forbids, and they pull because nearly every other oral medication the patient has taken is either chewed or swallowed. Swallowing nystatin immediately does not poison the patient; it means the drug never treated the mouth at all.

Q: A patient hands back the dosage cup after swallowing his nystatin oral suspension in one gulp. Which instruction did he miss?
A) Shake the suspension, place half in each cheek, and swish it as long as possible before swallowing
B) Dilute the suspension in juice before taking it
C) Follow each dose with a commercial mouthwash
D) Take the suspension with food to reduce oral irritation
Answer: A
Rationale: The suspension "needs to be shaken thoroughly before measuring out each dose," is to be "placed half in each cheek and then swallow," and the patient is instructed to "swish the medication solution thoroughly in the mouth for as long as possible before swallowing." The teaching box adds that it should not be mixed with food.
Trap: (C) treats mouthwash as harmless adjunct hygiene, which is a plausible move for a patient with a sore mouth. The chapter directs patients to avoid commercial mouthwashes during therapy because they may increase irritation to the oral mucosa. (D) contradicts the instruction not to mix the dose with food.

Subtopic: dilution, food, and timing rules

Q: A nurse is preparing an intravenous dose of caspofungin. Which action is correct?
A) Dilute the dose with 5% dextrose in water
B) Dilute the dose with the recommended amount of normal saline
C) Give the dose as an intravenous bolus
D) Use the solution even if slightly cloudy, after shaking it
Answer: B
Rationale: "Only use clear solutions of caspofungin and dilute doses with the recommended amount of normal saline. Do not use with dextrose-containing products for diluting and do not give as a bolus."
Trap: (A) is the correct diluent for a different drug in the same chapter — voriconazole's intravenous doses may be diluted with 5% dextrose in water or normal saline. The echinocandin is the one carrying the dextrose prohibition, and the two opposite rules sit two paragraphs apart in the same implementation section.

Q: A patient is prescribed voriconazole tablets and asks when to take them. Which instruction is correct?
A) Take each dose with meals to reduce nausea
B) Take each dose 1 hour before or 1 hour after a meal
C) Sprinkle the dose on applesauce and swallow without chewing
D) Take each dose with a full glass of orange juice
Answer: B
Rationale: Voriconazole "oral doses are to be given 1 hour before or 1 hour after a meal," and the patient teaching box restates that rule, which is the tell that it is the one meant to be remembered. Terbinafine tablets, by contrast, may be taken without regard to food.
Trap: (C) borrows terbinafine's granule instruction, which does direct sprinkling and swallowing without chewing — but it specifies pudding or another nonacidic food item, so applesauce and citrus are wrong vehicles even for that drug. Terbinafine's own tablet and granule forms carry opposite food rules in consecutive sentences, which is what makes the food question feel unsettled.

Q: A patient using a vaginal antifungal preparation calls to say her menstrual period has started and asks whether she should stop the medication. Which response is correct?
A) Stop the medication and resume it when the period ends
B) Menstruation is not a contraindication, so continue the medication as prescribed
C) Switch to an oral form for the duration of the period
D) Double each dose to compensate for medication loss
Answer: B
Rationale: The teaching box states that "menstruation is not a contraindication" and that the medication "must be continued as prescribed." Patients are also taught to abstain from sexual intercourse until treatment is completed and the infection is resolved, and to contact the prescriber if symptoms persist once treatment is finished.
Trap: (A) is written into the chapter as a correction of exactly this common patient belief, which is why it is the option patients and students reach for. Stopping mid-course is the adherence failure the chapter's own outcome statements are built to prevent.

Q: A patient is being discharged on caspofungin. Which symptoms should the nurse teach the patient to report to the prescriber immediately or call 911 for? Select all that apply.
A) Shortness of breath
B) A mild headache on the day of the infusion
C) Itching
D) Facial swelling
E) Rash
Answer: A, C, D, E
Rationale: The teaching box directs caspofungin patients to "immediately report to the prescriber or call 911 if they have any problems with shortness of breath, itching, facial swelling, and/or a rash." Table 42.2 supports the picture with caspofungin's integumentary row of rash, facial edema, and itching.
Trap: (B) is a genuine caspofungin adverse effect — headache appears in the drug's central nervous row alongside fever and chills — but it is not one of the four emergency symptoms, and including a real adverse effect is what makes the option hard to reject. The four correct items form an anaphylaxis picture, and caspofungin is the only drug in this chapter whose teaching says to call 911.
