# Chapter 39 drills — Antibiotics Part 2

Authored from `sources/Ch39_Fact_Bank.md` (Lilley, Collins & Snyder, 10th ed.).

Blueprint weight is **6 questions of 121 (5.0%), depth `standard`** — 4 items on
Exam 1 and 2 on the Final, one-third the weight of Chapter 38 even though this
chapter covers more drug classes. That earns **44 drills**, near the top of the
`standard` band of 30 to 45, because the chapter's testable content is unusually
number-dense: two separate therapeutic-drug-monitoring schemes whose values are
an order of magnitude apart, three FDA quinolone actions in three different
years, and a set of infusion-rate rules that each carry a named patient-safety
consequence.

Topics follow the chapter's own class sequence — resistant organisms, then
aminoglycosides split into pharmacology, monitoring, and toxicity because those
are three different things to restudy, then quinolones, then the miscellaneous
drugs grouped by class, then vancomycin on its own. Aminoglycoside monitoring is
separated from aminoglycoside toxicity deliberately: a learner can hold the
peak-and-trough timing perfectly and still invert nephrotoxicity against
ototoxicity, and the dashboard should be able to say which one is red.

Every distinction the chapter states in both directions is drilled from both
ends: MRSA against VRE treatment lists, ESBL against CRO escalation,
concentration-dependent against time-dependent killing, once-daily against
traditional aminoglycoside sampling, nephrotoxicity against ototoxicity, red man
syndrome against true hypersensitivity, and oral against parenteral vancomycin.
Inverting those is the reflex error this chapter produces.

Topic: resistant organism vocabulary and treatment
Subtopic: which organism resists what

Q: A patient's blood cultures grow vancomycin-resistant Enterococcus faecium. Which drugs does the chapter list as treatment options for this organism? Select all that apply.
A) daptomycin
B) linezolid
C) tedizolid
D) vancomycin
E) ceftaroline
Answer: A, B, C
Rationale: Box 39.1 lists daptomycin, linezolid, and tedizolid as the treatments for VRE. Vancomycin-resistant Enterococcus is defined in the key term box as Enterococcus species resistant to beta-lactam antibiotics and vancomycin, most commonly Enterococcus faecium.
Trap: (D) is wrong by definition of the organism's own name, and it still pulls because vancomycin is the reflex answer for any resistant gram-positive organism. (E) ceftaroline appears on the MRSA treatment list only; the two lists overlap in daptomycin, linezolid, and tedizolid, and everything else on the MRSA list is a discriminator.

Q: A different patient has an MRSA bloodstream infection. Which drug appears on the chapter's MRSA treatment list but not on its VRE list?
A) vancomycin
B) daptomycin
C) linezolid
D) tedizolid
Answer: A
Rationale: Box 39.1 gives vancomycin, daptomycin, linezolid, tedizolid, telavancin, oritavancin, dalbavancin, and ceftaroline for MRSA, but only daptomycin, linezolid, and tedizolid for VRE. Vancomycin is separately named as the antibiotic of choice for the treatment of MRSA infection.
Trap: This is the mirror error of the previous item. (B), (C), and (D) are the three drugs the two lists share, so a learner who remembers only that the lists overlap will pick one of them; the discriminator is what MRSA gets that VRE cannot.

Subtopic: the ESBL to CRO escalation

Q: A urine culture grows an extended-spectrum beta-lactamase-producing Klebsiella. The nurse anticipates an order for a drug from which class?
A) Carbapenems
B) Penicillins
C) Cephalosporins
D) Monobactams such as aztreonam
Answer: A
Rationale: The chapter states that organisms producing ESBL are resistant to all beta-lactam antibiotics, meaning penicillins and cephalosporins, and to aztreonam, and can be treated only with carbapenems or sometimes fluoroquinolones. Box 39.1 gives carbapenems as the ESBL treatment.
Trap: (D) pulls hardest because aztreonam is a monobactam rather than a penicillin or cephalosporin, so it reads as an escape from beta-lactam resistance. The key term definition explicitly includes aztreonam in what ESBL producers resist.

Q: A week later the same patient's culture grows a carbapenem-resistant organism, and meropenem is ordered. Which agents does the chapter name as the remaining options for this infection?
A) colistin and tigecycline
B) carbapenems such as meropenem
C) penicillins and cephalosporins
D) aztreonam
Answer: A
Rationale: Box 39.1 names colistin and tigecycline as the treatment for carbapenem-resistant organisms. The chapter tells the escalation as cause and effect: heavy carbapenem use against ESBL producers drove bacteria to produce carbapenemase, which renders all carbapenems ineffective, leaving few known antibiotics.
Trap: (B) is the mirror error of the previous item — carbapenems are the correct escalation for ESBL, and a learner who banked that rule will apply it one organism too far. A carbapenem for a carbapenem-resistant organism names the exact drug class the organism is defined by resisting.

Subtopic: preventing spread

Q: A nurse is assigned to a patient in contact isolation for a multidrug-resistant organism and asks what single action most reduces the risk of spreading it to other patients. Which response reflects the chapter's repeated emphasis?
A) Washing hands before and after every patient contact
B) Wearing a mask whenever entering the room
C) Restricting the patient's visitors to immediate family
D) Obtaining repeat cultures every 48 hours
Answer: A
Rationale: The chapter states three separate times that these organisms are spread by contact, that all health care professionals must wash their hands before and after all patient contact, and that the importance of handwashing cannot be overemphasized. The vancomycin case study asks directly for the single best action to prevent spread.
Trap: (B) is the reflex pick because isolation and masks travel together in the learner's memory, but the chapter pairs multidrug-resistant organisms with contact isolation, not droplet or airborne precautions, and names handwashing rather than any barrier as the action of utmost importance.

Topic: aminoglycoside pharmacology and spectrum
Subtopic: mechanism and route

Q: A student notes that aminoglycosides and tetracyclines bind the same ribosomal subunit and asks whether the two classes therefore do the same thing to bacteria. Which statement is accurate?
A) Both bind the 30S ribosome, but aminoglycosides are bactericidal while tetracyclines are bacteriostatic
B) Both bind the 30S ribosome and both are bacteriostatic
C) Aminoglycosides bind the 50S ribosome, which is why they kill rather than suppress
D) Both bind the 50S ribosome, but only the tetracyclines are bactericidal
Answer: A
Rationale: The chapter states that aminoglycosides work in a way similar to that of the tetracyclines, in that they bind to ribosomes, specifically the 30S ribosome, and thereby prevent protein synthesis in bacteria. It separately classifies aminoglycosides as bactericidal and potent, making them drugs of choice for particularly virulent infections.
Trap: (B) is the pull, because same binding site reads as same behavior. Shared target does not mean shared outcome here: the 30S site is common ground and the killing behavior is opposite.

Q: An oral aminoglycoside is ordered for a patient with hepatic encephalopathy. Which statement explains why this order is not the class exception it first appears to be?
A) neomycin is given orally precisely because it is too toxic for systemic use, and it acts locally on gut bacteria
B) All aminoglycosides are well absorbed orally when given with food
C) Oral aminoglycosides are absorbed systemically and reach the liver in high concentration
D) neomycin is the only aminoglycoside available in an injectable form
Answer: A
Rationale: The chapter states that aminoglycosides are not given orally because of their poor oral absorption, with neomycin as the exception, and that neomycin's toxicity limits its use to the gastrointestinal tract and topical application. In hepatic encephalopathy it reduces the number of ammonia-producing bacteria in the gastrointestinal tract, and the resulting drop in blood ammonia levels sometimes improves neurologic symptoms.
Trap: (D) inverts the fact that matters most about this drug: neomycin is not available in injectable form at all. A learner who remembers neomycin as the odd one out but not which way it is odd will pick it.

Subtopic: choosing among the aminoglycosides

Q: A patient's wound culture reports an organism resistant to both gentamicin and tobramycin. Which aminoglycoside does the chapter describe as reserved for exactly this situation?
A) amikacin
B) neomycin
C) streptomycin
D) paromomycin
Answer: A
Rationale: Table 39.2 describes amikacin as generally reserved for resistant infections and states that it is often used to treat infections that are resistant to gentamicin or tobramycin.
Trap: (C) streptomycin looks like a reserve agent because its listed uses are unusual ones, but those uses are organism-specific rather than resistance-driven: plague, tularemia, tuberculosis, and granuloma inguinale. Reserve status for resistance belongs to amikacin.

Q: A child with cystic fibrosis has recurrent Pseudomonas pulmonary infections and is started on an inhaled aminoglycoside. Which drug is the chapter describing?
A) tobramycin
B) amikacin
C) gentamicin
D) neomycin
Answer: A
Rationale: Table 39.2 states that tobramycin is commonly used to treat recurrent pulmonary infections in patients with cystic fibrosis, by both injectable and inhaled dosing. The chapter separately notes that aminoglycosides as a class can be given by inhalation for the treatment of lung infections.
Trap: (C) gentamicin is the default workhorse of the class and is the reflex pick whenever no other cue is obvious, but the cystic fibrosis and inhalation cues belong to tobramycin specifically. Gentamicin's signature pairing is with penicillin for gram-positive synergy.

Subtopic: combination therapy and sequence

Q: Gentamicin 300 mg IV and a beta-lactam antibiotic are both scheduled at 0900. Which should the nurse infuse first, and why?
A) The beta-lactam, because it breaks down the bacterial cell wall so the aminoglycoside can reach the ribosomes
B) The aminoglycoside, because it needs the highest possible peak concentration and should not be delayed
C) The beta-lactam, because giving the aminoglycoside first inactivates the beta-lactam in the tubing
D) Either order is acceptable, because the synergistic effect does not depend on sequence
Answer: A
Rationale: The chapter states that when aminoglycosides are used in combination with beta-lactam antibiotics, the beta-lactam antibiotic is given first, because the beta-lactams break down the cell wall of the bacteria and allow the aminoglycoside to gain access to the ribosomes, where they work. Critical thinking exercise 2 is built on this sequence.
Trap: (B) is the reflex pick for anyone who has just learned concentration-dependent killing, since it argues from a real property of the class. Concentration matters for dosing interval, not for which bag hangs first; the sequence rule is mechanistic and runs the other way.

Topic: aminoglycoside therapeutic drug monitoring
Subtopic: concentration-dependent versus time-dependent killing

Q: A pharmacist converts a patient from gentamicin three times daily to a single daily dose and explains that this drug kills best when a high plasma concentration is achieved even briefly. Which property is being described?
A) Concentration-dependent killing
B) Time-dependent killing
C) The postantibiotic effect
D) The synergistic effect
Answer: A
Rationale: The key term box defines concentration-dependent killing as a property of some antibiotics, especially aminoglycosides, whereby achieving high plasma drug concentrations, even if briefly, results in the most effective bacterial kill. This is the rationale for moving aminoglycosides toward once-daily dosing.
Trap: (C) is adjacent and genuinely true of aminoglycosides, which makes it tempting: the postantibiotic effect is continued bacterial suppression after brief exposure. But that names what happens after the drug is gone, not the property that determines how high the level must go.

Q: A different antibiotic's monograph states that prolonged high plasma concentrations are required for effective bacterial kill, which is why it is infused frequently rather than once daily. Which property is this, and which classes does the chapter say show it?
A) Time-dependent killing, and it is the property of most antibiotic classes
B) Time-dependent killing, and it is unique to the aminoglycosides
C) Concentration-dependent killing, and it is the property of most antibiotic classes
D) Concentration-dependent killing, and it is unique to the beta-lactams
Answer: A
Rationale: The key term box defines time-dependent killing as a property of most antibiotic classes whereby prolonged high plasma drug concentrations are required for effective bacterial kill, and cross-references it to concentration-dependent killing. The two definitions point at each other in the source text.
Trap: This is the mirror error of the previous item, and (B) is where it lands: a learner who has just attached a monitoring rule to aminoglycosides tends to attach the second property to them as well. The aminoglycosides are the concentration-dependent exception; most other classes, including the beta-lactams, are time-dependent.

Subtopic: when peaks and troughs are drawn

Q: A patient is receiving tobramycin 5 mg/kg once daily. When should the nurse expect the laboratory sample for a drug level to be drawn?
A) A trough only, 8 to 12 hours after completion of the dose
B) A peak only, 30 to 60 minutes after the infusion ends
C) Both a peak and a trough, with the trough drawn just before the next dose
D) A trough only, drawn immediately before the next scheduled dose
Answer: A
Rationale: For once-daily regimens the chapter states that peak levels are usually not measured, because the peak for a single daily dose is assumed to be short-lived, and that trough levels are drawn at least 8 to 12 hours after completion of dose administration, with a therapeutic goal at or below 1 mcg/mL.
Trap: (D) is the near miss and the most dangerous one, because drawing immediately before the next dose is the correct rule for traditional aminoglycoside dosing and for vancomycin. The sampling rule splits by dosing regimen, not by drug.

Q: Another patient receives gentamicin three times daily. Which sampling schedule matches the chapter's rule for traditional dosing?
A) Peak 30 to 60 minutes after a 30-minute infusion, and trough just before the next dose
B) Peak 8 to 12 hours after the dose, and no trough
C) Trough only, 8 to 12 hours after completion of the dose
D) Peak just before the next dose, and trough 30 minutes after the infusion
Answer: A
Rationale: With traditional three-times-daily dosing both peak and trough levels are measured. Samples for peak levels are drawn 30 to 60 minutes after a 30-minute infusion, and samples for trough levels are drawn just before the next dose.
Trap: This is the mirror error of the previous item, and (D) is its full inversion — a peak drawn just before the next dose has the two definitions reversed. A trough is the lowest level and therefore comes at the end of the interval; a peak is the highest and comes shortly after the infusion.

Subtopic: interpreting the numbers

Q: A patient on once-daily tobramycin has a reported trough of 3 mcg/mL, and the next dose is due now. What does the nurse do?
A) Hold the dose and notify the prescriber, because this trough is above the level associated with toxicity
B) Give the dose, because troughs up to 5 mcg/mL are therapeutic for tobramycin
C) Repeat the level in 24 hours and give the dose now
D) Skip one dose only and resume the schedule without notifying anyone
Answer: A
Rationale: Table 39.1 gives a once-daily trough goal of less than 1 mcg/mL for gentamicin and tobramycin, and the text states that trough levels above 2 mcg/mL are associated with a greater risk for both ototoxicity and nephrotoxicity. The chapter also states that alteration in auditory, vestibular, or renal function may indicate the need for a possible dosage adjustment or withdrawal of the drug.
Trap: (C) and (D) both delay a decision the number already supports, which is what makes them attractive to a learner who is unsure of the threshold. Repeating a level or skipping a single dose without notification leaves the prescriber unaware that the patient is in the toxic range.

Q: An amikacin trough returns at 8 mcg/mL on once-daily dosing, and a new nurse says this must be toxic because gentamicin troughs are kept below 1. Which response is accurate?
A) Amikacin's therapeutic range runs an order of magnitude higher, and less than 10 mcg/mL is the once-daily trough goal
B) The nurse is correct; all aminoglycoside troughs share the same goal of less than 1 mcg/mL
C) Amikacin troughs should be reported in mg/mL rather than mcg/mL, so the value must be converted
D) Amikacin peaks rather than troughs are the monitored value on once-daily dosing
Answer: A
Rationale: Table 39.1 gives amikacin a once-daily trough goal of less than 10 mcg/mL and a traditional-dosing trough range of 5 to 10 mcg/mL, against 1 to 2 mcg/mL for gentamicin and tobramycin. The chapter states that peak levels are usually not measured on once-daily regimens for any of these drugs.
Trap: (C) is the unit trap the chapter gives its own key term for, warning that confusion of microgram and milligram sometimes results in drug dosage errors. Every level in this chapter is in mcg/mL, and a value reported in mg/mL is off by a factor of 1000.

Topic: aminoglycoside toxicity and monitoring priorities
Subtopic: nephrotoxicity versus ototoxicity

Q: A patient has been on IV gentamicin for six days. Which findings would the nurse interpret as evidence of nephrotoxicity? Select all that apply.
A) Urinary casts
B) Proteinuria
C) Rising BUN and serum creatinine levels
D) Tinnitus
E) A sense of fullness in the ears
Answer: A, B, C
Rationale: The chapter lists urinary casts, described as visible remnants of destroyed renal cells, along with proteinuria and increased BUN and serum creatinine levels, as the manifestations of aminoglycoside nephrotoxicity. Serum creatinine is measured at least every 3 days as an index of renal function.
Trap: (D) and (E) are the wrong side of the same toxicity table. Tinnitus and a sense of fullness in the ears are ototoxicity symptoms, and they pull because both toxicities are aminoglycoside toxicities monitored by the same drug levels. The distinction that matters clinically is that nephrotoxicity is usually reversible and ototoxicity likely is not.

Q: A patient receiving an IV aminoglycoside reports new ringing in the ears partway through the infusion. Which action is the priority?
A) Stop the infusion and notify the prescriber
B) Reassure the patient that ringing is expected and will resolve when therapy ends
C) Slow the infusion rate and continue the dose
D) Increase the infusion rate so the dose finishes sooner
Answer: A
Rationale: Tinnitus is a symptom of ototoxicity, which the chapter says often is not reversible and can result in varying degrees of permanent hearing loss from injury to the eighth cranial nerve. Alteration in auditory, vestibular, or renal function may indicate the need for a possible dosage adjustment or withdrawal of the drug, and patient teaching states that hearing loss may occur even after completion of therapy.
Trap: (C) is the mirror error of a different drug's rule — slowing the rate is the correct response to vancomycin red man syndrome, where rate is the actual mechanism. Ototoxicity is not rate-related, so the rate-adjustment options are answering the wrong drug's problem. (B) is wrong because the loss is likely permanent, not self-limiting.

Subtopic: interactions that raise each toxicity

Q: A patient on IV tobramycin is also receiving furosemide. Which risk does this combination specifically raise?
A) Ototoxicity
B) Nephrotoxicity
C) Neuromuscular blockade
D) Bleeding
Answer: A
Rationale: The chapter's interaction table pairs loop diuretics with an increased risk for ototoxicity, while vancomycin, cyclosporine, and amphotericin B, as other nephrotoxic drugs, raise the risk for nephrotoxicity.
Trap: (B) is the reflex pick because furosemide acts on the kidney, so a renal consequence feels obvious. The chapter assigns loop diuretics to the ears; the nephrotoxicity partners are the other nephrotoxic drugs, plus IV contrast media for CT scans.

Q: A patient stabilized on warfarin is started on IV gentamicin. Which effect does the chapter predict, and by what mechanism?
A) Potentiated warfarin toxicity, because the aminoglycoside kills gut flora and reduces vitamin K production
B) Reduced warfarin effect, because the aminoglycoside induces hepatic metabolism of warfarin
C) No interaction, because aminoglycosides are not absorbed from the gut
D) Potentiated warfarin toxicity, because the aminoglycoside displaces warfarin from albumin
Answer: A
Rationale: The chapter states that aminoglycosides kill intestinal bacterial flora and so reduce the amount of vitamin K produced by these gut bacteria, and therefore can potentiate warfarin toxicity. The quinolone section gives the same mechanism for that class, describing antibiotic-induced alteration of the intestinal flora, which affects vitamin K synthesis.
Trap: (C) borrows a true fact and draws the wrong conclusion from it. Poor oral absorption is why aminoglycosides are not given by mouth, but a systemically administered aminoglycoside still reaches and kills gut flora, which is exactly why the warfarin interaction exists.

Topic: quinolone mechanism and drug selection
Subtopic: mechanism and oral absorption

Q: A patient asks whether an antibiotic that works by destroying bacterial DNA will also damage human DNA. Which response reflects the chapter's description of the quinolones?
A) They interfere with the bacterial enzymes DNA gyrase and topoisomerase IV and do not inhibit the production of human DNA
B) They bind the 30S ribosome, so human DNA is not involved at all
C) They inhibit both bacterial and human DNA synthesis, which is why courses are kept short
D) They disrupt the bacterial cell membrane rather than acting on DNA
Answer: A
Rationale: The chapter states that quinolones destroy bacteria by altering their deoxyribonucleic acid, accomplishing this by interfering with the bacterial enzymes DNA gyrase and topoisomerase IV, and that quinolones do not inhibit the production of human DNA.
Trap: (B) is the aminoglycoside and tetracycline mechanism imported into the wrong class, and it pulls because both answers sound like plausible bacteria-specific targets. Ribosomal binding belongs to the protein-synthesis inhibitors; the quinolones act on the bacterial DNA enzymes.

Q: A hospitalized patient on IV levofloxacin is ready for discharge, and the prescriber changes the order to the oral form at the same dose. The patient's family questions whether pills can work as well as an IV drug. Which statement is accurate?
A) Quinolones other than norfloxacin have excellent oral absorption, comparable in most cases to intravenous injection
B) Oral quinolones reach roughly half the blood level of the IV form, so the dose is usually doubled
C) All quinolones including norfloxacin are equally well absorbed orally
D) Oral quinolones are absorbed well only when taken with milk or an antacid
Answer: A
Rationale: The chapter states that with the exception of norfloxacin these antibiotics have excellent oral absorption, and that in most cases the extent of oral absorption is comparable to that of intravenous injection. Ciprofloxacin is described as working orally as well as many intravenous antibiotics.
Trap: (D) inverts the class's central interaction. Dairy products and antacids reduce quinolone absorption and must be separated by at least 1 hour on either side, so the option names the exact pairing that defeats the oral route.

Subtopic: matching the quinolone to the infection

Q: A prescriber wants a single quinolone for a mixed infection that includes anaerobic organisms. Which drug does the chapter identify as carrying real anaerobic coverage?
A) moxifloxacin
B) norfloxacin
C) ciprofloxacin
D) gemifloxacin
Answer: A
Rationale: Table 39.3 describes moxifloxacin's spectrum as comparable to that of levofloxacin plus anaerobic coverage, with anaerobic infections among its indications, and Box 39.2 notes that moxifloxacin is strongest against the listed anaerobes.
Trap: (C) ciprofloxacin is the reflex pick because it carries the broadest general use in the table and is the drug most learners can name. Broad general use is not the same as anaerobic coverage, and the table builds each drug's spectrum by reference to the one above it, so the anaerobic link belongs only to moxifloxacin.

Q: A public health nurse is arranging postexposure prophylaxis after a suspected inhalational anthrax exposure. Which quinolone does the chapter name for this indication?
A) ciprofloxacin
B) levofloxacin
C) delafloxacin
D) norfloxacin
Answer: A
Rationale: Table 39.3 lists inhalational anthrax postexposure among ciprofloxacin's common indications, along with respiratory, skin, urinary tract, prostate, intraabdominal, gastrointestinal, bone, and joint infections, typhoid fever, and selected health care-associated pneumonias.
Trap: (D) norfloxacin is the trap for anyone who remembers it as the oldest and therefore most established member of the class. It is oral only with limited absorption, and its use is limited to genitourinary tract infections, which excludes it from a systemic exposure indication.

Topic: quinolone safety warnings and interactions
Subtopic: the black box and the FDA alerts

Q: A nurse reviews the black box warning that applies to all quinolones. Which risks does that warning cover? Select all that apply.
A) Tendinitis and tendon rupture
B) Peripheral neuropathy
C) Central nervous system effects including seizures
D) Exacerbation of myasthenia gravis
E) Aortic rupture
Answer: A, B, C, D
Rationale: The chapter states that the FDA requires a black box warning for all quinolones because of the increased risk for tendinitis and tendon rupture, as well as peripheral neuropathy, central nervous system effects such as seizures, and exacerbation of myasthenia gravis. Table 39.4 dates the tendon black box to 2008.
Trap: (E) aortic rupture is real and quinolone-related, but it belongs to the 2018 labeling change alongside mental health side effects and serious blood glucose disturbances, not to the black box. The chapter's three FDA actions fall in three different years with three different contents, and crossing them is the economical distractor here.

Q: An older adult on prednisone with chronic kidney disease is started on levofloxacin. Which teaching is most important?
A) Report any new joint, tendon, or heel pain immediately and stop the drug
B) Expect mild joint aching for the first three days of therapy
C) Take the drug with milk to reduce gastrointestinal upset
D) Restrict fluids to reduce the drug's renal load
Answer: A
Rationale: The chapter names older adult patients, patients with renal failure, and those receiving concurrent glucocorticoid therapy such as prednisone as the group at risk for tendon rupture, which is the subject of the 2008 black box warning. This patient carries all three risk factors at once.
Trap: (B) reframes a black box event as an expected minor effect, which is exactly how tendon symptoms get dismissed in practice. (C) names the dairy interaction that reduces quinolone absorption, so it is wrong for a second reason as well.

Subtopic: cardiac effect versus interaction

Q: A patient on amiodarone is prescribed a quinolone. Which cardiac concern does the chapter attach to the quinolone itself, and which to this combination?
A) The drug prolongs the QT interval; the combination with a class Ia or class III antidysrhythmic makes dangerous dysrhythmias more likely
B) The drug causes dysrhythmias; the combination prolongs the QT interval
C) The drug causes bradycardia; the combination causes tachycardia
D) The drug has no cardiac effect; only the combination is a concern
Answer: A
Rationale: The chapter names prolongation of the QT interval on the electrocardiogram as the quinolone cardiac effect, and states that dangerous cardiac dysrhythmias are more likely to occur when quinolones are taken by patients also receiving class Ia and class III antidysrhythmic drugs such as disopyramide and amiodarone, so such combinations are best avoided.
Trap: (B) is the direct swap and the one review question 1 is built around, offering bradycardia, dysrhythmias, tachycardia, and prolonged QT interval side by side. The effect of the drug is the electrocardiogram finding; the dysrhythmia is what the interaction produces.

Subtopic: absorption interactions and teaching

Q: A patient taking ciprofloxacin also takes calcium carbonate antacids and an iron supplement. What spacing does the chapter direct?
A) Take the interacting products at least 1 hour before or after the quinolone
B) Take the interacting products 2 hours before or 3 hours after the quinolone
C) Take everything together to improve tolerance
D) Discontinue the antacid and iron for the full course of therapy
Answer: A
Rationale: The chapter states that antacids, calcium, magnesium, iron, zinc preparations, and sucralfate cause a reduction in the oral absorption of the quinolone, and that patients need to take the interacting drugs at least 1 hour before or after taking quinolones. Dairy products and enteral tube feedings reduce absorption in the same way and are separated the same way.
Trap: (B) is the tetracycline interval from Chapter 38 applied to the wrong class. Both classes are blocked by antacids, calcium, magnesium, and iron, and both are fixed by spacing, which makes borrowing the wrong interval the most economical distractor available. Quinolone is 1 hour either side; tetracycline is 2 before and 3 after.

Q: A patient with a stubborn urinary tract infection is taking nitrofurantoin, and a quinolone is now proposed to be added. Why does the nurse question this?
A) nitrofurantoin can antagonize the antibacterial activity of the quinolones
B) The combination produces a synergistic effect that would require dose reduction of both
C) nitrofurantoin increases quinolone absorption to toxic levels
D) Both drugs are excreted unchanged in urine, so the combination causes crystalluria
Answer: A
Rationale: The quinolone interaction list states that nitrofurantoin can antagonize the antibacterial activity of the quinolones, and it appears again in the patient teaching material.
Trap: (B) is the reflex pick because two urinary anti-infectives look like a sensible pairing for a stubborn infection, and synergy is the combination concept this chapter teaches elsewhere with the aminoglycosides. Here the two drugs work against each other rather than together.

Topic: clindamycin linezolid and metronidazole profiles
Subtopic: clindamycin administration and adverse effects

Q: A new graduate prepares to give IV clindamycin by slow intravenous push. Which response by the charge nurse is correct?
A) Most references state never to give this drug by intravenous push, because too rapid infusion may lead to severe hypotension and possible cardiac arrest
B) Intravenous push is acceptable if given over at least 5 minutes
C) Intravenous push is preferred, because piggyback infusion inactivates the drug
D) Intravenous push is acceptable only for doses under 300 mg
Answer: A
Rationale: The implementation section states that clindamycin is given intravenously by piggyback technique and that most references state never to give these drugs by intravenous push, because too rapid an intravenous infusion may lead to severe hypotension and possible cardiac arrest.
Trap: (B) is the pull because slowing a push feels like an adequate safeguard and is how many other drugs are handled. The chapter's prohibition is categorical rather than rate-qualified, and it pairs with the same prohibition on vancomycin, where the consequence is severe hypotension and red man syndrome.

Q: A prescriber orders clindamycin for a patient with a history of ulcerative colitis. Why does the nurse question the order?
A) Ulcerative colitis is a listed contraindication, and clindamycin is the antibiotic most associated with pseudomembranous colitis
B) Clindamycin has no activity against the anaerobes that colonize an inflamed bowel
C) Ulcerative colitis increases clindamycin absorption to toxic levels
D) Clindamycin is contraindicated only in patients older than 65 years
Answer: A
Rationale: Clindamycin is contraindicated in known hypersensitivity, in those with ulcerative colitis or enteritis, and in infants younger than 1 month of age. Its most common adverse effects are gastrointestinal, including pseudomembranous colitis, which the chapter says is often associated with antibiotic therapy, especially clindamycin therapy.
Trap: (B) states the opposite of the drug's main strength, which is that it is active against several anaerobic organisms and is most often used for this purpose. A learner who knows the bowel is the problem may reach for the wrong reason and land on a false pharmacologic claim.

Subtopic: linezolid interactions

Q: A patient is starting linezolid. Which concurrent therapies does the chapter name as interacting with it? Select all that apply.
A) A selective serotonin reuptake inhibitor antidepressant
B) A vasopressor such as dopamine
C) An oral anticoagulant
D) Thyroid replacement hormone
E) A beta blocker
Answer: A, B
Rationale: The chapter names two drug interactions for linezolid: vasopressors such as dopamine, whose prohypertensive effects linezolid has the potential to strengthen, and serotonergic drugs, with postmarketing case reports of serotonin syndrome. It names no interaction with anticoagulants, thyroid hormone, or beta blockers.
Trap: (C) pulls because warfarin interacts with both the aminoglycosides and the quinolones in this same chapter, so an anticoagulator feels like a safe pick on any antibiotic list. Linezolid's named interactions are the two blood-pressure and serotonin pathways plus tyramine-containing foods, not the vitamin K pathway.

Q: A patient on linezolid reports eating aged cheese, smoked fish, and sauerkraut at a family gathering. Which effect does the nurse assess for, and how does it differ from the SSRI interaction?
A) Increased blood pressure from tyramine, whereas the SSRI interaction produces serotonin syndrome
B) Serotonin syndrome from tyramine, whereas the SSRI interaction produces hypertension
C) Decreased blood pressure from tyramine, whereas the SSRI interaction produces hypertension
D) No effect from food, because only injected vasopressors interact
Answer: A
Rationale: The chapter states that tyramine-containing foods such as aged cheese or wine, soy sauce, smoked meats or fish, and sauerkraut can interact with linezolid to raise blood pressure, and separately reports serotonin syndrome with serotonergic drugs. It explains that tyramine is normally broken down by monoamine oxidase, and that large amounts of tyramine lead to release of adrenalin-like substances and an increased blood pressure.
Trap: (B) is the direct swap of the two interactions, which pulls because both must be assessed on the same patient and both are consequences of the same drug. The food and vasopressor routes converge on hypertension; only the serotonergic drugs produce serotonin syndrome.

Subtopic: metronidazole and alcohol

Q: A patient is discharged on oral metronidazole and mentions that the annual office party is tomorrow night. What does the nurse teach?
A) Avoid alcohol entirely, starting 24 hours before therapy began and continuing at least 36 hours after the last dose
B) Avoid alcohol for 72 hours after the last dose only
C) One drink is acceptable if taken at least 2 hours after a dose
D) Alcohol is a concern only with the intravenous form
Answer: A
Rationale: The chapter states that metronidazole causes acute alcohol intolerance when taken with alcoholic beverages, because of the accumulation of acetaldehyde, the principal alcohol metabolite, and that patients must avoid alcohol for 24 hours before initiation of therapy and for at least 36 hours after the last dose. Critical thinking exercise 1 is built on this exact scenario.
Trap: (B) is the cefotetan window from Chapter 38, where the rule is to avoid alcohol within 72 hours after. Two disulfiram-like reactions with two different windows invite the swap, and metronidazole is the only drug across these two chapters that also carries a restriction before therapy starts.

Topic: daptomycin nitrofurantoin and streptogramin profiles
Subtopic: daptomycin

Q: A patient with MRSA pneumonia is prescribed daptomycin. Why does the nurse question this order?
A) daptomycin cannot treat pneumonia, because it is inactivated by lung surfactant
B) daptomycin cannot treat pneumonia, because of an increased risk for mortality in that population
C) daptomycin has no activity against MRSA
D) daptomycin is available only in an oral form, which is inadequate for pneumonia
Answer: A
Rationale: The chapter states plainly that daptomycin cannot be used to treat pneumonia, because it is inactivated by lung surfactant. It does treat complicated skin and soft-tissue infections caused by susceptible gram-positive bacteria including MRSA and VRE, and is injectable only.
Trap: (B) gives the right conclusion with the wrong mechanism, and it is the reason doripenem in Chapter 38 must not be used for pneumonia. Two drugs across these two chapters carry a pneumonia exclusion for entirely different reasons, so a stem that supplies the wrong reason is testing mechanism rather than recall.

Q: A patient on daptomycin also takes atorvastatin. Which laboratory value does the chapter direct the nurse to monitor throughout therapy?
A) Creatine phosphokinase
B) Platelet count
C) Serum ammonia
D) Prothrombin time
Answer: A
Rationale: The chapter directs monitoring of creatine phosphokinase levels throughout daptomycin therapy because daptomycin may increase these levels, and notes a theoretical risk for increased myopathy with statins.
Trap: (B) platelet count is the linezolid monitoring parameter, since that drug has been shown to decrease platelet count. The miscellaneous antibiotics each carry one signature laboratory value, and swapping them between drugs is the reflex error this group produces.

Subtopic: nitrofurantoin

Q: nitrofurantoin is proposed for a urinary tract infection in a patient with significantly impaired renal function. What is the nurse's concern?
A) It is contraindicated in significant renal impairment because the drug concentrates in the urine
B) It should be given at a doubled dose to compensate for reduced urinary concentration
C) It is contraindicated because it is available only in intravenous form
D) It is safe in renal impairment but must be avoided in liver disease alone
Answer: A
Rationale: nitrofurantoin is contraindicated in significant renal function impairment because the drug concentrates in the urine, and the dose must be reduced for older adult patients or those with decreased renal function. It is oral only and is indicated primarily for urinary tract infections.
Trap: (B) reasons from the true fact that impaired kidneys deliver less drug to the urine and arrives at exactly the wrong action. The chapter treats concentration in the urine as the source of toxicity risk in renal impairment, not as a reason to escalate the dose.

Subtopic: quinupristin and dalfopristin

Q: A nurse is preparing quinupristin and dalfopristin for a patient with a VRE bloodstream infection through a peripheral line. Which preparation step is required?
A) Reconstitute and infuse with 5% dextrose in water only, and avoid saline and heparinized flushes
B) Reconstitute with normal saline and infuse over 10 minutes
C) Shake the vial vigorously to ensure the two components fully dissolve
D) Flush the line with heparin immediately before and after the infusion
Answer: A
Rationale: The chapter states that this drug must be infused with 5% dextrose in water only and cannot be mixed with saline or heparin, including heparinized flushes. Reconstitution uses only that diluent, mixing is done with a gentle swirling action instead of shaking to minimize foaming, and infusions are generally given over at least 60 minutes.
Trap: (C) is the pull for anyone reconstituting a stubborn powder, and shaking is exactly what the chapter forbids because of foaming. (D) names the prohibition a distractor most often omits: the ban extends past the infusion itself to heparinized flushes in the same line.

Topic: vancomycin monitoring and administration
Subtopic: red man syndrome versus hypersensitivity

Q: Two days into IV vancomycin, a patient reports feeling hot and itchy in the face and neck, and those areas are flushed. What does the nurse do?
A) Recognize red man syndrome and slow the infusion to run over at least 1 hour
B) Stop the drug, treat for anaphylaxis, and document a vancomycin allergy
C) Continue the infusion at the current rate and apply a cool cloth
D) Increase the rate so the infusion finishes before symptoms worsen
Answer: A
Rationale: The chapter describes red man syndrome as bothersome but usually not harmful, characterized by flushing or itching of the head, face, neck, and upper trunk area, most commonly seen when the drug is infused too rapidly, and usually alleviated by slowing the rate of infusion to at least 1 hour. Vancomycin must be infused over at least 60 minutes, and longer for higher doses.
Trap: (B) is the reflex pick because flushing and itching read as an allergic reaction, and rapid infusion can also cause hypotension, which makes the anaphylaxis reading more tempting still. Red man syndrome is rate-related rather than immune, and documenting a false allergy would remove the antibiotic of choice for MRSA from this patient's options.

Q: A different vancomycin patient develops hives, then stridor, wheezing, and a falling blood pressure. Which interpretation is correct?
A) This is a hypersensitivity reaction, because laryngeal edema and bronchospasm are outside the red man picture
B) This is red man syndrome, because hypotension occurs with rapid infusion
C) This is red man syndrome, because vancomycin does not cause hypersensitivity
D) This is expected and requires only that the rate be slowed
Answer: A
Rationale: The assessment section describes the hypersensitivity spectrum as ranging from mild reactions with rash, pruritus, or hives to severe reactions with laryngeal edema, bronchospasm, hypotension, and possible cardiac arrest. Red man syndrome is confined to flushing and itching of the head, face, neck, and upper trunk.
Trap: This is the mirror error of the previous item, and (B) is where it lands: a learner who has just banked the rule that vancomycin flushing is harmless will apply it to a genuine airway reaction. Hypotension appears in both pictures, so it does not discriminate; laryngeal edema and bronchospasm do.

Subtopic: oral versus parenteral vancomycin

Q: Which conditions require the parenteral rather than the oral form of vancomycin? Select all that apply.
A) Bone and joint infection caused by Staphylococcus
B) Bacterial bloodstream infection caused by Staphylococcus
C) Clostridioides difficile antibiotic-induced colitis
D) Staphylococcal enterocolitis
Answer: A, B
Rationale: The chapter assigns bone and joint infections and bacterial bloodstream infections caused by Staphylococcus species to the parenteral route, and antibiotic-induced colitis and staphylococcal enterocolitis to the oral route. The implementation section restates it: oral dosage forms are only used to treat microbes in the gastrointestinal tract, and parenteral forms must be used to treat infection outside it.
Trap: (C) and (D) are the wrong side of the same route table, and they pull because both are serious infections and seriousness reads as a reason to go intravenous. The oral formulation is poorly absorbed and is used for its local effects on the tract's surface, so intravenous vancomycin would never reach the gut lumen where those two infections live.

Q: A patient develops Clostridioides difficile diarrhea, and the prescriber orders IV vancomycin. Why does the nurse question the route?
A) Oral vancomycin is required, because the drug is not absorbed and works only on the surface of the gastrointestinal tract
B) Oral vancomycin is required, because the intravenous form is inactivated by gastric acid
C) The intravenous route is correct, because oral vancomycin is too well absorbed to stay in the gut
D) Either route is acceptable, because vancomycin distributes freely between blood and bowel lumen
Answer: A
Rationale: The dosage table footnote states flatly that oral vancomycin is not absorbed and is used only for Clostridioides difficile diarrhea. Because the oral formulation is poorly absorbed from the gastrointestinal tract, it is used for its local effects on the tract's surface.
Trap: This is the mirror error of the previous item. (C) inverts the absorption fact that makes the whole rule work — the oral form is useful in the gut precisely because it stays there, not despite it.

Subtopic: trough monitoring

Q: A patient is on IV vancomycin every 12 hours. When and why is the blood sample for a level drawn?
A) A trough, drawn immediately before the next dose, generally before the third or fourth dose of a new regimen
B) A peak, drawn 30 to 60 minutes after the infusion ends
C) A trough, drawn 8 to 12 hours after the infusion ends
D) Both a peak and a trough with every dose
Answer: A
Rationale: The chapter states that measurement of peak levels is no longer routinely recommended and that only trough levels are commonly monitored, with blood samples for trough levels drawn immediately before administration of the next dose. The assessment section says troughs are generally done before the fourth dose and need to be obtained within 30 minutes before the next scheduled dose; the implementation section says before the third or fourth dose.
Trap: (C) imports the once-daily aminoglycoside rule, where the trough is drawn at least 8 to 12 hours after the dose ends. (B) is out of date by the chapter's own statement even though an optimal peak range of 18 to 50 mcg/mL is still printed in the same paragraph, which is what keeps the option alive.

Q: A vancomycin trough returns at 1 mcg/mL, and a nurse familiar with gentamicin says the level looks reassuringly low. Which response is accurate?
A) A vancomycin trough of 1 mcg/mL is subtherapeutic; the optimal trough is 10 to 20 mcg/mL
B) A vancomycin trough of 1 mcg/mL is therapeutic, matching the aminoglycoside goal
C) A vancomycin trough of 1 mcg/mL is toxic and the next dose should be held
D) Vancomycin troughs are not interpreted, because only peaks are meaningful
Answer: A
Rationale: The chapter gives subtherapeutic vancomycin levels as less than 5 mcg/mL, an optimal trough of 10 to 20 mcg/mL with many practitioners working toward 15 to 20 depending on indication, and toxic levels as over 50 mcg/mL.
Trap: (B) is the reflex pick because both drugs use troughs drawn before the next dose and both guard against nephrotoxicity and ototoxicity, so the numbers feel interchangeable. They are an order of magnitude apart: a gentamicin trough of 15 is frankly toxic while a vancomycin trough of 15 is the working goal, and the same number means opposite things depending on the drug.
