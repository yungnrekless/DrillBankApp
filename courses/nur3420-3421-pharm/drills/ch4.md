# Chapter 4 drills — Cultural, Legal, and Ethical Considerations

Authored from `sources/Ch4_Fact_Bank.md` (Lilley, Collins & Snyder, 10th ed.).

Blueprint weight is **6 questions of 121 (5.0%), depth `standard`** — split
evenly, 3 items on Exam 1 and 3 on the Final, the most evenly divided chapter in
the blueprint.

That earns **45 drills**, the top of the `standard` band of 30 to 45. The
chapter sits high in its band for a structural reason rather than a weight one:
it is not drug-based, so it carries no drug profiles, no dosages, and no
pharmacokinetics, and what it has instead is enumerable lists with levels — five
controlled-substance schedules, four clinical trial phases, three recall
classes, six ethical principles, four elements of malpractice liability, and a
legislation timeline pairing each act with its year and its provision. Those
lists are what the items are built from, and a list drilled from only one end
lets a learner pass on an order they hold backwards.

Topics follow the chapter's own four subjects — cultural variation in drug
response, federal drug law, drug development and its safety machinery, and the
legal and ethical frame around nursing practice — rather than its section
numbers. Each is qualified by what the chapter is about so it stays distinct
once the whole course is banked.

Every distinction the chapter states in both directions is drilled from both
ends: polymorphism and pharmacogenomics, rapid and slow acetylator, safety and
efficacy legislation, healthy and diseased trial subjects, blinded and
double-blind, most and least severe recall, negligence and malpractice,
beneficence and nonmaleficence.

The cultural-practices table on p. 48 is not drilled cell by cell, because the
fact bank deliberately did not transcribe it: the chapter instructs the reader
to consult the patient rather than assume beliefs from group membership, and
flashcarding that table would teach the opposite. The drug-response box on
p. 49, which is pharmacologic and specific, is drilled in full.

Topic: ethnopharmacology and drug response variation
Subtopic: polymorphism versus pharmacogenomics

Q: Two patients receive the same drug at the same dose and respond differently. The nurse attributes the difference to their age, sex, size, and body composition. Which term does the chapter's key-term box use for that variation?
A) Bias
B) Pharmacogenomics
C) Drug polymorphism
D) Ethnopharmacology
Answer: C
Rationale: The key term defines drug polymorphism as "variation in response to a drug because of a patient's age, sex, size, and/or body composition." That list notably does not name genetics. The body text on p. 48 broadens it to environmental factors such as diet and nutritional status, cultural factors, and genetic factors, and the chapter is inconsistent between the two.
Trap: (B) pulls because all three terms in this box explain why patients respond differently, and the box defines them within a few lines of each other. Pharmacogenomics is the narrow one: "the study of genetics in drug response."

Q: A researcher is studying how one inherited gene variant changes a patient's response to a drug. Which term names that study?
A) Pharmacogenomics
B) Drug polymorphism
C) Adherence
D) Bias
Answer: A
Rationale: Pharmacogenomics is "the study of genetics in drug response." The chapter cross-references Chapter 8 for it. Ethnopharmacology is the broader field, defined as the effect of ethnicity on absorption, metabolism, distribution, and excretion as well as genetic variation.
Trap: (B) is the mirror error of the previous item. The key-term definition of polymorphism lists age, sex, size, and body composition, not genes, so a stem naming a specific gene variant is not asking about polymorphism.

Subtopic: acetylator status

Q: A patient who is a rapid acetylator is started on a drug that undergoes acetylation. What should the nurse anticipate?
A) Drug accumulation that may require lower dosages
B) Delayed absorption requiring a change of route
C) No effect on blood levels, because acetylation does not alter drug concentration
D) Subtherapeutic drug concentrations that may require higher dosages
Answer: D
Rationale: The chapter states that rapid acetylators "metabolize drugs more quickly, which predisposes the patient to subtherapeutic drug concentrations and may require higher drug dosages." Its clinical pearl on the same page reads, "Ethnicity can have an impact on drug metabolism."
Trap: (A) is the reflex pick for anyone who reads "rapid metabolism" as "more drug in the body." Faster metabolism clears the drug faster, so levels fall rather than rise, and the dose goes up rather than down.

Q: A slow acetylator is prescribed that same drug at a standard dose. Which risk is the nurse monitoring for?
A) Toxicity from drug accumulation
B) Subtherapeutic levels from rapid clearance
C) Failure of the drug to be absorbed at all
D) An allergic reaction to the acetyl group
Answer: A
Rationale: Slow acetylation is decreased metabolism, so the drug accumulates and toxicity is the risk. Chapter 2 lists the same pair in its Table 2.6, fast acetylator with increased metabolism and slow acetylator with decreased, and Chapter 38 names slow acetylation as one of its two genetic host factors alongside G6PD deficiency.
Trap: (B) is the mirror error of the previous item. Slow acetylation is the accumulation side of this pair; rapid clearance and subtherapeutic levels belong to the fast acetylator.

Subtopic: racial and ethnic differences in drug response

Q: A Black patient with newly diagnosed hypertension is started on drug therapy. According to the chapter's cultural implications box, which statement reflects the expected response?
A) Response is best to beta blockers and poorest to calcium channel blockers
B) Response is better to diuretics than to beta blockers and ACE inhibitors, and best to calcium channel blockers, especially diltiazem
C) Response to single-drug therapy is better than to combination therapy
D) Response to thiazide diuretics is poorer than in White patients
Answer: B
Rationale: The box states that Black patients respond "better to diuretics than to beta blockers and angiotensin-converting enzyme inhibitors," "less effectively to beta blockers," "best to calcium channel blockers, especially diltiazem," and "less effectively to single-drug therapy." The body text reinforces the diuretic half separately: thiazide diuretics "appear to be more effective in Blacks than in Whites."
Trap: (A) and (D) each invert exactly one claim in a four-claim row, which is all a distractor has to do here. (C) reverses the single-drug statement. Hold the row as four separate claims rather than one impression.

Q: A psychiatric unit reviews the chapter's box on varying drug responses in different racial or ethnic groups. Which statements are supported by that box? Select all that apply.
A) Asian patients may need lower dosages of certain drugs such as haloperidol
B) Asian and Hispanic patients respond better to lower dosages of antidepressants
C) Japanese patients require higher dosages of antimanic drugs
D) Chinese patients require lower dosages of antipsychotics
E) The comparison group for all of these responses is White patients
Answer: A, B, D, E
Rationale: The box lists lower haloperidol dosages for Asian patients, better response to lower antidepressant dosages for Asian and Hispanic patients, and lower antipsychotic dosages for Chinese patients. Its footnote reads, "Note: The comparison group for all responses is Whites." The body text adds that Japanese and Taiwanese patients may require lower dosages of lithium.
Trap: (C) is the one option taken from the wrong side of its row. The box says Japanese patients "require lower dosages of antimanic drugs." Reading these rows for drug class while skipping the direction word is what loses this item, and an option that states an absolute dose requirement rather than a relative one has also dropped the comparator that makes the claim mean anything.

Topic: cultural assessment in drug therapy
Subtopic: assessment before administration

Q: A nurse is preparing to give a first dose to a patient who recently immigrated. Which action does the chapter direct before that medication is administered?
A) Apply the health beliefs listed for the patient's cultural group in the chapter's table of common practices
B) Withhold the drug until a family member confirms what the patient believes
C) Complete a cultural assessment covering language needs, health beliefs, past medicine use, herbal and home remedies, over-the-counter drugs, religious practices, community support, and dietary habits
D) Document that a cultural assessment is not indicated for a first dose
Answer: C
Rationale: The chapter's checklist directs a thorough cultural assessment before any medication is administered, covering languages spoken and the need for an interpreter, health beliefs and practices, past uses of medicine, herbal and folk and home remedies or supplements, over-the-counter drug use, usual responses to illness, responsiveness to medical treatment, religious practices and beliefs, support from the patient's cultural community, and dietary habits.
Trap: (A) is the assumption the chapter explicitly forbids in the most important sentence of the section: "consult with the patient rather than assume that the patient holds certain beliefs because he or she belongs to a certain ethnic group." The table exists to describe variation, not to predict an individual.

Q: An older adult Asian patient is admitted for drug therapy. Which cultural issue does the chapter tie most directly to that therapy?
A) Hospital diets will be unacceptable to this patient
B) The use of herbal products may be an important practice for this patient
C) X-ray studies will be refused by this patient
D) Hospitalization will be valued mainly as an opportunity for socialization
Answer: B
Rationale: The assessment checklist names "use of herbal treatments, folk remedies, home remedies, or supplements" as a required question, and the body text warns that alternative therapies such as herbal and homeopathic remedies "can inhibit or accelerate drug metabolism" and therefore alter a drug's response. The chapter also notes that a diet high in fat "has been documented to increase the absorption of some drugs."
Trap: (A), (C), and (D) are group-level assumptions rather than assessment findings, and none has a pharmacologic mechanism attached to it. The herbal option is the only one the chapter connects to drug metabolism.

Subtopic: communication and teaching across cultures

Q: A nurse is writing discharge medication instructions for a patient whose first language is Chinese. Which approach does the chapter specifically recommend?
A) Use contractions so the instructions stay short and conversational
B) Give general instructions and let the patient work out the specifics
C) Rely on a family member to translate rather than requesting an interpreter
D) Write "cannot," "will not," and "do not" instead of the contractions "can't," "won't," and "don't"
Answer: D
Rationale: The chapter notes that some languages, such as Chinese, "do not have numerous verb tenses" compared with English, so "very precise instructions must be included in patient education," and it states that avoiding contractions "is important with patients from other countries to prevent confusion."
Trap: (A) inverts the chapter's own instruction, and it pulls because plain conversational English is usually the teaching advice. Here precision outranks informality. (B) breaks the same requirement for very precise instructions from the other side.

Q: A unit adopts Leininger's Sunrise Model to structure its cultural assessments. How many major areas does the chapter say this model covers?
A) Four, matching the columns of the chapter's table of common cultural practices
B) Nine, matching the provisions of the ANA Code of Ethics
C) Seven, including educational, economic, familial and social, political, technologic, religious and philosophic, and cultural values, beliefs, and practices
D) Three, matching the bodies that regulate drug therapy
Answer: C
Rationale: Box 4.3 states that the Sunrise Model "focuses on seven major areas of cultural assessment, including educational, economic, familial and social, political, technologic, religious and philosophic, and cultural values, beliefs, and practices." Other tools are named in the same box by author and year only.
Trap: (B) borrows the nine provisions of the ANA Code of Ethics, and (D) borrows the FDA, DEA, and state law trio. Numbers lifted from a different list in the same chapter are the standard distractor for any counted item here.

Topic: us drug legislation timeline
Subtopic: the tragedies and the acts they produced

Q: Over 100 people died in the United States after ingesting a sulfanilamide preparation made with diethylene glycol, a component of automobile antifreeze, which had never been tested for toxicity. Which legislation did this produce, and what did it require?
A) The Kefauver-Harris Amendments of 1962, requiring proof of therapeutic efficacy
B) The Federal Food, Drug, and Cosmetic Act of 1938, requiring data proving drug safety and establishing the investigational new drug application process
C) The Durham-Humphrey Amendment of 1951, establishing legend drugs
D) The Controlled Substance Act of 1970, establishing the schedules
Answer: B
Rationale: Table 4.1 credits the Federal Food, Drug, and Cosmetic Act of 1938 with requiring manufacturers to "provide data proving drug safety" with FDA review and with establishing the investigational new drug application process, and names the sulfanilamide elixir tragedy as its prompt.
Trap: (A) is the reflex pick for anyone who remembers only that a tragedy produced a law. The pairing is chronological and it is the mnemonic: the earlier disaster produced the earlier, weaker requirement, which was safety alone.

Q: Pregnant women in Europe who took thalidomide, a sedative-hypnotic, gave birth to seriously deformed infants. Which US legislation followed, and what did it add?
A) The Sherley Amendment of 1912, prohibiting fraudulent claims
B) The Orphan Drug Act of 1983, promoting research on drugs for rare diseases
C) The Federal Food, Drug, and Cosmetic Act of 1938, establishing the investigational new drug application process
D) The Kefauver-Harris Amendments of 1962, requiring manufacturers to demonstrate both therapeutic efficacy and safety
Answer: D
Rationale: Table 4.1 pairs the Kefauver-Harris Amendments with the thalidomide tragedy and states they required manufacturers to "demonstrate both therapeutic efficacy and safety" of new drugs.
Trap: (C) is the mirror error of the previous item. An option crediting Kefauver-Harris with the investigational new drug process has the two acts crossed; that process came from the 1938 act, and efficacy is what 1962 added on top of safety.

Subtopic: labeling, privacy, and supplements

Q: A drug label carries the statement "Caution—Federal law prohibits dispensing without a prescription." Which act required that legend, and what does the term legend drug mean?
A) The Durham-Humphrey Amendment of 1951, and legend drug means prescription drug
B) The Durham-Humphrey Amendment of 1951, and legend drug means nonprescription drug
C) The Federal Food and Drugs Act of 1906, and legend drug means a drug with addicting potential
D) The Harrison Narcotic Act of 1914, and legend drug means a narcotic
Answer: A
Rationale: Table 4.1 states that the Durham-Humphrey Amendment "established legend drugs or prescription drugs" and required the quoted legend on the label. The key term gives legend drugs as "another name for prescription drugs," while over-the-counter drugs are the ones available without a prescription.
Trap: (B) keeps the correct act and flips the definition, which is the error the word itself invites. "Legend" here means the printed caution required on the label, not "legendary" or "listed."

Q: A caller identifying himself as a hospitalized patient's grandson telephones the unit and asks for her diagnosis and prognosis. Which understanding guides the nurse's response?
A) The privacy rule applies only to written records, so verbal information may be shared with family
B) Diagnosis may be released but payment information may not
C) HIPAA requires health care providers, insurers, public health authorities, employers, and schools to protect individually identifying health information, and severe fines may follow a breach
D) HIPAA binds only clinicians, so information a school or employer holds may be released freely
Answer: C
Rationale: The chapter states HIPAA "requires all health care providers, health and life insurance companies, public health authorities, employers, and schools to maintain patient privacy regarding protected health information," which "includes any individually identifying information such as patients' health conditions, account numbers, prescription numbers, medications, and payment information." The key term adds that if confidentiality is breached, "severe fines may be imposed."
Trap: (D) narrows the covered entities down to clinicians, which is the usual misreading of HIPAA. The chapter's own list names employers and schools right alongside providers, which is why this act reaches so much further than the chart.

Q: A patient brings in an herbal product whose label reads "For depression." What does the Dietary Supplement Health and Education Act require of that product's manufacturer?
A) To prove both safety and efficacy before marketing, as for any prescription drug
B) At least to ensure the product's safety, though not necessarily its efficacy, and to make no unsubstantiated claims such as "Known to cure depression"
C) To prove efficacy only, since supplements are presumed safe
D) Nothing, because dietary supplements are entirely unregulated
Answer: B
Rationale: The chapter states the 1994 act requires manufacturers "at least to ensure their safety (although not necessarily their efficacy)" and prohibits "any unsubstantiated claims" in product labeling, and gives this exact worked example: a label "may read 'For depression' but cannot read 'Known to cure depression.'" Chapter 7 develops this framework further.
Trap: (A) applies the drug standard to a supplement, which is the reflex assumption. The FDA has less regulatory authority over vitamin, herbal, and homeopathic preparations precisely because they are designated dietary supplements rather than drugs.

Topic: clinical drug trial phases
Subtopic: who is enrolled in each phase

Q: A pharmaceutical company begins the first human testing of a new antihypertensive. Who receives the drug, and what is being determined?
A) Small numbers of volunteers who have hypertension, to determine effectiveness
B) Large numbers of patients with hypertension, to detect rare adverse effects
C) The general population after marketing, to compare the drug against an existing agent
D) Small numbers of healthy subjects, to determine the optimal dosage range and the pharmacokinetics of the drug
Answer: D
Rationale: Phase I uses "small numbers of healthy subjects rather than those who have the disease" and is designed "to determine the optimal dosage range and the pharmacokinetics of the drug and to ascertain if further testing is needed." Blood tests, urinalyses, vital signs, and specific monitoring tests are performed. All of this follows preclinical in vitro and animal studies, which rule many compounds out before any human is dosed.
Trap: (A) is the reflex pick, and it is the single most counterintuitive fact in the sequence: the first humans to receive a new drug do not have the disease it treats. Volunteers who have the disease enter at phase II.

Q: The same drug moves on to a study enrolling a small number of volunteers who actually have hypertension. What is this phase determining?
A) The optimal dosage range and pharmacokinetics in healthy volunteers
B) The drug's effectiveness and any adverse effects, with therapeutic dosage ranges refined
C) Infrequent or rare adverse effects in large numbers of patients
D) Postmarketing comparison against another drug in the same therapeutic category
Answer: B
Rationale: Phase II uses "small numbers of volunteers who have the disease that the drug is designed to diagnose or treat," to "determine the drug's effectiveness and identify any adverse effects," and "therapeutic dosage ranges are refined during this phase."
Trap: (A) is the mirror error of the previous item. The switch from healthy subjects to subjects who have the disease is the boundary between phases I and II, and the change in purpose follows from it.

Subtopic: reaching the market and staying watched

Q: A drug is being studied in large numbers of patients followed by medical research centers, with objectives of establishing clinical effectiveness, safety, and dosage range. What happens when this phase is complete?
A) The manufacturer submits an investigational new drug application so that human testing may begin
B) The drug automatically enters a further trial phase before any marketing can occur
C) The manufacturer submits a new drug application, and the drug goes on the market if the FDA approves it
D) The drug is marketed immediately with no further FDA review
Answer: C
Rationale: Phase III uses large numbers of patients and provides information about "infrequent or rare adverse effects," with the three objectives of establishing "clinical effectiveness, safety, and dosage range." After phase III the FDA receives a report and the company submits a new drug application, and the drug is put on the market once that application is approved.
Trap: (A) swaps the two applications, which is the most common error here because both are applications to the same agency. The investigational new drug application comes first and permits study in humans; the new drug application comes after phase III and permits marketing.

Q: Six months after a drug reaches the market, reports of severe adverse effects never seen during the trials begin arriving. Which phase does this belong to, and why did the effects appear only now?
A) Phase III, because rare adverse effects are its stated purpose
B) Phase II, because adverse effects are identified there
C) Phase I, because pharmacokinetics were established there
D) Phase IV, because these are postmarketing studies and severe effects may appear for the first time once a drug is used in the larger general population
Answer: D
Rationale: Phase IV consists of "postmarketing studies that are voluntarily conducted by pharmaceutical companies to obtain further proof of the therapeutic and adverse effects," with data gathered "for at least 2 years after the drug's release," often comparing the new drug with another in the same therapeutic category. The chapter notes that some drugs pass every trial phase without problems and then produce severe adverse effects in the general population.
Trap: (A) pulls because phase III is the phase whose stated purpose is infrequent or rare adverse effects. Timing settles it: anything happening after the drug is on sale is by definition after phase III.

Topic: investigational study design and informed consent
Subtopic: blinding and bias

Q: In a drug study, the subjects do not know whether they are receiving the investigational drug or the placebo, but the investigator does know. What is this design called, and whose bias does it minimize?
A) A double-blind investigational drug study, minimizing bias on the part of both investigator and subjects
B) An open-label study, which minimizes no bias at all
C) A blinded investigational drug study, minimizing bias on the part of research subjects
D) A postmarketing study, minimizing investigator bias
Answer: C
Rationale: The key term defines a blinded study as one in which "the subjects are purposely unaware" of whether they receive the drug or a placebo, and states that this "serves to minimize bias on the part of research subjects." The body text confirms that in a blinded study the subject does not know but "the investigator does know."
Trap: (A) is the reflex pick because both terms contain the word blind. Count who is unaware: one party is blinded, two parties is double-blind.

Q: In a second study, neither the research staff nor the subjects know which subjects receive the real drug. What is this design called, and what does it add?
A) A double-blind investigational drug study, minimizing bias on the part of both the investigator and the subject
B) A blinded investigational drug study, minimizing bias on the part of the subject only
C) A placebo-free design, since blinding removes the need for a placebo
D) A preclinical design, since the researchers are not yet involved
Answer: A
Rationale: The key term defines a double-blind study as one in which "both the investigator(s) and the subjects" are purposely unaware, minimizing bias "on the part of both." Dosage forms often look identical except for a secret code, which is revealed at the end of the study and can be broken earlier by the principal investigator in a clinical emergency.
Trap: (B) is the mirror error of the previous item. Both definitions turn on the word bias, which is why the chapter gives bias its own key term, defined as "any systematic error in a measurement process."

Subtopic: what informed consent requires

Q: A nurse is asked to obtain informed consent for a drug study. Which set of requirements matches the chapter?
A) Explain purpose, procedures, benefits, and risks; the document is written in a language the patient understands and is dated and signed by the patient and at least one witness
B) Explain purpose and benefits only; the document is signed by the patient alone
C) Explain risks only; the document is signed by the prescriber and a witness
D) Explain purpose, procedures, benefits, and risks; the document is signed once therapy has begun
Answer: A
Rationale: The chapter requires that the "purpose of the study, the procedures to be used, the possible benefits, and the risks involved" be explained, and that the document be "written in a language understood by the patient" and "dated and signed by the patient and at least one witness." Obtaining consent may be carried out by a nurse or other health care professional depending on how the study is designed.
Trap: (D) keeps all four content elements intact and breaks only the timing, which is the half people stop reading. Consent "must be obtained more than a given number of days or hours before certain procedures are performed and must always be obtained when the patient is fully mentally competent."

Q: A patient enrolled in an investigational drug study tells the nurse she wants to stop participating. Which response reflects the chapter?
A) Once therapy has begun she cannot withdraw until the study period ends
B) Any individual may decline to participate or quit the study at any time, without affecting delivery of any previously agreed-upon health care services
C) Withdrawing means she forfeits the right to bring any future legal action
D) Withdrawal requires the prescriber's written approval
Answer: B
Rationale: Informed consent "is always voluntary," participants must be "informed volunteers and not be coerced to participate in any way," and any individual "can either decline to participate or quit the study at any time without affecting the delivery of any previously agreed-upon health care services."
Trap: (A) and (C) both contradict the chapter directly, and both appear among the distractors in its own review question on informed consent. Consent is a permission that can be withdrawn, not a waiver of the patient's rights.

Q: A patient with a life-threatening illness asks why some drugs reach patients faster than others. Which explanation matches the chapter?
A) Expedited approval skips the preclinical phase so human testing can start sooner
B) Expedited approval was created for the COVID-19 pandemic and has no earlier precedent
C) Expedited approval removes the need for a new drug application entirely
D) Expedited approval accelerates the usual process for life-threatening diseases; AIDS was the first major public health crisis for which the FDA granted it, allowing use of drugs that showed promise during early phase I and phase II trials
Answer: D
Rationale: The key term defines expedited drug approval as "acceleration of the usual investigational new drug approval process by the FDA, usually for drugs used to treat life-threatening diseases," and the chapter states that AIDS "was the first major public health crisis for which the FDA began granting expedited drug approval." The rofecoxib recall in 2004 later made the process controversial and reduced the number of drugs approved through it.
Trap: (B) picks the most recent example the chapter names. COVID-19 vaccines and drugs were approved under Emergency Use Authorization, and even there the chapter states the product "must first be proven safe and effective."

Topic: drug safety warnings and recalls
Subtopic: black box warnings

Q: A newly hired nurse reads that a drug on the unit carries a black box warning and asks whether the drug has been taken off the market. What is the accurate answer?
A) The drug has been withdrawn and may no longer be dispensed
B) The warning applies only to investigational drugs still in trials
C) The drug can still be prescribed; the black box warning is the strictest FDA warning, indicating that serious adverse effects have been reported, so the prescriber must be aware of the risk and the patient must be warned
D) The drug may now be given only under an approved protocol
Answer: C
Rationale: A black box warning is "the strictest warning from the FDA," indicating "that serious adverse effects have been reported" with the drug. The chapter states plainly, "The drug can still be prescribed," though the prescriber must be aware of the potential risk and the patient must be warned. The warning text is bordered in solid black, which is where the name comes from.
Trap: (A) confuses the warning with the withdrawal mechanism. Removal from the market is a recall; a black box warning is a warning, not a withdrawal. (D) borrows the dispensing restriction that belongs to schedule I controlled substances.

Subtopic: recall classes

Q: A drug product is recalled because its use carries a reasonable probability of serious adverse health effects or death. Which class is this, and where does it sit on the scale?
A) Class III, the most serious type of recall
B) Class II, because the effects may be temporary or medically reversible
C) Class I, the least serious type of recall
D) Class I, the most serious type of recall
Answer: D
Rationale: A class I recall is "the most serious type of recall," in which use "carries a reasonable probability of serious adverse health effects or death." Class II is less severe, with temporary or medically reversible effects and a low probability of lasting major harm, and class III is least severe.
Trap: (A) inverts the direction of the scale, which is worth guarding against because plenty of clinical severity scales run the other way. Recall classes run from I as the worst down to III, the same direction as the controlled substance schedules.

Q: A manufacturer recalls a product whose use is not likely to result in any significant health problems. Which class is this?
A) Class III
B) Class I
C) Class II
D) Class IV
Answer: A
Rationale: Class III is the least severe recall class, defined as use that "is not likely to result in any significant health problems." The chapter gives three recall classes, not four.
Trap: (B) is the mirror error of the previous item. (D) invents a fourth class by borrowing the length of the five-schedule controlled substance list; the recall system stops at three.

Q: A nurse observes an unexpected adverse event with a recently approved drug. Which program does the chapter name for reporting it, and what kind of program is it?
A) MedWatch, a mandatory federal reporting system carrying penalties for nonreporting
B) MedWatch, a voluntary program in which professionals are encouraged to report any adverse events seen with newly approved drugs
C) A required postmarketing trial the nurse must enroll the patient into
D) The Drug Enforcement Agency, which collects all adverse event reports
Answer: B
Rationale: MedWatch is described as "a voluntary program in which professionals are encouraged to report any adverse events seen with newly approved drugs."
Trap: (A) keeps the right program and makes it mandatory, which is how most people assume drug safety reporting must work. The chapter's word is voluntary, the same word it uses for the phase IV postmarketing studies that gather the same kind of information.

Topic: controlled substance schedules
Subtopic: what separates the schedules

Q: Two controlled substances both carry high abuse potential and severe physical and psychological dependency potential, yet they sit in different schedules. What separates them?
A) The schedule I drug has a higher abuse potential than the schedule II drug
B) The schedule I drug produces only psychological dependency
C) The schedule II drug may be sold over the counter in some states
D) The schedule I drug has no currently accepted medical use, while the schedule II drug has an accepted medical use
Answer: D
Rationale: Table 4.2 gives both schedule I and schedule II as high abuse potential with severe physical and psychological dependency; medical use is the only column that differs, listing none for schedule I and accepted for schedule II. The body text states that schedule I drugs have "no currently accepted medical use and a high potential for abuse" while schedule II drugs have "a medical use and a high potential for abuse."
Trap: (A) assumes every step down the ladder must also lower abuse potential, which is true of every pair except this top one. Read Table 4.2 across the row, not down the column. (C) belongs to schedule V.

Q: A nursing student asks how to tell a schedule III drug from a schedule IV drug by its dispensing rules. What should the nurse explain?
A) Schedule III allows five refills and schedule IV allows none
B) The dispensing rules cannot distinguish them, because both allow a written or oral prescription that expires in 6 months with no more than five refills; only abuse and dependency potential differ
C) Schedule IV expires in 6 months while schedule III expires in 12 months
D) Schedule III requires a written prescription only, the same as schedule II
Answer: B
Rationale: Table 4.3 gives schedule III and schedule IV identical dispensing restrictions: "written or oral prescription that expires in 6 months; no more than five refills in a 6-month period; container must have warning label." Table 4.2 is what separates them, with abuse potential less than schedule II for the one and less than schedule III for the other.
Trap: (A) manufactures a distinction where the tables give none, which is what a test taker does when a question seems to demand that two adjacent categories differ on the axis being asked about. Here the honest answer is that this axis does not separate them.

Subtopic: dispensing restrictions in practice

Q: A prescriber telephones an order for oxycodone to the unit and asks that it be refillable twice. Which response is correct?
A) A schedule II drug may be telephoned in routinely and refilled up to five times in 6 months
B) A schedule II drug may be refilled but never telephoned in
C) A schedule II drug requires a written prescription with no refills; telephoning is permitted for major emergencies only, and a written prescription is then required within 72 hours
D) A schedule II drug may be telephoned in, and the prescription then expires in 6 months
Answer: C
Rationale: Table 4.3 gives schedule II restrictions as "written prescription only; no prescription refills," with a container warning label, and its footnote states such drugs are "legally permitted to be telephoned in for major emergencies only. If telephoned in, written prescription is required within 72 hours."
Trap: (A) applies the schedule III and IV rules one rung too high. Five refills in 6 months belongs to the schedules below; no refills at all is the schedule II signature, and this is the order to question.

Q: One patient is discharged on a codeine and acetaminophen combination product and another on codeine alone. How are the two scheduled?
A) The combination is schedule II and codeine alone is schedule III
B) Both are schedule II, because both contain codeine
C) The combination is schedule III and codeine alone is schedule II
D) Both are schedule V, because combination opioid products contain limited quantities
Answer: C
Rationale: Table 4.3 lists codeine among the schedule II examples and "codeine with selected other medications (e.g., acetaminophen)" among the schedule III examples. Chapter 10 states the same from the drug side: the combination "is classified as a Schedule III controlled substance," while codeine not combined with other drugs "is classified as a Schedule II."
Trap: (A) is the reflex inversion, because adding a second drug feels as though it ought to raise the level of control. The combination product sits at the lower schedule, not the higher one.

Q: A patient asks how an antitussive containing a small amount of an opioid can be sold without a prescription in her state. What explains this?
A) Antitussives are never classified as controlled substances
B) Every schedule V drug is prescription-only nationwide, so the product must be mislabeled
C) The product was moved to schedule IV, which permits over-the-counter sale
D) Schedule V drugs may be dispensed by written prescription or over the counter depending on state law, and consist of preparations containing limited quantities of certain opioids
Answer: D
Rationale: Table 4.3 gives schedule V dispensing as "written prescription or over the counter (varies with state law)," with examples being medications "generally for relief of coughs or diarrhea containing limited quantities of certain opioid controlled substances." The body text adds that schedule V drugs have lower potential for abuse than schedule IV.
Trap: (B) reflects the widespread assumption that "controlled substance" always means "requires a prescription." The lowest schedule is the exception, and the chapter also notes that a drug such as gabapentin may carry a higher schedule in some states than in others.

Q: A nursing student reviews the controlled substance schedules. Which statements are accurate? Select all that apply.
A) There are five classes of controlled substances, designated from C-I to C-V
B) Schedule I drugs may be dispensed only with an approved protocol
C) In 2014 hydrocodone was rescheduled from schedule III to schedule II
D) Schedule IV examples include phenobarbital, benzodiazepines such as diazepam and lorazepam, and tramadol
E) Schedule III drugs may be dispensed over the counter depending on state law
Answer: A, B, C, D
Rationale: The chapter states that the Controlled Substance Act "requires the scheduling of every controlled drug" and that there are "five classes of controlled substances, designated from C-I to C-V." Table 4.3 gives "only with approved protocol" for schedule I and lists phenobarbital, benzodiazepines, and tramadol among the schedule IV examples, and the chapter records that in 2014 hydrocodone "was rescheduled from C-III to C-II," which Chapter 10 confirms.
Trap: (E) moves the over-the-counter provision up two rungs. That provision belongs to schedule V alone, and only as state law allows. Note also that the 2014 change made hydrocodone stricter even though its schedule number went down, which is the direction most people reverse.

Topic: nursing liability and malpractice elements
Subtopic: negligence versus malpractice

Q: A nurse fails to give the care a reasonably prudent nurse would render under similar circumstances, and a patient is harmed. Which term names this, and why?
A) Negligence only, because malpractice applies solely to physicians
B) Fraud, because a professional standard was breached
C) Malpractice, because it is a special type of negligence committed by a professional with specialized education and training
D) Neither, because a licensed nurse is not held to a legal standard of care
Answer: C
Rationale: Negligence is "the failure to act in a reasonable and prudent manner" or failure of the nurse to give the care that a "reasonably prudent (cautious) nurse would render or use under similar circumstances." Malpractice is "a special type of negligence" or the failure of a professional with specialized education and training to act in a reasonable and prudent way. Every malpractice case is negligence; not every negligence case is malpractice.
Trap: (A) restricts malpractice to medicine, which is the common lay understanding of the word. The definition turns on specialized education and training, not on which profession holds it.

Q: A visitor in the hallway fails to act in a reasonable and prudent manner and a patient is injured. Which term applies?
A) Negligence, but not malpractice
B) Malpractice, because harm resulted
C) Malpractice, because it happened inside a health care facility
D) Neither, because only documented acts create liability
Answer: A
Rationale: Negligence is the failure to act in a reasonable and prudent manner, and nothing in that definition requires professional status. Malpractice is the narrower subset committed by "a professional and/or individual with specialized education and training."
Trap: (B) is the mirror error of the previous item: harm alone does not convert a failure into malpractice, the actor's professional standing does. (C) substitutes the setting for the actor, which is the same mistake in different clothing.

Subtopic: elements and categories of liability

Q: A nurse fails to notice an infiltrated IV of a caustic drug, and a second nurse catches it before any tissue injury occurs. Which element of malpractice liability is missing?
A) Duty, because the second nurse took over responsibility
B) Breach of duty, because the error was corrected in time
C) Causation, because monitoring an IV site is not the nurse's duty
D) Damage, because no harm resulted
Answer: D
Rationale: The four elements are duty, breach of duty, causation, and damage, and all must be present. The chapter's own worked example runs duty as accurate assessment and monitoring of the IV site with timely reporting of changes, breach as not noticing that the site is swollen, red, painful, and warm, causation as failing to note extravasation that results in the need for skin grafting, and damage as extensive skin and nerve damage with limited use of the arm.
Trap: (B) treats an error that was caught as no breach at all. The breach happened; what is absent is the harm at the end of the chain, and without damage the set is incomplete.

Q: A nurse charts that a scheduled dose was given, but the dose was never administered. Under the chapter's areas of potential liability, how is this classified?
A) A medication error, because a dose was omitted
B) Fraud, because documentation was falsified and care that was documented was not provided
C) Failure to assess, because the patient was not monitored afterward
D) Failure to ensure safety, because monitoring was inadequate
Answer: B
Rationale: Box 4.2 lists fraud as its own category, separate from medication errors, and gives as its examples "falsification of documentation on the medication profile or patient's record" and "failure to provide the nursing care that was documented."
Trap: (A) is the reflex pick because a missed dose is the textbook medication error. The falsified entry is what moves this into a different category with different consequences: an error is a failure of practice, fraud is a falsification of the record.

Q: A hospital is preparing for accreditation and the staff nurses ask why this concerns them. Which statements match the chapter? Select all that apply.
A) The Joint Commission, the Healthcare Facilities Accreditation Program, and Det Norske Veritas all accredit health care facilities
B) The Joint Commission has for years been the number one choice for hospital accreditation, focusing on leadership standards and quality management
C) Det Norske Veritas emerged in 2008 and is seen as more facility friendly, with a more quality-management approach
D) Nurses should understand the credentialing process at their place of employment because of their priority of monitoring standard of patient care
E) These accrediting bodies issue the individual nurse's license and define her scope of practice
Answer: A, B, C, D
Rationale: The chapter names all three bodies, describes The Joint Commission as the number one choice with a focus on leadership standards and quality management, dates Det Norske Veritas to 2008 and calls it more "facility friendly," and says nurses need to understand both their own licensing and nurse practice acts and the credentialing of their employer because of their priority of monitoring standard of patient care.
Trap: (E) is the option from the wrong side of the state-versus-facility line, and it is the distinction worth holding: nurse practice acts are state law overseen by state boards of nursing, and they are what define scope of practice, while accreditation applies to the institution. Drug scheduling and approval, by contrast, are federal.

Topic: nursing ethical principles and placebo use
Subtopic: the six principles

Q: A nurse withholds an intervention that would cause a patient avoidable harm. Which ethical principle is she acting on?
A) Beneficence
B) Justice
C) Nonmaleficence
D) Veracity
Answer: C
Rationale: Nonmaleficence is "the duty to do no harm to a patient," and the ethical terms box gives its related nursing action as "avoiding doing any deliberate harm while rendering nursing care."
Trap: (A) is the reflex pick because the two words sit one Latin element apart and appear in the same box. Nonmaleficence is the negative duty to refrain from harm; beneficence is the positive duty to actively promote good.

Q: A nurse determines how a patient is best served and actively arranges that care. Which principle is this?
A) Nonmaleficence
B) Beneficence
C) Confidentiality
D) Autonomy
Answer: B
Rationale: Beneficence is "the ethical principle of doing or actively promoting good," with its related nursing action given as "determining how the patient is best served."
Trap: (A) is the mirror error of the previous item. Doing good and refraining from harm are separate duties, which is why the box lists them as two principles rather than one.

Q: A patient with late-stage cancer says he wants to live to see his granddaughter's graduation but wants no extra treatments after that, and the nurse supports his decision. Which principle is being upheld?
A) Veracity
B) Beneficence
C) Maleficence
D) Autonomy
Answer: D
Rationale: Autonomy is "self-determination and the ability to act on one's own," and its related nursing actions include "promoting a patient's decision making," "supporting informed consent," and assisting in decisions or making a decision when a patient is posing harm to himself or herself.
Trap: (C) is not a principle in this chapter at all: the principle is nonmaleficence, and this distractor simply drops the negation. It appears among the options in the chapter's own review question on this scenario, which is why it is worth recognizing as a non-word rather than reasoning about it.

Q: A patient enrolled in a drug study asks the nurse directly whether he is receiving a placebo. Which principle does the chapter attach most specifically to this situation?
A) Justice
B) Nonmaleficence
C) Confidentiality
D) Veracity
Answer: D
Rationale: Veracity is "the duty to tell the truth," and the box gives its related nursing action as "telling the truth with regard to placebos, investigational new drugs, and informed consent." All three of the chapter's truth-telling situations are gathered under this one principle.
Trap: (A) and (B) are principles from the same box, which is what makes them plausible, but neither is the one the chapter names by these exact words. Autonomy is also touched by this scenario, yet veracity is the principle the box ties to placebos.

Q: A unit reviews the chapter's ethical terms box. Which pairings of principle and related nursing action are as the chapter gives them? Select all that apply.
A) Justice, with ensuring fairness in distributing resources for patient care and determining when to treat
B) Confidentiality, with not talking about a patient in public or outside the context of the health care setting
C) Autonomy, with assisting in decisions or making a decision when a patient is posing harm to himself or herself
D) Beneficence, with determining how the patient is best served
E) Veracity, with avoiding doing any deliberate harm while rendering nursing care
Answer: A, B, C, D
Rationale: The ethical terms box gives each of these four pairings, defining justice as "being fair or equal in one's actions" and confidentiality as "the duty to respect privileged information about a patient."
Trap: (E) attaches nonmaleficence's related action to veracity. Avoiding deliberate harm belongs to the do-no-harm principle; veracity's action is telling the truth about placebos, investigational new drugs, and informed consent.

Subtopic: placebo orders outside research

Q: A prescriber orders a placebo for a hospitalized patient who is not enrolled in any drug study. What is the nurse's appropriate action?
A) Administer it, since a placebo has no pharmacologic activity and therefore cannot harm the patient
B) Refuse to discuss the order with the prescriber and document the refusal
C) Inquire about the order and ask why a placebo is being prescribed, since this is within the legal purview of a professional nurse and the order must never be taken lightly
D) Administer it and tell the patient it is a strong new medication
Answer: C
Rationale: The chapter states that if an order is received for a placebo, "it is within the legal purview of a professional nurse to inquire about the order and to ask why a placebo is being prescribed; the order must never be taken lightly." Except in new drug studies, placebo use "is often considered to be unethical and deceitful, possibly creating mistrust" among nurse, prescriber, and patient, and the American Pain Society and the Agency for Health Care Policy and Research recommend avoiding placebos.
Trap: (A) reasons from the placebo's inertness straight to its harmlessness, which misses the objection entirely: the harm the chapter names is deception and mistrust, not pharmacologic injury. (D) is that deception carried out, and it violates veracity outright.
