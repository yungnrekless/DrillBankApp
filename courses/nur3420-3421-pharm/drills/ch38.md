# Chapter 38 drills — Antibiotics Part 1

Authored from `sources/Ch38_Fact_Bank.md` (Lilley, Collins & Snyder, 10th ed.).

Blueprint weight is **18 questions of 121 (14.9%), depth `full`** — the
second-heaviest chapter in the whole Lilley blueprint, behind only Ch 10
(Analgesic Drugs, 19), and front-loaded into Exam 1 (15 items on Exam 1, 3 on
the Final).

That earns **74 drills**, the top of the `full` band (55 to 75), which is where
a chapter with this weight belongs. The chapter also carries more enumerable
structure than any other in this part of the book — a 26-term vocabulary box,
four penicillin subclasses, five cephalosporin generations, four carbapenems,
and a class-by-class administration table — and every one of those lists is a
ready-made option set for an item writer.

Topics follow the chapter's own sequence: the general antibiotic frame first
(infection terminology, the three uses, the four mechanisms, host factors),
then class by class in the order the chapter presents them, then superinfection,
which is the chapter's own objective 10 and cuts across every class.

Every distinction the chapter states in both directions is drilled from both
ends: colonization and infection, gram positive and gram negative, community-
and health care-associated, empiric and prophylactic, cidal and static, 50S and
30S, beta-lactamase and its inhibitor, penicillin G and penicillin V,
amoxicillin and ampicillin, the generation trend and the cefepime exception,
and the two numbered senses of superinfection. Inverting those is the reflex
error this chapter produces.

Nothing here is filled from the GAPS list: the chapter never lists specific
antiseptics and disinfectants (deferred to Ch 56), so no drill asks for them,
and the obsolete pregnancy letter categories are not drilled at all.

Topic: infection terminology and gram stain
Subtopic: colonization versus infection

Q: A nurse reviews a swab result from a patient's pressure injury, which has grown Staphylococcus aureus. The patient is afebrile, the wound has no redness, drainage, or pain, and the white blood cell count is normal. What should the nurse anticipate?
A) No antibiotic will be started, because bacteria growing without overt signs is colonization
B) A broad-spectrum antibiotic will be started immediately because the culture is positive
C) A narrow-spectrum antibiotic will be started because the organism has been identified
D) The wound will be recultured because the first result must be a contaminant
Answer: A
Rationale: Colonization is the establishment and growth of microorganisms on the skin, open wounds, or mucous membranes or in secretions without causing an infection, and in colonization "these patients do not have any overt signs of infection." The chapter states plainly that colonization does not require antibiotic treatment, and that treating it anyway "may be one way in which drug-resistant organisms emerge."
Trap: (C) is the reflex pick because a named organism looks like an invitation to switch to definitive therapy, and (B) pulls for the same reason with less discipline. Definitive therapy still needs an infection to treat; a positive culture by itself proves only that the organism is present.

Q: Two days later the same pressure injury is red and swollen, the patient has a fever with chills, purulent drainage is present, and the white blood cell count is elevated. How should the nurse interpret this change?
A) Colonization, which still does not require antibiotic treatment
B) Infection, because microorganisms are now invading and multiplying in body tissue
C) A superinfection arising from the wound flora
D) An expected inflammatory response to daily dressing changes
Answer: B
Rationale: Infections are invasions and multiplications of microorganisms in body tissues. The chapter's classic signs and symptoms are "fever, chills, sweats, redness, pain and swelling, fatigue, weight loss, increased white blood cell (WBC) count, and the formation of pus," and this patient now has most of them.
Trap: (A) is the mirror error of the previous item. The same organism in the same wound crossed from colonization into infection when the overt signs appeared, so the presence of bacteria is never the discriminator — the signs are.

Q: A culture report describes an organism that stained red, has two cell membranes, and has a smaller outer capsule and peptidoglycan layer. What should the nurse expect about treating it?
A) It is gram positive, and drug molecules generally penetrate it easily
B) It is gram negative, and infections with it are generally more difficult to treat
C) It is gram positive, so a narrow-spectrum drug will be sufficient
D) It is gram negative, so any cell wall inhibitor will be effective
Answer: B
Rationale: The chapter's Clinical Pearl is "Gram stain purple = Gram positive. Gram stain red = Gram negative." Gram-negative organisms have more complex cell walls with two cell membranes, which makes "gram-negative bacterial infections more difficult to treat, because the drug molecules have a harder time penetrating the more complex cell walls."
Trap: (A) inverts the color rule, which is the most swapped fact in this section. It also attaches the right consequence to the wrong organism: it is the gram-positive cell, with its very thick peptidoglycan wall, that drugs penetrate more easily.

Subtopic: where the infection came from

Q: A patient admitted for an elective hip replacement develops pneumonia on hospital day 4. Nothing at admission suggested a respiratory infection. How should this infection be classified?
A) Community-associated, because the patient came from home
B) Health care-associated, because it was not present or incubating at admission and began more than 48 hours after admission
C) Community-associated, because the patient had not been hospitalized within the past year
D) Health care-associated, because postoperative pneumonia is always the more severe form
Answer: B
Rationale: A health care-associated infection is acquired during the course of receiving treatment for another condition in a health care institution; it is "not present or incubating at the time of admission but occurs more than 48 hours after admission." These infections matter because the organisms "have been exposed to strong antibiotics in the past and are the most drug resistant and the most virulent."
Trap: (C) is the dangerous distractor because it quotes the correct community-associated criterion — no hospitalization within the past year — and applies it to the wrong question. That criterion describes where the patient was before admission, not where this pneumonia came from. (D) makes severity the discriminator, which it never is; timing plus setting are.

Q: A unit council is reviewing its rate of health care-associated infections. Which action should the nurse identify as the single most important thing health care professionals can do to prevent their spread?
A) Wearing gloves for all patient contact
B) Handwashing
C) Placing high-risk patients in isolation
D) Disinfecting shared equipment between patients
Answer: B
Rationale: The chapter states it in superlative form: "Handwashing is the single most important thing health care professionals can do to prevent the spread of these potentially deadly infections." Direct contact is the most common mode of transmission, and over 70% of health care-associated infections are preventable.
Trap: (A), (C), and (D) are all genuine infection-control measures, which is what makes this hard — the stem asks for the superlative, not for a correct action. When a chapter names one intervention "the single most important," that phrasing is how a priority item is keyed.

Topic: antibiotic therapy types and response
Subtopic: empiric, definitive, and prophylactic therapy

Q: A patient is scheduled for colorectal surgery. Temperature and white blood cell count are normal and there is no sign of infection, but an antibiotic is ordered to be given on call, about 30 minutes before the incision. Which type of therapy is this?
A) Empiric therapy
B) Prophylactic therapy
C) Definitive therapy
D) Treatment of a superinfection
Answer: B
Rationale: Prophylactic antibiotic therapy is given before anticipated exposure to an infectious organism in an effort to prevent the development of infection, and the chapter times the surgical dose "generally 30 minutes before the incision to ensure adequate tissue penetration." Nothing in this patient's findings shows an infection to treat.
Trap: (A) pulls because prophylactic and empiric therapy both happen before any organism has been named, so timing alone will not separate them. The discriminator is whether an infection exists yet: prophylaxis has none, while empiric therapy has one whose identity is not yet known.

Q: A patient arrives with severe headache, fever, and nuchal rigidity, and acute meningitis is suspected. Cultures are drawn and an antibiotic is started within the hour, before any results are back. Which type of therapy is this?
A) Prophylactic therapy
B) Empiric therapy
C) Definitive therapy
D) Subtherapeutic therapy
Answer: B
Rationale: Empiric therapy is administration based on the practitioner's judgment of the pathogens most likely to be causing an apparent infection, used "to avoid treatment delay before specific culture information has been obtained." The chapter names suspected acute meningitis as its own example of an infection whose risk of severe complications is too high to wait on.
Trap: (A) is the mirror error of the previous item. This patient already has an apparent infection, so nothing is being prevented; the drug is treating something real whose name is still unknown.

Q: Culture and sensitivity results return and identify the organism, and the prescriber narrows the regimen. Which statement best explains this change?
A) Definitive therapy uses the most narrow-spectrum, least toxic drug based on sensitivity results
B) Definitive therapy uses the broadest-spectrum drug available to prevent relapse
C) Empiric therapy resumes once the organism is known
D) Narrowing therapy is done mainly to reduce the cost of the admission
Answer: A
Rationale: Definitive therapy is administration based on known results of culture and sensitivity testing, which are usually available in 48 to 72 hours, "using the most narrow-spectrum, least toxic drug based on sensitivity results." Overuse of broad-spectrum antibiotics contributes to resistance.
Trap: (B) inverts the spectrum rule, and it is the intuitive inversion because broader coverage feels safer in an already sick patient. Broad coverage is what empiric therapy buys while waiting; definitive therapy gives it up on purpose.

Q: A wound culture and an antibiotic are both ordered for a patient with a draining surgical wound. Which action should the nurse take first?
A) Give the first antibiotic dose, then collect the culture at the next dressing change
B) Collect the culture specimen, then give the first antibiotic dose
C) Give the antibiotic and hold the culture until the patient is afebrile
D) Collect the culture only if the first dose fails to lower the fever
Answer: B
Rationale: "Culture specimens must be obtained before drug therapy is initiated whenever possible." Otherwise the presence of antibiotics in the tissues may result in misleading culture results, and the nursing section restates the consequence directly: "Taking antibiotics before a culture would lead to false negative results."
Trap: (A) is the reflex pick because giving the drug feels like the urgent intervention and the swab feels like paperwork. Collecting first delays treatment by minutes; giving first can cost the team its only accurate organism identification.

Subtopic: therapeutic, subtherapeutic, and toxic response

Q: A patient's fever, wound drainage, and white blood cell count have not improved after several days of antibiotic therapy, and the response is judged subtherapeutic. Which of the following are causes of a subtherapeutic response? Select all that apply.
A) An incorrect route of drug administration
B) Inadequate drainage of an abscess
C) Poor drug penetration to the infected area
D) Insufficient serum levels of the drug
E) Bacterial resistance
F) Serum levels of the antibiotic that are too high
Answer: A, B, C, D, E
Rationale: The chapter defines a subtherapeutic response as one in which the signs and symptoms do not improve, and lists exactly five causes: "an incorrect route of drug administration, inadequate drainage of an abscess, poor drug penetration to the infected area, insufficient serum levels of the drug, or bacterial resistance."
Trap: (F) is kept from the wrong side of the same paragraph — serum levels that are too high define a toxic response. The larger error this item guards against is treating "no improvement" as automatic proof of resistance, when resistance is only one of the five listed causes.

Topic: antibiotic mechanisms and cidal versus static
Subtopic: bactericidal versus bacteriostatic

Q: A nurse explains that an ordered penicillin causes defective bacterial cell walls that are unstable and rupture. How should the nurse describe this drug's action?
A) Bactericidal, because it kills the bacteria
B) Bacteriostatic, because it inhibits growth without killing
C) Antimetabolite, because it blocks folic acid synthesis
D) Bacteriostatic, because lysis only slows reproduction
Answer: A
Rationale: Penicillins inhibit synthesis of the bacterial peptidoglycan cell wall by binding penicillin-binding proteins, which "causes the formation of defective cell walls that are unstable and easily broken down," and "bacterial death usually results from lysis (rupture) of the bacterial cells." Bactericidal antibiotics are those that kill bacteria.
Trap: (D) is built on the word lysis. A student who does not know the term reads "rupture" as damage short of death and downgrades a cidal drug to a static one. Cue: cidal kills; static holds the population stationary.

Q: The same patient's second drug is doxycycline, which binds the bacterial ribosome so the organism cannot make protein and cannot multiply while host defenses clear it. How should the nurse describe this action?
A) Bactericidal, because loss of protein synthesis is always fatal to bacteria
B) Bacteriostatic, because it inhibits growth rather than killing the bacteria
C) Antimetabolite, because it competes with para-aminobenzoic acid
D) Cell wall inhibitor, because ribosomes are part of the cell wall
Answer: B
Rationale: Tetracyclines are bacteriostatic drugs that inhibit bacterial protein synthesis by binding to the 30S bacterial ribosome. Bacteriostatic antibiotics "do not actually kill bacteria but rather inhibit their growth."
Trap: (A) is the mirror error of the previous item, upgrading a static drug to a cidal one. (D) pulls on a separate confusion: the ribosome is inside the cell, and no protein synthesis inhibitor is a cell wall drug.

Subtopic: mechanisms by class

Q: A patient asks how a sulfonamide can poison bacteria without poisoning human cells. Which explanation is accurate?
A) Sulfonamides compete with para-aminobenzoic acid to block bacterial folic acid synthesis, and human cells use folic acid taken in from outside
B) Sulfonamides destroy the bacterial cell wall, and human cells have no cell wall
C) Sulfonamides bind the 50S ribosome, and human ribosomes are 30S
D) Sulfonamides are activated only by bacterial beta-lactamase
Answer: A
Rationale: In competitive inhibition, sulfonamides compete with para-aminobenzoic acid for the bacterial enzyme tetrahydropteroic acid synthetase, preventing bacterial synthesis of folic acid. "Microorganisms that require exogenous folic acid (not synthesized by the bacterium itself) are not affected by sulfonamide antibiotics. Therefore these drugs do not affect folic acid metabolism in human cells."
Trap: (B) is the reflex pick because the chapter's own key point says most antibiotics work by inhibiting bacterial cell wall synthesis in some way. That generalization does not reach the sulfonamides, macrolides, or tetracyclines in this same chapter, so it must not be carried onto a named-class stem. A stem mentioning folic acid is never a beta-lactam item.

Q: A patient receiving azithromycin asks how it stops the bacteria. Which response is accurate?
A) It binds reversibly to the 50S ribosomal subunit and inhibits protein synthesis
B) It binds to the 30S ribosomal subunit and inhibits protein synthesis
C) It binds irreversibly to the 30S subunit and kills the organism outright
D) It prevents cross-linking of the bacterial cell wall
Answer: A
Rationale: Macrolides are bacteriostatic drugs that inhibit protein synthesis by binding reversibly to the 50S ribosomal subunits of susceptible microorganisms, although "in high enough concentrations they may be bactericidal to some susceptible bacteria."
Trap: (B) is the swap this pair exists for. Macrolides and tetracyclines are both protein synthesis inhibitors and both bacteriostatic, so the subunit number is the only discriminator: macrolides bind 50S, tetracyclines bind 30S.

Q: Which antibiotic classes covered in this chapter work by inhibiting bacterial cell wall synthesis? Select all that apply.
A) Penicillins
B) Cephalosporins
C) Carbapenems
D) Monobactams
E) Macrolides
F) Tetracyclines
G) Sulfonamides
Answer: A, B, C, D
Rationale: The four beta-lactam subclasses — penicillins, cephalosporins, carbapenems, and monobactams — "share a common structure and mechanism of action: they inhibit the synthesis of the bacterial peptidoglycan cell wall." Fig. 38.3 places macrolides and tetracyclines with the protein synthesis inhibitors and sulfonamides with the antimetabolites.
Trap: (E), (F), and (G) are kept from the wrong side of Fig. 38.3 deliberately. The key point that "most antibiotics work by inhibiting bacterial cell wall synthesis in some way" tempts a learner to sweep in all seven classes, but three of the classes in this one chapter break that generalization.

Topic: antibiotic host factors and allergy assessment
Subtopic: allergy versus adverse effect

Q: A patient tells the nurse, "I'm allergic to erythromycin — it upsets my stomach." What is the nurse's best action?
A) Document a drug allergy to erythromycin and request an alternative from the prescriber
B) Ask open-ended questions about the reaction and document nausea as an adverse effect rather than an allergy
C) Document a sensitivity to all macrolides
D) Plan to give the drug with an antihistamine
Answer: B
Rationale: "Many patients will say that they are 'allergic' to a medication when in fact what they experienced was a common mild adverse effect such as stomach upset or nausea," and patients who report drug allergies "need to be asked open-ended questions to elicit descriptions of prior allergic reactions so that the actual severity of the reaction can be assessed."
Trap: (A) is the safe-feeling pick, and its cost is invisible at the bedside: mislabeling an intolerance as an allergy removes an effective drug from the patient's chart permanently. (C) compounds the same error by generalizing it to a whole class.

Q: Which findings reported during antibiotic therapy should be documented as a true allergic reaction? Select all that apply.
A) Difficulty breathing
B) Hives
C) A significant rash or other skin reaction
D) Severe gastrointestinal intolerance
E) Mild nausea after the morning dose
F) One loose stool on the second day of therapy
Answer: A, B, C, D
Rationale: The allergy interview must capture "any difficulty breathing; significant rash, hives, or other skin reaction; and severe gastrointestinal intolerance." Anaphylaxis itself presents with flushing, itching, hives, anxiety, fast irregular pulse, and throat and tongue swelling, and the most dangerous form is anaphylactic shock, in which the patient can go into respiratory arrest.
Trap: (E) and (F) are exactly the mild adverse effects the chapter names as the ones patients misreport as allergy. The word separating the fourth option from the sixth is severe; ordinary stomach upset and antibiotic-associated loose stool are expected adverse effects, not allergy.

Q: During an intravenous penicillin infusion, a patient develops wheezing and swelling of the face. Which action should the nurse take first?
A) Stop the infusion and remove the intravenous catheter
B) Stop the infusion, leave the intravenous line in place, contact the prescriber, and monitor the patient closely
C) Slow the infusion rate and reassess in 30 minutes
D) Stop the infusion and give the remaining dose by mouth
Answer: B
Rationale: The chapter's instruction is that "the first thing to do is stop the dosage form immediately (if intravenously, stop the infusion; leave the intravenous line in place, contact the prescriber, and monitor the patient closely." Manifestations of hypersensitivity include wheezing; shortness of breath; swelling of the face, tongue, or hands; itching; or rash.
Trap: (A) is the reflex pick, because pulling the offending drug feels like it should include pulling the line. The line is the route for emergency drugs, so the infusion stops and the catheter stays. (C) treats a hypersensitivity reaction as a rate problem.

Q: A patient who completed a course of antibiotics four days ago develops a diffuse rash. How should the nurse classify this reaction?
A) An immediate reaction, which may not occur for up to 30 minutes
B) An accelerated reaction, which may occur within 1 to 72 hours
C) A delayed reaction, which may occur after 72 hours
D) Not drug related, because reactions cannot begin after the drug is stopped
Answer: C
Rationale: The chapter times hypersensitivity in three bands: immediate reactions "may not occur for up to 30 minutes," accelerated reactions "may occur within 1 to 72 hours," and delayed reactions "may occur after 72 hours." Four days is past the 72-hour boundary.
Trap: (B) is the near miss, because 72 hours is the shared edge of both bands — accelerated runs up to it, delayed begins after it. (D) is the assumption that ends assessment with the last dose, which is precisely what the three-band table exists to prevent.

Subtopic: age and genetic host factors

Q: A pediatric nurse reviews antibiotic orders for several children. Which pairing of drug and pediatric risk is stated correctly by the chapter?
A) Tetracyclines and discoloration of developing teeth
B) Quinolones and kernicterus in neonates
C) Sulfonamides and impaired bone or cartilage development
D) Tetracyclines and hemolysis of red blood cells in G6PD deficiency
Answer: A
Rationale: The chapter lists three drugs and three different pediatric harms in a single sentence: tetracyclines "affect developing teeth or bones"; quinolones "may affect bone or cartilage development in children"; and sulfonamides "may displace bilirubin from albumin and precipitate kernicterus (hyperbilirubinemia) in neonates."
Trap: (B) and (C) swap the quinolone and sulfonamide harms with each other, which is the rotation an item writer builds from a single three-part sentence. (D) borrows the G6PD effect, which the chapter attaches to sulfonamides, nitrofurantoin, and dapsone, not to tetracyclines.

Q: A patient with glucose-6-phosphate dehydrogenase deficiency is prescribed a sulfonamide. What is the nurse's primary concern?
A) The drug will accumulate because metabolism is reduced, producing toxicity
B) The red blood cells will be destroyed, producing hemolytic anemia
C) The drug will be eliminated too quickly, producing a subtherapeutic level
D) The patient will develop kernicterus
Answer: B
Rationale: In G6PD deficiency, administration of antibiotics such as sulfonamides, nitrofurantoin, and dapsone "may result in the hemolysis, or destruction, of red blood cells." The nursing implementation section repeats the caution, noting that sulfonamides "antagonize folate synthesis and may cause hemolytic anemia."
Trap: (A) describes slow acetylation, the other genetic host factor named in the very same sentence of the chapter, in which reduced metabolism leads to toxicity from drug accumulation. Cue: G6PD bursts the red cells, slow acetylation piles the drug up — one is a blood problem, the other a metabolism problem.

Topic: sulfonamide therapy
Subtopic: indications and contraindications

Q: A patient with an uncomplicated urinary tract infection is prescribed sulfamethoxazole/trimethoprim. Which explanation of why this drug was chosen is accurate?
A) Sulfonamides achieve very high concentrations in the kidneys, through which they are eliminated
B) Sulfonamides are excreted unchanged in the stool and sterilize the bowel reservoir
C) Sulfonamides are bactericidal against all gram-negative organisms
D) Sulfonamides are the only antibiotic class safe in pregnancy at term
Answer: A
Rationale: "Sulfonamides achieve very high concentrations in the kidneys, through which they are eliminated. Therefore SMX-TMP is often used in the treatment of UTIs." The fixed combination pairs a sulfonamide with trimethoprim, a nonsulfonamide antibiotic, in a 5:1 ratio, and the pairing allows a synergistic antibacterial effect.
Trap: (C) upgrades a bacteriostatic antimetabolite to a bactericidal drug, the most common class error with this group. (D) inverts a contraindication outright: sulfonamides are contraindicated in pregnant women at term and in infants younger than 2 months of age.

Q: A patient with a documented sulfonamide allergy is admitted taking several home medications. Which one should the nurse question?
A) hydrochlorothiazide, a thiazide diuretic
B) glipizide, a sulfonylurea
C) celecoxib, a COX-2 inhibitor
D) acetazolamide, a carbonic anhydrase inhibitor
Answer: C
Rationale: The chapter sorts the chemically related drugs into two groups: sulfonylureas, thiazide and loop diuretics, and carbonic anhydrase inhibitors are "generally considered relatively safe" in sulfonamide-allergic patients, while celecoxib "should not be used."
Trap: (A), (B), and (D) all pull because each really is chemically related to the sulfonamides, and withholding them looks like caution. Over-applying a sulfa allergy is its own error; the chapter names exactly one related drug to withhold. Note also that sulfite preservatives are a different substance again — a person allergic to sulfonamide drugs may or may not be allergic to sulfites.

Subtopic: sulfonamide adverse effects and teaching

Q: A patient who started sulfamethoxazole/trimethoprim six days ago reports a fever followed by a spreading rash. How should the nurse interpret this?
A) An expected effect that resolves if therapy continues
B) The characteristic delayed cutaneous reaction to sulfonamides, which begins with fever followed by rash
C) Too late to be drug related, because sulfonamide reactions occur within 30 minutes of a dose
D) Photosensitivity, which needs only sun precautions
Answer: B
Rationale: "Sulfonamides typically cause delayed cutaneous reactions. These reactions frequently begin with fever followed by a rash (morbilliform eruptions, erythema multiforme, or toxic epidermal necrolysis)." A thorough skin assessment is performed before and during therapy because of the potential for Stevens-Johnson syndrome, which Table 38.2 lists among the integumentary effects.
Trap: (C) applies the penicillin pattern of an immediate reaction within 30 minutes to a class whose signature reaction is delayed by days. (D) mistakes a systemic reaction for the sun-exposure rash, which is a separate sulfonamide effect with a separate intervention.

Q: A nurse teaches a patient starting a sulfonamide to drink 2000 to 3000 mL of fluid every 24 hours, preferably water. What is the reason for this instruction?
A) To prevent drug-related crystalluria
B) To prevent the photosensitivity reaction
C) To prevent Stevens-Johnson syndrome
D) To speed absorption of the drug from the stomach
Answer: A
Rationale: The implementation section directs the nurse to "encourage an increase in fluids (2000 to 3000 mL/24 hr), preferably water, to prevent drug-related crystalluria." Crystalluria appears in Table 38.2, and renal function studies including BUN, creatinine, and urinalysis are assessed with frequent or long-term therapy. Each individual dose is taken with 8 ounces of water.
Trap: (B) is the paired teaching point with the wrong intervention. Sulfonamides carry both risks, but crystalluria is answered with fluids and photosensitivity is answered with sun protection — staying out of direct sunlight between 10:00 am and 3:00 pm, protective clothing, and a sun-blocking product with an SPF of at least 15. A stem asking why fluids are pushed has a renal answer, not a skin answer.

Q: Which of the following are documented sulfonamide drug interactions? Select all that apply.
A) Potentiated hypoglycemic effects of sulfonylureas
B) Potentiated toxic effects of phenytoin
C) Potentiated anticoagulant effects of warfarin, which can lead to hemorrhage
D) Increased likelihood of cyclosporine-induced nephrotoxicity
E) Reduced efficacy of oral contraceptives
F) Increased efficacy of oral contraceptives, allowing a lower dose
Answer: A, B, C, D, E
Rationale: The chapter lists sulfonamides as potentiating the hypoglycemic effects of sulfonylureas, the toxic effects of phenytoin, and the anticoagulant effects of warfarin, and as increasing the likelihood of cyclosporine-induced nephrotoxicity. It adds that "sulfonamides, and all antibiotics, may also reduce the efficacy of oral contraceptives." Its Clinical Pearl on the class is simply "Sulfonamides have many drug interactions!"
Trap: (F) reverses the contraceptive interaction, and direction is the entire clinical point — the wrong direction produces an unintended pregnancy. Reliable backup methods of contraception must be used in addition to the oral contraceptive during antibiotic use.

Topic: beta-lactam ring and enzyme inhibitors
Subtopic: beta-lactamase versus its inhibitors

Q: A culture report notes that the organism produces beta-lactamase. What does this mean for the patient's therapy?
A) The bacteria make an enzyme that opens the beta-lactam ring, so all beta-lactam drugs lose their antibacterial efficacy
B) The patient produces an enzyme that inactivates the drug in the liver
C) A medication has already been added to the antibiotic to protect it
D) The organism is resistant to penicillins only, not to the other beta-lactams
Answer: A
Rationale: Beta-lactamases are "any of a group of enzymes produced by bacteria that catalyze the chemical opening of the crucial beta-lactam ring structures in beta-lactam antibiotics." The enzyme breaks the bond between the carbon and nitrogen atoms of the ring, and "when this happens, all beta-lactam drugs lose their antibacterial efficacy." Penicillinase is the subset that specifically inactivates penicillins.
Trap: (B) relocates the enzyme into the patient, which is where most drug-metabolizing enzymes live; this one is bacterial. Cue: if the bacteria make it, it is the enzyme; if a pharmacist dispenses it, it is the inhibitor. (C) describes the countermeasure rather than the problem.

Q: The order is changed to piperacillin/tazobactam. What is tazobactam's role in this product?
A) A second antibiotic that broadens gram-negative coverage
B) A beta-lactamase inhibitor that binds the bacterial enzyme so it cannot break down the penicillin
C) A kidney enzyme inhibitor that keeps the penicillin from being destroyed in the renal tubule
D) A preservative that stabilizes the intravenous solution
Answer: B
Rationale: Clavulanic acid, tazobactam, sulbactam, and avibactam are the four beta-lactamase inhibitors, medications combined with certain penicillin drugs to block the effect of beta-lactamase enzymes. They "bind with the beta-lactamase enzyme itself to prevent the enzyme from breaking down the penicillin molecule, although they are not always effective."
Trap: (C) is cilastatin's job, not an inhibitor's: cilastatin inhibits dehydropeptidase, an enzyme in the kidneys, so that imipenem is not broken down. Cue: inhibitors block a bacterial enzyme, cilastatin blocks a kidney enzyme. (A) is attractive because this product genuinely has wide coverage, but that width comes from piperacillin being an extended-spectrum penicillin.

Q: A patient has a documented penicillin allergy. Which of the following ordered products contain a penicillin? Select all that apply.
A) Unasyn
B) Augmentin
C) Zosyn
D) Timentin
E) Rocephin
F) Zithromax
Answer: A, B, C, D
Rationale: The chapter pairs each trade name with its penicillin: Unasyn is ampicillin with sulbactam, Augmentin is amoxicillin with clavulanic acid, Timentin is ticarcillin with clavulanic acid, and Zosyn is piperacillin with tazobactam.
Trap: (E) and (F) are the two safe options — Rocephin is ceftriaxone, a cephalosporin, and Zithromax is azithromycin, a macrolide. The chapter documents real medication errors from exactly this list in its Safety and Quality Improvement box, because "the drugs were referred to by their trade names, which lack the 'cillin' suffix." Its Clinical Pearl is blunt: trade names do not always end in cillin, for example Zosyn. The stated fix is to use both trade and generic names when ordering.

Q: Nafcillin is ordered for a staphylococcal infection. How does nafcillin withstand the staphylococcal enzyme that destroys natural penicillins?
A) It is packaged with a beta-lactamase inhibitor
B) Its own structure carries a large, bulky side chain near the beta-lactam ring that acts as a barrier to the penicillinase enzyme
C) It has no beta-lactam ring for the enzyme to open
D) It is given only intravenously, so the enzyme never reaches it
Answer: B
Rationale: The penicillinase-resistant drugs, also called antistaphylococcal penicillins, are "stable against hydrolysis by most staphylococcal penicillinases." Their chemical structure "features a large, bulky side chain near the beta-lactam ring. This side chain serves as a barrier to the penicillinase enzyme."
Trap: (A) is the reflex pick because it is the other solution to the same problem, and the two get fused. The combination products resist by carrying a separate inhibitor drug; the penicillinase-resistant penicillins resist by their own shape, with nothing added. Note that penicillinase-resistant does not mean proof against every beta-lactamase — certain strains of S. aureus are resistant to these drugs and require alternative regimens.

Subtopic: time-dependent killing

Q: A beta-lactam antibiotic is ordered as a continuous intravenous infusion rather than as one large daily dose. Which drug property explains this?
A) Beta-lactams exhibit time-dependent killing, so their concentration must stay above the minimum inhibitory concentration
B) Beta-lactams work best as a single high peak concentration
C) Continuous infusion lowers the risk of an allergic reaction
D) Beta-lactams are inactivated by gastric acid, so infusion is the only route
Answer: A
Rationale: "The beta-lactams exhibit time-dependent killing (meaning their concentration must be above the minimum inhibitory concentration to kill bacteria). Because of this property, it is not unusual to see them given by continuous intravenous infusion. They are also given by intermittent infusion (i.e., every 4 to 6 hours)."
Trap: (B) inverts the pharmacodynamic property, and it is the intuitive inversion because a tall peak reads as a stronger dose. Time above the threshold, not peak height, is what kills with this class. The same logic sits behind the all-antibiotic teaching that doses are taken around the clock to maintain effective blood levels and are not to be omitted or doubled up.

Topic: penicillin subclasses and routes
Subtopic: natural penicillins

Q: A prescriber wants an oral penicillin for an outpatient with streptococcal pharyngitis. Which drug fits that order?
A) penicillin G potassium, the oral natural penicillin
B) penicillin V potassium, the oral dosage form
C) nafcillin, the oral penicillinase-resistant drug
D) piperacillin, for oral extended-spectrum coverage
Answer: B
Rationale: Table 38.3 states that the natural penicillins are the only two of their subclass in current clinical use: "Penicillin G is the injectable form for intravenous or intramuscular use; penicillin V is an oral dosage form (tablet and liquid)."
Trap: (A) swaps the two letters, which is the entire item. Cue: G is the shot, V is the pill. (C) fails on route as well — nafcillin is injectable only — and (D) fails on route and tier at once, since the extended-spectrum combinations are intravenous only.

Q: The same patient later requires a single intramuscular injection to treat syphilis. Which preparation is appropriate, and what must the nurse never do with it?
A) penicillin V potassium; never give it with food
B) penicillin G benzathine; never give it intravenously, because its consistency is too thick and such use can be fatal
C) penicillin G potassium; never give it into a large muscle mass
D) nafcillin; never give it by the intramuscular route
Answer: B
Rationale: Penicillin G has three salt forms — benzathine, procaine, and potassium. The benzathine and procaine salts are longer-acting intramuscular injections, "especially helpful for treating the sexually transmitted disease syphilis, because often only one injection is needed." The warning attached to them is explicit: "Never give these preparations intravenously, because their consistency is too thick for intravenous administration, and such use can be fatal."
Trap: (A) is the mirror error of the previous item, sending the oral form down the injectable path. (C) reverses the administration rule for the thick salts: they are given into a large muscle mass with a 21- to 23-gauge needle, and the site that is prohibited is the dorsogluteal.

Subtopic: aminopenicillins and extended-spectrum drugs

Q: Which statement about the two aminopenicillins is accurate?
A) Amoxicillin is oral only and may be given with or without food, while ampicillin may be given by mouth, intravenously, or intramuscularly
B) Ampicillin is oral only, while amoxicillin has three salt forms
C) Both drugs are available by injection only
D) Amoxicillin is a natural penicillin and ampicillin is penicillinase resistant
Answer: A
Rationale: Amoxicillin is oral only and "can be given with or without food"; ampicillin is given by mouth, intravenously, or intramuscularly and has three salt forms — anhydrous and trihydrate for oral use and sodium for parenteral use. Amoxicillin is an analogue of ampicillin, and both are aminopenicillins.
Trap: (B) swaps the two drugs, which are one syllable apart in the middle and are the most confused pair in the class. Cue: amoxicillin is oral, ampicillin has ample routes. Ampicillin also carries the higher rash rate — 5.2% to 9.5% of treatment courses against about 2% with natural penicillin.

Q: A patient with a gram-negative infection is switched from penicillin V to ampicillin. What is the rationale for the change?
A) Aminopenicillins carry an amino group that enhances their activity against gram-negative bacteria compared with natural penicillins
B) Aminopenicillins are penicillinase resistant, so beta-lactamase cannot destroy them
C) Natural penicillins have the widest spectrum of the four subclasses
D) Aminopenicillins are the only penicillins that cover anaerobes
Answer: A
Rationale: The aminopenicillins "have an amino group attached to the basic penicillin structure that enhances their activity against gram-negative bacteria compared with natural penicillins," and are named for the free amino group in their chemical structure. "Most natural penicillins have little if any ability to kill gram-negative bacteria."
Trap: (C) is the tier swap that the four-subclass table is built to catch: the widest spectra belong to the extended-spectrum drugs. (B) borrows the penicillinase-resistant subclass's defining feature, which the aminopenicillins do not have — which is exactly why amoxicillin is paired with clavulanic acid in Augmentin.

Q: A hospitalized patient with a suspected serious intraabdominal infection and sepsis is started on intravenous piperacillin/tazobactam before culture results return. Which statement is accurate?
A) It is an extended-spectrum penicillin with excellent gram-positive, gram-negative, and anaerobic coverage, often used as empiric therapy
B) It is a natural penicillin used for definitive therapy once cultures return
C) It can be converted to an oral form of the same drug at discharge
D) It is a penicillinase-resistant penicillin chosen specifically for staphylococcal infection
Answer: A
Rationale: The extended-spectrum drugs have "excellent gram-positive, gram-negative, and anaerobic coverage" and are used for many health care-associated infections, including pneumonia, intraabdominal infections, and sepsis. Zosyn "is commonly used in hospitalized patients with suspected or documented serious infections... it is often used as empiric therapy."
Trap: (C) is the practical error rather than the classification one, and it is the kind that reaches a discharge order: Zosyn and Timentin are available for intravenous use only, so there is no oral step-down of the same drug. (D) describes nafcillin and oxacillin.

Q: Which penicillin preparations are available for oral administration? Select all that apply.
A) penicillin V potassium
B) amoxicillin
C) dicloxacillin
D) ampicillin
E) nafcillin
F) piperacillin/tazobactam
Answer: A, B, C, D
Rationale: The chapter's route facts give penicillin V potassium as oral only, cloxacillin and dicloxacillin as oral only, amoxicillin as oral only, and ampicillin as oral, intravenous, or intramuscular. Nafcillin is injectable only, and Zosyn and Timentin are available for intravenous use only. Oxacillin, unlike nafcillin, is available both orally and by injection.
Trap: (E) is the option most often added, because nafcillin sits in the same subclass as the oral cloxacillin and dicloxacillin — subclass does not predict route, and this is the one subclass that splits. (F) is the extended-spectrum combination, which never leaves the intravenous line.

Topic: penicillin adverse effects and interactions
Subtopic: electrolyte and allergy risk

Q: A patient with heart failure is receiving high-dose intravenous penicillin G. Which laboratory values require close monitoring, and why?
A) Sodium and potassium, because the intravenous formulations contain large amounts of sodium and potassium
B) Calcium and magnesium, because penicillins chelate divalent ions
C) Sodium and potassium, because penicillins cause renal wasting of both
D) Serum glucose, because penicillins potentiate hypoglycemic drugs
Answer: A
Rationale: "The intravenous formulations of some penicillins contain large amounts of sodium and/or potassium," and the chapter quantifies it: penicillin G contains 1.7 mEq of potassium ion per million units and 2 mEq of sodium ion per million units. In heart failure, fluid overload, or dysrhythmias, hypernatremia or hyperkalemia "could lead to exacerbation of these health issues." Table 38.4 lists both among the metabolic adverse effects, and doses must be adjusted for renal dysfunction.
Trap: (C) picks the right two electrolytes and reverses the direction — the risk comes from the drug's own salt load, not from losing either ion. (B) is the tetracycline property and (D) the sulfonamide interaction, both pulled into a penicillin stem.

Q: Before giving a first dose of penicillin, which history findings should the nurse recognize as associated with a higher risk for penicillin allergy? Select all that apply.
A) Asthma
B) Sensitivity to multiple allergens
C) Aspirin allergy
D) Sensitivity to cephalosporins
E) A family history of type 2 diabetes
F) Nausea after a previous course of amoxicillin
Answer: A, B, C, D
Rationale: The assessment section names "a history of asthma, sensitivity to multiple allergens, aspirin allergy, and sensitivity to cephalosporins; these factors are associated with a higher risk for penicillin allergy." If procaine penicillin is ordered, procaine hypersensitivity is assessed as well. Allergic reactions occur in 0.7% to 4% of treatment courses, and anaphylactic reactions in 0.004% to 0.015% of patients.
Trap: (F) carries the intolerance-as-allergy error into a risk screen; nausea is a common adverse effect, not an allergic reaction. (E) is unrelated history that reads as thoroughness, which is how a padded distractor usually earns a check mark.

Q: A patient reports that penicillin once caused throat swelling and hives. A cephalosporin is now ordered. What should the nurse do?
A) Give the cephalosporin, since current data put cross-reactivity at less than 1%
B) Hold the drug and contact the prescriber, because patients with a history of throat swelling or hives from penicillin should not receive cephalosporins
C) Give the cephalosporin after premedicating with an antihistamine
D) Give the cephalosporin, because cross-reactivity applies only to the carbapenems
Answer: B
Rationale: The chapter's clinical rule is stated as a rule: "In general, only those patients with a history of throat swelling or hives from penicillin should not receive cephalosporins." Cephalosporin use is also contraindicated in patients with a history of life-threatening allergic reaction to penicillins, and some patients may require skin testing and desensitization.
Trap: (A) quotes a real figure from the chapter and still reaches the wrong action, which is the most instructive kind of wrong answer. Cross-reactivity was historically reported at 1% to 4%, while current data suggest less than 1% without a positive skin test and 2% with one — but those numbers describe populations, and this patient already has the exact history the rule names.

Subtopic: penicillin drug interactions

Q: Probenecid is added for a patient receiving a penicillin. Which effect should the nurse anticipate?
A) The penicillin will be eliminated faster and the dose will need to be increased
B) Probenecid competes for elimination and prolongs the effects of the penicillin
C) The penicillin will be inactivated in the gut before absorption
D) The combination raises the risk of hyperkalemia and nothing else
Answer: B
Rationale: Table 38.5 lists probenecid as competing for elimination, with the result that it "prolongs the effects of penicillins." Table 38.7 gives the parallel entry for cephalosporins: decreased renal excretion, producing increased cephalosporin levels.
Trap: (A) is the intuitive inversion, because "competes" sounds like the penicillin loses and leaves sooner. What the two drugs are competing for is the exit, so the penicillin stays longer — which is why this interaction has historically been used on purpose.

Q: A patient stabilized on warfarin is started on a penicillin. What should the nurse monitor for?
A) Reduced anticoagulant effect, requiring a higher warfarin dose
B) Enhanced anticoagulant effect and bleeding, because gut flora that produce vitamin K are reduced
C) No interaction, because penicillins are not protein bound
D) Faster warfarin metabolism from cytochrome P-450 induction
Answer: B
Rationale: Table 38.5 gives the mechanism as reduced vitamin K from gut flora, with the result of an enhanced anticoagulant effect of warfarin. Warfarin recurs across this chapter: sulfonamides potentiate its anticoagulant effects as well, and macrolides delay its metabolic clearance.
Trap: (A) reverses the direction, and direction is the whole clinical point — this patient bleeds rather than clots. (D) borrows the macrolide mechanism; the penicillin interaction happens in the intestine, not the liver.

Topic: cephalosporin generations and coverage
Subtopic: the generation trend

Q: A nurse compares a first-generation with a third-generation cephalosporin. Which statement describes the trend across the first three generations?
A) Gram-positive coverage decreases and gram-negative coverage increases
B) Gram-positive coverage increases and gram-negative coverage decreases
C) Both gram-positive and gram-negative coverage increase
D) Anaerobic coverage increases with each successive generation
Answer: A
Rationale: The Clinical Pearl states it directly: "As the generations of cephalosporins increase, their gram-positive coverage decreases and gram-negative coverage increases." The body text agrees that the first-generation drugs have the most gram-positive coverage and the later generations the most gram-negative coverage.
Trap: (B) is the intuitive inversion, because "newer" reads as more of everything. (D) is a different rule that fails in the same direction: anaerobic coverage does not climb with generation, it sits in the second generation alone.

Q: A student says that because later cephalosporin generations lose gram-positive coverage, cefepime cannot be appropriate for an infection with gram-positive organisms. What is the accurate response?
A) The student is correct; cefepime has no gram-positive activity
B) Cefepime provides better gram-positive coverage than the third-generation drugs, along with increased activity against many Enterobacter species
C) Cefepime is actually a second-generation drug
D) Cefepime is the only cephalosporin that treats MRSA
Answer: B
Rationale: Cefepime is the prototypical fourth-generation cephalosporin and "most closely resembles ceftazidime," but with "increased activity against many Enterobacter species (gram-negative) as well as gram-positive organisms"; the chapter's own drug table credits it with better gram-positive coverage than the third generation. The Clinical Pearl describes generations 1 through 3, and a named-drug fact overrides a trend.
Trap: (A) is the mirror error of the previous item — carrying a general trend past the range it actually describes. (D) belongs to ceftaroline, the fifth-generation drug and the only cephalosporin that treats MRSA.

Subtopic: coverage by drug

Q: A patient is scheduled for abdominal surgery and cefoxitin is ordered preoperatively. Why was this drug chosen?
A) It is a second-generation cephamycin covering anaerobes such as Bacteroides fragilis, which is what an intestinal field requires
B) It is a third-generation drug that penetrates the blood-brain barrier
C) It is the only cephalosporin that covers enterococci
D) It is a first-generation drug preferred for all surgical prophylaxis
Answer: A
Rationale: Cefoxitin and cefotetan are the cephamycins, with "better coverage against various anaerobic bacteria such as Bacteroides fragilis, Peptostreptococcus species, and Clostridioides species," and cefoxitin is "used extensively as a prophylactic antibiotic in patients undergoing abdominal surgery because it can effectively kill intestinal bacteria, including anaerobes." Anaerobic coverage is found only with the second-generation drugs.
Trap: (D) pulls because cefazolin, a first-generation drug, genuinely is a common surgical prophylaxis choice — but not for a field full of intestinal anaerobes. (C) claims something no cephalosporin does: the class is not active against fungi, viruses, or enterococci.

Q: A patient with an anaerobic intraabdominal infection has an order for cefuroxime. Why should the nurse question this order?
A) Cefuroxime is a first-generation drug with no gram-negative coverage
B) Cefuroxime is second generation, but the chapter states that it does not kill anaerobic bacteria
C) Cefuroxime is available only as an oral prodrug
D) Cefuroxime cannot be given with food
Answer: B
Rationale: Cefuroxime is a second-generation cephalosporin, but unlike the cephamycins cefoxitin and cefotetan, "it does not kill anaerobic bacteria." Anaerobic coverage in this class belongs specifically to the cephamycins.
Trap: (A) is the reflex pick for anyone reasoning only from generation numbers, and it also misplaces the drug's generation. The near-miss here sits inside the correct generation, which is what makes it dangerous: the generation rule alone would predict anaerobic coverage and pass this order. (C) is half true of one salt form, since cefuroxime axetil is the oral prodrug while cefuroxime sodium is injectable.

Q: A patient with bacterial meningitis needs a third-generation cephalosporin. Which drug and which property fit this indication?
A) ceftazidime, because it covers Pseudomonas
B) ceftriaxone, because it passes easily through the blood-brain barrier
C) cefazolin, because it is the prototypical first-generation drug
D) cefepime, because it is injectable only
Answer: B
Rationale: Ceftriaxone is "extremely long-acting," is given once a day, and is "able to pass easily through the blood-brain barrier," so it is "one of the few cephalosporins that is indicated for the treatment of meningitis." Its dosage line adds that meningitis is the one exception to once-daily dosing, for which it is given twice daily. It is 93% to 96% protein bound and is metabolized in the intestine after biliary excretion.
Trap: (A) names a correct discriminator attached to the wrong drug. Ceftriaxone's coverage is comparable to ceftazidime's "except for Pseudomonas species," which is exactly what ceftazidime adds — and ceftazidime is generally given in combination with an aminoglycoside. Cue: ceftriaxone crosses into the brain, ceftazidime hits Pseudomonas.

Q: Which statements about the cephalosporins as a class are accurate? Select all that apply.
A) They are bactericidal and interfere with bacterial cell wall synthesis
B) They bind the same penicillin-binding proteins as the penicillins
C) They are not active against fungi, viruses, or enterococci
D) Anaerobic coverage is found only with the second-generation drugs
E) Ceftaroline is the only cephalosporin that treats MRSA
F) Every generation provides reliable coverage of enterococci
Answer: A, B, C, D, E
Rationale: Cephalosporins are "structurally and pharmacologically related to the penicillins," are bactericidal, work by interfering with bacterial cell wall synthesis, and "also bind to the same penicillin-binding proteins." The chapter states that they are "not active against fungi, viruses, or enterococci," that anaerobic coverage is found only with the second-generation drugs, and that ceftaroline is the only cephalosporin covering MRSA.
Trap: (F) directly contradicts the third option, and it is what survives when a learner fuses two beta-lactam facts into a false general rule. Penicillins do cover Enterococcus species; cephalosporins do not, and neither does ertapenem — but that shared gap is not a property of beta-lactams in general.

Topic: cephalosporin cautions and administration
Subtopic: cephalosporin interactions

Q: A patient finished a course of cefotetan yesterday and asks whether it is safe to have a drink at a wedding tonight. What should the nurse say?
A) Alcohol is safe once the last dose has been taken
B) Alcohol must be avoided, because drinking within 72 hours of taking cefotetan can produce an acute alcohol intolerance reaction
C) Alcohol is safe if it is limited to one drink taken with food
D) Alcohol should be avoided for 2 weeks after any cephalosporin
Answer: B
Rationale: Table 38.7 gives "acute alcohol intolerance (disulfiram-like reaction) after drinking alcoholic beverages within 72 hr of taking cefotetan," produced by accumulation of the acetaldehyde metabolite of ethanol. Symptoms are stomach cramps, nausea, vomiting, diaphoresis, pruritus, headache, and hypotension, and the implementation section directs the nurse to have patients avoid alcohol and alcohol-containing products.
Trap: (A) is the reflex pick because most drug-alcohol cautions end with the last dose, and this window deliberately extends past the course. (D) overshoots in the other direction and invents a duration the chapter never states, which is no safer as an answer. Cue: cefotetan is the teetotal drug.

Q: Which of the following are documented cephalosporin drug interactions? Select all that apply.
A) Ethanol, producing a disulfiram-like reaction with cefotetan
B) Antacids and iron, decreasing absorption of cefdinir and cefditoren
C) Probenecid, decreasing renal excretion and increasing cephalosporin levels
D) Oral contraceptives, with an increased risk for unintended pregnancy
E) Warfarin, whose anticoagulant effect is abolished by cephalosporins
Answer: A, B, C, D
Rationale: Table 38.7 lists exactly four interactions — ethanol, antacids and iron, probenecid, and oral contraceptives — with those mechanisms and results.
Trap: (E) reverses a real direction taken from a different table: penicillins enhance rather than abolish the anticoagulant effect of warfarin, by reducing vitamin K produced by gut flora. An option that flips the direction of a true interaction is much harder to reject than one that invents a drug.

Subtopic: cephalosporin administration and sound-alike names

Q: A neonate with hyperbilirubinemia is receiving a calcium-containing intravenous infusion. Which antibiotic order should the nurse question?
A) ceftriaxone
B) cefazolin
C) ampicillin
D) aztreonam
Answer: A
Rationale: Ceftriaxone carries two contraindication clauses, and this patient meets both: it is "not given to hyperbilirubinemic neonates or to patients with severe liver dysfunction," and it "should not be administered with calcium infusions."
Trap: (C) pulls because ampicillin is the drug most associated with neonates in this chapter, and because the neonatal contraindication most students hold is the sulfonamide one — sulfonamides are withheld in infants younger than 2 months because they displace bilirubin from albumin. Ceftriaxone is the drug the chapter pins to hyperbilirubinemic neonates specifically, and the calcium clause has no parallel anywhere else in the class.

Q: An order reads "cefuroxime 500 mg by mouth twice daily." Which formulation is correct, and why does the distinction matter?
A) Cefuroxime sodium, the injectable salt
B) Cefuroxime axetil, an oral prodrug hydrolyzed in the liver to active cefuroxime
C) Either salt, since they are interchangeable by route
D) Neither, since cefuroxime has no oral form
Answer: B
Rationale: The intravenous form is cefuroxime sodium and the oral form is a different salt, cefuroxime axetil, which is a prodrug "hydrolyzed in the liver to its active cefuroxime form."
Trap: (C) assumes that a shared generic name means a shared route, and that is precisely the sound-alike error the chapter warns about: "Many of the cephalosporin drug names sound alike, potentially leading to medication errors." Cue: axetil is the tablet, sodium is the shot.

Q: A patient starting an oral cephalosporin at home asks how to take it and what to watch for. Which teaching is accurate?
A) Take it with food if it upsets the stomach, and report diarrhea, abdominal cramps, rash, or itching
B) Take it on an empty stomach only, and expect a rise in serum potassium
C) Take it with an antacid to prevent stomach upset
D) Take it with milk to improve its absorption
Answer: A
Rationale: "Orally administered cephalosporins may be given with food to decrease gastrointestinal upset," and the most commonly reported adverse effects are "mild diarrhea, abdominal cramps, rash, pruritus, redness, and edema."
Trap: (C) contradicts the interaction table, where antacids and iron decrease the absorption of certain oral cephalosporins, specifically cefdinir and cefditoren. (B) borrows the penicillin electrolyte effect, which comes from the salt content of intravenous penicillin formulations rather than from anything a patient swallows.

Topic: carbapenem and monobactam therapy
Subtopic: carbapenem class rules

Q: An intravenous carbapenem is ordered for an acutely ill hospitalized patient. Over what period should the nurse infuse it, and why is the class held in reserve?
A) Over 60 minutes; carbapenems have the broadest antibacterial action of any antibiotics to date and are reserved for complicated infections in acutely ill hospitalized patients
B) Over 15 minutes; carbapenems are narrow-spectrum drugs used only for urinary infections
C) By intravenous push; carbapenems are unstable in solution
D) Over 60 minutes; carbapenems are bacteriostatic and need prolonged contact with the organism
Answer: A
Rationale: "Carbapenems must be infused over 60 minutes." They "have the broadest antibacterial action of any antibiotics to date. They are bactericidal and inhibit cell wall synthesis. Because of this, they are often reserved for complicated body cavity and connective tissue infections in acutely ill hospitalized patients."
Trap: (D) gets the infusion time right and the class action wrong, which is how a half-remembered fact passes as a whole answer. Carbapenems are bactericidal cell wall inhibitors, not bacteriostatic, and the 60-minute infusion has nothing to do with contact time.

Q: An older adult with renal impairment and a history of seizures is to receive a carbapenem. Which assessment and drug concern are most relevant?
A) Assess for a history of seizure activity; imipenem/cilastatin carries the highest seizure risk, especially in older adults and renally impaired patients
B) Assess for a history of seizure activity; carbapenems as a class do not cause seizures
C) Assess baseline hearing; seizures are not a carbapenem effect
D) Assess for a history of seizure activity; doripenem carries the highest seizure risk
Answer: A
Rationale: The assessment section directs the nurse to "assess patients for a history of seizure activity because of the potential seizure-type drug-induced reactions." Seizures have been associated with all of the carbapenems but are most likely to occur with imipenem/cilastatin, are more likely in older adults and renally impaired patients, and can be reduced by proper dosage adjustment in impaired patients. The imipenem figures run from up to 1.5% at less than 500 mg every 6 hours to a 10% incidence above that dose.
Trap: (D) pins the highest risk to the wrong end of the class: doripenem is the newest carbapenem and has less seizure potential than imipenem/cilastatin. (B) denies a class effect the chapter states outright, and (C) transplants a macrolide assessment into a carbapenem stem.

Subtopic: choosing among the carbapenems and aztreonam

Q: A patient with bacterial meningitis requires a carbapenem. Which drug is indicated?
A) imipenem/cilastatin
B) meropenem
C) ertapenem
D) doripenem
Answer: B
Rationale: Meropenem is "the only carbapenem currently indicated for treatment of bacterial meningitis." Compared with imipenem/cilastatin it is somewhat less active against gram-positive organisms, more active against Enterobacteriaceae, and equally active against P. aeruginosa.
Trap: (A) is the reflex pick because imipenem/cilastatin is the best-known carbapenem and reads as the all-purpose one. Its seizure risk is exactly why it is not the central nervous system drug. Cue: meropenem for meningitis.

Q: A patient with severe hospital-acquired pneumonia has an order for doripenem. Why should the nurse question it?
A) Doripenem must not be used for pneumonia because of an increased mortality risk
B) Doripenem has no activity against gram-negative organisms
C) Doripenem cannot be given intravenously
D) Doripenem is contraindicated in any patient with a penicillin allergy
Answer: A
Rationale: "Doripenem must not be used for pneumonia because of an increased mortality risk." Its indications are intraabdominal infections, pyelonephritis, and urinary tract infections.
Trap: (B) assumes a prohibition must reflect a coverage gap. A class with the broadest spectrum of any antibiotics looks like the obvious answer for a severe pneumonia, and the reason this drug is withheld is mortality data, not spectrum. (D) overstates the cross-allergy rule: only patients with anaphylactic-type reactions to penicillins must avoid a carbapenem.

Q: A patient with a documented penicillin allergy needs coverage for a gram-negative infection. Which beta-lactam does the chapter identify for this situation, and why?
A) cefazolin, because first-generation cephalosporins have no cross-reactivity
B) aztreonam, because it is thought to have less allergic cross-reactivity and is commonly given to patients with a penicillin allergy
C) ampicillin/sulbactam, because the inhibitor prevents the allergic reaction
D) imipenem/cilastatin, because carbapenems are not beta-lactams
Answer: B
Rationale: Aztreonam is the only monobactam in clinical use. It is bactericidal, destroys bacteria by inhibiting cell wall synthesis, and is "primarily active against aerobic gram-negative bacteria," including Escherichia coli, Klebsiella species, and Pseudomonas species. It is "most commonly used when gram-negative coverage is needed in patients with a penicillin allergy" and "is commonly given to patients who have a penicillin allergy." It is injectable only.
Trap: (C) is the most seductive wrong answer in this chapter's own vocabulary: a beta-lactamase inhibitor blocks a bacterial enzyme and has nothing to do with the patient's immune response, and this product still contains ampicillin. (D) misclassifies the carbapenems, which are one of the four beta-lactam subclasses. Note the three different thresholds the chapter sets — cephalosporins are withheld from patients with throat swelling or hives, carbapenems from those with anaphylactic-type reactions, and aztreonam is given outright.

Topic: macrolide therapy
Subtopic: choosing among the macrolides

Q: A patient with diabetes and delayed gastric emptying is prescribed erythromycin, and no infection is documented. What is the rationale for the order?
A) Erythromycin irritates the gastrointestinal tract, which stimulates smooth muscle and gastrointestinal motility
B) Erythromycin is being used to treat an occult infection causing the symptoms
C) Erythromycin neutralizes gastric acid
D) Erythromycin slows motility so that food is retained longer
Answer: A
Rationale: "A therapeutic effect of erythromycin outside its antibiotic actions is its ability to irritate the gastrointestinal tract, which stimulates smooth muscle and gastrointestinal motility." The chapter names diabetic gastroparesis as the benefiting condition and adds that erythromycin has been shown helpful in facilitating the passage of feeding tubes from the stomach into the small bowel.
Trap: (D) inverts the direction of the effect, and it is the intuitive inversion because nausea and vomiting are erythromycin's best-known adverse effects and those read as slowing rather than speeding. This is the chapter's clearest case of a drug prescribed for one of its adverse effects.

Q: A patient taking carbamazepine, warfarin, and theophylline needs a macrolide. Which choice minimizes interaction risk, and why?
A) erythromycin, because it is a strong inhibitor of cytochrome P450 enzymes
B) azithromycin, because it has minimal effects on the cytochrome P-450 enzymes
C) clarithromycin, because it is available orally only
D) Any macrolide, because macrolides are not protein bound
Answer: B
Rationale: Erythromycin "is a strong inhibitor of cytochrome P450 enzymes" and is "associated with many drug interactions," while azithromycin "is not as prone to such interactions... because of its minimal effects on the cytochrome P-450 enzymes." Carbamazepine, cyclosporine, theophylline, and warfarin compete for hepatic metabolism with macrolides, and the nursing section adds digoxin to that list.
Trap: (A) states a true fact and draws the opposite conclusion from it, which is the most common route by which an accurate memory produces a wrong answer. (D) reverses a class property: macrolides are highly protein bound and are metabolized in the liver, and those two properties are exactly what generate the interactions.

Q: A patient with known heart disease is to start clarithromycin, and the order specifies the intravenous route. Which two concerns should the nurse raise?
A) Clarithromycin has no intravenous form, and a 2018 FDA statement warns of a potential increased risk for heart problems or death in patients with heart disease
B) Clarithromycin has no oral form, and it is contraindicated in liver disease
C) Clarithromycin must be crushed before administration, and it causes hyperkalemia
D) Clarithromycin is safe in heart disease, but it must be given with fruit juice
Answer: A
Rationale: Clarithromycin is oral only and is dosed twice daily, while azithromycin is available both orally and by injection. In 2018 the FDA issued a cautionary statement that there is "a potential increased risk for heart problems or death when given to patients with heart disease."
Trap: (C) reverses the crushing rule, which is the sort of instruction that survives in memory without its direction: the extended-release preparation must not be crushed. (D) adds a second error on top of the first, since macrolides are not to be given with or immediately before or after fruit juices.

Subtopic: macrolide monitoring and indications

Q: Which baseline assessments are indicated before a patient begins macrolide therapy? Select all that apply.
A) Cardiac function and vital signs, because of palpitations, chest pain, and electrocardiographic changes
B) Hearing status, because of drug-induced hearing loss and tinnitus
C) Liver function and any history of liver disease
D) A review of other drugs the patient takes that prolong the QT interval
E) Serum calcium, because macrolides chelate calcium
Answer: A, B, C, D
Rationale: The assessment section directs the nurse to assess baseline cardiac function and vital signs, baseline hearing status, and liver function and history of liver disease. Table 38.8 lists QT prolongation among cardiovascular effects and hearing loss and tinnitus under other effects, and clarithromycin and erythromycin "are not to be used with moxifloxacin, pimozide, thioridazine, or other drugs that prolong the QT interval, because malignant dysrhythmias can occur."
Trap: (E) inserts the tetracycline property into a macrolide screen, since chelation of metallic ions belongs to that class. The hearing option is the one most often rejected, because ototoxicity is usually taught with a class that appears in the next chapter — but Table 38.8 genuinely lists hearing loss and tinnitus, and the assessment section reinforces it.

Q: A patient with a documented anaphylactic reaction to penicillin needs treatment for a streptococcal respiratory infection. Why is a macrolide an appropriate choice?
A) Macrolides are beta-lactams with a modified ring that lowers allergenicity
B) Macrolides are often used as alternative drugs for patients with allergies to beta-lactam antibiotics
C) Macrolides are bactericidal and therefore more reliable
D) Macrolides are the only class effective against Streptococcus pyogenes
Answer: B
Rationale: "Macrolides are often used as alternative drugs for patients with allergies to beta-lactam antibiotics," and infections caused by Streptococcus pyogenes, the group A beta-hemolytic streptococci, are inhibited by macrolides.
Trap: (A) assumes that a drug used in place of a beta-lactam must be chemically related to one. Macrolides have no beta-lactam ring and no cross-reactivity with penicillins, which is exactly why they can fill that role. (C) is wrong on class action as well: macrolides are bacteriostatic, though in high enough concentrations they may be bactericidal to some susceptible bacteria.

Q: One patient with C. difficile-associated diarrhea is prescribed fidaxomicin, and another receives bezlotoxumab. How do these two drugs differ?
A) Fidaxomicin is a macrolide antibiotic given orally with minimal absorption, while bezlotoxumab is not a macrolide and not an antibiotic but a monoclonal antibody given as a single intravenous dose
B) Both are macrolide antibiotics, differing only in route
C) Fidaxomicin is a monoclonal antibody and bezlotoxumab is the newest macrolide
D) Both are indicated for any antibiotic-associated diarrhea
Answer: A
Rationale: Fidaxomicin is "the newest macrolide antibiotic," indicated only for the treatment of C. difficile-associated diarrhea, with minimal absorption; its most common adverse effects are nausea, vomiting, and gastrointestinal bleed. The chapter flags the other drug itself: "Although, not a macrolide antibiotic," bezlotoxumab is a monoclonal antibody given as a single intravenous dose for the same condition.
Trap: (C) swaps the two drugs, which is easy because they are introduced in the same paragraph for the same indication. (D) widens the indication past what the chapter allows: fidaxomicin is indicated only for C. difficile-associated diarrhea, and antibiotic-associated diarrhea without infection is a common adverse effect rather than a superinfection.

Topic: tetracycline therapy
Subtopic: chelation and administration

Q: A patient's morning medications include doxycycline 200 mg, a multivitamin with iron, and an antacid that is also ordered twice daily. What is the priority teaching?
A) Take all three together with a full glass of water
B) Take the multivitamin and the antacid 2 hours before or 3 hours after the doxycycline
C) Take the doxycycline with milk to prevent stomach upset
D) Stop the antacid permanently while taking doxycycline
Answer: B
Rationale: Tetracyclines bind, or chelate, divalent and trivalent metallic ions to form insoluble complexes, so coadministration "with milk, antacids, or iron salts causes a considerable reduction in the oral absorption of the tetracycline." The administration rule is to take any of these foods, fluids, or drugs 2 hours before or 3 hours after the tetracycline.
Trap: (C) is the classic error, because milk reads as a stomach protector when here it is the interaction — milk or cheese with tetracycline is the chapter's named food-drug interaction. (A) fails on the same fact. Note too that the spacing numbers are deliberately asymmetric, 2 hours before and 3 hours after, and a distractor usually makes them match.

Q: A 6-year-old with a respiratory infection has an order for tetracycline. Why should the nurse question it?
A) Tetracyclines are avoided in children younger than 8 years because they can cause significant tooth discoloration
B) Tetracyclines are avoided in infants younger than 2 months
C) Tetracyclines are avoided in children younger than 6 months
D) Tetracyclines are safe at any age when given with food
Answer: A
Rationale: The strong affinity of tetracyclines for calcium "usually precludes their use in pediatric patients younger than 8 years of age, because it can result in significant tooth discoloration." They can also cause tooth enamel hypoplasia and possibly retard fetal skeletal development, and they must be avoided in pregnant women and nursing mothers, since the drugs pass into breast milk.
Trap: (B) and (C) are the two other age numbers in this chapter, held close enough to blur: sulfonamides are contraindicated in infants younger than 2 months, and clarithromycin is dosed in children older than 6 months. Cue: tetracyclines wait until 8.

Subtopic: individual tetracyclines

Q: A patient with the syndrome of inappropriate antidiuretic hormone secretion and hyponatremia is prescribed demeclocycline, and no infection is documented. What is the rationale?
A) Demeclocycline inhibits the action of antidiuretic hormone
B) Demeclocycline is treating an occult urinary infection causing the sodium loss
C) Demeclocycline replaces antidiuretic hormone
D) Demeclocycline is the tetracycline least likely to cause photosensitivity
Answer: A
Rationale: "Demeclocycline possesses a unique drug effect in that it inhibits the action of antidiuretic hormone, which makes it useful in the treatment of the syndrome of inappropriate secretion of antidiuretic hormone (SIADH)." It is derived from strains of Streptomyces and is available only for oral use.
Trap: (C) reverses the direction of the hormone effect, and the reversal matters clinically — replacing antidiuretic hormone would deepen the very hyponatremia the order is meant to correct. (D) inverts a second fact: photosensitivity is most frequent in patients taking demeclocycline.

Q: A patient with a complicated skin infection caused by MRSA is started on tigecycline. Which statement about this drug is accurate?
A) It is a glycylcycline given by injection only, effective against many organisms resistant to others in its class, with nausea and vomiting in 20% to 30% of patients
B) It is an oral tetracycline whose principal adverse effect is photosensitivity
C) It cannot be effective here, because resistance to one tetracycline implies resistance to all
D) It is a first-generation cephalosporin used for skin infections
Answer: A
Rationale: Tigecycline is the newest tetracycline, "referred to as a glycylcycline," and is structurally related to minocycline. It "differs from other tetracyclines in that it is effective against many organisms resistant to others in its class," is indicated for complicated skin and skin structure infections including MRSA and vancomycin-sensitive Enterococcus faecalis, is given by injection only, and "nausea and vomiting are the most common adverse effects, occurring in 20% to 30% of patients."
Trap: (C) applies a real class rule — "resistance to one tetracycline implies resistance to all" — to the single drug built to escape it. The two facts have to be held together rather than treated as a contradiction, because the class rule is what makes this drug's existence worth noting.

Q: Which teaching points are appropriate for a patient starting doxycycline? Select all that apply.
A) Take each dose with at least 8 ounces of water
B) Avoid dairy products, antacids, and iron preparations
C) Avoid tanning beds and direct sunlight, or use sunscreen and protective clothing
D) Report vaginal itching or a cottage cheese-like discharge
E) Use a reliable backup method of contraception in addition to an oral contraceptive
F) Take the drug with milk to improve absorption
Answer: A, B, C, D, E
Rationale: The chapter directs the patient to take oral doses with at least 8 oz of water and with food to minimize gastrointestinal upset, to avoid calcium, magnesium, iron, dairy products, and antacids, and to avoid tanning beds and direct sunlight; photosensitive effects may be noticed within a few minutes to hours and may last up to several days after the drug is discontinued. Altered intestinal and vaginal flora require monitoring for diarrhea and vaginal yeast infection, and reliable backup contraception must be used during antibiotic therapy.
Trap: (F) is the wrong-side option and reads as ordinary medication advice, because milk is the standard suggestion for a stomach-irritating drug. With this class milk is the interaction, not the remedy, and it reduces absorption rather than improving it.

Topic: superinfection and c difficile
Subtopic: recognizing superinfection

Q: A woman being treated with an antibiotic for a urinary tract infection reports severe vaginal itching and a thick whitish discharge. How should the nurse interpret this finding?
A) An expected response to antibiotic therapy that requires no action
B) A superinfection from overgrowth of an organism not susceptible to the antibiotic being used
C) A sign that the urinary tract infection is worsening
D) A sign that the causative organism has become resistant
Answer: B
Rationale: Superinfection can occur when antibiotics "reduce or completely eliminate the normal bacterial flora," after which "other bacteria or fungi are permitted to take over and cause infection." The textbook's canonical example is the development of a vaginal yeast infection when normal vaginal flora are reduced by antibiotic therapy. Signs to assess for include fever, perineal itching, oral lesions, vaginal irritation and discharge, cough, and lethargy.
Trap: (A) is the seductive option, because nausea, vomiting, and diarrhea genuinely are common with antibiotic therapy and usually are not severe enough to require discontinuation. The discriminator is whether the finding is a new infection or a direct gastrointestinal adverse effect, and candidiasis is a new organism.

Q: A patient recovering from influenza develops a bacterial pneumonia. Which description fits this event?
A) The second sense of superinfection, in which a second infection closely follows the initial one and comes from an external source
B) The first sense of superinfection, in which the patient's own normal flora overgrow during antibiotic therapy
C) A health care-associated infection by definition
D) Colonization of the respiratory tract
Answer: A
Rationale: The key term box gives two numbered senses of superinfection. The second is "a secondary microbial infection that occurs in addition to an earlier primary infection, often because of weakening of the patient's immune system function by the first infection," and the chapter's example is a patient with a viral respiratory infection who develops a secondary bacterial infection.
Trap: (B) is the mirror error of the previous item, which described the other sense. Thrush or vaginal candidiasis during antibiotic therapy is overgrowth of the patient's own flora, while this pneumonia arrived from outside; the two senses share a name and not an event.

Subtopic: C. difficile and prevention

Q: A patient treated with antibiotics last week develops watery diarrhea, abdominal pain, and fever. What should the nurse anticipate?
A) The patient will be tested for C. difficile infection and, if positive, treated and placed in isolation
B) The antibiotic will be continued, since diarrhea is expected with it
C) An antidiarrheal will be given and the finding charted as an adverse effect
D) Testing will be deferred unless the diarrhea persists another week
Answer: A
Rationale: The nursing rule is stated plainly: "Whenever a patient who was previously treated with antibiotics develops watery diarrhea, the patient needs to be tested for C. difficile infection. If the results are positive, the patient will need to be treated for this serious superinfection and placed in isolation." The symptom triad given is watery diarrhea, abdominal pain, and fever, and these infections are increasingly resistant to standard therapy.
Trap: (B) and (C) both rest on a true premise — antibiotic-associated diarrhea is a common adverse effect of antibiotics — and both stop one step short. Cue: diarrhea alone is an adverse effect, but watery diarrhea with abdominal pain and fever after antibiotics is a test-and-isolate item.

Q: A patient with a viral upper respiratory infection reports green sputum and asks for an antibiotic prescription. Which response is accurate?
A) Colored sputum confirms a bacterial superinfection and warrants an antibiotic
B) Colored sputum may or may not indicate a bacterial superinfection, and unnecessary antibiotics for a viral infection are avoided
C) Colored sputum always indicates C. difficile infection
D) An antibiotic should be started as prophylaxis against a bacterial superinfection
Answer: B
Rationale: "The presence of colored sputum (e.g., green or yellow) may or may not be a sign of a bacterial superinfection" during a viral respiratory illness, and the prescriber "needs to avoid prescribing unnecessary antibiotics for a viral infection."
Trap: (A) and (D) both act on the patient's expectation rather than on evidence, and the chapter names that pressure directly: patients "will often expect to receive an antibiotic prescription even when they show no signs of a bacterial superinfection." Overuse of broad-spectrum antibiotics is what contributes to resistance in the first place.

Q: Which points should the nurse include when teaching a patient about preventing and recognizing superinfection during antibiotic therapy? Select all that apply.
A) Consume probiotics such as products containing Lactobacillus, or cultured dairy products such as yogurt, buttermilk, or kefir
B) Report fever, perineal itching, oral lesions, or vaginal irritation and discharge
C) Take the antibiotic for the full prescribed length of time and around the clock
D) Report whitish, sore patches on the oral mucosa
E) Stop the antibiotic once the symptoms of the original infection improve
Answer: A, B, C, D
Rationale: Superinfection "may be prevented by the consumption of probiotics, such as products containing Lactobacillus; supplements; or cultured dairy products such as yogurt, buttermilk, and kefir," and there is some evidence that probiotics may help prevent C. difficile infections. The signs to report are fever, perineal itching, oral lesions, vaginal irritation and discharge, cough, and lethargy, and with tetracyclines specifically the whitish, sore patches of oral candidiasis. Antibiotics are taken as ordered, for the prescribed length of time and around the clock, and doses are not to be omitted or doubled up.
Trap: (E) is one of the two behaviors the chapter names as a cause of antibiotic resistance, alongside overprescribing: patients not completing the regimen. It reads as sensible self-care, which is exactly why it belongs on the wrong side of this list.
