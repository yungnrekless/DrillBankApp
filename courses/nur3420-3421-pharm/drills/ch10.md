# Chapter 10 drills — Analgesic Drugs

Authored from `sources/Ch10_Fact_Bank.md` (Lilley, Collins & Snyder, 10th ed.).

Blueprint weight is **19 questions of 121 (15.7%), depth `full`** — the heaviest
chapter in the entire Lilley blueprint, ahead of Ch 38 (18) and Ch 2 / Ch 44
(12 each). The split is 15 items on Exam 1 and 4 on the Final, so the weight is
front-loaded but does not disappear afterward.

That earns **75 drills**, the top of the `full` band (55 to 75). Nothing lighter
would cover it: the chapter carries a 40-term vocabulary box built almost
entirely from confusable pairs, an equianalgesia box the chapter attaches a
death warning to, and a transdermal fentanyl safety cluster with more named
hazards than any other single dosage form in the course.

Topics follow the chapter's own arc — how pain is classified, how it is
transmitted, the situations that change the rules, then opioid pharmacology
from receptor to reversal, then the nonopioids, then the nursing numbers.
Every topic name is qualified with what it is about (`opioid adverse effects`,
not `adverse effects`) so it stays distinct once all twenty chapters are banked.

Every distinction the chapter states in both directions is drilled from both
ends: threshold and tolerance, physical and psychologic dependence, naive and
tolerant, somatic and visceral, A fibers and C fibers, gate open and gate
closed, mu and kappa, agonist and antagonist, oral and parenteral, natural and
synthetic histamine release, short-acting and long half-life withdrawal,
immediate-release and extended-release, naloxone and naltrexone, acetaminophen
and the NSAIDs. Inverting those is the reflex error this chapter produces.

Topic: pain classification by onset, source, and cause
Subtopic: acute versus chronic pain

Q: A patient arrives with pain that began 40 minutes ago, is sharp and well localized over the right flank, and is accompanied by tachycardia, sweating, pallor, and an elevated blood pressure. Kidney stones are suspected. How does the nurse classify this pain, and what accounts for the vital sign changes?
A) Chronic pain, because the vital sign changes show the body has adapted to a long-standing stimulus
B) Acute pain, because the onset is sudden and the sympathetic nervous system is responding
C) Central pain, because an autonomic response localizes the problem to the central nervous system
D) Referred pain, because flank pain always originates outside the flank
Answer: B
Rationale: Table 10.2 gives acute pain as sudden in onset (minutes to hours), usually sharp and localized, with a physiologic sympathetic response of tachycardia, sweating, pallor, and increased blood pressure. Kidney stones are one of the table's own acute examples, alongside myocardial infarction, appendicitis, dental procedures, and surgical procedures.
Trap: (A) inverts the table. Chronic pain is slow in onset (days to months), dull and persistent, and carries no sympathetic surge, which is exactly why a chronic pain patient with normal vital signs is so often disbelieved.

Q: A patient with peripheral neuropathy reports a dull aching pain that has been present for the past 8 months. Vital signs are normal and the patient is calmly watching television. What is the correct interpretation?
A) The pain is chronic, and normal vital signs do not argue against it
B) The pain is acute, because it is still present
C) The normal vital signs indicate the reported pain level is exaggerated
D) The absence of a sympathetic response means the pain has resolved
Answer: A
Rationale: Table 10.2 lists peripheral neuropathy among its chronic examples, alongside arthritis, cancer, and lower back pain, and gives chronic pain as persistent or recurring and endless in duration. The chapter defines chronic pain as lasting 3 to 6 months or longer. The physiologic sympathetic response belongs to the acute column only.
Trap: (C) is the mirror error of the previous item and is the clinically dangerous one. Having just learned that acute pain comes with visible vital sign changes, a learner reads calm vital signs as evidence against pain.

Subtopic: somatic versus visceral versus superficial pain

Q: Two patients report severe pain. The first has pain arising from skeletal muscles, ligaments, and joints; the second has pain arising from an obstructed hollow organ. On the chapter's own pairing of source to drug class, which statement is correct?
A) The first responds best to nonopioid analgesics such as NSAIDs, and the second usually requires opioids
B) Both are visceral in origin and both require opioids
C) The first usually requires opioids, and the second responds best to NSAIDs
D) The source of pain does not predict which analgesic class is effective
Answer: A
Rationale: Somatic pain originates from skeletal muscles, ligaments, and joints and responds best to nonopioid analgesics such as NSAIDs, and the chapter includes bone pain in that group. Visceral pain originates from organs and smooth muscles and usually requires opioids for relief.
Trap: (C) is the exact reversal, and it is the reflex answer because severe pain pulls for the strong drug regardless of source. Cue: soma is the body wall, so skeletal muscle, ligaments, joints, and bone go to nonopioids.

Q: A patient has severe pain from extensive breakdown of the mucous membranes of the mouth. Which analgesic class does the chapter pair with pain of this source?
A) Nonopioids, because superficial pain is grouped with somatic pain for treatment
B) Opioids, because superficial pain, like visceral pain, usually requires opioids for relief
C) No analgesic, because superficial pain is by definition mild
D) Adjuvant anticonvulsants, because all mucous membrane pain is neuropathic
Answer: B
Rationale: Superficial pain originates from the skin and mucous membranes and, like visceral pain, usually requires opioids for relief. Deep pain occurs in tissues below skin level and is defined only as the opposite of superficial pain.
Trap: (A) exploits an asymmetry the chapter builds in. Superficial and deep are the anatomically opposed pair, so a learner expects superficial to be treated as the opposite of visceral. For treatment it groups with visceral instead.

Subtopic: pain classified by cause

Q: Three patients describe pain felt somewhere other than where the nurse would expect it: one has cholecystitis with pain in the back and scapular area, one has burning and tingling in a leg amputated 6 months ago, and one has pain that began after a stroke. Which set of classifications is correct?
A) Referred pain, phantom pain, central pain
B) Phantom pain, referred pain, central pain
C) Referred pain, central pain, phantom pain
D) Central pain, phantom pain, referred pain
Answer: A
Rationale: Referred pain occurs when visceral nerve fibers synapse at a level in the spinal cord close to fibers supplying specific subcutaneous tissues, and the chapter's own example is cholecystitis pain referred to the back and scapular areas. Phantom pain occurs in the area of a body part removed surgically or traumatically and is often described as burning, itching, tingling, or stabbing. Central pain results from a disorder causing central nervous system damage, and the chapter names stroke among its causes.
Trap: (C) swaps the last two, which is the common error because an amputation and a stroke both involve damaged neural tissue. The discriminator is whether the body part is gone (phantom) or the central nervous system itself is damaged in an intact body (central). Note the chapter's own edge case: pain in a paralyzed limb after spinal cord injury is filed under phantom.

Q: A nurse is reviewing the causes of pain named in this chapter. Which statements match the chapter's descriptions? Select all that apply.
A) Vascular pain is thought to account for a large percentage of migraine headaches
B) Neuropathic pain usually results from damage to peripheral or central nervous system nerve fibers and may also be idiopathic
C) Cancer pain most often results from pressure of the tumor mass against nerves, organs, or tissues
D) Cancer pain is by definition chronic and is never acute
E) Phantom pain cannot occur in a limb that is still attached to the body
Answer: A, B, C
Rationale: The chapter states that vascular pain originates from vascular or perivascular tissues and accounts for a large percentage of migraine headaches; that neuropathic pain usually results from damage to peripheral or central nervous system nerve fibers by disease or injury but may also be idiopathic; and that cancer pain most often results from pressure of the tumor mass against nerves, organs, or tissues, with hypoxia, metastases, pathologic fractures, muscle spasms, and treatment adverse effects as other causes.
Trap: (D) is the reflex exclusion, because cancer is a chronic illness. The chapter says cancer pain can be acute or chronic or both. (E) contradicts the chapter's own edge case, which is that phantom pain can also occur in paralyzed limbs after spinal cord injury.

Topic: pain threshold, tolerance, and dependence terms
Subtopic: threshold versus tolerance

Q: Two patients receive the same stimulus during a dressing change. Both report that the stimulus registers as painful at the same intensity, but one continues the activity while the other stops. Which pair of terms describes what is the same and what differs?
A) Pain threshold is the same, and pain tolerance differs
B) Pain tolerance is the same, and pain threshold differs
C) Both threshold and tolerance are identical, and the difference is drug tolerance
D) The two terms describe the same phenomenon and cannot differ within one patient
Answer: A
Rationale: Pain threshold is the level of a stimulus that results in the sensation of pain, and the chapter describes it as similar for most individuals. Pain tolerance is the amount of pain a patient can endure without it interfering with normal function, and it can vary from patient to patient.
Trap: (B) is the reflex inversion, because both terms sound like "how much pain a person can take." Cue: threshold is physiologic and is where pain first registers, while tolerance is psychologic and is how long you keep going.

Q: A nurse reads that a patient's cultural background, attitude, and environment have changed how much pain the patient will endure before stopping an activity. Which term is being described?
A) Pain threshold, which varies widely with culture and attitude
B) Pain tolerance, which can vary from patient to patient
C) Opioid tolerance, which develops after a week of opioid use
D) Analgesic ceiling effect, which limits how much relief a drug can give
Answer: B
Rationale: Pain tolerance is the amount of pain a patient can endure without it interfering with normal function and can vary from patient to patient, unlike pain threshold, which the chapter describes as similar for most individuals. If a stem varies by culture, attitude, or environment, it is describing tolerance.
Trap: (A) is the mirror error of the previous item. (C) is the second-order trap the key terms box sets: pain tolerance and drug tolerance share a word but are unrelated concepts, and only the drug one is defined as needing larger doses for the same effect.

Subtopic: tolerance, physical dependence, and addiction

Q: A patient on long-term opioid therapy for chronic back pain reports that the same dose no longer controls the pain, and states plainly that the only reason for wanting more is relief. There is no craving and no use for other effects. Which term applies?
A) Psychologic dependence
B) Addiction
C) Tolerance
D) Analgesic ceiling effect
Answer: C
Rationale: Tolerance is the state in which repetitive exposure to a given drug over time induces changes in drug receptors that reduce the drug's effects, so larger doses are required to maintain the same level of analgesia. The key terms box equates opioid tolerance with physical dependence and calls it a normal physiologic condition.
Trap: (A) and (B) are the same thing in this chapter's own equation, and they pull because needing more sounds like addiction. The discriminator the chapter gives is why the patient wants the drug: relief is not addiction, and use for effects other than pain relief is.

Q: A patient who has been receiving opioids has doses stopped abruptly and develops unpleasant physical withdrawal symptoms, but has never craved the drug or used it for anything but pain. What does this demonstrate?
A) Psychologic dependence, which the chapter calls addiction
B) Physical dependence, the physical adaptation of the body to the presence of an opioid
C) That the patient has been diverting the medication
D) An analgesic ceiling effect
Answer: B
Rationale: Physical dependence is the condition in which unpleasant physical withdrawal symptoms occur if a drug taken over a period of time is stopped abruptly or smaller doses are given, and the chapter calls it the physical adaptation of the body to the presence of an opioid. Psychologic dependence, which the chapter equates with addiction, is a pattern of compulsive use with continuous craving and use for effects other than pain relief.
Trap: (A) is the mirror error of the previous item, and it is the one that causes real harm. Withdrawal on abrupt discontinuation is expected physiology, not evidence of addiction, and the chapter says outright that psychologic dependence in patients on escalating opioids for cancer pain is unusual.

Subtopic: opioid naive versus opioid tolerant

Q: A patient who has been buying opioids on the street for several months is admitted after a fracture. The prescriber asks the nurse which term applies and what the dosing implication is. Which response is correct?
A) Opioid naive, and doses should be reduced because of possible sensitivity
B) Opioid tolerant, and the patient will generally require higher dosages
C) Opioid naive, because the drug was not legally prescribed
D) Opioid tolerant, but the illicit source means standard opioid-naive dosing applies
Answer: B
Rationale: Opioid tolerant is defined as the opposite of opioid naive and describes patients who have been receiving opioid analgesics, legally or otherwise, for a period of 1 week or longer. The chapter states that habitual street opioid users and patients with chronic pain are opioid tolerant and generally require high dosages.
Trap: (C) and (D) both turn on the phrase "legally or otherwise," which the definition includes precisely so that an illicit source cannot be used to justify naive dosing. That is the undertreatment error the chapter condemns as inappropriate and inhumane clinical practice.

Topic: nociception and gate theory
Subtopic: the four processes of nociception

Q: A patient's injured tissue releases chemicals that act on peripheral nerve fiber receptors and convert the noxious stimulus into a pain impulse. Which of the four processes of nociception is this, and which process runs in the opposite direction?
A) Transduction, and modulation is the process that runs downward from the brain stem
B) Transmission, and perception runs downward from the brain
C) Modulation, and transduction runs downward from the brain
D) Perception, and transmission runs downward from the spinal cord
Answer: A
Rationale: Fig. 10.1 numbers the four processes as transduction (noxious stimuli propagate the pain message at the injury), transmission (the pain impulse moves from the spinal cord to the brain), perception of pain at the brain, and modulation, in which neurons from the brain stem release neurotransmitters that block the pain impulse. Only modulation runs downward. The chemicals released by injured cells are bradykinin, histamine, potassium, prostaglandins, and serotonin.
Trap: (B) is the reflex pick, because transduction and transmission differ by three letters and both describe early events. Transduction happens at the injury; transmission carries the impulse from cord to brain.

Subtopic: the gate and the nerve fibers that control it

Q: A nurse rubs a patient's shoulder and the patient's pain eases. Using gate theory, what has happened at the dorsal horn of the spinal cord?
A) Large myelinated A fibers were recruited, closing the gate so that impulses are not transmitted to the higher centers of the brain
B) The gate was opened, which is what prevents pain from being sensed
C) Small unmyelinated C fibers were recruited, which closes the gate
D) The threshold of the gate cells was lowered, allowing more impulses through
Answer: A
Rationale: Closing of the gate is affected by activation of A fibers, which inhibits impulse transmission to the brain and avoids pain sensation. The chapter gives massage and liniment as its own example: large sensory A nerve fibers from peripheral receptors carry pain-modulating impulses to the spinal cord, and the A fibers tend to close the gate. If impulses are stopped by a gate at this junction, no impulses are transmitted to the higher centers of the brain.
Trap: (B) inverts the gate's logic, which is counterintuitive by exactly one step: a closed gate means no pain, so effective analgesia is a closed gate. (C) has the two fiber types crossed.

Q: A patient's dull, poorly localized pain worsens. Which nerve fiber is carrying it, and what is that fiber doing to the gate?
A) A fibers, which are unmyelinated and slow, opening the gate
B) C fibers, which are small, unmyelinated, and slow, opening the gate so that pain is sensed
C) C fibers, which are large and fast, closing the gate
D) A fibers, which are large and fast, closing the gate
Answer: B
Rationale: Table 10.3 gives C fibers as having no myelin sheath, small fiber size, and slow conduction, producing dull and nonlocalized pain. Opening of the gate is affected by the stimulation of C fibers, which allows impulses to be transmitted to the brain and pain to be sensed.
Trap: (D) is the mirror error of the previous item, and every attribute it lists is genuinely true of A fibers, which is what makes it attractive. A fibers carry sharp, well-localized pain and close the gate. (A) attaches one fiber's name to the other fiber's attributes.

Subtopic: endogenous opioids and conditions that alter the threshold

Q: A runner reports euphoria and reduced pain after prolonged exertion. Which explanation matches the chapter?
A) Endogenous substances bond with opioid receptors and close the spinal cord gates, inhibiting the transmission of pain impulses
B) Endogenous substances open the spinal cord gates so that pain impulses bypass the brain
C) Exertion depletes prostaglandins, which are the body's only pain mediator
D) Enkephalins are synthetic drugs given to produce the runner's high
Answer: A
Rationale: Enkephalins and endorphins are produced within the body to fight pain and are considered the body's painkillers. Both are capable of bonding with opioid receptors and inhibiting the transmission of pain impulses by closing the spinal cord gates. Endorphin is a condensed version of endogenous morphine, and these substances are released whenever the body experiences pain or prolonged exertion, which is responsible for the phenomenon of runner's high.
Trap: (B) is the gate inversion again in a new setting. (D) forgets that enkephalins are endogenous; Table 10.5 lists enkephalins as the prototypical agonist at the delta receptor, and that prototype is a body substance rather than a marketed drug.

Q: A nurse plans care so that a patient's pain threshold is not lowered further. Per Table 10.1, which conditions lower the pain threshold? Select all that apply.
A) Anxiety
B) Sleeplessness
C) Diversion
D) Anger
E) Rest
F) Empathy
Answer: A, B, D
Rationale: Table 10.1 lists anger, anxiety, depression, discomfort, fear, isolation, chronic pain, sleeplessness, and tiredness in the lowered column, and diversion, empathy, rest, sympathy, and medications (analgesics, antianxiety drugs, antidepressants) in the raised column.
Trap: (C), (E), and (F) all come from the raising column, and they pull because each is something a nurse actively provides, so they feel like the nursing answers. Note the table's own internal inconsistency: it is titled Conditions That Alter Pain Tolerance while its first column header reads Pain Threshold, so do not read it as evidence that the two terms are interchangeable.

Topic: special pain situations and pca safety
Subtopic: patient-controlled analgesia

Q: A nurse finds a patient's spouse pressing the PCA button while the patient sleeps, explaining that the patient was hurting earlier and should not wake up in pain. What is the correct nursing response and why?
A) Document the spouse as an approved second operator so that the dosing is recorded
B) Stop the practice, because this is PCA by proxy, which the Institute for Safe Medication Practices advises against and which has caused numerous deaths
C) Allow it as long as the spouse presses no more often than the lockout permits
D) Allow it only during sleep, when the patient cannot press the button
Answer: B
Rationale: PCA is designed so that patients self-medicate by pressing a button on the infusion pump. The chapter names the hazard directly: well-meaning family members pressing the dosing button rather than letting able patients do so on their own, called PCA by proxy, and it states that numerous deaths have occurred when family members administered too much of the opioid drug. The Institute for Safe Medication Practices advises against PCA by proxy.
Trap: (C) is the reflex compromise, because the lockout feels like a hardware guarantee of safety. The lockout limits the dose, not the patient's level of sedation, and the whole safety logic of PCA is that an oversedated patient cannot press the button.

Q: A postoperative patient is too confused to operate a PCA pump reliably. What does the chapter direct?
A) Have the nurse press the button on the patient's behalf at scheduled intervals
B) Have a family member press the button whenever the patient appears uncomfortable
C) Use a different method of pain control
D) Continue the PCA and accept that the patient will receive less drug
Answer: C
Rationale: The chapter states that for patients who are unable to self-medicate using the PCA pump, a different method of pain control must be used. Morphine and hydromorphone are the drugs commonly given by PCA, and the method has been shown to be very effective and to reduce the total opioid dose used.
Trap: (A) is the same proxy error wearing a uniform, and it pulls harder than (B) because a nurse pressing the button feels professional rather than well-meaning. The chapter's alternative is not a different operator but a different method.

Subtopic: opioid-tolerant patients and breakthrough pain

Q: A patient who takes long-acting opioids daily for chronic back pain is admitted with a new fracture. How should the acute pain be managed?
A) Hold the chronic regimen and start standard opioid-naive doses for the fracture
B) Continue an equivalent amount of the chronic pain medication and add an extra 20% to 40% more opioid to treat the acute pain
C) Substitute an agonist-antagonist to reduce the risk for oversedation
D) Treat with nonopioids only, because the patient is already opioid tolerant
Answer: B
Rationale: The chapter states that effective management of acute-on-chronic pain requires patients to receive equivalent amounts of their chronic pain medication in addition to an extra 20% to 40% more opioids to treat the acute pain. Longer-acting opioids such as extended-release oxycodone are usually better choices for these patients, because shorter-acting drugs are more likely to produce a psychologic high that reinforces addictive tendencies.
Trap: (A) is the undertreatment error the chapter spends a paragraph condemning; a tolerant patient started at naive doses gets neither baseline nor acute coverage. (C) is worse than it looks, because an agonist-antagonist given to an opioid-tolerant patient can both reduce analgesic effects and cause withdrawal symptoms.

Q: A patient on scheduled extended-release oxycodone reports pain returning between doses. Which regimen matches the chapter?
A) Give immediate-release oxycodone as needed between the scheduled extended-release doses
B) Give extended-release oxycodone as needed between scheduled immediate-release doses
C) Crush the extended-release tablet so that it acts faster during the painful interval
D) Discontinue the extended-release product and use as-needed dosing only
Answer: A
Rationale: Breakthrough pain is pain that occurs between doses of pain medication, and the chapter's regimen is as-needed doses of immediate-release dosage forms given between scheduled doses of extended-release dosage forms. If the patient requires larger doses for breakthrough pain, the interval of the scheduled extended-release opioid may need to be shortened or a more potent drug started.
Trap: (B) reverses which form is scheduled and which is as needed, producing a regimen that undertreats baseline pain and oversedates at the same time. (C) is the fatal one: chewing or crushing any extended-release opioid can cause oversedation, respiratory depression, and even death from rapid drug absorption.

Subtopic: cancer pain priorities

Q: A patient with cancer requires escalating opioid doses and develops withdrawal symptoms when a dose is missed. The family asks whether the patient is becoming an addict. Which response reflects the chapter?
A) The patient is showing physical dependence, which is expected, and actual addiction in such patients is unusual
B) The patient is showing psychologic dependence, and the dose should be reduced
C) Any withdrawal symptom confirms addiction and requires an addiction referral
D) Preventing addiction is the main consideration in cancer pain management
Answer: A
Rationale: The chapter states that cancer patients usually require increasingly higher opioid doses and do become physically dependent, but that actual psychologic dependence or addiction in such patients is unusual. It also states that the main consideration in pain management associated with cancer is patient comfort and not trying to prevent drug addiction.
Trap: (D) is the reflex pick for anyone carrying the general cultural anxiety about opioids, and the chapter reverses it explicitly for this population: control of the patient's pain takes ethical and clinical priority over concerns regarding drug addiction.

Topic: who analgesic ladder and adjuvant analgesics
Subtopic: the three-step ladder

Q: A patient's newly assessed pain is being treated with a nonopioid alone, with an adjuvant added. Which step of the WHO analgesic ladder is this, and what does the presence of the adjuvant tell you?
A) Step 1, and the adjuvant does not change the step, because adjuvants are optional at every step
B) Step 2, because adding any second drug advances the patient to step 2
C) Step 2, because adjuvants are what define step 2
D) Step 3, because adjuvants are reserved for the highest step
Answer: A
Rationale: Step 1 of the WHO ladder is the use of nonopioids, with or without adjuvant medications, once the pain has been identified and assessed. Steps 2 and 3 are both described as with or without adjuvants, so adjuvants are optional at every step and it is the opioid that advances the step.
Trap: (C) and (D) both try to make adjuvants the discriminator, which is the single most common way this ladder is misread. What moves a patient from step 1 to step 2 is the addition of an opioid, and from step 2 to step 3 an opioid indicated for moderate to severe pain.

Q: A patient on a nonopioid plus an adjuvant reports pain that persists and is increasing. According to the ladder, what comes next?
A) Add an opioid, with or without nonopioids and with or without adjuvants
B) Remove the adjuvant so that the nonopioid can be titrated upward
C) Move directly to opioids indicated for moderate to severe pain
D) Repeat step 1 with a different nonopioid before advancing
Answer: A
Rationale: Step 2 of the ladder states that if pain persists or increases, treatment is the use of opioids with or without nonopioids and with or without adjuvants. Step 3, opioids indicated for moderate to severe pain, follows only if pain persists or increases again.
Trap: (C) is the mirror error of skipping a rung, and it is tempting because increasing pain feels like a step 3 problem. Note the chapter's own caveat as well: not all patients will be treated effectively using the ladder method, and some may need to seek an experienced pain management physician.

Subtopic: adjuvants and neuropathic pain

Q: A patient describes burning, tingling, electrical sensations in both feet and says that the bed sheets hurt. Opioid doses have been increased twice without benefit. What does the chapter indicate?
A) Increase the opioid again, because the dose is simply not high enough yet
B) Add an adjuvant such as gabapentin, pregabalin, or amitriptyline, because opioids are not completely effective in neuropathic pain
C) Switch to an opioid agonist-antagonist for its ceiling effect
D) Nothing further is available, because the pain is idiopathic
Answer: B
Rationale: Adjuvant drugs are commonly used in the treatment of neuropathic pain, in which opioids are not completely effective, and the chapter names the antidepressant amitriptyline and the anticonvulsants gabapentin and pregabalin. Hypersensitivity or hyperalgesia to mild stimuli such as light touch or the bed sheets on a person's feet is also known as allodynia, and neuropathic descriptors include heat, cold, numbness and tingling, burning, or electrical sensations.
Trap: (A) is the reflex escalation and it is the cleanest right-drug-class, wrong-pain-type error the chapter sets up. Burning, tingling, electrical sensations, and allodynia are the cue to change class, not to raise the dose.

Q: Which adjuvants does the chapter's nursing-section list identify specifically as treatments for neuropathic pain? Select all that apply.
A) Anticonvulsants
B) Tricyclic antidepressants
C) Local anesthetics
D) Psychostimulants
E) Neuroleptics
F) Hydroxyzine
Answer: A, B, C
Rationale: The adjuvant list gives anticonvulsants for treatment of neuropathic pain, tricyclic antidepressants for neuropathic pain and for their innate analgesic and opioid-potentiating effects, and local anesthetics for treatment of neuropathic pain. Exactly three of the nine adjuvants on that list are assigned to neuropathic pain.
Trap: (D) is the one most often added, because psychostimulants sit on the same list; they are there for reduction of opioid-induced sedation when opioid dosage adjustment is not effective. (E) is listed for chronic pain syndromes, and (F) for mild antianxiety properties plus sedating, antihistamine, and mild antiemetic actions.

Q: A prescriber adds an NSAID and a corticosteroid to a patient's opioid regimen. When the patient asks why more drugs are being added rather than simply more opioid, which explanation matches the chapter?
A) Adjuvants permit smaller dosages of opioids and reduce adverse effects such as respiratory depression, constipation, and urinary retention
B) Adjuvants replace the opioid entirely once therapeutic levels are reached
C) Adjuvants are added because opioids have an analgesic ceiling that limits their dosing
D) Adjuvants prevent the development of physical dependence on the opioid
Answer: A
Rationale: The chapter states that the adjuvant approach allows the use of smaller dosages of opioids and reduces some of the adverse effects seen with higher dosages, such as respiratory depression, constipation, and urinary retention, and that it permits drugs with different mechanisms of action to produce synergistic effects. Antiemetics and laxatives may also be needed alongside.
Trap: (C) borrows a real term from the wrong drugs. The analgesic ceiling belongs to the agonists-antagonists such as pentazocine and nalbuphine, and among full agonists to codeine alone; it is not why adjuvants are combined with morphine.

Topic: opioid receptors and chemical classes
Subtopic: mu, kappa, and delta

Q: A patient receiving an opioid develops respiratory depression and euphoria. Stimulation of which receptor type accounts for these effects, and what is that receptor's prototypical agonist?
A) kappa, with ketocyclazocine as the prototype
B) mu, with morphine as the prototype
C) delta, with enkephalins as the prototype
D) kappa, with morphine as the prototype
Answer: B
Rationale: Table 10.5 gives the mu receptor's effects as supraspinal analgesia, respiratory depression, euphoria, and sedation, with morphine as the prototypical agonist. The chapter adds that the mu receptors in the dorsal horn of the spinal cord appear to play the most crucial role, with kappa and delta less important but still involved.
Trap: (A) pins respiratory depression to kappa, which is the standard crossing of the two rows. Both mu and kappa produce analgesia and sedation, so the discriminators are the other effects listed on each row.

Q: A patient receiving an opioid shows spinal analgesia, sedation, and marked pupillary constriction. Which row of Table 10.5 does this match?
A) mu, whose analgesia is supraspinal
B) kappa, whose listed effects are spinal analgesia, sedation, and miosis
C) delta, whose only listed effect is analgesia
D) mu, because miosis is listed as a mu effect
Answer: B
Rationale: Table 10.5 gives kappa as producing spinal analgesia, sedation, and miosis, with ketocyclazocine as its prototypical agonist. Delta is the sparse row, listing analgesia only, with enkephalins as its prototype.
Trap: (D) is the mirror error of the previous item. Cue: mu is supraspinal analgesia plus respiratory depression and euphoria, which are the two effects that make opioids dangerous and abusable, and kappa is spinal analgesia plus miosis.

Subtopic: chemical class and cross-allergy

Q: A patient has documented anaphylaxis to morphine and now needs an opioid. Using Table 10.4, which choice is appropriate and why?
A) fentanyl, because it is a meperidine-like drug and therefore structurally unrelated to morphine
B) hydrocodone, because it is weaker than morphine
C) oxycodone, because it is a synthetic and cannot cross-react
D) codeine, because only about 10% of a dose is converted to morphine
Answer: A
Rationale: Table 10.4 groups meperidine, fentanyl, remifentanil, sufentanil, and alfentanil as meperidine-like, and morphine, heroin, hydromorphone, codeine, hydrocodone, and oxycodone as morphine-like. The chapter's own example is that a patient who experiences anaphylaxis from morphine may appropriately be given a drug with an unrelated structure such as fentanyl.
Trap: (B), (C), and (D) are all morphine-like drugs on Table 10.4, and each is chosen for a reason that has nothing to do with structure. Chemical class predicts cross-allergy, not potency, and the trap inside the trap is that the three weaker-sounding options are exactly the ones that share morphine's structure.

Q: Which statements about opioid classification and abuse potential match the chapter? Select all that apply.
A) Codeine and hydrocodone are classified as mild agonists
B) Morphine, hydromorphone, oxycodone, meperidine, fentanyl, and methadone are classified as strong agonists
C) Opioids with an affinity for mu receptors and a rapid onset of action produce marked euphoria and are most likely to be abused
D) Of the three clinically useful opium alkaloids, only morphine and codeine relieve pain, and papaverine is a smooth muscle relaxant
E) Oxycodone is classified as a mild agonist because it is dispensed in small milligram doses
Answer: A, B, C, D
Rationale: The chapter lists codeine and hydrocodone as mild agonists and morphine, hydromorphone, oxycodone, meperidine, fentanyl, and methadone as strong agonists. Topic: opioid agonists, antagonists, and ceiling effect
Subtopic: the three action types

Q: A drug binds to a pain receptor and produces a weaker analgesic response than a full agonist. Two names for this class appear in the key terms box. Which statement is correct?
A) Agonist-antagonist and partial agonist name the same class in this chapter
B) Agonist-antagonist and partial agonist are two distinct drug classes
C) A partial agonist blocks all response at the receptor
D) An agonist-antagonist produces a stronger response than a full agonist
Answer: A
Rationale: The key terms box defines agonists-antagonists as substances that bind to a receptor and cause a partial response that is not as strong as that caused by an agonist, also known as a partial agonist, and defines partial agonist as a drug that binds a receptor and causes a response less than that caused by a full agonist, same as agonist-antagonist. Different drugs in the class exert their effects by binding in different degrees to kappa and mu receptors.
Trap: (B) is the reflex pick, because the two names sound as though they must describe different things and because the antagonist half of the compound word suggests blockade. The chapter equates them explicitly, so an option treating them as separate classes is wrong on the chapter's own equation.

Q: A drug binds to the opioid receptor, produces no analgesia, and reverses the effects of drugs already bound there. Which term applies?
A) Agonist
B) Partial agonist
C) Competitive antagonist
D) Adjuvant analgesic
Answer: C
Rationale: An antagonist binds to a pain receptor but does not reduce pain signals, and functions as a competitive antagonist because it competes with and reverses the effects of agonist and agonist-antagonist drugs at the receptor sites. An agonist by contrast binds an opioid pain receptor and causes an analgesic response.
Trap: (B) is the mirror error of the previous item. A partial agonist still produces a response, only a weaker one; an antagonist produces none and undoes what is already bound.

Subtopic: the analgesic ceiling and when agonists-antagonists are used

Q: A patient receiving nalbuphine has the dose increased twice with no further pain relief. What explains this, and which full agonist shares the property?
A) An analgesic ceiling effect, and codeine is the only full agonist the chapter says possesses one
B) An analgesic ceiling effect, which every opioid including morphine possesses
C) Opioid tolerance, which develops within hours of the first dose
D) Physical dependence, which blunts analgesia at higher doses
Answer: A
Rationale: The opiate agonists-antagonists such as pentazocine and nalbuphine are associated with an analgesic ceiling effect, meaning the drug reaches a maximum analgesic effect so that analgesia does not improve even with higher dosages. Separately, codeine is less effective as an analgesic and is the only agonist to possess a ceiling effect. Such drugs are useful only in patients who have not been previously exposed to opioids.
Trap: (B) overgeneralizes a real property to every opioid and is wrong on morphine. The mirror version, that no full agonist has a ceiling, is wrong on codeine, so the chapter penalizes both sweeping answers.

Q: An opioid-tolerant patient on scheduled morphine is prescribed butorphanol for additional relief. What is the nurse's concern?
A) Butorphanol is a Schedule II drug and cannot be combined with another Schedule II drug
B) Agonists-antagonists are not to be given concurrently with full agonists, because they may both reduce analgesic effects and cause withdrawal symptoms in opioid-tolerant patients
C) Butorphanol carries a higher incidence of respiratory depression than morphine
D) Agonists-antagonists must not be given to patients with a history of opioid addiction
Answer: B
Rationale: The chapter states that agonists-antagonists are not to be given concurrently with full opioid agonists, because they may both reduce analgesic effects and cause withdrawal symptoms in opioid-tolerant patients. The class is Schedule IV, has a lower risk for misuse and addiction, and produces a lower incidence of respiratory depression than the opioids. Table 10.8 bars pentazocine, butorphanol, and nalbuphine from cancer pain for precipitated withdrawal, the ceiling effect, and dysphoria, delusions, and hallucinations.
Trap: (C) reverses one of the class's genuine advantages, and (D) reverses another. A history of opioid addiction is one of the two settings the chapter names for choosing these drugs, along with short-term pain control after obstetric procedures, where they avoid oversedation of the mother and fetus.

Topic: opioid equianalgesic conversion
Subtopic: oral versus parenteral direction

Q: A patient taking oral morphine must be switched to the intravenous route. Using Box 10.2, what happens to the milligram number, and why?
A) It goes down, because 30 mg oral morphine equals 10 mg parenteral morphine, a 3 to 1 ratio
B) It goes up, because parenteral drug is less efficient than oral drug
C) It stays the same, because the drug itself is unchanged
D) It goes down by a factor of 7, the same ratio the chapter uses for hydromorphone
Answer: A
Rationale: Box 10.2 gives morphine as 30 mg oral and 10 mg parenteral, an oral-to-parenteral ratio of 3 to 1. The oral number is always the larger one, because oral drug is partly lost to first-pass metabolism.
Trap: (B) inverts the ratio, and it is the direction that kills: a conversion that raises the milligram number when switching from oral to intravenous triples the delivered dose. (D) imports hydromorphone's potency figure into a route conversion.

Q: A patient on oral hydromorphone is converted to the parenteral route and the nurse applies morphine's 3 to 1 ratio. What is wrong with this?
A) Nothing, because one oral-to-parenteral ratio applies to every opioid
B) Hydromorphone's ratio is 5 to 1 (7.5 mg oral to 1.5 mg parenteral), so the 3 to 1 ratio overdoses the patient
C) Hydromorphone's ratio is 7 to 1, the same figure as its potency relative to morphine
D) Hydromorphone has no parenteral form, so no conversion is possible
Answer: B
Rationale: Box 10.2 gives hydromorphone as 7.5 mg oral to 1.5 mg parenteral, an oral-to-parenteral ratio of 5 to 1, against morphine's 3 to 1. Applying morphine's ratio to hydromorphone overdoses the patient by two-thirds.
Trap: (C) is the mirror trap the chapter builds by printing two different numbers about the same drug. The 5 to 1 figure is hydromorphone oral to parenteral; the 7 to 1 figure is hydromorphone versus morphine. Confusing them turns a route conversion into a drug conversion.

Subtopic: potency versus dose size

Q: A nurse gives a patient morphine and, a short time later, gives hydromorphone 1 mg on the reasoning that a dose that small cannot amount to much. What error is this?
A) Assuming that a low dose means low potency, when 1 mg of parenteral hydromorphone is equivalent to 7 mg of morphine
B) Giving two opioids of the same chemical class in sequence
C) Failing to let the first drug reach peak effect before reassessing
D) No error, because 1 mg is below any meaningful analgesic dose
Answer: A
Rationale: Hydromorphone is approximately seven times more potent than morphine, and 1 mg of intravenous or intramuscular hydromorphone is equivalent to 7 mg of morphine. The chapter states that because it is given in low doses of 0.2 to 1 mg, some nurses inadvertently assume low dose means low potency, and that many medication errors and deaths have occurred from lack of knowledge of this potency difference.
Trap: (D) states the reflex belief out loud, which is the point of including it: the small milligram number is a consequence of the high potency, not evidence of a weak drug. The chapter reports deaths in which a nurse gave morphine and then hydromorphone without realizing the equianalgesic equivalency.

Subtopic: working a conversion

Q: A patient with colon cancer takes oral oxycodone 80 mg every 12 hours and must be converted to intravenous morphine because of a bowel obstruction. Using Box 10.2, which 24-hour intravenous morphine dose is correct?
A) Approximately 30 mg
B) Approximately 100 mg
C) Approximately 160 mg
D) Approximately 240 mg
Answer: B
Rationale: The chapter works this example. The 24-hour oxycodone total is 80 mg times 2 doses, or 160 mg. Box 10.2 gives 15 mg oxycodone as equivalent to 10 mg parenteral morphine. Solving 160 divided by x equals 15 divided by 10 yields 107 mg, which the chapter rounds to approximately 100 mg of injectable morphine per 24 hours.
Trap: (C) is the reflex answer for anyone who carries the 24-hour total across without converting between drugs at all. (D) results from inverting the ratio and multiplying by 15 over 10 instead of dividing.

Q: A prescriber asks the nurse to convert a patient's oral oxycodone to intravenous oxycodone. What is the problem?
A) Box 10.2 lists no oral-to-parenteral ratio for oxycodone, because all available oxycodone dosage forms are orally administered
B) A parenteral oxycodone form exists but requires a 3 to 1 conversion
C) Oxycodone cannot be converted to any other opioid
D) Oxycodone is available only in combination with acetaminophen
Answer: A
Rationale: Box 10.2 lists oxycodone at 15 mg orally with N/A in both the parenteral and ratio columns, as it does for hydrocodone, and the chapter states that all available oxycodone dosage forms are orally administered.
Trap: (B) invents a dosage form the chapter says does not exist, and does so by borrowing morphine's genuine 3 to 1 ratio, which is what makes it read as a legitimate calculation. (D) confuses combination products such as Percocet and Percodan with the availability of single-entity oxycodone.

Topic: opioid indications and contraindications
Subtopic: stated allergy versus adverse effect

Q: A patient states, "I am allergic to morphine, it makes me itch." What is the correct nursing action and rationale?
A) Document a morphine allergy and request a structurally unrelated opioid
B) Determine the exact nature of the reaction, because itching is a pharmacologic effect of histamine release rather than an allergy
C) Administer diphenhydramine and record the reaction as anaphylaxis
D) Withhold all opioids, because histamine release occurs with every drug in the class
Answer: B
Rationale: The chapter states that many patients claim to be allergic to morphine because it causes itching, that itching is a pharmacologic effect resulting from histamine release and not an allergic reaction, and that it is important to determine the exact nature of a patient's stated allergy. True anaphylaxis is rare even with intravenously administered opioids, and flushing, itching, or wheal formation at the injection site is usually local and histamine mediated.
Trap: (A) is the reflex documentation move, and it is how a patient loses access to an entire chemical group for the rest of their care on the basis of an expected adverse effect. The same pattern applies to codeine, where the chapter says nausea and gastrointestinal upset are what most patients are actually describing.

Q: A different patient reports that morphine once caused sudden wheezing, hypotension, and swelling that required epinephrine. What does the chapter support here?
A) Documenting the reaction as an adverse effect and rechallenging with morphine
B) Treating this as a true allergic reaction and selecting an opioid of an unrelated chemical structure, such as fentanyl
C) Selecting hydrocodone, because it is a weaker morphine-like drug
D) Selecting hydromorphone, because it is more potent and can be given in a smaller dose
Answer: B
Rationale: Knowing the chemical structure of the different opioids is important in determining the appropriate drug for patients who experience significant allergic reactions to a specific opioid, and the chapter's example is that a patient with anaphylaxis from morphine may appropriately receive a drug with an unrelated structure such as fentanyl.
Trap: (C) and (D) are the mirror error of the previous item, over-corrected: this reaction really is an allergy, but both drugs offered sit in the morphine-like group on Table 10.4. A smaller dose and a lower potency are irrelevant to a structural cross-reaction.

Subtopic: contraindications versus extreme caution

Q: A nurse reviews the chapter's contraindication and caution lists before giving an opioid. Which of the following does the chapter list as an actual contraindication rather than a condition requiring extreme caution? Select all that apply.
A) Known drug allergy
B) Severe asthma
C) Elevated intracranial pressure from severe head injury
D) Morbid obesity or sleep apnea
E) Paralytic ileus
Answer: A, B
Rationale: The chapter gives the contraindications as known drug allergy and severe asthma. It separately lists respiratory insufficiency (especially when resuscitative equipment is not available), conditions involving elevated intracranial pressure, morbid obesity and sleep apnea, myasthenia gravis, paralytic ileus, and pregnancy as conditions that are not absolute contraindications but call for extreme caution.
Trap: (C), (D), and (E) all appear in the chapter and all sound severe enough to bar the drug, which is exactly why they are offered. The chapter keeps two separate lists on the same page, and only two items sit on the absolute one.

Subtopic: non-analgesic opioid indications

Q: Besides analgesia, which additional use does the chapter attach to codeine specifically?
A) Suppression of the medullary cough center, for which codeine is the most commonly used opioid
B) Reversal of opioid-induced respiratory depression
C) Treatment of neuropathic pain
D) Detoxification maintenance in opioid addiction
Answer: A
Rationale: Opioids suppress the medullary cough center, and the most commonly used opioid for this purpose is codeine; hydrocodone is also used in many cough suppressants. The chapter separately notes that opioid-induced constipation is sometimes helpful in treating diarrhea, naming camphorated opium tincture (paregoric) and diphenoxylate with atropine (Lomotil).
Trap: (D) belongs to methadone, which the chapter calls the opioid of choice for detoxification treatment of opioid addicts in methadone maintenance programs. Both are non-analgesic uses of an opioid, which is what makes the swap easy to make.

Topic: opioid adverse effects
Subtopic: sedation and respiratory depression

Q: A patient two hours into an opioid infusion has become difficult to rouse but is breathing 14 breaths per minute. The family says the patient is finally comfortable. What does the increasing sedation indicate?
A) Effective analgesia, with no action needed while the respiratory rate stays above 12
B) Impending respiratory depression, because respiratory depression is strongly related to the degree of sedation
C) An expected histamine effect that will pass
D) Early opioid withdrawal
Answer: B
Rationale: The most serious adverse effect of opioid use is central nervous system depression, which may lead to respiratory depression, and when death occurs from opioid overdose it is almost always because of respiratory depression. The chapter states twice that respiratory depression is strongly related to, and strongly associated with, excessive sedation.
Trap: (A) is the reassurance answer, and it is wrong because it waits for the number to fall before acting. Sedation is the earlier sign; by the time the respiratory rate has dropped, the warning has already been missed.

Subtopic: histamine release and constipation

Q: A patient develops marked itching and flushing on morphine and asks whether a different opioid would be better tolerated. What does the chapter support?
A) A synthetic opioid such as meperidine, because the synthetics elicit the least histamine release
B) Another naturally occurring opiate, because histamine release is unrelated to chemical class
C) No change, because all opioids release histamine equally
D) An opioid antagonist as a substitute analgesic
Answer: A
Rationale: The amount of histamine release is related to chemical class: the naturally occurring opiates such as morphine elicit the most histamine release, and the synthetic opioids such as meperidine elicit the least. Histamine release causes peripheral arteries and veins to dilate, leading to flushing and orthostatic hypotension, and is thought to be responsible for itching or pruritus and rash.
Trap: (B) reverses the gradient, which sounds equally plausible to anyone who has not read the sentence. (D) misreads a real fact: small doses of naloxone can be used to treat opioid-related itching, but an antagonist is not an analgesic.

Q: A patient starting long-term opioid therapy asks which adverse effects will ease with time. Which response matches the chapter?
A) Constipation will resolve as the body adjusts, but respiratory depression will not
B) Long-term opioid use is generally associated with tolerance to respiratory depression, while constipation requires an ongoing bowel regimen
C) Both constipation and respiratory depression resolve within one week
D) Neither effect changes with continued therapy
Answer: B
Rationale: The chapter states that long-term opioid use is generally associated with tolerance to respiratory depression. Constipation is managed with ongoing measures, and the chapter names five drugs used specifically for opioid-induced constipation in patients taking opioids chronically: naloxegol, methylnaltrexone, naldemedine, lubiprostone, and alvimopan.
Trap: (A) is the exact reversal, and it is the version patients are most often told, because constipation feels like a nuisance that will pass and respiratory depression feels permanent. Holding it backwards stops the bowel regimen and relaxes the monitoring at the same time.

Subtopic: the adverse effect table and subacute overdose

Q: A patient is receiving intravenous morphine. Using Table 10.6, which adverse effects should the nurse anticipate? Select all that apply.
A) Constipation
B) Pruritus
C) Nausea
D) Diarrhea
E) Urinary frequency
Answer: A, B, C
Rationale: Table 10.6 lists nausea, vomiting, constipation, and biliary tract spasm under gastrointestinal; itching, rash, and wheal formation under integumentary; and urinary retention under genitourinary. Urinary retention is described as the inability to void, caused by increasing bladder tone.
Trap: (D) and (E) are both direction reversals of a real opioid effect, which is the tidiest way to build a five-option select-all. Opioids slow peristalsis and increase water absorption, producing constipation rather than diarrhea, and they increase bladder tone, producing retention rather than frequency.

Q: Over two days, a dying patient on stable opioid doses becomes progressively somnolent with slowing respirations. Which action does the chapter direct?
A) Administer naloxone immediately
B) Hold one or two doses to judge whether the depression is opioid related, considering advancing disease before the dosage is changed
C) Double the antiemetic and continue the current opioid dose
D) Discontinue the opioid permanently
Answer: B
Rationale: Subacute overdose may be more common than acute respiratory depression and may progress slowly over hours to days with somnolence and respiratory depression. Before analgesic dosages are changed or reduced, advancing disease must be considered, especially in the dying patient. Holding one or two doses is enough to judge whether the depression is associated with the opioid, and if there is improvement the dosage is often decreased by 25%.
Trap: (A) is the reflex reach for the antidote. Naloxone is attached to severe respiratory depression in this chapter; in the subacute case it strips away analgesia unnecessarily and, in an opioid-tolerant patient, precipitates withdrawal.

Topic: opioid toxicity, reversal, and withdrawal
Subtopic: the two antagonists

Q: A patient is found unresponsive with pinpoint pupils and a respiratory rate of 6 and needs immediate reversal. Which drug and route, and how does it differ from its near-namesake?
A) naltrexone orally, while naloxone is the oral drug used for addiction
B) naloxone by injection for overdose, while naltrexone is oral and is used for alcohol and opioid addiction
C) Either drug, because both are available in injectable form
D) naltrexone by injection, because it has a longer duration than naloxone
Answer: B
Rationale: Naloxone is used in the management of opioid overdose and is available only in injectable dosage forms, including an intravenous form with a nasal adapter for first responders, and has been available without a prescription since 2016. Naltrexone is used for alcohol and opioid addiction and is available only orally. Both bind and occupy all of the receptor sites (mu, kappa, and delta) as competitive antagonists with strong affinity.
Trap: (A) is the direct swap of two drugs one letter and one syllable apart. The fastest discriminator is the route: if the stem needs it now, it is naloxone.

Q: While giving naloxone to a patient with opioid-induced respiratory depression, what endpoint should the nurse titrate to?
A) Until the patient is fully alert and oriented
B) Until the patient begins to breathe independently
C) Until the pupils return to a normal size
D) Until the full ordered dose has been given regardless of response
Answer: B
Rationale: Careful titration of dose until the patient begins to breathe independently will prevent over-reversal. Naloxone has no agonistic morphine-like properties and does not produce analgesia or respiratory depression, and Table 10.8 lists it among drugs not recommended for cancer pain precisely because it reverses analgesia as well as central nervous system depressant effects.
Trap: (A) is the intuitive endpoint, and it is over-reversal. Pushing past the breathing endpoint wakes the patient into severe pain and, in an opioid-tolerant patient, precipitates opioid withdrawal syndrome, along with raised or lowered blood pressure, dysrhythmias, and pulmonary edema.

Q: A patient receiving extended-release morphine responds well to naloxone and is breathing comfortably 45 minutes later. What must the nurse anticipate?
A) The crisis has resolved and monitoring can be reduced
B) The effects of naloxone last about 1 hour, so respiratory depressant effects may reappear and naloxone may need to be re-dosed periodically
C) The patient will now need a higher opioid dose to achieve the same relief
D) Naloxone's long half-life will cover the remaining duration of the opioid
Answer: B
Rationale: The effects of naloxone are short-lived and usually last about 1 hour, and with long-acting opioids respiratory depressant effects may reappear, so naloxone may need to be re-dosed periodically until symptoms resolve. Its pharmacokinetics support this: onset under 2 minutes, half-life 64 minutes, duration 0.5 to 2 hours.
Trap: (A) is the reflex conclusion after a dramatic response, and it is how re-sedation deaths happen. (D) states the opposite of the drug's own numbers, which exist to explain the re-dosing rule rather than as a separate fact to memorize.

Subtopic: escalation and withdrawal timing

Q: A patient on an opioid has mild hypoventilation and is drowsy but arousable. According to the chapter's escalation sequence, what comes first?
A) Stimulating the patient, which may be adequate to reverse mild hypoventilation
B) Bag-mask ventilation
C) Endotracheal intubation
D) Naloxone administration
Answer: A
Rationale: The chapter's order is that stimulating the patient may be adequate to reverse mild hypoventilation; if this is unsuccessful, ventilatory assistance using a bag and mask or endotracheal intubation may be needed; and administration of opioid antagonists such as naloxone may also be necessary to reverse severe respiratory depression.
Trap: (D) skips two rungs. Naloxone is attached to severe respiratory depression in the chapter's own wording, and reaching for it during mild hypoventilation reverses analgesia the patient still needs. Note that a rate of 8 with unresponsiveness is a different stem: that one sits below every threshold in the chapter and does call for the antagonist.

Q: Two patients stop their opioids abruptly: one was taking morphine, the other methadone. What should the nurse expect?
A) The morphine patient's withdrawal begins within 6 to 12 hours and peaks at 24 to 72 hours, while the methadone patient's may not appear for 24 hours or longer and may be milder
B) The methadone patient's withdrawal begins first and is more severe, because methadone is the more potent drug
C) Both begin at the same time, because withdrawal timing is the same for all opioids
D) Neither will experience withdrawal unless physically dependent for more than 6 months
Answer: A
Rationale: The chapter groups codeine, hydrocodone, morphine, and hydromorphone as short-acting, with withdrawal onset within 6 to 12 hours and peak at 24 to 72 hours, and methadone, levorphanol, and transdermal fentanyl as long half-life drugs, with withdrawal that may not appear for 24 hours or longer and may be milder. Withdrawal, also known as abstinence syndrome, can occur after as little as 2 weeks of opioid therapy in opioid-naive patients, and gradual dosage reduction minimizes its risk and severity.
Trap: (B) has it backwards on both axes, and it pulls because methadone is associated with addiction treatment and therefore feels like the more severe drug. (D) overstates the exposure required; the chapter's figure is 2 weeks.

Topic: individual opioid drug profiles
Subtopic: meperidine and methadone

Q: A prescriber orders meperidine for ongoing pain in an older adult with kidney dysfunction. What is the nurse's concern?
A) Meperidine has no analgesic effect in older adults
B) The active metabolite normeperidine can accumulate to toxic levels and lead to seizures, so meperidine is not recommended for long-term treatment and must be used with caution in older adults and in kidney dysfunction
C) Meperidine is contraindicated because it is morphine-like and shares morphine's allergy profile
D) Meperidine can be given only by the intravenous route
Answer: B
Rationale: An active metabolite, normeperidine, can accumulate to toxic levels and lead to seizures, and for this reason meperidine is now rarely used and is not recommended for long-term pain treatment. It must be used with caution, if at all, in older adult patients and in patients who require long-term analgesia or who have kidney dysfunction. Table 10.8 bars it from cancer pain for its short 2 to 3 hour duration and its central nervous system toxicity of tremor, confusion, or seizures.
Trap: (C) misplaces meperidine on Table 10.4. It heads the meperidine-like group and is structurally unrelated to morphine, which is why fentanyl and meperidine are the alternatives in a true morphine allergy rather than casualties of it. The two surviving uses the chapter names are emergency department treatment of acute migraine and reduction of shivering in the immediate postoperative period.

Q: A patient on methadone for chronic pain reports that the pain returns before the next scheduled dose and has begun taking extra tablets. What is the danger?
A) Accumulation, because methadone is unique in that its half-life is longer than its duration of action
B) Tolerance, which calls for an immediate dose increase
C) Withdrawal, because methadone has an unusually short half-life
D) No danger, because methadone has no ceiling and may be titrated freely
Answer: A
Rationale: Methadone is unique in that its half-life is longer than its duration of action, because it is bound into the tissues of the liver, kidneys, and brain; with repeated doses the drug accumulates in these tissues and is slowly released, which allows 24-hour dosing. FDA reports have cited the prolonged half-life as a cause of unintentional overdoses and deaths, and there is concern that methadone may cause cardiac dysrhythmias.
Trap: (B) is the reflex reading of "the pain came back," and it is exactly the inference that kills here. The analgesia wears off before the drug does, so a patient dosing to comfort accumulates drug they cannot feel.

Subtopic: choosing an opioid in renal impairment

Q: A patient with renal insufficiency needs an opioid. Which drug does the chapter specifically rule out, and why?
A) morphine, because its toxic metabolite morphine-6-glucuronide is more likely to accumulate in renal impairment
B) methadone, because it is eliminated by the kidneys
C) hydromorphone, because it is seven times more potent than morphine
D) fentanyl, because its transdermal form has a delayed offset
Answer: A
Rationale: Morphine has a potentially toxic metabolite, morphine-6-glucuronide, whose accumulation is more likely in patients with renal impairment, and for this reason other Schedule II opioids such as hydromorphone and fentanyl may be safer analgesic choices for patients with renal insufficiency. Methadone is eliminated through the liver, which makes it a safer choice than some other opioids for patients with renal impairment.
Trap: (B) reverses methadone's elimination route, which is the very property that puts it among the acceptable choices here rather than the excluded ones. Of the four drugs offered, the chapter supports three and rules out only morphine.

Subtopic: codeine and the rest of the profiles

Q: A patient is discharged with a codeine and acetaminophen combination tablet and asks why this product is a Schedule III drug when codeine alone is Schedule II. Which response is accurate?
A) Codeine combined with acetaminophen is classified as Schedule III, and when codeine is not combined with other drugs it is classified as Schedule II
B) The pharmacy made an error, because all codeine products are Schedule II
C) Acetaminophen is itself a controlled substance and raises the schedule of the product
D) Combination products are always one schedule higher than the single ingredient
Answer: A
Rationale: Codeine combined with acetaminophen in tablets or elixir is classified as a Schedule III controlled substance, and when codeine is not combined with other drugs it is classified as Schedule II. Codeine is a natural opiate alkaloid obtained from opium, about 10% of a dose is metabolized to morphine in the body, and it is the only agonist to possess a ceiling effect.
Trap: (D) generalizes the correct fact in the wrong direction. Schedule III is a lower level of control than Schedule II, not a higher one, and the chapter is explicit that the combination carries the lower schedule.

Q: Which statements about the individual opioids match the chapter? Select all that apply.
A) Morphine is the drug prototype for all opioid drugs
B) Hydromorphone is approximately seven times more potent than morphine
C) Methadone is the opioid of choice for detoxification treatment in methadone maintenance programs
D) Oxycodone is structurally related to morphine and has comparable analgesic activity
E) Codeine is contraindicated in pediatric patients and in laboring or breastfeeding mothers
F) Meperidine is the preferred opioid for long-term cancer pain
Answer: A, B, C, D, E
Rationale: Each of the first five statements is given in the drug profiles: morphine as a naturally occurring alkaloid and the drug prototype for all opioid drugs, hydromorphone as approximately seven times more potent than morphine, methadone as the opioid of choice for detoxification treatment of opioid addicts in methadone maintenance programs, oxycodone as structurally related to morphine with comparable analgesic activity, and codeine as contraindicated in pediatric patients and laboring or breastfeeding mothers.

Topic: transdermal fentanyl patch safety
Subtopic: who may receive a patch

Q: A transdermal fentanyl patch is ordered for an opioid-naive patient with new postoperative pain. What is the nurse's concern?
A) The patch may be used only by patients who are considered opioid tolerant, and giving it to a non-opioid-tolerant patient may result in severe respiratory depression
B) The patch works too quickly for postoperative use
C) The patch cannot be used together with any nonopioid analgesic
D) There is no concern, because fentanyl is standard postoperative therapy
Answer: A
Rationale: Transdermal fentanyl is to be used only by patients considered opioid tolerant, and giving it to non-opioid-tolerant patients may result in severe respiratory depression. It is not to be used in opioid-naive patients or for acute pain situations such as postoperative pain. Opioid tolerant here means taking, for a week or longer, morphine 60 mg daily, oral oxycodone 30 mg daily, a minimum of 8 mg of oral hydromorphone daily, or an equianalgesic dose of another opioid.
Trap: (B) inverts the patch's actual problem, which is that it is far too slow. (D) is true of fentanyl injection, which is favored postoperatively for its rapid onset and short duration, and the route rather than the molecule is what bars the patch here.

Subtopic: the patch time constants

Q: A patient's first fentanyl patch was applied 3 hours ago and the patient still reports pain. What should the nurse expect and do?
A) The patch has failed and should be removed and replaced
B) The patch takes 6 to 12 hours to reach steady-state pain control, and supplemental short-acting therapy may be required
C) The patch should be cut in half to speed absorption
D) A heating pad over the patch will accelerate the onset
Answer: B
Rationale: Transdermal fentanyl takes 6 to 12 hours to reach steady-state pain control after the first patch is applied, and supplemental short-acting therapy may be required. A new patch is applied every 72 hours, and all other around-the-clock opioids must be discontinued or tapered before the patch is started.
Trap: (C) and (D) are both named hazards rather than shortcuts. Cutting the patch accelerates diffusion uncontrollably, and heat increases circulation and therefore absorption, which the chapter names as an overdose mechanism.

Q: A patient with excessive sedation has a fentanyl patch removed. What must the nurse understand about the exposure after removal, and when are peak effects expected after a patch is started?
A) Exposure ends immediately on removal, and peak effects occur within the first hour
B) It takes about 17 hours for the amount of fentanyl to reduce by 50% after removal, and peak serum effects occur within the initial 24 to 72 hours after application
C) Exposure ends within 1 hour of removal, the same as naloxone's duration
D) Peak effects occur only at the end of the 72-hour wear period
Answer: B
Rationale: It takes about 17 hours for the amount of fentanyl to reduce by 50% once the patch is removed, and the safety box directs close monitoring for respiratory depression especially within the initial 24 to 72 hours, which is the timeframe for peak effects of serum concentrations.
Trap: (A) is the reflex assumption that removing a topical product ends the dose, and it turns a patch removal into false reassurance. Note that the chapter prints a second, conflicting set of figures in its pharmacokinetics table, giving transdermal onset as 12 to 24 hours and peak as 48 to 72 hours; both sets are the chapter's own, so read a stem for which figure it is quoting.

Subtopic: heat, disposal, and family safety

Q: A patient wearing a fentanyl patch places a heating pad over the site for comfort. What is the priority teaching point?
A) Heat must never be applied over a transdermal fentanyl patch, because increased circulation may increase absorption of medication and cause an overdose
B) Heat is acceptable if it is applied for less than 20 minutes at a time
C) Heat is acceptable as long as the patch is less than 24 hours old
D) Heat has no effect on transdermal absorption
Answer: A
Rationale: The chapter states that heat, such as in the form of a heating pad or pack, must never be applied over a transdermal fentanyl patch, because the increased circulation may result in increased absorption of medication, causing an overdose. The related storage rule is not to store medications in warm, moist places such as bathroom medicine cabinets.
Trap: (B) and (C) both offer a bounded exception, which is what makes them attractive to a learner looking for a workable compromise. The chapter's word is never, with no limit on time or patch age.

Q: A nurse is teaching a patient and family about transdermal fentanyl. Which instructions match the chapter? Select all that apply.
A) Remove the old patch when applying a new one
B) Fold the used patch in half and dispose of it by flushing it down the toilet
C) Apply a new patch every 72 hours
D) Place used patches in the household trash where children cannot climb
E) Cut the patch to reduce the dose once pain is well controlled
Answer: A, B, C
Rationale: The chapter states that it is important to remove the old patch when applying a new one, that the patch is to be folded in half and disposed of by flushing it down the toilet because a used patch may still contain a significant amount of medication, and that a new patch is applied every 72 hours.
Trap: (D) is the specific error the chapter reports as fatal: children have pulled used fentanyl patches from the trash, which has resulted in death, and patches have also become displaced and adhered to the skin of an infant or toddler. (E) makes cutting sound like titration; it is a named hazard, and the chapter's stated titration philosophy is to underestimate the 24-hour requirement and plan for rescue medication.

Topic: nonopioid and miscellaneous analgesics
Subtopic: acetaminophen versus the NSAIDs

Q: A patient with rheumatoid arthritis is taking acetaminophen for joint pain and inflammation with poor results. What does the chapter identify as the reason?
A) In contrast to the NSAIDs, acetaminophen lacks antiinflammatory effects
B) Acetaminophen has no analgesic effect at all
C) Acetaminophen is not an antipyretic
D) Acetaminophen cannot be combined with any other analgesic
Answer: A
Rationale: Acetaminophen blocks peripheral pain impulses by inhibition of prostaglandin synthesis and lowers febrile body temperature by acting on the hypothalamus, with heat dissipated through vasodilation and increased peripheral blood flow. In contrast to the NSAIDs, it lacks antiinflammatory effects. NSAIDs are used especially for pain associated with inflammatory conditions such as arthritis, because they have significant antiinflammatory effects in addition to their analgesic effects.
Trap: (B) and (C) overshoot in the wrong direction. Acetaminophen shares analgesic and antipyretic activity with the NSAIDs, so the third property is the entire discriminator, and everything else follows from it.

Q: A patient taking warfarin who has a history of gastrointestinal bleeding needs an analgesic and antipyretic. Why does the chapter favor acetaminophen here?
A) Because it is not usually associated with platelet effects such as bleeding, cardiovascular effects such as edema, or the gastrointestinal irritation and bleeding seen with aspirin
B) Because it has stronger antiinflammatory effects than the NSAIDs
C) Because it has no maximum daily dose
D) Because it is safe in severe liver disease
Answer: A
Rationale: Acetaminophen is not usually associated with the cardiovascular effects such as edema or the platelet effects such as bleeding seen with aspirin and other NSAIDs, and it does not cause the aspirin-related gastrointestinal tract irritation or bleeding or the aspirin-related acid-base changes.
Trap: (B) is the mirror error of the previous item. (D) reverses one of the drug's three contraindications, which are known drug allergy, severe liver disease, and glucose-6-phosphate dehydrogenase deficiency.

Subtopic: acetaminophen ceilings and toxicity

Q: Three patients take acetaminophen: a healthy adult, an older adult, and a patient with liver disease and chronic alcohol consumption. Which set of maximum daily doses matches the Clinical Pearl?
A) 4000 mg, 3000 mg, 2000 mg
B) 2000 mg, 3000 mg, 4000 mg
C) 4000 mg for all three
D) 3000 mg, 2000 mg, 1000 mg
Answer: A
Rationale: The Clinical Pearl gives 4000 mg/day for a healthy patient, 3000 mg/day for an older adult, and 2000 mg/day for liver disease or chronic alcohol consumption. The body text adds that the FDA limits total daily doses to 4000 mg while the manufacturer of Tylenol suggests 3000 mg/day, and the dosage table prints 3 to 4 g/day with a limit of 2 g/day in alcoholics.
Trap: (C) is the reflex answer for anyone who remembers only the famous 4 g figure. A stem that names an older adult or a drinker is asking for a lower number, and (B) inverts the whole scale so that the sickest patient gets the most drug.

Q: A patient taking Percocet for postoperative pain also takes over-the-counter Tylenol for a headache. What is the risk the chapter identifies?
A) The patient may exceed the recommended acetaminophen limit without knowing that the prescription product also contains acetaminophen
B) Percocet contains no acetaminophen, so there is no risk
C) The opioid component doubles, causing respiratory depression
D) The combination increases the risk for Reye's syndrome
Answer: A
Rationale: Acetaminophen is combined with hydrocodone (Vicodin, Norco) and with oxycodone (Percocet, Tylox), and patients may exceed the recommended limit of acetaminophen without knowing these products also contain acetaminophen. In 2011 the FDA announced that combination products are to be limited to 325 mg of acetaminophen, and the chapter supports retiring the abbreviation APAP for the same reason.
Trap: (D) borrows a real syndrome from the wrong drug. Reye's syndrome is an aspirin problem, and acetaminophen is the chapter's antipyretic of choice in children and adolescents with flu syndromes precisely because aspirin is not.

Q: A patient presents 2 hours after an intentional acetaminophen overdose, and the laboratory cannot report a serum acetaminophen level. What does the chapter direct?
A) Wait for a level before treating, because acetylcysteine is itself toxic if given unnecessarily
B) Assume the overdose is potentially toxic and start acetylcysteine, since a level is drawn no sooner than 4 hours after ingestion and the antidote is most effective within 10 hours
C) Give acetylcysteine only if the ingestion exceeded 20 g
D) Give naloxone, which reverses acetaminophen toxicity
Answer: B
Rationale: A serum acetaminophen concentration is determined no sooner than 4 hours after ingestion, and if a level cannot be determined it is assumed the overdose is potentially toxic and treatment with acetylcysteine needs to be started. Acetylcysteine is the recommended antidote, works by preventing the hepatotoxic metabolites of acetaminophen from forming, and is most effective when given within 10 hours of an overdose. Acute ingestion of 150 mg/kg, approximately 7 to 10 g or more, may result in hepatotoxicity.
Trap: (A) inverts the chapter's own instruction and burns the antidote window waiting for a number the chapter says not to wait for. Note also that oral acetylcysteine is notoriously bad tasting with an odor of rotten eggs, and the dose is repeated if vomiting occurs within 1 hour of dosing.

Subtopic: tramadol, tapentadol, and the topical agents

Q: Two structurally related drugs are ordered on the unit: tramadol and tapentadol. Which statement distinguishes them as the chapter does?
A) Tramadol is a nonopioid analgesic with opioid-like activity, and tapentadol is a Schedule II narcotic with a dual mechanism as a mu agonist and norepinephrine reuptake inhibitor
B) Tapentadol is the nonopioid, and tramadol is the Schedule II narcotic
C) Both are Schedule II narcotics with identical mechanisms
D) Both are pure mu antagonists used to reverse opioid effects
Answer: A
Rationale: The chapter calls tramadol a nonopioid analgesic with opioid-like activity, available orally including Ultracet with acetaminophen, extended-release forms, and an orally disintegrating tablet. Tapentadol is structurally related to tramadol with a dual mechanism of action, a mu agonist and a norepinephrine reuptake inhibitor, and is a Schedule II narcotic.
Trap: (B) is the direct swap of two drugs that differ by three letters in the middle. The scheduling is the fastest discriminator, because only tapentadol carries a controlled-substance schedule in this chapter's description.

Q: A nurse reviews the miscellaneous analgesics in this chapter. Which pairings match the chapter? Select all that apply.
A) transdermal lidocaine (Lidoderm) is a topical anesthetic in a patch, indicated for postherpetic neuralgia
B) capsaicin is an over-the-counter topical product that works by decreasing or interfering with substance P and is to be used on a scheduled basis rather than as needed
C) milnacipran (Savella) is a selective serotonin and norepinephrine dual-uptake inhibitor indicated for fibromyalgia
D) celecoxib (Celebrex) is an opioid agonist-antagonist
E) capsaicin is an oral opioid reserved for neuropathic pain
Answer: A, B, C
Rationale: The chapter gives transdermal lidocaine as a topical anesthetic formulated in a patch and placed onto painful areas of skin, indicated for postherpetic neuralgia; capsaicin as a topical product made from several types of peppers that works by decreasing or interfering with substance P, is available over the counter, and is used on a scheduled basis rather than as needed; and milnacipran as a selective serotonin and norepinephrine dual-uptake inhibitor indicated for fibromyalgia, on the rationale that these patients have reduced levels of norepinephrine in their brains.
Trap: (D) misfiles celecoxib, which the chapter names as an NSAID and therefore a nonopioid analgesic, deferring its full treatment to Chapter 44. (E) contradicts capsaicin's topical, over-the-counter status while borrowing the scheduled-dosing detail that makes it sound like a systemic drug.

Topic: opioid nursing administration and teaching
Subtopic: respiratory thresholds

Q: A patient is due for a scheduled opioid dose and the respiratory rate is 10 breaths/min. What do the chapter's thresholds direct?
A) Give the dose, because 10 breaths/min falls inside the expected outcome range of 10 to 20
B) Withhold the opioid and report the rate, since an opioid is usually withheld if respirations are less than 12 and a rate of 10 may indicate respiratory depression that must be reported to the prescriber
C) Give half the dose and reassess in one hour
D) Give the dose and administer naloxone as a precaution
Answer: B
Rationale: The chapter states that an opioid medication is usually withheld if a patient's respirations are less than 12 breaths/min, and that a respiratory rate of 10 breaths/min, with some protocols still adhering to the parameter of 12, may indicate respiratory depression and must be reported to the prescriber. Reassessment is directed especially if the respiratory rate is less than 10.
Trap: (A) is the trap the chapter's own numbers create, because 10 appears both as the alarm threshold and as the bottom of the expected-outcome range, so a learner can defend giving the dose from the same chapter. Cue: 12 is the withholding threshold and 10 is the alarm, which is why a rate of 11 in a stem is placed there deliberately.

Subtopic: scheduling and evaluating analgesia

Q: A patient with metastatic bone pain has pain present for most of the day. How should analgesia be scheduled?
A) As needed, whenever the patient requests it
B) On schedule during waking hours only
C) Around the clock, with additional doses as needed for breakthrough pain
D) Only when the reported pain score exceeds 7 of 10
Answer: C
Rationale: When pain is present for more than 12 hours a day, analgesic doses are individualized and are best administered around the clock rather than on an as-needed basis, because around-the-clock or scheduled dosing maintains steady-state levels of the medication and prevents drug troughs and pain escalation. It is best to treat the patient's pain before it becomes severe, which is the rationale for considering pain the fifth vital sign.
Trap: (B) is the option built to look responsible, and it reintroduces a nightly trough the patient wakes up in. (D) waits for severe pain, which is exactly what scheduled dosing exists to prevent.

Q: A patient rates pain 3 of 10 after a scheduled dose and asks whether that is good enough. Which evaluation matches the chapter?
A) A patient-rated pain level of less than 4 on a scale of 1 to 10 indicates effective pain relief, though this may vary by provider, setting, or unit
B) Only a rating of 0 counts as effective relief
C) Any rating below 7 counts as effective relief
D) Pain ratings are not a valid measure of analgesic effectiveness
Answer: A
Rationale: The chapter states that a patient-rated pain level of less than 4 on a scale of 1 to 10 is considered to indicate effective pain relief, and adds that this may vary depending on the health care provider, health care setting, or unit.
Trap: (B) sets an unreachable standard, and it pulls because complete relief sounds like the only humane goal; pursued literally it escalates opioids past the point of benefit. (C) borrows a number from severity language rather than from this chapter's effectiveness threshold.

Subtopic: teaching and special populations

Q: A nurse is providing discharge teaching to a patient starting opioid therapy. Which instructions match the chapter's patient teaching points? Select all that apply.
A) Do not use opioids with alcohol or other central nervous system depressants unless ordered
B) Force fluids up to 3 L/day unless contraindicated, increase fiber consumption, and exercise as tolerated
C) Report dizziness, difficulty breathing, low blood pressure, excessive sleepiness, confusion, or loss of memory promptly
D) If pain increases on the prescribed dosage, double the next dose before contacting the prescriber
E) Avoid activities requiring mental clarity or alertness while drowsy or sedated
Answer: A, B, C, E
Rationale: The chapter's patient teaching highlights state that opioids are not to be used with alcohol or other central nervous system depressants unless ordered; that constipation is managed by forcing fluids up to 3 L/day unless contraindicated, increasing fiber consumption, and exercising as tolerated, with stool softeners as needed; that dizziness, difficulty breathing, low blood pressure, excessive sleepiness, confusion, or loss of memory must be promptly reported; and that activities requiring mental clarity or alertness may need to be avoided during drowsiness or sedation.
Trap: (D) is the one to leave unchecked. The chapter's rule is that if the level of pain increases while the patient remains on the prescribed dosage the prescriber must be contacted, and that dosages must not be changed, increased, or doubled unless prescribed.

Q: An older adult is starting opioid therapy. Which approach matches the chapter's key points?
A) Older adults feel less pain than younger patients, so dose reduction is usually unnecessary
B) Older adults experience pain the same as the general population, may be reluctant to report it, and may metabolize opiates more slowly, so start with low dosages, reevaluate often, and titrate upward slowly
C) Older adults metabolize opioids faster and require higher starting doses
D) Opioids are contraindicated in patients older than 65 years
Answer: B
Rationale: The chapter's key points state that older adult patients experience pain the same as the general population does, but may be reluctant to report pain and may metabolize opiates at a slower rate, and are therefore at increased risk for adverse effects such as sedation and respiratory depression. The best rule is to start with low dosages, reevaluate often, and go slowly during upward titration.
Trap: (A) is the belief that produces undertreatment in this population, and the chapter contradicts it in its first clause. (C) reverses the metabolic direction, which would justify exactly the wrong starting dose.
