# Chapter 40 drills — Antiviral Drugs

Authored from `sources/Ch40_Fact_Bank.md` (Lilley, Collins & Snyder, 10th ed.).

Blueprint weight is **3 questions of 121 (2.5%), depth `focused`** — one of the
lightest chapters in the course attached to one of its largest bodies of
content: 20 printed pages, 26 key terms, and dozens of named drugs. A `focused`
chapter earns 20 to 30 drills, so this file carries **30**, at the top of the
band, and still leaves most of the fact bank undrilled on purpose.

Topics follow the chapter's own two-part split — the non-HIV antivirals first,
then HIV and the antiretrovirals — with vocabulary pulled to the front because
the class names and the drug suffixes are built out of it, and with toxicity and
teaching pulled to the back because that is where the drugs stop being
interchangeable in a stem.

The fact bank flags roughly thirty confusable pairs. Thirty questions cannot
carry all of them as mirror pairs, so the pairs kept are the ones that decide a
drug choice or a safety action: antiviral versus antiretroviral, nucleoside
versus nucleotide, influenza A alone versus A and B, oseltamivir versus
zanamivir, acyclovir versus ganciclovir, Zovirax versus Zostrix, the 2-day
influenza window versus the 72-hour zoster window, hepatitis B drugs versus
hepatitis C drugs, NRTI versus NNRTI, enfuvirtide versus maraviroc, CD4 200
versus viral load 50, PrEP versus occupational postexposure prophylaxis, and
marrow toxicity versus renal toxicity in the CMV drugs.

Left out, and why: **per-drug pharmacokinetics, dosage ranges, and infusion
details**, which the fact bank itself dropped at `focused` depth; **amantadine
versus rimantadine**, because the CDC no longer recommends either drug for
influenza, so the pair decides nothing; **baloxavir and peramivir**, single-drug
mechanism and route trivia; **cell-mediated versus humoral immunity**, which
belongs to the immunity chapters this one defers to; **HSV-1 versus HSV-2** and
**chickenpox versus shingles**, kept only as distractor material inside other
items; **the WHO four stages**, **HIV epidemiology**, **COVID-19 and
remdesivir**, which the chapter itself calls beyond its scope; and the
**obsolete pregnancy letter categories**, which the fact bank flags as no longer
current labeling. The CYP3A4 material is drilled in the Chapter 2 file, where
the substrate, inhibitor, and inducer vocabulary is actually defined.

Topic: antiviral terminology and viral genome vocabulary
Subtopic: general versus specific drug terms

Q: A nurse is charting for a patient whose medication list includes acyclovir for recurrent genital herpes and zidovudine for HIV infection. Which single term correctly describes both drugs?
A) Antiretroviral drugs, because both suppress viral replication
B) Antiviral drugs, the general term for drugs that destroy viruses either directly or indirectly by suppressing their replication
C) Antibiotics, because both act against an infectious organism
D) Immunoglobulins, because both attack a foreign antigen
Answer: B
Rationale: The key-terms box makes the relationship a general-to-specific one: antiviral drugs are "a general term for drugs that destroy viruses, either directly or indirectly by suppressing their replication," while antiretroviral drugs are "a specific term for antiviral drugs that work against retroviruses such as HIV." Every antiretroviral is an antiviral, so only the general term covers both drugs on this list.
Trap: (A) is the reflex pick the moment HIV appears anywhere in a stem, but acyclovir treats herpesviruses, not retroviruses, so it is not an antiretroviral. The chapter's own structure and its key points split the two halves the same way.

Q: The same nurse must identify which of those two drugs belongs to the narrower category the chapter reserves for HIV therapy. Which statement is accurate?
A) Only zidovudine is an antiretroviral, because antiretroviral names drugs that work against retroviruses such as HIV
B) Only acyclovir is an antiretroviral, because herpesviruses replicate by reverse transcription
C) Both are antiretrovirals, because the two terms are interchangeable
D) Neither is an antiretroviral, because that term refers only to vaccines
Answer: A
Rationale: Retroviruses are the family Retroviridae, they carry RNA as their genome, and they replicate using reverse transcriptase; HIV is named as the currently most clinically significant retrovirus. Acyclovir's targets, HSV-1, HSV-2, and varicella-zoster virus, are herpesviruses and replicate without reverse transcriptase.
Trap: (C) is the mirror error of the previous item, collapsing the general term into the specific one so that everything antiviral gets called antiretroviral. (B) hands reverse transcription to the herpesviruses, which is the same confusion running the other way.

Subtopic: nucleic acid vocabulary

Q: A patient's antiretroviral regimen lists tenofovir alafenamide as a nucleotide reverse transcriptase inhibitor and zidovudine as a nucleoside reverse transcriptase inhibitor. What structural difference does that labeling describe?
A) A nucleotide is a nucleoside with an attached phosphate unit
B) A nucleoside is a nucleotide with an attached phosphate unit
C) A nucleotide is built from RNA and a nucleoside from DNA
D) A nucleoside is found only in host cells and a nucleotide only in viruses
Answer: A
Rationale: The chapter states it twice: "A nucleoside is a single unit consisting of a base and its attached sugar... A nucleotide is a nucleoside plus an attached phosphate." The key-terms box adds that the phosphate unit is what makes up the side chain backbone of a DNA or RNA molecule. The labeling is not cosmetic, since tenofovir alafenamide is the nucleotide agent while zidovudine, lamivudine, abacavir, emtricitabine, stavudine, and didanosine are nucleosides.
Trap: (B) is the inversion, and it is the common one because the two words differ by three letters and both appear in the same drug class. The cue is that nucleoTide equals phosphaTe.

Q: A patient newly diagnosed with HIV asks why the virus is called a retrovirus. Which explanation is accurate?
A) It carries both DNA and RNA within the same virion
B) It carries RNA as its genome and uses reverse transcriptase to build DNA from that RNA, the reverse of the usual direction
C) It carries DNA as its genome and copies that DNA into RNA inside the nucleus
D) It replicates outside host cells and then enters them as mature virions
Answer: B
Rationale: Retroviruses "contain RNA (as opposed to DNA) as their genome and replicate using the enzyme reverse transcriptase," and reverse transcriptase is defined as "an RNA-directed DNA polymerase" that promotes "the synthesis of a DNA molecule from an RNA molecule, which is the reverse of the usual process."
Trap: (A) is chosen by students who remember that a viral genome may be either nucleic acid and conclude it can be both; the chapter states a virion's genome is DNA or RNA "but not both." (D) contradicts the definition of a virus as the smallest known class of microorganisms, which "can only replicate inside host cells."

Topic: influenza antiviral selection
Subtopic: spectrum by drug

Q: A patient with laboratory-confirmed influenza B has a new order for amantadine. What should the nurse recognize about this order?
A) Amantadine is only effective against influenza A, so the drug does not match the diagnosis
B) Amantadine covers influenza A and B, so the order is appropriate
C) Amantadine is a neuraminidase inhibitor and is preferred for influenza B
D) Amantadine is appropriate here only if the patient is younger than 12 months
Answer: A
Rationale: Amantadine is "only effective against influenza A," and Table 40.1 sorts it and rimantadine under influenza A while listing zanamivir and oseltamivir under influenza A and B. A further problem sits behind the spectrum issue: CDC guidelines "do not recommend the use of amantadine or rimantadine to prevent or treat the flu."
Trap: (D) inverts the age rule, since being younger than 12 months is a printed contraindication for amantadine, not an indication. (C) borrows the neuraminidase inhibitor label that belongs to oseltamivir and zanamivir.

Q: The prescriber asks which antiviral would actually cover this patient's influenza B. Which pair of drugs is effective against both influenza A and influenza B?
A) Amantadine and rimantadine
B) Oseltamivir and zanamivir
C) Acyclovir and valacyclovir
D) Entecavir and lamivudine
Answer: B
Rationale: Table 40.1 gives zanamivir and oseltamivir for influenza A and B. Both are neuraminidase inhibitors, and the chapter explains that this enzyme "enables budding virions to escape from infected cells," so blocking it traps new virions in the cell they were made in.
Trap: (A) is the mirror error of the previous item, since the two adamantanes are the influenza A only pair. (D) names two hepatitis B drugs, and (C) names two herpesvirus drugs, both of which read as plausible only if the drug is not sorted by the virus it treats.

Q: A patient whose household member has active influenza asks for something to keep from getting sick. Which neuraminidase inhibitor fits, and why?
A) Zanamivir, because the inhaled route reaches the respiratory tract before infection takes hold
B) Oseltamivir, because it is oral and is indicated for both prophylaxis and treatment
C) Either drug, because both are approved for prophylaxis and for treatment
D) Neither drug, because neuraminidase inhibitors are approved only for hospitalized patients
Answer: B
Rationale: Oseltamivir is oral and is used for both prophylaxis and treatment, while zanamivir is inhaled by Diskhaler and is indicated for treatment of active illness only. Their adverse effects split the same way: oseltamivir causes nausea and vomiting, zanamivir causes diarrhea, nausea, and sinusitis.
Trap: (A) is the reflex pick because an inhaled drug sounds preventive at the site of viral entry, and the route is the very thing that makes the two drugs feel interchangeable. The indication, not the route, is the discriminator.

Topic: herpesvirus antiviral selection and look-alike names
Subtopic: matching the drug to the herpesvirus

Q: A patient who received a kidney transplant 3 months ago develops cytomegalovirus retinitis. Which antiviral does the chapter identify for this infection?
A) Acyclovir
B) Ganciclovir
C) Oseltamivir
D) Ribavirin
Answer: B
Rationale: Ganciclovir is the nucleoside analogue for CMV, which is HHV-5, and it is also given prophylactically "in high-risk patients, such as those receiving organ transplants." Its ophthalmic form must be surgically inserted.
Trap: (A) is the reflex pick because acyclovir is the antiviral students know best and the two names differ by four letters, but acyclovir's spectrum is HSV-1, HSV-2, and varicella-zoster virus, not CMV. Review question 5 works this exact swap on a post-transplant patient.

Q: A patient presents with a recurrent episode of genital herpes caused by HSV-2. Which drug does the chapter name as the drug of choice?
A) Ganciclovir, because it covers all of the herpesviruses
B) Acyclovir, which is the drug of choice for both initial and recurrent episodes
C) Foscarnet, because it is the only injectable option
D) Trifluridine, which is used for herpes simplex infections at any site
Answer: B
Rationale: Acyclovir is a nucleoside analogue for HSV-1, HSV-2, and varicella-zoster virus, and the chapter calls it "the drug of choice" for both initial and recurrent episodes. Trifluridine's indication in Table 40.1 is herpes simplex keratitis specifically, not herpes simplex at any site.
Trap: (A) is the mirror error of the previous item, generalizing ganciclovir from CMV to the whole herpesvirus family. Note also that the chapter cautions "there can be anatomic overlap between the two types," so an option claiming HSV-1 can never cause genital lesions would be a distractor rather than a fact.

Subtopic: look-alike names and routes

Q: A patient with severe disseminated herpes infection needs intravenous antiviral therapy, and a separate order calls for a topical antiviral ointment. Which drug can supply both of these forms?
A) Valacyclovir
B) Famciclovir
C) Acyclovir
D) Entecavir
Answer: C
Rationale: Acyclovir is the one drug in this group available in oral, topical, and injectable forms; valacyclovir and famciclovir are oral only. Valacyclovir is "a prodrug that is metabolized to acyclovir," dosed "three times daily versus five times daily."
Trap: (A) is the reflex pick because valacyclovir is the form most outpatients actually take, but it is oral only. The chapter warns twice that "acyclovir may be mistaken for valacyclovir and famciclovir" and to "note the slight inconsistencies in the spelling," so the working cue is that any stem naming an infusion, a finger cot, or an ointment is acyclovir, while the prefix val- signals a prodrug formulated for oral use, as in valacyclovir to acyclovir and valganciclovir to ganciclovir.

Q: A prescription written by trade name reads Zostrix for a patient being treated for genital herpes lesions. What should the nurse do?
A) Administer it, because Zostrix is the topical acyclovir product
B) Clarify the order, because Zostrix is capsaicin for arthritic pain while Zovirax 5% ointment is the topical acyclovir product
C) Administer it, but only once the lesions have crusted over
D) Clarify the order, because Zostrix is an oral drug and this patient needs a topical one
Answer: B
Rationale: The chapter's safety box, written up from a real reported error, contrasts Zostrix, capsaicin "derived from hot chili peppers" and used for arthritic pain, with Zovirax 5% ointment, topical acyclovir used for genital herpes. The fix the box names is using generic names.
Trap: (A) is the reflex pick because the two trade names differ by three letters and both begin with the same syllable, and nothing in the trade name signals which drug is inside. (D) accepts the wrong drug and then argues about its route, which is the error that lets a look-alike order through.

Subtopic: treatment windows

Q: A patient telephones on the third day of a painful dermatomal rash diagnosed as herpes zoster and asks whether antiviral therapy is still worth starting. Within what window does the chapter say acyclovir should be started for herpes zoster?
A) Within 2 days of onset, the same window used for the influenza antivirals
B) Within 72 hours of onset, although the effect on recovery is usually not dramatic
C) Within 7 days of onset
D) At any point during the outbreak, because the window applies only to influenza
Answer: B
Rationale: Acyclovir in herpes zoster is started within 72 hours of onset and "may speed recovery, but this effect is usually not dramatic." The influenza figure is a different number: oseltamivir and zanamivir must be started within 2 days of symptom onset.
Trap: (A) is the reflex pick because the 2-day influenza figure is repeated in both the assessment and the implementation sections and is the number that sticks. The cue is 2 days for influenza, 72 hours for zoster. A third figure, 24 to 48 hours, appears only in patient teaching and is keyed to the patient noticing a recurrence, not to a clinician's clock.

Topic: hepatitis b and c antiviral therapy
Subtopic: sorting the drug to the hepatitis type

Q: A patient with chronic hepatitis B is started on entecavir. Which instruction and expectation match the chapter's description of this drug?
A) Take it with a full meal, and expect a 4-week course
B) Take it on an empty stomach, and expect a course of at least 12 months
C) Take it with ribavirin, and expect a 12-week course
D) Take it on an empty stomach, and expect a single dose
Answer: B
Rationale: Entecavir is a nucleoside reverse transcriptase inhibitor for hepatitis B, given for a minimum of 12 months and on an empty stomach. Its black box warning covers acute exacerbations of hepatitis B, lactic acidosis, hepatomegaly, and resistance developing in unrecognized HIV infection, which is why HIV status matters before it is started.
Trap: (A) is the reflex pick because the chapter's general rule really is to take oral antivirals with meals; entecavir and indinavir are the two printed empty-stomach exceptions. Ribavirin in (C) belongs to the hepatitis C regimens, not to hepatitis B, whose drugs are entecavir, lamivudine, and tenofovir.

Q: A patient with chronic hepatitis C is prescribed sofosbuvir. Which statement about this drug is accurate?
A) It is the first-in-class RNA polymerase inhibitor and is not to be used as monotherapy
B) It is a nucleoside reverse transcriptase inhibitor used for hepatitis B
C) It is given as monotherapy, because it was the first regimen that does not require interferon
D) It is a neuraminidase inhibitor active against RNA viruses generally
Answer: A
Rationale: Sofosbuvir is "the first-in-class RNA polymerase inhibitor" for chronic hepatitis C and the first treatment "that can be given without interferon," but it is used with ribavirin and is "not to be used as monotherapy." Harvoni, sofosbuvir with ledipasvir, was the separate milestone: "the first treatment for hepatitis C that does not require the combined use of ribavirin," one pill once a day.
Trap: (C) is the reflex pick because "without interferon" and "as monotherapy" sound like the same claim, and they are two different claims about two different drugs. (B) is the mirror error of the previous item, sorting a hepatitis C drug into the hepatitis B group; the cue is that hepatitis B gets entecavir, lamivudine, and tenofovir while hepatitis C gets the -buvir, -asvir, and -previr combinations headed by sofosbuvir.

Subtopic: ribavirin and route

Q: An infant is hospitalized with severe respiratory syncytial virus infection and ribavirin is ordered. Which route matches this indication, and what does that route change for staff?
A) Oral, and no special staff precaution applies
B) Inhaled aerosol, and pregnant health care providers must stay out of the room during administration
C) Intravenous, and the drug must be infused over 1 hour
D) Inhaled aerosol, and staff need only wear gloves
Answer: B
Rationale: Inhaled ribavirin by small-particle aerosol generator is for hospitalized infants with severe RSV, while the oral form is used for hepatitis C, so the route names both the disease and the population. Ribavirin carries a black box warning for significant teratogenic and embryocidal effects, and the chapter states that "pregnant health care providers, or those wishing to become pregnant, must stay out of the patient rooms when the aerosol form is being administered."
Trap: (D) is the reflex pick because personal protective equipment is the usual answer to an exposure hazard; here the rule removes the at-risk staff member from the room instead. The same exposure logic extends to the patient's household, since ribavirin is contraindicated in pregnant women and their male sexual partners, may be present in the body for up to 6 months after exposure, and requires at least two reliable forms of contraception during treatment and for 6 months after.

Topic: antiretroviral classes and mechanisms
Subtopic: the three viral enzymes and the class suffixes

Q: A patient's regimen includes raltegravir. Which viral enzyme does this drug inhibit, and what does that enzyme normally do?
A) Protease, which chemically separates new viral RNA from viral protein molecules
B) Integrase, which splices the newly made viral DNA into the host cell DNA to form a provirus
C) Reverse transcriptase, which builds DNA from the viral RNA genome
D) Neuraminidase, which lets budding virions escape the infected cell
Answer: B
Rationale: Integrase strand transfer inhibitors work by "inhibiting the catalytic activity of the enzyme integrase," and integrase "promotes the integration of this viral DNA into the host cell DNA. This hybrid DNA complex is known as a provirus." The chapter says the naming is deliberate: "the name of each class... provides a reminder of its role," and the suffix -tegravir marks this class, as in raltegravir, dolutegravir, and cabotegravir.
Trap: (C) is the reflex pick because reverse transcriptase is the HIV enzyme students meet first and it acts immediately before integrase in the replication sequence, so the wrong step feels like the right one. (D) names an influenza enzyme rather than an HIV one.

Q: A different patient is taking indinavir. Which enzyme does this drug inhibit?
A) Integrase, and the suffix -navir marks that class
B) Protease, which chemically separates the new viral RNA from viral protein molecules
C) Reverse transcriptase, an RNA-directed DNA polymerase
D) Protease, which splices the viral DNA into the host chromosome
Answer: B
Rationale: Protease inhibitors work by "inhibiting the protease retroviral enzyme," and protease "serves to chemically separate the new viral RNA from viral protein molecules"; the key-terms box adds that a protease cleaves the peptide bonds linking amino acids. The suffix -navir marks this class, as -tegravir marks the integrase inhibitors.
Trap: (A) is the mirror error of the previous item, attaching the -navir suffix to the integrase class. (D) is the half-right pick that catches more students: it names the correct class but gives integrase's job, so the enzyme name alone is not enough to answer on.

Subtopic: reverse transcriptase inhibitors and the entry blockers

Q: A regimen contains both zidovudine and nevirapine, and the patient asks why two drugs that hit the same enzyme are both needed. What distinguishes an NRTI from an NNRTI?
A) NRTIs block reverse transcriptase and NNRTIs block integrase
B) Both block reverse transcriptase; the NRTIs are nucleoside or nucleotide analogues and the NNRTIs are not
C) Both block protease; the NRTIs are analogues and the NNRTIs are not
D) NRTIs are used only in pregnancy and NNRTIs only in treatment-experienced patients
Answer: B
Rationale: Both classes block reverse transcriptase, so membership is the reliable discriminator: zidovudine, abacavir, didanosine, emtricitabine, lamivudine, stavudine, and the tenofovirs are the NRTIs, while nevirapine, delavirdine, doravirine, efavirenz, etravirine, and rilpivirine are the NNRTIs. The chapter also notes a clinical use for the distinction, that switching a patient from a protease inhibitor to an NNRTI may help reduce lipodystrophy.
Trap: (A) is the reflex pick because the extra N looks as though it must signal a different target; it signals a different chemical structure instead, since the two classes are one letter apart, hit the same enzyme, and appear in the same HAART recipes.

Q: A treatment-experienced patient with HIV is being taught to give an antiretroviral by subcutaneous injection. Which drug and class is this?
A) Enfuvirtide, the only fusion inhibitor and the only injected antiretroviral named in this chapter
B) Maraviroc, the only entry inhibitor, given subcutaneously
C) Raltegravir, an integrase inhibitor available only by injection
D) Tenofovir, a nucleotide analogue given subcutaneously
Answer: A
Rationale: Enfuvirtide works by "inhibiting viral fusion" and is the only drug in its class as well as the only subcutaneously injected antiretroviral the chapter names. Fusion is defined as "the process by which viruses attach themselves to, or fuse with, the cell membranes of host cells."
Trap: (B) is the reflex pick because enfuvirtide and maraviroc are twinned in every other respect: both block HIV before it gets inside, both are the sole member of their class, and both are indicated for treatment-experienced patients. Maraviroc is oral, so route is what separates them.

Q: A patient is starting maraviroc and the pharmacy sends an FDA-approved medication guide along with it. Which description of this drug is accurate?
A) It is a fusion inhibitor that binds the viral envelope before attachment
B) It is an entry inhibitor and CCR5 antagonist that binds the type 5 chemokine coreceptors on the CD4 cells, and it carries a risk of hepatotoxicity with allergic-type features
C) It is an integrase inhibitor, and it does not interact with CYP3A4 inducers or inhibitors
D) It is a protease inhibitor associated with nephrolithiasis
Answer: B
Rationale: Maraviroc works by "selectively and reversibly binding to the type 5 chemokine coreceptors located on the CD4 cells," is the only drug in its class, requires an FDA-approved medication guide before dispensing, and carries "hepatotoxicity with allergic-type features."
Trap: (A) is the mirror error of the previous item, since fusion inhibition is enfuvirtide's mechanism and maraviroc binds the host cell's coreceptor rather than the virus. (C) states raltegravir's CYP3A4 exemption and (D) states indinavir's signature toxicity, both real facts moved onto the wrong drug.

Topic: hiv monitoring and prophylaxis regimens
Subtopic: the two numbers

Q: A patient with HIV has a CD4 count that has fallen to 180 cells/mm3. What does this number most directly signal?
A) The viral load is now undetectable
B) This is the level at which prophylaxis for opportunistic infections is most frequently given
C) The patient has reached the primary goal of antiretroviral therapy
D) The patient no longer requires antiretroviral therapy
Answer: B
Rationale: A CD4 count below 200 cells/mm3 is the point at which prophylaxis for opportunistic infections "is most frequently given." Opportunistic infections are defined by the host rather than the organism: infections caused by "any type of microorganism that occur in an immunocompromised host but normally would not occur in an immunocompetent host."
Trap: (C) is the reflex pick because CD4 count and viral load are both numbers a patient tracks and the two merge in memory. The cue is that CD4 200 is a floor you fall below and act on, while viral load below 50 copies/mL is a target you drive down to.

Q: The same patient's viral load report reads fewer than 50 copies/mL. How should the nurse interpret this result?
A) It represents an undetectable viral load and a primary goal of antiretroviral therapy
B) It is the threshold for beginning prophylaxis against opportunistic infections
C) It is evidence of drug resistance
D) It means the virus has been eliminated from the body
Answer: A
Rationale: A viral load below 50 copies/mL is "an undetectable" viral load and "a primary goal of antiretroviral therapy." Evidence of drug resistance is a different pattern entirely, a falling CD4 count and a rising viral load moving in opposite directions at the same time.
Trap: (B) is the mirror error of the previous item, swapping the two numbers so that the therapy target becomes the prophylaxis trigger. (D) is the eradication distractor the chapter plants throughout, since these drugs suppress but do not cure the infection and undetectable is not the same as eliminated.

Subtopic: prophylaxis before versus after exposure

Q: A nurse sustains a needlestick from a device used on a patient with HIV. Which regimen does the chapter give for occupational exposure prophylaxis?
A) Emtricitabine and tenofovir alone, taken daily
B) Raltegravir plus emtricitabine and tenofovir, started after the exposure
C) Acyclovir, started within 72 hours
D) Didanosine alone
Answer: B
Rationale: Table 40.5 gives raltegravir plus Truvada, which is emtricitabine and tenofovir, following exposure to "blood; fluid containing visible blood or other potentially infectious fluid or tissue." The transmission risk from a percutaneous injury is 0.3%.
Trap: (A) is the reflex pick because Truvada appears in both prophylaxis regimens, which is precisely what makes them swappable; Truvada alone and daily is the preexposure regimen, not the postexposure one. (C) imports a herpes zoster drug and its window into an HIV exposure.

Q: The HIV-negative partner of a patient with HIV asks about a daily pill that would keep them from becoming infected. What should the nurse describe?
A) Raltegravir plus Truvada, taken daily for life
B) Preexposure prophylaxis with tenofovir and emtricitabine, up to 90% effective if it is used as prescribed
C) An HIV vaccine, now available to high-risk partners
D) Zidovudine monotherapy, begun after any exposure
Answer: B
Rationale: PrEP is tenofovir and emtricitabine taken daily before exposure by people at "very high risk," and it is "up to 90%" effective "if it is used as prescribed."
Trap: (A) is the mirror error of the previous item, applying the postexposure regimen to a preexposure situation because Truvada sits inside both. (C) is wrong for a mechanistic reason worth holding onto: reverse transcriptase "has a high rate of errors," which allows frequent genetic mutations and hampers the development of an effective vaccine.

Subtopic: why combination therapy

Q: A patient asks why HIV is treated with a combination of at least three drugs rather than with one. Which statements support the use of combination therapy? Select all that apply.
A) The combination is more effective against resistant strains, because drugs that work by different mechanisms reduce the likelihood of viral resistance
B) The combination reduces viral load, which is a primary goal of therapy
C) The combination carries fewer toxicities than single-drug therapy
D) The combination reduces T-cell counts
E) The combination increases the incidence of opportunistic infections
Answer: A, B
Rationale: HAART uses "combinations of antiretroviral drugs" that work by different mechanisms, "reducing the likelihood of viral resistance," and driving the viral load below 50 copies/mL is a primary goal of therapy. Therapy is "normally begun immediately on confirmation of HIV infection" and usually uses "at least three medications."
Trap: (C), (D), and (E) are each a true statement with its direction reversed, so check direction before content. Antiretrovirals "carry significant toxicities," treatment "significantly increases T-cell counts," and treatment produces "a significant reduction in mortality and incidence of opportunistic infections."

Topic: antiviral toxicity and patient teaching
Subtopic: matching toxicity to the organ already compromised

Q: A heart transplant recipient taking cyclosporine develops a CMV infection. Why does the chapter prefer ganciclovir over foscarnet for this patient?
A) Cyclosporine is nephrotoxic, and the dose-limiting toxicity of foscarnet and cidofovir is renal toxicity
B) Cyclosporine suppresses the bone marrow, and ganciclovir spares the marrow
C) Foscarnet has no activity against CMV
D) Ganciclovir is the only one of the two available in an oral form
Answer: A
Rationale: The chapter reasons it out as a decision: cyclosporine "is nephrotoxic. Therefore using foscarnet in this patient may be more dangerous than using ganciclovir." Ganciclovir's dose-limiting toxicity is bone marrow suppression, while "that of foscarnet and cidofovir is renal toxicity."
Trap: (B) inverts the toxicity assignment, since marrow suppression is ganciclovir's own dose-limiting problem and cannot be the reason to choose it. The cue that survives the exam is to match the drug to the organ the patient has not already compromised.

Q: A patient immunocompromised after a bone marrow transplant develops a CMV infection. Which drug does the chapter suggest may be the better choice here, and why?
A) Ganciclovir, because its dose-limiting toxicity is renal
B) Foscarnet, because ganciclovir's dose-limiting toxicity is bone marrow suppression
C) Acyclovir, because it spares both the marrow and the kidney
D) Zidovudine, because it treats CMV in immunocompromised hosts
Answer: B
Rationale: The chapter states that "a patient... immunocompromised because of a bone marrow transplant might be better treated using foscarnet," because ganciclovir's dose-limiting toxicity is bone marrow suppression and this patient's marrow is already the compromised organ.
Trap: (A) is the mirror error of the previous item, swapping the two organ toxicities so the same drug is chosen for the opposite reason. (D) is wrong twice over: zidovudine is an HIV drug, and its major dose-limiting adverse effect is also bone marrow suppression, which is the reason a patient is often switched off it.

Q: A patient with HIV is starting zidovudine. Which of the following are adverse effects of the drug itself? Select all that apply.
A) Nausea
B) Headache
C) Bone marrow suppression
D) Retinitis
E) Kaposi's sarcoma
Answer: A, B, C
Rationale: Table 40.2 lists nausea, headaches, and bone marrow suppression for zidovudine, and the chapter singles out bone marrow suppression as "the major dose-limiting adverse effect... often the reason a patient... must be switched to another anti-HIV drug."
Trap: (D) and (E) are manifestations of HIV disease rather than adverse effects of any drug, and that separation is the axis this item sorts on. Retinitis belongs to CMV disease, which is HHV-5, and Kaposi's sarcoma is caused by HHV-8, an oncogenic herpesvirus treated with antineoplastics, radiation, and surgery rather than with an antiviral.

Subtopic: what to teach and when to escalate

Q: A patient starting acyclovir for recurrent genital herpes says, "So this will get rid of the virus for good." Which response is accurate?
A) "Yes, a full course will eradicate the virus."
B) "No. These drugs suppress the infection but do not cure it. What you should see is that lesions crust over and recurrences become less frequent."
C) "Yes, as long as your partner is treated at the same time."
D) "No, but taking it every day will keep you from passing the virus to a partner."
Answer: B
Rationale: The patient teaching section states that these drugs "suppress but do not cure the viral infection," and what the patient can realistically expect is that "herpetic lesions will crust over, and the frequency of recurrence will decrease."
Trap: (A) is the eradication distractor the chapter plants in more than one review question, once worded as "these drugs will eventually eliminate the virus from my body," and it pulls because a completed antibiotic course really does clear a bacterial infection. (D) sounds responsible but credits the drug with preventing transmission, which abstinence and condoms do.

Q: A patient taking nevirapine calls to report a rash that now includes blistering, oral lesions, fever, and myalgias. What is the priority nursing response?
A) Reassure the patient that rash is a listed adverse effect of nevirapine and continue the drug
B) Tell the patient to contact the prescriber immediately or seek prompt emergency medical attention
C) Advise an antihistamine and reassess at the next scheduled visit
D) Hold the next dose and call back in 24 to 48 hours
Answer: B
Rationale: Rash is a listed adverse effect of nevirapine along with fever, nausea, headache, and abnormal liver function test results, but the chapter directs that blistering, fever, malaise, myalgias, oral lesions, swelling or edema, or conjunctivitis calls for contacting the prescriber immediately or seeking prompt emergency medical attention.
Trap: (A) is the reflex pick because rash genuinely is on nevirapine's adverse effect list, so the drug name appears to settle the question; it is the accompanying findings that set the priority. The cue is that an isolated rash is monitored, while a rash plus mucous membrane or systemic signs is an emergency. (D) borrows the 24-to-48-hour figure from the teaching about recurrent herpes episodes, where it has nothing to do with an adverse reaction.
