# Chapter 45 drills — Antineoplastic Drugs Part 1: Cancer Overview and Cell Cycle–Specific Drugs

Authored from `sources/Ch45_Fact_Bank.md` (Lilley, Collins & Snyder, 10th ed.).

Blueprint weight is **2 questions of 121 (1.7%), depth `focused`** — the
second-lightest chapter on the blueprint, sitting on a 140 KB source pull. That
earns **29 drills**, near the top of the 20–30 `focused` band because the
chapter's four toxicities are transferable to any chemotherapy patient, but
nowhere near what the source could support. The job here is subtraction.

Six topics, following the fact bank's own emphasis: the organizing distinction
the chapter is named for, the five cell cycle–specific classes, and the four
toxicities that generate nearly every testable item — myelosuppression and
nadir, emetic potential, extravasation and vesicants, and tumor lysis syndrome.

**Left out, deliberately.** All dosing, pharmacokinetics, and contraindications,
because the book itself declines to print them. The per-drug profiles: fifteen
drugs get a paragraph each across pp. 710–715, and at two blueprint items what
is testable is the class and its one distinguishing feature, so individual drug
facts survive only where they carry safety weight (vincristine's route,
vincristine's neurotoxicity, asparaginase's pancreatitis, irinotecan's diarrhea
as a distractor). Also cut: Table 45.6's twelve-system toxicity inventory, the
targeted-therapy tables, the four interaction tables, carcinogenesis and
etiology, tumor markers, and — the one real loss — §4's tumor vocabulary,
including the benign-versus-malignant table and the tissue-of-origin SATA. That
material is definitional rather than decisional, and at this weight the
toxicities that change what a nurse does at the bedside outrank it. If the
instructor's two items turn out to be tumor classification, this file
underserves them; the fact bank's GAPS section flags that the blueprint carries
no topic list for this chapter.

Every invertible distinction the fact bank flags is drilled from both ends:
specific and nonspecific, vinca and taxane, topoisomerase I and II,
myelosuppression and nadir, allopurinol and rasburicase, and the two dose-tiered
emetic drugs.

Topic: cell cycle specificity in cancer chemotherapy
Subtopic: specific versus nonspecific action

Q: A combination regimen pairs a drug that is cytotoxic only while DNA is being synthesized with a second drug that kills cells at any point in the cycle. How should the nurse classify the first drug?
A) A cell cycle–specific drug, because it is cytotoxic during a specific phase of the cellular growth cycle
B) A cell cycle–nonspecific drug, because it is cytotoxic in any phase of the cellular growth cycle
C) A cell cycle–specific drug, because it is the more potent of the two agents
D) A cell cycle–nonspecific drug, because anything acting on DNA acts throughout the cycle
Answer: A
Rationale: Cell cycle–specific denotes antineoplastic drugs that are cytotoxic during a specific phase of the cellular growth cycle; the antimetabolites are the example, working primarily in the S phase during which DNA synthesis is most active. Chapter 45 covers the cell cycle–specific drugs and Chapter 46 the nonspecific ones, and the chapter states that split outright on p. 706.
Trap: (C) reaches the right label by the wrong reasoning and will carry a learner to the wrong answer on the mirror item. The book defeats potency reasoning itself: regardless of the cell cycle characteristics of a drug, it is more effective on rapidly growing tumors. Specific and nonspecific describe when a drug kills, not how hard.

Q: The second drug in that regimen is described as cytotoxic in any phase of the cellular growth cycle. What does that tell the nurse?
A) It is cell cycle–nonspecific, and being phase-indifferent does not make it the stronger of the two drugs
B) It is cell cycle–specific, since acting in every phase means it must be targeted to each one
C) It is cell cycle–nonspecific and therefore more effective than the phase-limited drug
D) It is cell cycle–nonspecific and is therefore reserved for slowly growing tumors
Answer: A
Rationale: Antineoplastic drugs that are cytotoxic in any phase of the cycle are cell cycle–nonspecific. The chapter adds that drugs may have actions falling into both categories, and that regardless of the cell cycle characteristics of a drug, it is more effective on rapidly growing tumors (p. 706).
Trap: (C) is the mirror error of the previous item, now attaching potency to the other label. (D) inverts the chapter's one comparative claim, which is that rapid growth predicts response for both categories alike.

Subtopic: phases of the cell growth cycle

Q: A patient asks why chemotherapy must be repeated in cycles rather than given once until every cancer cell is dead. Which explanation is grounded in the cell growth cycle?
A) Cancer cells sitting in the resting phase are not susceptible to cell cycle–specific drugs, so any single dose misses them
B) Cancer cells develop resistance to the drug within hours of the first dose
C) The drugs are cleared from the body too quickly to reach all of the cells at once
D) Repeated dosing is required to keep the tumor's doubling time from shortening
Answer: A
Rationale: Table 45.5 states that cancer cells in the G0 resting phase are not susceptible to the toxic effects of cell cycle–specific drugs, and p. 704 describes G0 cells as considered out of the cell cycle. That escape hatch is the rationale for combination regimens and for the existence of a separate cell cycle–nonspecific class in Chapter 46.
Trap: (B) is the reflex pick because acquired resistance is the familiar explanation for an incomplete kill. The chapter's stated reason is phase location, not resistance. When a stem asks why chemotherapy fails to eradicate every cell, the resting phase is the answer.

Q: A student lists the phases of the cell growth cycle as G1, S, G2, prophase, and metaphase. Which correction should the nurse make?
A) The five phases are G0, G1, S, G2, and M; prophase and metaphase are subphases within M
B) The five phases are correct, but prophase and metaphase come before G1 rather than after G2
C) The list is accurate as given, since mitosis is subdivided rather than named as a phase
D) The five phases are G0, G1, S, G2, and telophase
Answer: A
Rationale: Table 45.5 gives five phases: G0 resting, G1 first gap or postmitotic, S DNA synthesis, G2 second gap or premitotic, and M mitosis. M is divided into four subphases — prophase, metaphase, anaphase, and telophase — after which the cell divides into two daughter cells.
Trap: (D) is the same level swap with a different subphase promoted, which is why it survives a quick scan. Any option that lists a mitotic subphase alongside G1 and S has collapsed two levels of the outline into one.

Q: A pathology report describes a tumor with a high percentage of its cells in mitosis at any given time and a short doubling time. Which conclusion is supported?
A) The tumor has a high growth fraction, and its short doubling time makes it difficult to cure because of rapid regrowth
B) The tumor has a high mitotic index, which is another name for the same measurement as growth fraction
C) The short doubling time predicts a better cure rate, since rapidly dividing tumors respond to chemotherapy
D) The tumor is largely in the resting phase and will respond poorly to any chemotherapy
Answer: A
Rationale: Growth fraction is the percentage of cells in mitosis at any given time, and p. 704 states that tumors with shorter doubling times are often difficult to cure because of rapid regrowth. Chemotherapy is most effective in a rapidly dividing or highly proliferative tumor, so rapid proliferation cuts both ways.
Trap: (B) treats growth fraction and mitotic index as synonyms because both count dividing cells. Growth fraction is a percentage; the mitotic index is the actual number of cells per unit, usually 1000 cells, undergoing mitosis. Doubling time is neither of those — it is a clock.

Topic: cell cycle–specific drug classes and mechanisms
Subtopic: which classes belong to this chapter

Q: A nurse is reviewing which antineoplastic drug classes are cell cycle–specific. Select all that apply.
A) Antimetabolites
B) Mitotic inhibitors
C) Alkylating drugs
D) Alkaloid topoisomerase II inhibitors
E) Topoisomerase I inhibitors
F) Antineoplastic enzymes
G) Cytotoxic antibiotics
H) Hormonal and antihormonal drugs
Answer: A, B, D, E, F
Rationale: The chapter's key points name exactly five cell cycle–specific classes: antimetabolites, mitotic inhibitors, alkaloid topoisomerase II inhibitors, topoisomerase I inhibitors, and antineoplastic enzymes (p. 724). Alkylating drugs, cytotoxic antibiotics, and hormonal and antihormonal drugs are cell cycle–nonspecific and belong to Chapter 46.
Trap: (C) is the strongest pull, because alkylating drugs are the most familiar chemotherapy class and the name sounds mechanism-directed; they are in fact the flagship nonspecific class. (G) and (H) pull for the same reason. Scoring is all-or-nothing, so the five are learned as a set rather than judged one at a time.

Subtopic: mechanism by class

Q: Methotrexate is started, and the nurse explains that it belongs to the antimetabolites. Which statement describes how this class works?
A) It is an antagonist analogue that interferes with DNA synthesis, working primarily in the S phase
B) It is an agonist analogue that supplies the folic acid a rapidly dividing tumor demands
C) It acts in any phase of the cycle by cross-linking DNA strands directly
D) It halts mitosis during metaphase by producing nonfunctional microtubules
Answer: A
Rationale: Antimetabolites are cell cycle–specific analogues that antagonize folic acid, purines, or pyrimidines, and they work primarily in the S phase during which DNA synthesis is most active (p. 709). An analogue may have agonist or antagonist activity, and an antagonist analogue is also known as an antimetabolite.
Trap: (B) collapses analogue and antimetabolite into synonyms and then picks the wrong half. Every antimetabolite is an analogue, but not every analogue is an antimetabolite — the antimetabolite is specifically the antagonist kind. (D) is the taxane mechanism borrowed from a class two pages later.

Q: A patient receives vincristine. By which mechanism do the vinca alkaloids kill dividing cells?
A) They bind to tubulin and prevent the assembly of microtubules, so the mitotic spindle dissolves
B) They cause the formation of nonfunctional microtubules, which halts mitosis during metaphase
C) They inhibit topoisomerase II, which causes breaks in DNA strands
D) They convert asparagine to aspartic acid so the cell cannot synthesize protein
Answer: A
Rationale: The vinca alkaloids are plant-derived mitotic inhibitors that bind tubulin and prevent the assembly of microtubules, dissolving the mitotic spindle. The mitotic inhibitors as a group all work shortly before or during mitosis (p. 712).
Trap: (B) is the taxane mechanism, and the two classes swap constantly because both are plant-derived drugs acting on microtubules around mitosis. The cue is that vincas prevent microtubules from being built while taxanes build them wrong.

Q: The same patient is later switched to paclitaxel. How does its action on microtubules differ from vincristine's?
A) Paclitaxel causes the formation of nonfunctional microtubules, which halts mitosis during metaphase
B) Paclitaxel binds tubulin and prevents microtubules from assembling at all
C) Paclitaxel leaves microtubules intact and instead binds the DNA–topoisomerase I complex
D) Paclitaxel acts in the S phase rather than around mitosis
Answer: A
Rationale: The taxanes cause the formation of nonfunctional microtubules, which halts mitosis during metaphase (p. 712). Both taxanes and vincas are mitotic inhibitors reaching the same endpoint — a cell that cannot complete mitosis — by opposite routes.
Trap: (B) is the mirror error of the previous item. Prevented assembly is the vinca mechanism; a built but nonfunctional spindle is the taxane one. The name cue is that vincas come from periwinkle and taxanes carry the "-taxel" stem from the yew.

Q: A patient with metastatic colorectal cancer is to receive irinotecan. Which description of this drug's class is accurate?
A) It is a camptothecin that binds the DNA–topoisomerase I complex in the S phase, retarding religation and producing a DNA strand break
B) It is a derivative of epipodophyllotoxin that inhibits topoisomerase II in late S and G2
C) It is a vinca alkaloid that dissolves the mitotic spindle during metaphase
D) It is an antineoplastic enzyme approved exclusively for acute lymphocytic leukemia
Answer: A
Rationale: The camptothecins — topotecan and irinotecan — inhibit proper DNA function in the S phase by binding to the DNA–topoisomerase I complex, retarding religation and producing a DNA strand break (p. 714).
Trap: (B) names the other topoisomerase, and a single numeral is the whole distinction between the two classes. Topoisomerase I means the camptothecins in S phase; topoisomerase II means the epipodophyllotoxins in late S and G2.

Q: A patient with testicular cancer receives etoposide. Which statement about this drug is accurate?
A) It is a derivative of epipodophyllotoxin that inhibits topoisomerase II, causing breaks in DNA strands, and acts in late S and G2
B) It is a camptothecin that binds the DNA–topoisomerase I complex during the S phase
C) It is an alkylating drug and therefore cell cycle–nonspecific
D) It depletes circulating asparagine so that leukemic cells cannot synthesize protein
Answer: A
Rationale: Etoposide and teniposide are derivatives of epipodophyllotoxin and act by inhibiting the enzyme topoisomerase II, which causes breaks in DNA strands (p. 712).
Trap: (B) is the mirror error of the previous item, with the numeral flipped. Note also that the book files the topoisomerase II inhibitors under the mitotic-inhibitor heading on p. 712 while listing them separately in its key points, so an option that calls etoposide a mitotic inhibitor is not thereby wrong.

Q: A child with acute lymphocytic leukemia is receiving pegaspargase. Beyond the usual chemotherapy toxicities, which assessment is specific to this drug class?
A) Monitor blood glucose and for abdominal pain, because impaired pancreatic function can produce hyperglycemia and severe or fatal pancreatitis
B) Monitor for peripheral neuropathy, because this class is the most significant neurotoxin among the cytotoxic drugs
C) Monitor for delayed diarrhea beginning 2 to 10 days after the infusion
D) Monitor the palms and soles for hand-foot syndrome
Answer: A
Rationale: The antineoplastic enzymes asparaginase and pegaspargase are approved exclusively for the treatment of acute lymphocytic leukemia. They convert asparagine to aspartic acid and ammonia, so leukemic cells are then unable to synthesize the asparagine required. Their distinguishing toxicity is impaired pancreatic function, with hyperglycemia and severe or fatal pancreatitis (p. 715).
Trap: (B) is vincristine's signature toxicity and (C) is irinotecan's. Both pull because they are the chapter's other two drug-specific assessments, and a learner who has memorized "chemotherapy has drug-specific toxicities" without pairing each to its drug picks whichever sounds most serious.

Topic: chemotherapy-induced myelosuppression and nadir
Subtopic: naming the condition and the moment

Q: A patient who received chemotherapy 14 days ago calls the clinic. Applying the concept of the nadir, when is this patient at greatest risk for infection?
A) Now, because the nadir normally occurs approximately 10 to 28 days after dosing
B) Within the first 24 hours after the infusion, when drug levels peak
C) Not until the next cycle begins and the doses accumulate
D) Only once a fever has actually developed
Answer: A
Rationale: The nadir is the lowest point in any fluctuating value over time — for example, the lowest white blood cell count measured after the count has been depressed by chemotherapy. It normally occurs approximately 10 to 28 days after dosing, and anticipating it guides the timing of prophylactic antibiotics and hematopoietic growth factors (p. 707).
Trap: (B) assumes toxicity tracks the infusion, which is true for acute reactions and false for marrow toxicity. The chapter puts the window at 10 to 28 days, so the patient feels best at the point of greatest danger.

Q: The same patient's counts return with a low white blood cell count, a low hemoglobin, and a low platelet count all at once. Which term names the condition producing all three?
A) Myelosuppression, the suppression of bone marrow function
B) Nadir, since the nadir is what makes all three counts fall
C) Tumor lysis syndrome
D) A paraneoplastic syndrome
Answer: A
Rationale: Myelosuppression, also known as bone marrow suppression, results from destruction of the rapidly dividing cellular precursors of white blood cells, red blood cells, and platelets, and leads to leukopenia, anemia, and thrombocytopenia (p. 707).
Trap: (B) is the mirror error of the previous item. Myelosuppression is the condition and the nadir is the moment, which is why the key-terms box prints them side by side. "Why are all three cell lines low" wants myelosuppression; "when is the risk greatest" wants nadir.

Subtopic: recognizing and prioritizing the cytopenias

Q: A patient is thrombocytopenic after chemotherapy. Which findings should the nurse attribute specifically to the low platelet count? Select all that apply.
A) Petechiae
B) Gingival bleeding
C) Fatigue and pallor of the conjunctiva
D) Prolonged bleeding from puncture sites
E) Ecchymosis
F) Fever and chills
Answer: A, B, D, E
Rationale: Thrombocytopenia makes the patient more susceptible to bleeding, and the chapter lists petechiae, purpura, ecchymosis, gingival bleeding, and prolonged bleeding from puncture sites. Fatigue and pallor sort to anemia, which often leads to hypoxia and fatigue; fever and chills sort to neutropenia.
Trap: (C) is the reliable decoy because fatigue feels universal in a chemotherapy patient, but this chapter files it under anemia. (F) sorts into the neutropenia bucket, and an option offering an elevated white blood cell count would be wrong on its face, since that count is the value that has fallen.

Q: A patient with severe bone marrow suppression has documented fatigue, altered body image from alopecia, impaired mobility, and a white blood cell count near its nadir. Which problem takes priority?
A) Risk for infection
B) Fatigue
C) Altered body image
D) Impaired physical mobility
Answer: A
Rationale: All four are documented in this chapter, which is what makes the distractors strong, but the physiologic threat to life wins. Here that is infection, because myelosuppression has cost the patient the ability to mount a defense. Because fever is a principal early sign of infection, an oral or axillary temperature is taken at least every 4 hours (p. 720).
Trap: (B) pulls because fatigue is the most commonly reported chemotherapy symptom and the chapter names it directly as an effect of anemia. Priority items in this chapter resolve to the physiologic threat unless the scenario supplies active bleeding or an airway or circulation problem.

Q: A patient's absolute neutrophil count returns at 420 cells/mm3 on the morning the next chemotherapy cycle is due. What does the nurse anticipate?
A) The dose will be held, because an ANC below 500 is severe neutropenia and carries a high risk for infection
B) The dose will proceed as scheduled, since 420 falls within the normal range of 1500 to 8000 cells/mm3
C) The dose will proceed with an added antibiotic, since the count only reflects marrow recovery
D) A platelet transfusion will be given first, since this value reflects the bleeding risk
Answer: A
Rationale: A normal absolute neutrophil count is 1500 to 8000 cells/mm3, severe neutropenia is less than 500, and Box 45.2 places an ANC of 500 or lower at high risk for infection. Chemotherapy is held below 500 (p. 708).
Trap: (D) reads any low chemotherapy value as a platelet problem, which is a common reflex because bleeding precautions are the most visible nursing intervention in this chapter. The ANC reports neutrophils; the separate bleeding threshold is a platelet count under 100,000/mm3, with some guidelines using 50,000.

Topic: vesicant extravasation and chemotherapy route safety
Subtopic: recognizing and managing extravasation

Q: During an infusion of a chemotherapy drug with vesicant potential, the nurse notes swelling and blanching at the intravenous site. What is the first action?
A) Stop the infusion and leave the intravenous cannula in place
B) Apply cold compresses and elevate the extremity
C) Inject the prescribed antidote through the catheter
D) Give subcutaneous epinephrine at the site
Answer: A
Rationale: The drug is usually discontinued immediately and the intravenous cannula is left in place for possible use of antidotes through the cannula (p. 721). Extravasation is the leakage of an intravenously administered medication into the tissue space surrounding the vein, and it is a serious complication of chemotherapy.
Trap: (C) is the strong distractor because it is eventually correct — just not first, and it depends on the cannula still being there. Stop the exposure before treating it.

Q: Two chemotherapy drugs infiltrate on the same unit. One is classified as an irritant and the other as a vesicant. What distinguishes the vesicant?
A) It causes cell death with extravasation, producing necrosis with ulcerations
B) It irritates the intravenous site and vein without causing tissue death
C) It produces a mild local reaction such as erythema or bruising
D) It causes a systemic hypersensitivity reaction rather than a local injury
Answer: A
Rationale: These drugs may be irritants, irritating the intravenous site and vein, or vesicants, causing cell death with extravasation and necrosis with ulcerations (p. 718). Only vesicant extravasation carries emergency status.
Trap: (C) describes what topotecan extravasation produces, a mild local reaction such as erythema or bruising, which sits at the irritant end of the spectrum and reads as reassuring rather than wrong.

Q: Vincristine has extravasated, and the protocol directs hyaluronidase 150 units/mL by multiple subcutaneous injections into the site. What else does Table 45.8 specify?
A) Warm compresses, since corticosteroids and topical cooling appear to worsen toxicity
B) Cold compresses, since cooling limits the spread of the drug through the tissue
C) A topical corticosteroid cream applied over the affected area
D) Elevation with an ice pack for the first 24 hours
Answer: A
Rationale: Table 45.8 covers etoposide, teniposide, vinblastine, and vincristine, and specifies hyaluronidase 1 to 6 mL by multiple subcutaneous injections into the site, repeated over the next few hours, plus warm compresses. Its footnote states that administration of corticosteroids and topical cooling appear to worsen toxicity (p. 712).
Trap: (B) is right elsewhere and wrong here. The chapter lists hot or cold packs among possible protocol measures on p. 721, which is exactly what makes cold read as safe. Compress temperature is drug-specific, and the instruction is to follow the hospital or infusion protocol without exception.

Subtopic: route safety and drug-specific assessment

Q: A pharmacy technician asks whether vincristine can be sent up for intrathecal administration, since cytarabine on the same unit is given by that route. What is the nurse's response?
A) Vincristine is for intravenous use only and is fatal if given by other routes; several deaths have been reported after inadvertent intrathecal administration
B) Vincristine may be given intrathecally provided it is diluted in 25 to 50 mL first
C) Either drug may be given intrathecally as long as it is dispensed in a syringe
D) Intrathecal vincristine is acceptable when the goal is to treat central nervous system disease
Answer: A
Rationale: Every vincristine product carries the warning that it is for intravenous use only and fatal if given by other routes. The safeguards are to dilute the dose in 25 to 50 mL and never dispense it in a syringe. Intrathecal is a legitimate route in this same chapter for cytarabine, which may be given intravenously, subcutaneously, or intrathecally (pp. 712 and 713).
Trap: (D) pulls because the intrathecal route exists precisely to deliver chemotherapy to the central nervous system and is correct for other drugs on the same unit. The axis of this question is route, not indication. (B) inverts the safeguard: intrathecal drugs are not normally dispensed in a minibag, so the 25 to 50 mL dilution is what makes the fatal error harder to commit.

Q: A patient receiving vincristine reports numbness and tingling in the fingertips and difficulty buttoning a shirt. Which assessment priority does this reflect?
A) Peripheral neuropathy, because vincristine is the most significant neurotoxin of the cytotoxic drug class
B) Early myelosuppression, so the complete blood count becomes the priority assessment
C) Hand-foot syndrome developing at the infusion site
D) Cytarabine syndrome, which appears 6 to 12 hours after administration
Answer: A
Rationale: Vincristine is described as the most significant neurotoxin of the cytotoxic drug class, and it continues to be used in part because of its relative lack of bone marrow suppression (p. 713). Assessment for peripheral neuropathy is the stated priority for this drug (p. 718).
Trap: (B) is the reflex pick because "monitor the complete blood count" is the right answer for nearly every other drug in the chapter. Vincristine is the exception whose whole selling point is relative marrow sparing, so the reflex answer is the wrong assessment here.

Topic: tumor lysis syndrome and uric acid prophylaxis
Subtopic: recognizing the syndrome

Q: A patient begins induction chemotherapy for acute leukemia. Which pattern of laboratory changes is consistent with tumor lysis syndrome?
A) Elevated potassium, elevated phosphate, elevated uric acid, and reduced calcium
B) Elevated potassium, phosphate, uric acid, and calcium
C) Reduced potassium, phosphate, and uric acid with elevated calcium
D) Elevated calcium and uric acid with a normal potassium
Answer: A
Rationale: The key term defines tumor lysis syndrome by excessive cellular waste products and electrolytes including uric acid, phosphate, and potassium, and by reduced serum calcium levels. The chapter names hyperphosphatemia, hyperkalemia, and hypocalcemia, and calls it a common postchemotherapy condition often associated with induction chemotherapy for rapidly growing hematologic malignancies (pp. 701 and 710).
Trap: (B) flips calcium, and it is the intuitive reading because cells bursting suggests everything spills upward at once. Calcium is the single value that falls, and the key term prints it as the discriminator. Note also the stage cue in the stem: this is an induction-phase event in a rapidly growing hematologic malignancy, not a maintenance-phase event in a slow-growing solid tumor.

Subtopic: the two rescues and what each rescues from

Q: Allopurinol is ordered before induction chemotherapy begins. A student asks whether it is an antineoplastic drug. What is the nurse's response?
A) No; it inhibits xanthine oxidase to reduce uric acid levels, and although it is chemically similar to purines it does not disrupt DNA synthesis
B) Yes; it is a purine antimetabolite that antagonizes DNA synthesis in the S phase
C) No; it is an enzyme that degrades the uric acid already circulating in the blood
D) Yes; it slows the rate of cell kill so that waste products are released gradually
Answer: A
Rationale: Allopurinol and rasburicase are both indicated for the hyperuricemia associated with tumor lysis syndrome and are usually given in anticipation of the condition rather than in response to it. Allopurinol inhibits xanthine oxidase, which reduces serum and urinary levels of uric acid, and it is also used commonly in oral form to treat gout (p. 709).
Trap: (B) is exactly the distractor the chapter pre-empts. Allopurinol looks like a purine, so it reads as a purine antagonist and therefore an antimetabolite. The text states plainly that it does not disrupt DNA synthesis, which means it has no direct antineoplastic action at all.

Q: The same patient's uric acid is already markedly elevated. Which drug acts on uric acid that has already formed?
A) Rasburicase, an enzyme that degrades uric acid to more soluble end products
B) Allopurinol, which degrades the uric acid already present in the circulation
C) Leucovorin, which rescues the patient from hyperuricemia
D) Filgrastim, which lowers uric acid as it raises the neutrophil count
Answer: A
Rationale: Rasburicase is an enzyme that degrades uric acid to more soluble end products, while allopurinol prevents uric acid from being formed by inhibiting xanthine oxidase. Both are indicated for the hyperuricemia associated with tumor lysis syndrome (p. 709).
Trap: (B) is the mirror error of the previous item. The two drugs address the same problem from opposite ends: allopurinol stops uric acid from being made, rasburicase destroys what is already there.

Q: A patient is to receive high-dose methotrexate. Which drug is given with it, and for what purpose?
A) Leucovorin, an antidote for folic acid antagonists, always given with high-dose methotrexate as a rescue
B) Allopurinol, which is the rescue drug for methotrexate toxicity
C) Rasburicase, which reverses the binding of methotrexate at its target
D) Hyaluronidase, which rescues the bone marrow from methotrexate
Answer: A
Rationale: High-dose methotrexate is always given in conjunction with the rescue drug leucovorin, and leucovorin is an antidote for folic acid antagonists (p. 710). Allopurinol and rasburicase rescue a different problem entirely, the hyperuricemia of tumor lysis syndrome.
Trap: (B) trades one rescue for the other, and the pull is real because both rescues appear within a page of each other in this chapter. Leucovorin also appears as a component of an irinotecan and fluorouracil regimen on p. 714, which is the adjacent-fact decoy that makes it look like a companion drug rather than an antidote.

Topic: chemotherapy emetic potential and antiemetic timing
Subtopic: prophylaxis versus rescue

Q: A patient scheduled for chemotherapy asks that the antiemetic be held until nausea actually starts. What is the nurse's best response?
A) Antiemetics are given 30 to 60 minutes before chemotherapy, because preventing nausea works better than treating it once it has begun
B) That is reasonable, since antiemetics are most effective when given only on request
C) Distraction with light activity is preferred over routine antiemetic dosing
D) Holding fluids before the infusion will control the nausea without medication
Answer: A
Rationale: Antiemetics are given before chemotherapy (p. 706). Anticancer drugs cause nausea and vomiting by stimulating the cells of the chemoreceptor trigger zone, and the chapter's own review question places the antiemetic 30 to 60 minutes before the dose.
Trap: (B) is the trap that sounds patient-centered, deferring to the patient's stated preference. (D) is the trap that sounds preventive while actually risking dehydration in a patient who may already be vomiting. The principle is prevent, do not rescue.

Subtopic: dose-tiered emetic potential

Q: A nurse is told a patient will receive methotrexate and asks what level of antiemetic coverage to plan for. Why can that not be answered from the drug name alone?
A) Methotrexate crosses all three emetic tiers depending on dose, at thresholds of 250 and 1000 mg/m2
B) Methotrexate has essentially no emetic potential at any dose
C) Emetic potential is determined by the patient's prior history rather than by the drug
D) Methotrexate always sits in the high tier, so maximum coverage is assumed
Answer: A
Rationale: Box 45.1 lists methotrexate under 250 mg/m2 in the low tier, 250 to 1000 mg/m2 in the moderate tier, and over 1000 mg/m2 in the high tier (p. 707).
Trap: (D) assigns a flat tier to the drug name, which is the swap Box 45.1 is built to catch. Emetic potential is dose-tiered rather than drug-tiered for methotrexate, cytarabine, and fluorouracil, each of which appears in more than one tier with a dose threshold printed beside it.

Q: Cytarabine is ordered at 1500 mg/m2. Which emetic tier applies, and what should the nurse plan for?
A) High, because cytarabine crosses from low to high above 1000 mg/m2, so aggressive prophylactic antiemetic coverage is planned
B) Low, because cytarabine is listed in the low tier
C) Moderate, because the antimetabolites as a class are moderately emetogenic
D) No tier applies, because cytarabine is not listed in Box 45.1
Answer: A
Rationale: Box 45.1 lists cytarabine under 1000 mg/m2 in the low tier and cytarabine over 1000 mg/m2 in the high tier. Most of this chapter's cell cycle–specific drugs cluster in the low tier; the high tier is dominated by the alkylating and platinum drugs covered in Chapter 46 (p. 707).
Trap: (B) is the mirror error of the previous item, reading the drug's most common listing and stopping before the dose qualifier. The threshold is printed next to the drug name for exactly this reason.
