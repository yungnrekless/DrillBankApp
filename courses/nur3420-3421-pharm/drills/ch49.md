# Chapter 49 drills — Immunizing Drugs

Authored from `sources/Ch49_Fact_Bank.md` (Lilley, Collins & Snyder, 10th ed.).

Blueprint weight is **1 question of 121 (0.8%), depth `light`** — the lightest
chapter in the course. That earns **18 drills** across three topics, inside the
12-to-20 band a `light` chapter carries.

The set is built from the fact bank's flagged traps and its key-terms table, and
from nothing else. Every distinction the chapter states in both directions is
drilled from both ends: active and passive, live and killed, DTaP and Tdap,
pregnancy as a contraindication and hepatitis B as the printed exception to it,
minor and severe adverse effects.

Left out on purpose, because one exam item cannot justify them: the entire Drug
Profiles block (ages, valences, dose counts, intervals, and routes for the
individual products), Box 49.1's drug list, the CDC schedule references, and the
nursing process section. The book prints no dosages or schedules of its own, so
a schedule question cannot be answered from this source at all. Box 49.1's
column headings are also wrong in the printed book — it files COVID-19 vaccines
under passive immunizing drugs — so nothing here is drilled from that box;
Table 49.1 is the authority for the active/passive split.

Topic: active versus passive immunization
Subtopic: what each type provides

Q: A patient is brought to the emergency department after a rattlesnake bite and is given antivenin. The patient's daughter asks how the drug works. Which explanation is correct?
A) It supplies antibodies made outside the patient's body, so protection begins quickly but fades as those antibodies are cleared
B) It supplies an antigen that stimulates the patient to produce his own antibodies over the next several weeks
C) It stimulates T cells to release cytokines that neutralize the venom permanently
D) It is a toxoid, so it will also protect against any future bite
Answer: A
Rationale: Antivenins are passive immunizing drugs. Table 49.1 describes artificial passive immunization as direct administration of exogenous antibodies, and notes that the antibody concentration will decrease over time. Passive immunization allows for more rapid prevention or treatment of disease, but its duration is transitory, or short-lived, lasting only until the immunoglobulins are cleared by the reticuloendothelial system.
Trap: (B) is the reflex pick because "immunizing drug" is heard as "vaccine," and it describes active immunization instead. The one-word test is that active means the host makes the antibodies, while passive means pre-made antibodies are passed to the host. (D) carries the second half of the same error, since a patient given passive antibodies keeps no lasting immunity at all.

Q: A child receives tetanus toxoid at a well-child visit, and the parent asks whether the child is protected from tetanus tonight. Which response reflects how active immunization works?
A) Yes, because a toxoid supplies antibodies within a few hours of injection
B) No, the child's own immune system must mount an antigen-antibody response first, but the immunity that results is long-lasting
C) No, protection will begin quickly but will fade within weeks as the injected antibodies are cleared
D) Yes, because the child has at least a partially functioning immune system
Answer: B
Rationale: Active immunization works through an antigen-antibody response similar to that after antigen exposure in the natural disease process, so the host must respond before any protection exists. Table 49.1 characterizes active immunity as long-lasting or permanent, in contrast to the transitory protection of passive immunization.
Trap: (C) is the mirror error of the previous item, applying passive immunization's fast-onset-then-fade profile to a toxoid. (A) sells active immunization on speed, which is exactly the property it does not have.

Q: A patient who finished cancer chemotherapy 2 weeks ago is exposed to hepatitis A at work. Which type of immunizing drug does the chapter direct the nurse to expect?
A) A live attenuated vaccine, to produce the strongest possible response
B) A toxoid, because the exposure has already occurred
C) A passive immunizing drug such as an immunoglobulin
D) No immunizing drug at all, because immunocompromised patients cannot be protected
Answer: C
Rationale: Active immunizing drugs are for people who have at least a partially functioning immune system. Passive immunizing drugs such as immunoglobulins are used for the immunodeficient, for those in whom active immunization is contraindicated, and for those exposed to or anticipating exposure to a disease, because they bypass the host's own immune system entirely.
Trap: (A) is the swap this chapter expects most often, offering active immunization to the one patient who cannot mount the response it depends on, and a live product at that. (D) overcorrects: immunocompromise rules out the active drug, not immunization.

Subtopic: toxoid, vaccine, and the three passive categories

Q: A nursing student says that a toxoid is the passive counterpart of a vaccine. How should the nurse correct this?
A) Both are active immunizing drugs; a toxoid is a bacterial exotoxin that has been inactivated, whereas a vaccine is a suspension of the organism itself
B) The student is correct, because a toxoid supplies ready-made antitoxin while a vaccine stimulates the host
C) Both are passive immunizing drugs; the immunoglobulins are the active ones
D) A toxoid is made from venom, whereas a vaccine is made from bacteria
Answer: A
Rationale: Toxoids are bacterial exotoxins modified or inactivated by chemicals or heat so that they are no longer toxic but can still bind to host B cells to stimulate the formation of antitoxin. Vaccines are suspensions of live, attenuated, or killed microorganisms. The chapter names both as types of active immunizing drug, so this distinction sits entirely inside the active column.
Trap: (B) pulls because toxoid and antitoxin differ by three letters and sound like a matched pair; the antitoxin is the passive drug, and the toxoid is what is injected into horses to obtain it. (D) swaps venom for exotoxin, which belongs to antivenins.

Q: A nurse is reviewing a unit's stock of immunizing biologics. Which of these are passive immunizing drugs? Select all that apply.
A) Rabies immunoglobulin
B) Tetanus antitoxin
C) Antivenin for a rattlesnake bite
D) Hepatitis B vaccine
E) Tetanus toxoid
Answer: A, B, C
Rationale: Passive immunizing drugs are divided into three groups: antitoxins, immunoglobulins, and snake and spider antivenins. A name ending in immunoglobulin, antitoxin, or antivenin is passive, while a vaccine or a toxoid is an active immunizing drug.
Trap: (E) pulls hardest because tetanus appears twice on this list, once as a passive antitoxin and once as an active toxoid, so the disease name decides nothing and only the drug-type word does. (D) is active despite being given to people who may already have been exposed.

Subtopic: natural versus artificial, and herd immunity

Q: A nurse is charting the immune status of a 2-month-old who is exclusively breastfed and has received no vaccines. Which term describes the protection this infant currently has?
A) Artificial active immunization
B) Natural active immunization
C) Natural passive immunization
D) Artificial passive immunization
Answer: C
Rationale: Passive immunization also occurs naturally when antibodies pass from mother to infant during breastfeeding or through the placenta during pregnancy. The infant manufactured none of these antibodies, so no host antibody response is stimulated and the protection lasts only until the maternal antibodies are cleared.
Trap: (B) is the standard mis-tag, because the transfer happens by nature rather than by needle and "natural" gets read as "the body's own." Natural versus artificial names how the antibodies arrived; active versus passive names who made them. That gives four states, not two: a shot is artificial active, having the disease is natural active, immunoglobulin or antivenin is artificial passive, and placenta or breast milk is natural passive.

Q: A parent asks why the clinic keeps pushing to vaccinate nearly every child in the county when her own child is already fully immunized. Which explanation describes herd immunity?
A) Vaccinating a whole community produces a stronger individual antibody titer in each child
B) When 95% or more of a population is successfully immunized, the entire population is protected, including members who are not themselves immune
C) Vaccine protection lasts longer when many people receive their doses at the same time
D) Widespread vaccination removes the need for booster doses in those already immunized
Answer: B
Rationale: Herd immunity is resistance to a disease on the part of an entire community or population because a large proportion of its members are immune to the disease. The chapter states that successful immunization of 95% or more of a population confers protection on the entire population.
Trap: (A) swaps population for person, which is the exact axis this term turns on: herd immunity changes who is protected, not how strongly any one person responds.

Topic: live versus inactivated vaccines and contraindications
Subtopic: live versus killed

Q: A patient asks why the measles-mumps-rubella vaccine is not repeated every few years the way her tetanus booster is. Which property of live attenuated vaccines explains this?
A) Live attenuated vaccines confer only partial immunity, so repeat doses would add nothing
B) Live attenuated vaccines produce lifelong immunity against that particular disease
C) Live attenuated vaccines are cleared by the reticuloendothelial system, so their timing is unpredictable
D) Live attenuated vaccines are passive immunizing drugs and cannot be boosted
Answer: B
Rationale: The chapter states that live attenuated vaccines confer lifelong immunity against that particular disease, which is why they are not described as requiring boosters. Killed or inactivated vaccines confer only partial immunity and must be given periodic booster shots.
Trap: (A) attaches the killed vaccine's partial-immunity property to the live one. The properties travel in pairs, so an inversion here costs both halves at once: live means lifelong, killed means partial and therefore boosted.

Q: A patient receiving an inactivated influenza vaccine asks why this one has to be repeated when her childhood varicella vaccine did not. Which response is correct?
A) Killed or inactivated vaccines confer only partial immunity and must be given periodic booster shots
B) Killed vaccines contain live virus that weakens during storage
C) Inactivated vaccines are passive immunizing drugs, so their antibodies are cleared over time
D) Inactivated vaccines confer lifelong immunity, so the repeat dose is only a formality
Answer: A
Rationale: Only partial immunity is conferred by killed or inactivated vaccines, and the chapter states that they must be given periodic booster shots. Varicella vaccine is named among the live attenuated products, which the chapter credits with lifelong immunity.
Trap: (D) is the mirror error of the previous item, assigning lifelong immunity to the inactivated product. (C) confuses inactivated with passive; an inactivated vaccine still makes the host produce its own antibodies, so it is an active immunizing drug.

Subtopic: dtap versus tdap

Q: A 4-year-old is due for the next dose in the primary diphtheria, tetanus, and pertussis series. Which product does the chapter indicate, and why?
A) Tdap, because its reduced diphtheria toxoid is better tolerated by young children
B) DTaP, because it contains higher amounts of antigens and is indicated for initial immunizations in patients 6 weeks to 6 years of age
C) Either product, because the two are interchangeable below 7 years of age
D) Neither product, because no dose is due between infancy and adolescence
Answer: B
Rationale: The chapter prints a separate safety box on this pair. DTaP contains higher amounts of antigens, is for pediatric patients 6 weeks to 6 years of age, and is indicated for initial immunizations. The capital letters carry the meaning: more capitals, more of that antigen.
Trap: (A) is the reflex pick because the two names differ only by capitalization and Tdap is the more familiar product to adults; the reduced diphtheria toxoid that makes Tdap a booster is exactly what makes it wrong for a primary series.

Q: A 26-year-old with a deep puncture wound needs a tetanus and pertussis booster. Which product fits, and on what basis?
A) DTaP, because adults need the higher antigen content
B) Tdap, because it contains reduced diphtheria toxoid and is the booster vaccine for older children, adolescents, and adults
C) Tetanus antitoxin, because it is the active immunizing drug used for wounds
D) Either product, because the age restrictions apply only below 6 weeks of age
Answer: B
Rationale: Tdap contains reduced diphtheria toxoid and is described as a booster vaccine for older children, adolescents, and adults. DTaP is restricted to pediatric patients 6 weeks to 6 years of age and is indicated for initial immunizations.
Trap: (A) is the mirror error of the previous item, reading the capital letters as a marker of adult strength rather than of antigen content. (C) mislabels an antitoxin as active, when antitoxins are one of the three passive categories.

Subtopic: who must not be vaccinated, and who may

Q: A woman at her first prenatal visit is found to be nonimmune to rubella and asks to be vaccinated today. Which response is correct?
A) The measles-mumps-rubella vaccine is given now, since rubella in pregnancy carries real risk
B) The vaccine is a live virus product and is contraindicated in pregnancy, so it is deferred until after delivery
C) The vaccine may be given, because only inactivated vaccines are withheld during pregnancy
D) The vaccine is given together with an immunoglobulin to neutralize the live virus
Answer: B
Rationale: The chapter states that use of these drugs in pregnant women is usually contraindicated, and names pregnant women specifically among those in whom the measles-mumps-rubella vaccine is contraindicated, on the theoretic risk that the live virus vaccine may cause a fetal infection. Pregnancy also needs to be avoided for 3 months after measles virus vaccination.
Trap: (C) inverts the rule that matters most here: it is the live product, not the inactivated one, that pregnancy rules out. (A) mistakes a real disease risk for permission to give a live vaccine.

Q: A pregnant patient in her second trimester is a health care worker who has never completed the hepatitis B series. Which action does the chapter support?
A) Defer the whole series until after delivery, because vaccines are withheld throughout pregnancy
B) Proceed with hepatitis B vaccination, because pregnancy is not considered a contraindication for it
C) Give hepatitis B immunoglobulin instead, because active immunization is never given in pregnancy
D) Give the vaccine only if she documents an exposure this week
Answer: B
Rationale: The chapter prints an explicit override for this product: pregnancy is not considered a contraindication to hepatitis B vaccination. The general caution about immunizing drugs in pregnancy is aimed at live virus products such as the measles-mumps-rubella vaccine.
Trap: (A) is the mirror error of the previous item, generalizing one live-vaccine rule into a blanket ban; the printed exceptions exist to break that blanket, and rabies vaccination after exposure is another the chapter says to give anyway.

Q: A 3-year-old is due for scheduled immunizations and has a mild ear infection with a temperature of 99.8 degrees Fahrenheit. The parent asks whether the shots should be delayed. Which response is correct?
A) Immunization proceeds, because a cold, mild infection, ear infection, or low-grade fever is not a reason to defer
B) Immunization is deferred until the child has been afebrile for a full week
C) Immunization is deferred, because any active infection is an absolute contraindication
D) Immunization proceeds only once an antibiotic has been started
Answer: A
Rationale: Immunization is deferred until after recovery from a febrile illness or a temporary immunocompromised state, such as the period after cancer chemotherapy, but the chapter explicitly excludes a cold, mild infection, ear infection, or low-grade fever from that rule.
Trap: (C) is the reflex pick because active infections do appear on the withhold list and an otitis media reads as an active infection. The chapter draws the line at febrile illness and names the ear infection as the case that does not cross it.

Topic: immunization adverse effects and reporting

Q: A nurse is following up by phone with parents after routine childhood immunizations. Which findings should be reported immediately as severe adverse effects? Select all that apply.
A) Temperature of 104.2 degrees Fahrenheit
B) Convulsions
C) Dyspnea with circumoral cyanosis
D) Soreness at the injection site
E) Temperature of 100.4 degrees Fahrenheit
F) Adenopathy
Answer: A, B, C
Rationale: Table 49.2 sorts adverse effects into minor and severe. Severe effects include fever higher than 103 degrees Fahrenheit, or 39.4 degrees Celsius, plus encephalitis, convulsions, peripheral neuropathy, anaphylactic reaction, shock, dyspnea, and cyanosis. Minor effects include fever, adenopathy, minor rash, soreness at the injection site, urticaria, and arthritis. Serious reactions are reported to the Vaccine Adverse Event Reporting System, cosponsored by the Food and Drug Administration and the CDC.
Trap: (E) is the option that decides this question, because fever sits on both lists and only the number discriminates: plain fever is minor, and fever higher than 103 degrees Fahrenheit is severe. (F) sounds alarming to a parent but adenopathy is printed on the minor side, so sort each finding against the table rather than against how frightening it sounds.

Q: A parent asks whether to give a toddler a dose of acetaminophen an hour before the immunization appointment to head off fever. Which response is correct?
A) Premedicating an hour ahead blunts postvaccine fever most effectively
B) Acetaminophen or ibuprofen should not be given until after the vaccine has been administered
C) Antipyretics are avoided entirely for 48 hours after any immunization
D) Ibuprofen may be given in advance but acetaminophen may not
Answer: B
Rationale: The overwhelming majority of adverse effects are minor and are treated with acetaminophen and rest, but the chapter specifies that acetaminophen or ibuprofen should not be given until after the vaccine has been administered.
Trap: (A) is the reflex pick because premedicating is familiar advice for other injections and sounds like good anticipatory care; the timing rule for immunizations runs the other way. (C) overcorrects into forbidding the very treatment the chapter recommends for minor effects.

Q: A patient who has received repeated injections of a horse-derived antitoxin develops fever, rash, and joint pain about a week later. Which reaction does this pattern suggest?
A) Serum sickness, which follows repeated injections of equine-derived immunizing drugs
B) Anaphylaxis from the most recent injection
C) Encephalitis, a severe adverse effect of active immunization
D) Natural passive immunization taking effect
Answer: A
Rationale: The chapter attributes serum sickness to repeated injections of equine, or horse-derived, immunizing drugs. Antitoxins are most often purified antiserum obtained from animals, usually horses, by the injection of a toxin or toxoid so that antibodies can be collected.
Trap: (B) pulls because both are hypersensitivity reactions to an injected biologic. Anaphylaxis is immediate and appears on the severe list of Table 49.2, whereas serum sickness is tied specifically to repeated equine-derived doses and appears later.

Q: A patient taking a long-term immunosuppressive drug is scheduled to receive an inactivated vaccine. Which concern does the chapter raise about this combination?
A) The vaccine will inactivate the immunosuppressive drug
B) Immunosuppressive drugs hinder the generation of the active immunity the vaccine is given to produce
C) Interactions between immunizing drugs and other drugs are common, and this is one of many
D) The combination is a known cause of serum sickness
Answer: B
Rationale: Interactions with immunizing drugs are not generally a problem, with the exception of immunosuppressive drugs, which hinder the generation of the active immunity. Patients who are immunocompromised are also at greater risk for experiencing serious adverse effects.
Trap: (C) overgeneralizes, and it is tempting because a stem naming an interaction implies a crowded field. The chapter's position is the opposite, so an interaction question about immunizing drugs is pointing at the one named exception.
