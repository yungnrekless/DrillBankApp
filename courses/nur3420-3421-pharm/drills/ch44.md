# Chapter 44 drills — Antiinflammatory and Antigout Drugs

Authored from `sources/Ch44_Fact_Bank.md` (Lilley, Collins & Snyder, 10th ed.).

Blueprint weight is **12 questions of 121 (9.9%), depth `full`** — tied with
Ch 2 for third-heaviest in the course behind Ch 10 (19) and Ch 38 (18), split
8 items on Exam 1 and 4 on the Final.

That earns **73 drills**, inside the 55 to 75 band a `full` chapter carries.
Ch 44 sits mid-band rather than at the top because, unlike Ch 2, it is not the
vocabulary chapter the rest of the course borrows from: its KEY TERMS box holds
only six terms, and the load here is in the drug names, the two pathways, and
the numbers. It also closes Ch 10's largest gap — Ch 10 names NSAIDs on nearly
every page and then defers the whole class here — so several items are written
to be answerable from either chapter's side.

Topics follow the clinical decisions the chapter forces rather than its section
numbers: what inflammation is, which enzyme you are blocking, why aspirin is
different from every other drug in the class, how to pick one, what it does to
the gut and the kidney, what the FDA made them print, what an overdose looks
like, who must not get one, what to teach, Reye's syndrome, and then the two
halves of gout.

Every distinction the chapter states in both directions is drilled from both
ends: COX-1 and COX-2, prostacyclin and thromboxane, cyclooxygenase and
lipoxygenase, aspirin and every other NSAID, ceiling and no ceiling, adult and
pediatric salicylism, chronic and acute intoxication, dialyzable and not,
overproduction and underexcretion, allopurinol and probenecid, and the drug
that treats the attack against the drugs that treat the uric acid. Inverting
those is the reflex error this chapter produces.

Topic: inflammation and the arachidonic acid pathway
Subtopic: how injury becomes inflammation

Q: A patient sprains an ankle, and within hours the joint is red, swollen, warm, and painful. The patient asks how a twisted ligament turns into all of that. Which sequence describes what happened?
A) Arachidonic acid was released from phospholipids in cell membranes and metabolized in either the prostaglandin or the leukotriene pathway
B) Arachidonic acid was synthesized in the preoptic hypothalamic region and carried in the blood to the injured joint
C) Leukotrienes in the joint were converted into arachidonic acid by cyclooxygenase
D) Uric acid crystals were released from the ligament and drew leukocytes to the joint
Answer: A
Rationale: Arachidonic acid is released from phospholipids in cell membranes in response to a triggering event such as an injury, and is then metabolized in either the prostaglandin pathway or the leukotriene pathway, both branches of the arachidonic acid pathway. Both branches lead to inflammation, edema, headache, and other pain.
Trap: (C) reverses the pathway, and it is the intuitive reversal because leukotrienes are the more familiar word. Arachidonic acid is the precursor, not a product, and leukotrienes come from lipoxygenase rather than cyclooxygenase.

Q: The same patient asks why simply moving the ankle through its normal range now hurts, when that motion never hurt before the injury. Which explanation is correct?
A) Prostaglandin-induced hyperalgesia has made stimuli that would normally not be painful, such as moving a joint through its natural range of motion, painful
B) The leukotrienes released by the injury bind directly to pain receptors in the joint capsule
C) The sensory nerves in the joint have been permanently damaged and are now firing on their own
D) Prostaglandin E2 has raised the temperature set point inside the joint
Answer: A
Rationale: The chapter attributes the pain of inflammation to prostaglandin-induced hyperalgesia, or excessive sensitivity, and uses range-of-motion pain as its own example.
Trap: (C) is the reflex pick because this looks like the allodynia defined in Ch 10, where a lowered pain threshold comes from nerve damage. Both chapters describe lowered pain thresholds, but the cause here is inflammatory prostaglandins acting on an intact nerve.

Subtopic: prostacyclin versus thromboxane

Q: A drug suppresses the thromboxane produced by thromboxane synthetase inside the platelets. Which effects will be reduced?
A) Platelet aggregation and vasoconstriction
B) Platelet aggregation and vasodilation
C) Bronchoconstriction and vascular permeability
D) Fever and hyperalgesia
Answer: A
Rationale: Fig. 44.1 places thromboxane in the platelets and labels its effects as increased platelet aggregation and vasoconstriction. Suppressing it therefore reduces both.
Trap: (B) crosses thromboxane with prostacyclin, which is the same pathway and the same parent enzyme but the opposite effect on vessels. Cue: thrombo- means thrombus, so thromboxane is the sticky one.

Q: A second mediator in the same pathway is made by prostacyclin synthetase in the blood vessel wall. What does that mediator do?
A) Decreases platelet aggregation and causes vasodilation
B) Increases platelet aggregation and causes vasoconstriction
C) Produces bronchospasm and increased vascular permeability
D) Blocks cyclooxygenase in the platelet irreversibly
Answer: A
Rationale: Fig. 44.1 places prostacyclin in the blood vessel wall and labels its effects as decreased platelet aggregation and vasodilation, the mirror image of thromboxane's.
Trap: (B) is the mirror error of the previous item. Same branch, same enzyme, opposite effects on both the platelet and the vessel, which is exactly why the pair is testable.

Subtopic: the two branches and their enzymes

Q: A patient with asthma who takes aspirin for headaches reports that the aspirin has never done anything for the wheezing. Which fact explains that?
A) The leukotriene pathway is inhibited by some antiinflammatory drugs but not by salicylates
B) Salicylates inhibit lipoxygenase, but leukotrienes are produced by cyclooxygenase
C) Leukotrienes cause vasodilation rather than bronchoconstriction, so they have nothing to do with wheezing
D) Aspirin blocks leukotrienes only at antiinflammatory doses
Answer: A
Rationale: The chapter states plainly that the leukotriene pathway is inhibited by some antiinflammatory drugs but not by salicylates. Leukotrienes promote vasoconstriction, bronchospasm, and increased vascular permeability with resultant edema.
Trap: (B) crosses the two enzymes, which is the single most common error on this pathway. Cue: lipoxygenase makes leukotrienes, both starting with L; cyclooxygenase makes prostaglandins.

Topic: cox-1 versus cox-2
Subtopic: which isoform you want to keep

Q: A patient who has taken ibuprofen daily for 6 months for osteoarthritis is admitted with a bleeding gastric ulcer. Which mechanism accounts for the ulcer?
A) Blockade of tissue COX-1, which normally produces the prostaglandins that maintain an intact gastrointestinal mucosa
B) Blockade of COX-2, which normally produces the prostaglandins that protect the stomach lining
C) Blockade of lipoxygenase, which normally produces protective leukotrienes in the gastric wall
D) A direct chemical burn of the mucosa from the tablet coating
Answer: A
Rationale: All NSAIDs can be ulcerogenic and induce gastrointestinal bleeding due to their activity against tissue COX-1. COX-1 makes prostaglandins with primarily beneficial effects, and their role in maintaining an intact gastrointestinal mucosa is the chapter's named example.
Trap: (B) crosses the isoforms, and it is the inversion the numbering invites. Cue: COX-1 is the one you need, COX-2 is the trouble.

Q: The same patient asks which enzyme the ibuprofen was blocking in order to help the arthritis in the first place. Which response is accurate?
A) COX-2, which produces the prostaglandins involved in inflammatory processes
B) COX-1, which produces the prostaglandins involved in inflammatory processes
C) Thromboxane synthetase, which produces the prostaglandins of inflammation
D) Lipoxygenase, which is the target of all NSAIDs
Answer: A
Rationale: COX-2 produces prostaglandins involved in inflammatory processes, so blocking it is the desired therapeutic action; blocking COX-1 is what produces the ulceration.
Trap: (B) is the mirror error of the previous item. The same student who blames COX-2 for ulcers will credit COX-1 with the antiinflammatory benefit, and both halves have to be right to get either one right.

Subtopic: the cox-2 inhibitors

Q: A patient with rheumatoid arthritis and a history of peptic ulcer disease is switched from naproxen to celecoxib. What is the rationale for the change?
A) Celecoxib specifically inhibits COX-2 and theoretically has limited or no effect on COX-1, which reduces the prevalence of gastrointestinal ulceration
B) Celecoxib inhibits both COX-1 and COX-2 more completely, which shortens the duration of exposure
C) Celecoxib is not an NSAID, so the class contraindications do not apply
D) Celecoxib provides antiplatelet protection that naproxen lacks
Answer: A
Rationale: The COX-2 inhibitors work by specifically inhibiting the COX-2 form of COX and theoretically have limited or no effect on COX-1. Previous NSAIDs nonspecifically inhibited both. That greater enzyme specificity allows the beneficial antiinflammatory effects while reducing the prevalence of adverse effects such as gastrointestinal ulceration.
Trap: (D) inverts a real fact. COX-2 inhibitors have little effect on platelet function, which is a benefit for bleeding risk and a liability if the patient needed antiplatelet action.

Q: The patient now taking celecoxib says, "So I can stop worrying about my stomach entirely." Which response is accurate?
A) COX-2 inhibitors are not totally devoid of gastrointestinal toxicity; gastritis and upper gastrointestinal bleeding have been reported, though much less frequently than with older NSAIDs
B) That is correct, since celecoxib has no effect on the gastrointestinal tract at all
C) That is correct as long as the drug is taken on an empty stomach
D) Celecoxib carries a higher risk for gastrointestinal bleeding than the older NSAIDs do
Answer: A
Rationale: The chapter states that COX-2 inhibitors are not totally devoid of gastrointestinal toxicity and that gastritis and upper gastrointestinal bleeding have been reported with their use, although much less frequently than with the older NSAIDs.
Trap: (B) overstates the chapter in the direction the drug was marketed. The claim is a reduced prevalence of gastrointestinal adverse effects, never their absence.

Q: A nurse is reviewing what the class of COX-2 inhibitors is and is not. Which statements are accurate? (Select all that apply.)
A) They specifically inhibit COX-2 and theoretically have limited or no effect on COX-1
B) They were approved in 1998 as the newest class of NSAIDs
C) They nonspecifically inhibit both COX-1 and COX-2, as previous NSAIDs did
D) They have little effect on platelet function
E) Celecoxib was the first COX-2 inhibitor and is the only one remaining on the market
Answer: A, B, D, E
Rationale: The COX-2 inhibitors were approved in 1998, inhibit COX-2 specifically, and have little effect on platelet function. Celecoxib is the first COX-2 inhibitor and the only one remaining on the market, since rofecoxib and valdecoxib were removed for increased cardiovascular risk.
Trap: (C) describes the previous NSAIDs, not this class, and it is the statement that sounds most like textbook language while contradicting the whole reason the class was developed.

Subtopic: celecoxib and sulfa allergy

Q: A patient with a documented sulfa antibiotic allergy is prescribed celecoxib for osteoarthritis. What should the nurse do?
A) Hold the drug and contact the prescriber, because celecoxib and sulfa antibiotics share a similar structure
B) Give the drug, because the allergy applies only to antibiotics
C) Give the drug with an antihistamine to blunt any reaction
D) Give the drug but hold all other NSAIDs
Answer: A
Rationale: Celecoxib and sulfa antibiotics share a similar structure; therefore celecoxib should not be used in patients with known sulfa allergy. Ch 38 names celecoxib as the drug that should not be used in sulfonamide allergy, and this chapter supplies the structural reason.
Trap: (B) is the reflex pick because celecoxib is not an antibiotic and the allergy history names antibiotics. The shared feature is the chemical structure, not the therapeutic class, which is why Ch 38 could list sulfonylureas, thiazides, and loop diuretics as relatively safe and still exclude this one drug.

Topic: aspirin's antiplatelet property and cardiac use
Subtopic: what makes aspirin different

Q: A patient asks why one small aspirin a day prevents clots when a full-strength ibuprofen does not. Which explanation is correct?
A) Aspirin is uniquely an irreversible inhibitor of COX-1 in the platelets themselves, which reduces the formation of thromboxane A2
B) Aspirin reversibly inhibits COX-2 in the platelets, which reduces thromboxane A2
C) Aspirin increases prostacyclin production in the platelets
D) Aspirin thins the blood by lowering the number of circulating platelets
Answer: A
Rationale: Aspirin has the unique property among NSAIDs of being an irreversible inhibitor of COX-1 receptors within the platelets themselves, which results in reduced formation of thromboxane A2, a substance that normally promotes platelet aggregation.
Trap: (D) is the lay explanation patients arrive with, and it is wrong in a way that matters: aspirin disables the platelets it meets rather than reducing their number, which is why the effect outlasts the drug.

Q: A patient taking aspirin 81 mg daily for stroke prevention asks to switch to ibuprofen, since ibuprofen also helps the arthritis. What is the problem with the substitution?
A) Other NSAIDs lack aspirin's antiplatelet effects, so the stroke protection would be lost
B) Ibuprofen has stronger antiplatelet effects, so the bleeding risk would rise
C) Ibuprofen has the same antiplatelet effect but requires a higher dose
D) Ibuprofen inhibits COX-1 irreversibly, so it cannot be stopped before surgery
Answer: A
Rationale: The chapter states that other NSAIDs lack these antiplatelet effects, and that NSAIDs may counteract the cardioprotective effects of aspirin. The substitution both removes the protection and interferes with it.
Trap: (C) is the mirror error of the previous item, and it is the reasonable-sounding one: same class, same enzyme, so surely the same effect at some dose. The irreversibility is a property of aspirin alone and no dose of ibuprofen reproduces it.

Q: A patient in the emergency department describes crushing chest pain that began 20 minutes ago and took no medication before arriving. Which drug from this chapter is expected first, in the absence of contraindications?
A) Aspirin, which reduces cardiac death after an MI and should be administered at the first sign of MI
B) Celecoxib, since it is the NSAID with the least platelet effect
C) Ketorolac, since its analgesia is comparable to that of morphine
D) Ibuprofen, since it is the safest NSAID adverse effect profile in the class
Answer: A
Rationale: Aspirin has been shown to reduce cardiac death after an MI and should be administered at the first sign of MI. If it was not given before arrival in the emergency department, aspirin is one of the first drugs given if there are no contraindications. The dosage table gives 325 mg orally at the first sign of MI.
Trap: (C) trades on ketorolac's genuine strength, which is analgesia comparable to that of narcotic drugs such as morphine. Analgesia is not the point here; the point is antiplatelet action, and ketorolac has none of aspirin's.

Subtopic: the dose decides the job

Q: A 68-year-old who takes aspirin 81 mg daily since an MI asks whether it will also help the arthritis in his hands. Which response is accurate?
A) The 81-mg dose is a thromboprevention dose; the antiinflammatory dose for arthritis is about 3 g/day divided every 4 to 6 hours
B) The 81-mg dose is sufficient for both purposes, since aspirin is antiinflammatory at any dose
C) Aspirin has no antiinflammatory effect at all, so another drug is needed
D) The arthritis dose is lower than the cardiac dose, so he is already taking too much
Answer: A
Rationale: The dosage table gives PO 81 to 325 mg once daily for thromboprevention and 3 g/day divided every 4 to 6 hours for arthritis, roughly a 37-fold difference. Aspirin does have antirheumatic and antiinflammatory effects, but not at a thromboprevention dose.
Trap: (D) inverts the relationship between the two ranges. Nothing in this chapter has an antiinflammatory dose lower than a prophylactic one; the antiinflammatory doses are always the higher ones.

Q: A patient with a history of peptic ulcer disease who takes warfarin is to begin aspirin for thromboprevention. Which strength is preferred, and why?
A) 81 mg, because the 81-mg and 325-mg strengths appear equally beneficial for preventing thrombotic events and the lower strength is chosen when bleeding risk is elevated
B) 325 mg, because the higher strength is more protective and the ulcer history is unrelated
C) 650 mg, because warfarin blunts aspirin's effect and a higher dose compensates
D) Neither, because aspirin at any dose is contraindicated with warfarin
Answer: A
Rationale: The 81-mg strength and the 325-mg strength appear to be equally beneficial for the prevention of thrombotic events. For this reason the lower strength is often chosen for patients who have any elevated risk for bleeding, such as those with stroke history or history of peptic ulcer disease and those taking the anticoagulant warfarin.
Trap: (B) assumes more milligrams means more protection, which is precisely the assumption the equal-benefit finding removes. The two strengths differ in bleeding risk, not in thrombotic protection.

Subtopic: three properties, or four

Q: A nurse is comparing aspirin, ibuprofen, and acetaminophen for a patient's teaching sheet. Which statements are accurate? (Select all that apply.)
A) NSAIDs as a class have analgesic, antiinflammatory, and antipyretic activity
B) Aspirin also has antiplatelet activity
C) Acetaminophen lacks antiinflammatory activity
D) Ibuprofen has antiplatelet activity comparable to that of aspirin
E) Acetaminophen is classified as an NSAID
Answer: A, B, C
Rationale: The key points state that NSAIDs have analgesic, antiinflammatory, and antipyretic activity and that aspirin also has antiplatelet activity. Acetaminophen, carried in Ch 10, is analgesic and antipyretic but is not an NSAID and lacks the antiinflammatory property.
Trap: (E) is the option that catches students who group drugs by where they sit on the pharmacy shelf. Three drugs, three different property sets, and the exam builds its options out of the differences.

Topic: nsaid indications and the ceiling effect
Subtopic: the ceiling

Q: A patient with osteoarthritis reports that the prescribed ibuprofen dose helps somewhat and asks whether doubling it would help twice as much. Which response is correct?
A) NSAIDs show a ceiling effect, so increasing the dosage beyond a certain level increases the risk for adverse effects without a corresponding increase in the therapeutic effect
B) Doubling the dose doubles the antiinflammatory effect but not the analgesic effect
C) NSAIDs have no ceiling and may be titrated upward as needed for relief
D) The ceiling effect means the drug stops working entirely above a certain dose
Answer: A
Rationale: Unlike opioids, NSAIDs show a ceiling effect that limits their effectiveness; any further increase in dosage beyond a certain level increases the risk for adverse effects without a corresponding increase in the therapeutic effect.
Trap: (D) overstates the ceiling into a cliff. Above the ceiling the effect does not vanish, it simply stops growing while the harm keeps growing, which is why the wording is "without a corresponding increase."

Q: A patient with severe cancer pain is on a morphine infusion that has been titrated upward several times. The family asks why the opioid can keep being increased when the ibuprofen could not. Which response is accurate?
A) Opioids may be titrated almost indefinitely to increasingly higher levels, especially in terminally ill patients with severe pain, whereas NSAIDs have a ceiling
B) Opioids also have a class-wide ceiling, but it is set much higher than the NSAID ceiling
C) NSAIDs have no ceiling; the limit on ibuprofen is a manufacturer recommendation only
D) Opioids lose effectiveness above a fixed dose, so the increases are for tolerance rather than pain
Answer: A
Rationale: The chapter draws this contrast directly: unlike opioids, NSAIDs show a ceiling effect, while opioids may be titrated almost indefinitely to increasingly higher levels, especially in terminally ill patients with severe pain.
Trap: (C) is the mirror error of the previous item. Note that the generalization about opioids has named exceptions in Ch 10, where codeine is the only agonist to possess a ceiling effect and the agonists-antagonists have one as well.

Subtopic: combining with opioids

Q: A postoperative patient receiving an opioid has an NSAID added to the regimen, and the opioid dose is then reduced. What accounts for this?
A) NSAIDs have an opioid-sparing effect when given with opioids, because the two attack pain by different mechanisms
B) NSAIDs displace opioids from their receptors, so less opioid is needed for the same occupancy
C) NSAIDs slow opioid metabolism, so the same relief is obtained from less drug
D) NSAIDs reverse opioid-induced respiratory depression, allowing lower monitoring
Answer: A
Rationale: For pain relief, NSAIDs are sometimes combined with an opioid. They tend to have an opioid-sparing effect when given together with opioids, because the drugs attack pain using two different mechanisms, which often allows less opioid to be used.
Trap: (B) sounds mechanistically sophisticated and is the wrong mechanism entirely. The two drugs do not compete at a receptor; they work at different points, which is the whole reason the combination adds up.

Subtopic: what the label covers

Q: A nurse reviews the FDA-approved indications for NSAIDs. Which are on that list? (Select all that apply.)
A) Acute gout and acute gouty arthritis
B) Rheumatoid arthritis and juvenile rheumatoid arthritis
C) Primary dysmenorrhea
D) Peptic ulcer disease
E) Fever
Answer: A, B, C, E
Rationale: Box 44.2 lists acute gout, acute gouty arthritis, ankylosing spondylitis, bursitis, fever, juvenile rheumatoid arthritis, mild to moderate pain, osteoarthritis, primary dysmenorrhea, rheumatoid arthritis, tendonitis, and various ophthalmic uses.
Trap: (D) is a contraindication rather than an indication, and it is the option that catches anyone reading quickly, because peptic ulcer disease appears in this chapter so often that its presence feels familiar.

Subtopic: how long until it works

Q: A patient started on ibuprofen for rheumatoid arthritis calls after 5 days to say the drug is not working and asks to stop it. What should the nurse explain?
A) The analgesic effect appears in 30 to 60 minutes, but the full antiinflammatory effect may take up to 7 days, and in arthritis 1 week or longer
B) If there is no effect within 5 days, the drug has failed and should be discontinued
C) The antiinflammatory effect appears first and the analgesic effect takes a week
D) Full effect requires 4 to 6 weeks of continuous therapy for every NSAID
Answer: A
Rationale: The pharmacokinetics table prints ibuprofen's duration as 4 to 6 hours for the analgesic effect and 7 days for the antiinflammatory effect in the same cell. The key points add that when NSAIDs are used to decrease joint inflammation in arthritis patients, full therapeutic effects may not be experienced for 1 week or longer.
Trap: (C) reverses the two timelines, which is the specific inversion this pairing produces. Pain relief is the fast effect and inflammation control is the slow one, and the chapter's own case study is built on a patient who calls exactly this early.

Topic: choosing an nsaid for the patient
Subtopic: selection by comorbidity

Q: A patient stabilized on warfarin needs an NSAID for musculoskeletal pain. Which agents does the chapter suggest for a patient on warfarin therapy?
A) Sulindac, tolmetin, naproxen, ibuprofen, or oxaprozin
B) Aspirin or another salicylate, since salicylates do not interact with warfarin
C) Indomethacin or diclofenac, since they are the shortest acting
D) Ketorolac, since it can be limited to 5 days
Answer: A
Rationale: Table 44.1 recommends NSAIDs by comorbidity rather than by diagnosis, and its warfarin therapy row lists sulindac, tolmetin, naproxen, ibuprofen, and oxaprozin.
Trap: (B) is wrong twice over: anticoagulants appear in the interactions table as producing increased bleeding tendencies through platelet inhibition and hypoprothrombinemia, and warfarin is one of the situations in which the lower aspirin strength is chosen because bleeding risk is already elevated.

Q: A patient with a documented aspirin allergy is prescribed naproxen for tendonitis. What should the nurse do?
A) Hold the drug and contact the prescriber, because patients with documented aspirin allergy must not receive NSAIDs
B) Administer it, because the allergy is to salicylates only and naproxen is a propionic acid derivative
C) Administer it with a reduced first dose to test tolerance
D) Substitute ibuprofen, which does not cross-react
Answer: A
Rationale: Contraindications include known drug allergy, and the chapter states that patients with documented aspirin allergy must not receive NSAIDs. The Clinical Pearl repeats it: if a patient has a true aspirin allergy, the patient cannot take an NSAID.
Trap: (B) is the reflex pick because Box 44.1 sorts NSAIDs into five chemical categories, which invites the assumption that a different category means a different allergy risk. Table 44.1's own row for prior aspirin or NSAID allergy says to avoid if possible, and only then to consider a nonacetylated salicylate.

Subtopic: ketorolac

Q: A patient in recovery from opioid addiction has severe pain after orthopedic surgery. Which NSAID does the chapter identify as a desirable choice, and why?
A) Ketorolac, because its analgesic effects are comparable to those of narcotic drugs such as morphine and it lacks the addictive properties of the opioids
B) Celecoxib, because its COX-2 selectivity gives it opioid-strength analgesia
C) Aspirin, because its irreversible action gives longer relief from a single dose
D) Nabumetone, because it is the best tolerated NSAID
Answer: A
Rationale: Ketorolac is somewhat unique in that, although it does have some antiinflammatory activity, it is used primarily for its powerful analgesic effects, which are comparable to those of narcotic drugs such as morphine. That can make it a desirable choice for opiate-addicted patients who have acute pain-control needs, because it lacks the addictive properties of the opioids.
Trap: (D) picks the drug with the best tolerance rather than the one with the needed potency. Nabumetone is better tolerated for gastrointestinal effects and is indicated only for osteoarthritis and rheumatoid arthritis, which does not cover acute postoperative pain.

Q: The same patient is still using ketorolac on postoperative day 5 and asks to continue it for another week at home. Which response is correct?
A) Ketorolac is indicated for short-term use up to 5 days only, because of its potential adverse effects on the kidney and gastrointestinal tract
B) Ketorolac may be continued for up to 30 days if renal function is normal
C) Ketorolac may be continued indefinitely as long as it is taken with food
D) Ketorolac is not indicated for acute pain, only for chronic pain
Answer: A
Rationale: The Clinical Pearl states that ketorolac can be used for only 5 days. It is indicated for short-term use up to 5 days, is not indicated for treatment of minor pain or chronic pain, and the limit exists because of its potential adverse effects on the kidney and gastrointestinal tract. The dosage table caps combined oral and intravenous treatment at 5 days.
Trap: (D) reverses the drug's indication. Ketorolac is for moderate to severe acute pain such as that from orthopedic injuries or surgery, and it is chronic and minor pain that are excluded.

Subtopic: an nsaid used for something other than pain

Q: An intravenous form of indomethacin is ordered for a premature infant. What is the expected purpose?
A) To promote closure of a patent ductus arteriosus
B) To control postoperative pain in the neonate
C) To reduce fever of unknown origin
D) To prevent Reye's syndrome during a viral illness
Answer: A
Rationale: The injectable form of indomethacin is used intravenously to promote closure of patent ductus arteriosus, a heart defect that sometimes occurs in premature infants. It is also used in the treatment of preterm labor.
Trap: (B) is the reflex pick because indomethacin's other forms are analgesic and antiinflammatory. The intravenous form belongs to an entirely different therapeutic category, and it follows from prostaglandin inhibition rather than from analgesia.

Topic: nsaid adverse effects and gastroprotection
Subtopic: the three that matter

Q: A nurse is preparing to teach a patient beginning long-term NSAID therapy. Which three adverse effects does the chapter name as the main ones?
A) Gastrointestinal intolerance, bleeding that is often gastrointestinal, and renal impairment
B) Sedation, constipation, and respiratory depression
C) Hepatic failure, ototoxicity, and bone marrow suppression
D) Hyperglycemia, hypertension, and weight gain
Answer: A
Rationale: The key points state that the three main adverse effects of NSAIDs are gastrointestinal intolerance, bleeding (often gastrointestinal bleeding), and renal impairment. The Clinical Pearl condenses it further: NSAIDs can cause GI bleed and renal failure, so take with food and stay well hydrated.
Trap: (B) lists the opioid adverse effects from Ch 10, which is exactly how this distractor set gets built when the two chapters are studied together.

Q: A patient will be on high-dose naproxen for several months and is at risk for gastric ulceration. Which drug is added to prevent serious gastrointestinal adverse effects?
A) Misoprostol, a synthetic prostaglandin E1 analogue that inhibits gastric acid secretion and has a cytoprotective component
B) Metoprolol, which reduces splanchnic blood flow
C) Metoclopramide, which speeds gastric emptying
D) Magnesium sulfate, which neutralizes gastric acid
Answer: A
Rationale: Misoprostol has proven successful in preventing gastric ulcers and gastrointestinal bleeding when given in conjunction with an NSAID. Its logic is mechanistic: NSAIDs inactivate the protective prostaglandins that maintain the normal integrity of the stomach lining, so a synthetic prostaglandin E1 analogue replaces one.
Trap: (C) is the strongest of the three sound-alike drugs, because a prokinetic really is a gastrointestinal drug. Only misoprostol appears anywhere in this chapter; the others are a beta blocker, a prokinetic, and an electrolyte.

Q: Misoprostol is about to be started for NSAID gastroprotection in a 29-year-old woman. Which assessment is essential before the first dose?
A) Whether she is pregnant, because misoprostol has abortifacient properties
B) Whether she has a sulfa allergy, because misoprostol shares a sulfa structure
C) Whether she has asthma, because misoprostol causes bronchospasm
D) Whether she takes warfarin, because misoprostol potentiates it
Answer: A
Rationale: The chapter flags misoprostol's abortifacient properties in the same paragraph that recommends it for NSAID gastroprotection, and refers the reader to Ch 34.
Trap: (B) borrows the sulfa structure warning that belongs to celecoxib. Two drugs in this chapter carry a structural warning, and they are not the same drug or the same structure.

Subtopic: the kidney

Q: Which patients on NSAID therapy are at greatest risk for acute renal failure? (Select all that apply.)
A) A patient who is dehydrated after 2 days of vomiting
B) A patient with heart failure
C) A patient with liver dysfunction
D) A patient taking a loop diuretic and an ACE inhibitor
E) A patient taking misoprostol with the NSAID
Answer: A, B, C, D
Rationale: Renal function depends partly on prostaglandins, and disruption of prostaglandin function by NSAIDs is sometimes strong enough to precipitate acute or chronic renal failure. Those at risk are patients who are dehydrated, those with heart failure or liver dysfunction, and those taking diuretics or angiotensin-converting enzyme inhibitors. Acute renal failure is quite common with NSAID use, especially if the patient is dehydrated.
Trap: (E) names the drug added to reduce risk, not one that raises it, and it is chosen by anyone who reads a second drug in the list as automatically additive. Misoprostol protects the stomach; it has no role in the renal risk list.

Subtopic: reporting the bleed

Q: A patient on chronic ibuprofen calls to report black, tarry stools that started yesterday and says there has been no abdominal pain at all. Which response is correct?
A) Stop the drug and be evaluated now, because this is gastrointestinal bleeding and these events can occur at any time and without warning symptoms
B) Continue the drug and take it with food, since the absence of pain rules out bleeding
C) Reduce the dose by half and call back in a week if the stools do not clear
D) Switch to enteric-coated aspirin, which will resolve the bleeding
Answer: A
Rationale: Black or tarry stools appear in the teaching list of findings to report, alongside bleeding around the gums, petechiae, ecchymosis, and purpura. Box 44.3 states that serious gastrointestinal events can occur at any time during use and without warning symptoms, and most fatalities associated with NSAID use are related to gastrointestinal bleeding.
Trap: (B) uses the absence of pain as reassurance, which is precisely the reasoning the black box warning was written to defeat. The warning names the absence of warning symptoms as a feature of these events.

Q: A patient taking prescription-strength ibuprofen reports new ringing in the ears. Which action is appropriate?
A) Report it, because tinnitus and hearing loss are listed adverse effects of NSAIDs
B) Reassure the patient that tinnitus occurs only with aspirin
C) Reassure the patient that tinnitus is expected during the first week of any NSAID
D) Hold the next dose only if hearing loss also develops
Answer: A
Rationale: Table 44.2 lists tinnitus and hearing loss under Other adverse effects for the NSAIDs as a class, and ringing in the ears heads the list of findings patients are taught to report.
Trap: (B) confines a class effect to one drug. The same symptom carries two levels of concern in this chapter: tinnitus on any NSAID is reportable, while tinnitus on aspirin specifically raises salicylism.

Topic: nsaid black box warnings
Subtopic: the cardiovascular warning

Q: A patient beginning a 3-week course of an NSAID asks when the cardiovascular risk described on the label starts to apply. Which response is accurate?
A) The risk for heart attack or stroke can occur as early as the first weeks of use, and it may increase with longer use and higher doses
B) The risk begins only after 6 months of continuous therapy
C) The risk applies only to patients who already have coronary artery disease
D) The risk applies only to doses above the maximum recommended daily dose
Answer: A
Rationale: Box 44.3 states that NSAIDs may cause an increased risk for serious cardiovascular thrombotic events, myocardial infarction, and stroke, which can be fatal, that patients with cardiovascular disease or risk factors may be at greater risk, that the risk can occur as early as the first weeks of use, and that it may increase with longer use and with higher doses.
Trap: (B) converts "may increase with longer use" into a threshold that has to be crossed before any risk exists. The warning describes a risk that rises with duration, not one that begins after it.

Q: A healthy 34-year-old with no cardiac history is prescribed an NSAID and says the warning cannot apply to someone with no risk factors. How should the nurse respond?
A) The 2015 warning states that patients without heart disease or risk factors are also at increased risk
B) That reasoning is correct, since the warning is restricted to patients with existing cardiovascular disease
C) The warning applies only to patients over 65
D) The warning applies only to the COX-2 inhibitors
Answer: A
Rationale: In 2015 the FDA strengthened the warning, adding that patients treated with NSAIDs after a first heart attack are more likely to die in the first year, that there is an increased risk for heart failure, and that patients without heart disease or risk factors are also at increased risk for heart attack or stroke.
Trap: (B) is the mirror error of the previous item and reads the "greater risk" clause as an "only" clause. The box says patients with cardiovascular disease may be at greater risk, which is a comparison among patients, not a limit on who is at risk.

Q: A patient is 1 day post coronary artery bypass graft surgery and reports incisional pain. An NSAID is ordered. What should the nurse do?
A) Question the order, because NSAIDs are contraindicated for the treatment of perioperative pain in the setting of coronary artery bypass graft surgery
B) Give it, since the black box warning concerns long-term use rather than perioperative use
C) Give it with food to reduce the gastrointestinal risk
D) Give half the ordered dose and monitor blood pressure
Answer: A
Rationale: The contraindication is printed inside the black box itself: NSAIDs are contraindicated for the treatment of perioperative pain in the setting of coronary artery bypass graft surgery.
Trap: (B) treats a flat contraindication as a caution to be managed, which is the reflex whenever a warning appears in the same box as a graded risk. This line is not graded; it is a contraindication.

Q: A patient on aspirin 81 mg daily for cardioprotection begins taking ibuprofen several times a day for arthritis. What is the concern?
A) All NSAIDs except aspirin carry a black box warning for cardiovascular thrombotic events, and NSAIDs may counteract the cardioprotective effects of aspirin
B) Ibuprofen and aspirin together produce excessive antiplatelet effect and a high bleeding risk from the aspirin
C) Ibuprofen will increase aspirin's cardioprotective effect, so the aspirin dose must be reduced
D) There is no concern, since both drugs are NSAIDs and their effects are the same
Answer: A
Rationale: All NSAIDs except aspirin share a black box warning regarding increased risk for adverse cardiovascular thrombotic events, including fatal MI and stroke, and NSAIDs may counteract the cardioprotective effects of aspirin. Aspirin is the NSAID that protects the heart; the rest carry a warning for threatening it.
Trap: (D) is chosen by anyone who reasons from class membership alone, and it is the answer most students give before they meet the aspirin exemption. Same class, opposite cardiovascular labeling.

Subtopic: the gastrointestinal warning

Q: Which statements appear in the FDA-required gastrointestinal warning on all NSAIDs? (Select all that apply.)
A) NSAIDs increase the risk for serious gastrointestinal events including bleeding, ulceration, and perforation of the stomach or intestines
B) These events can be fatal
C) These events can occur at any time during use and without warning symptoms
D) Older adult patients are at greater risk for serious gastrointestinal events
E) Warning symptoms such as dyspepsia reliably precede serious events
Answer: A, B, C, D
Rationale: Box 44.3 states that NSAIDs cause an increased risk for serious gastrointestinal adverse events, including bleeding, ulceration, and perforation of the stomach or intestines, which can be fatal, that these events can occur at any time during use and without warning symptoms, and that older adult patients are at greater risk.
Trap: (E) directly contradicts the box's own wording and is the option that feels clinically sensible, since dyspepsia and heartburn genuinely are common on NSAIDs. Common early symptoms do not mean the serious events announce themselves.

Topic: salicylate toxicity
Subtopic: chronic versus acute

Q: A patient has taken high-dose aspirin daily for months for rheumatoid arthritis and now has symptoms of salicylate toxicity. Which term names this, and what causes it?
A) Salicylism, which results from short-term administration of high dosages or prolonged therapy with high or even low dosages
B) Salicylism, which results only from a single ingestion of more than 150 mg/kg
C) Acute salicylate intoxication, since any toxicity from aspirin is acute by definition
D) Salicylism, which occurs only when the patient has renal impairment
Answer: A
Rationale: Chronic salicylate intoxication is also known as salicylism and results from either short-term administration of high dosages or prolonged therapy with high or even low dosages.
Trap: (B) imports the acute overdose grading into the chronic definition. The mg/kg thresholds describe a single toxic dose, and salicylism can develop on doses that were never single or large.

Q: An adult taking aspirin long term for arthritis reports ringing in the ears and difficulty hearing conversation. Which interpretation is correct?
A) These are the most common manifestations of chronic salicylate intoxication in adults
B) These findings occur only in acute overdose and rule out chronic toxicity
C) These findings are unrelated to salicylates and suggest a middle ear infection
D) These are expected effects of therapeutic aspirin and need no action
Answer: A
Rationale: In adults the most common manifestations of chronic salicylate intoxication are tinnitus and hearing loss. Tinnitus, hearing loss, increased heart rate, and dizziness are the salicylate toxicity signs patients are taught to report.
Trap: (D) treats a toxicity marker as a nuisance effect, which is easy to do because tinnitus also appears in Table 44.2 as a general NSAID adverse effect. On aspirin specifically, tinnitus points at salicylism.

Q: A 7-year-old on long-term salicylate therapy is brought in drowsy, dizzy, breathing rapidly, and behaving unlike herself, with no complaint of ringing in the ears. Which interpretation is correct?
A) This is the pediatric presentation of chronic salicylate intoxication, which features hyperventilation and CNS effects such as dizziness, drowsiness, and behavioral changes
B) Chronic salicylate toxicity is ruled out, because tinnitus is required for the diagnosis
C) This presentation indicates acute overdose rather than chronic toxicity
D) These findings reflect the respiratory acidosis typical of salicylate toxicity
Answer: A
Rationale: In children the most common manifestations are hyperventilation and central nervous system effects such as dizziness, drowsiness, and behavioral changes. Metabolic acidosis and respiratory alkalosis often occur to varying degrees in chronic salicylate intoxication, and hypoglycemia may also arise and can be life threatening.
Trap: (B) is the mirror error of the previous item. Adults ring, children breathe and change behavior, so requiring the adult finding in a child throws the diagnosis away.

Subtopic: what toxicity looks like

Q: Which findings are consistent with chronic salicylate intoxication? (Select all that apply.)
A) Tinnitus
B) Headache
C) Constipation
D) Nausea
E) Bradycardia
Answer: A, B, D
Rationale: Table 44.3 lists tinnitus, hearing loss, dimness of vision, headache, dizziness, mental confusion, lassitude, and drowsiness under the central nervous system; nausea, vomiting, and diarrhea under gastrointestinal; increased heart rate under cardiovascular; and sweating, thirst, hyperventilation, hypoglycemia, or hyperglycemia under metabolic.
Trap: (E) is a direction reversal of a real table entry, since the cardiovascular row reads increased heart rate. (C) is the same trick on the gastrointestinal row, which reads diarrhea. Both distractors are the opposite of something genuinely listed, which is why they read as familiar.

Subtopic: the acute overdose

Q: A teenager is brought in 7 hours after swallowing an unknown quantity of aspirin, and a serum salicylate level has been drawn. How will the Done nomogram be used?
A) It plots the serum salicylate level against time since ingestion to estimate the severity of this acute intoxication, and a level drawn 6 hours or longer after ingestion is usable
B) It estimates the severity of both acute and chronic salicylate intoxication
C) It should not be used, since a level drawn after 6 hours is too late to interpret
D) It is unnecessary here, because an estimated dose below 150 mg/kg means toxicity is not possible
Answer: A
Rationale: A serum salicylate concentration measured 6 hours or longer after ingestion may be used with the Done nomogram, a graphic plot of serum salicylate level as a function of time since ingestion, first published in a 1960 issue of Pediatrics. The nomogram is intended for estimating only the severity of acute intoxications and not the severity of chronic salicylate intoxication.
Trap: (D) treats the mg/kg severity table as absolute when the text immediately qualifies it: doses lower than 150 mg/kg have resulted in fatal toxicity. (B) extends the nomogram to chronic cases, which its own definition excludes.

Q: Two patients present with large single-drug ingestions, one of aspirin and one of naproxen. Which statement about their management is accurate?
A) Hemodialysis is written into the severe-treatment protocol for salicylates but appears to be of no value for other NSAIDs because of their high protein binding
B) Hemodialysis clears both drugs equally well, since both are salicylate derivatives
C) Hemodialysis is useless for salicylates and is the treatment of choice for other NSAIDs
D) Neither ingestion is treated with hemodialysis; activated charcoal is the only option for both
Answer: A
Rationale: For severe acute salicylate intoxication, treatment includes discontinuation, intensive symptomatic and supportive therapy, and dialysis for high salicylate levels, unresponsive acidosis with pH below 7.1, impaired renal function or renal failure, pulmonary edema, persistent CNS symptoms, or progressive deterioration. Unlike in the case of salicylates, hemodialysis appears to be of no value in enhancing the elimination of NSAIDs because of their high protein binding, and nonsalicylate NSAID overdose is treated with activated charcoal plus supportive and symptomatic care.
Trap: (C) is the exact inversion, and it is the one to guard against, because the same overdose question with a different drug has a different answer. High protein binding is what keeps the nonsalicylate NSAIDs out of the dialysate.

Topic: nsaid contraindications and drug interactions
Subtopic: who must not receive one

Q: Which finding in a patient's history is a contraindication to NSAID therapy?
A) Peptic ulcer disease
B) Osteoarthritis of the knee
C) A history of primary dysmenorrhea
D) A prior episode of gouty arthritis
Answer: A
Rationale: Contraindications include known drug allergy and conditions that place the patient at risk for bleeding, including vitamin K deficiency and peptic ulcer disease. The key points add gastrointestinal tract lesions, peptic ulcers, and bleeding disorders.
Trap: (D) is the option most likely to be read as a contraindication because gout has its own drug class later in the chapter. Acute gout and acute gouty arthritis are the first two FDA-approved indications in Box 44.2, and the NSAIDs are first-line therapy for most patients with gout.

Q: A patient in her third trimester of pregnancy asks whether she may keep taking the ibuprofen she used earlier in the pregnancy. Which response reflects the chapter?
A) NSAIDs are pregnancy category D and not recommended in the third trimester, because of excessive maternal bleeding, decreased amniotic fluid, and neonatal toxicity
B) NSAIDs are safest in the third trimester and are restricted only in the first
C) NSAIDs are unrestricted throughout pregnancy but not during breastfeeding
D) NSAIDs are contraindicated in all trimesters and in all patients of childbearing age
Answer: A
Rationale: NSAIDs are generally categorized as pregnancy category C drugs for use during the first two trimesters but as pregnancy category D, not recommended, for use during the third trimester, because NSAID use has been associated with excessive maternal bleeding, decreased amniotic fluid, and neonatal toxicity during the perinatal period. They are also not recommended for nursing mothers, since they are known to be excreted into human milk.
Trap: (B) reverses the two categories, and the split is easy to reverse because the chapter prints it as a single hybrid label whose footnote reads first trimester for one letter and third trimester for the other.

Q: A patient taking ibuprofen for arthritis is scheduled for a diagnostic laparoscopy in 2 weeks and says it is only a minor procedure. What should the nurse advise?
A) Stop the NSAID at least 1 week before surgery, because of the potential to increase bleeding
B) Continue the NSAID, since only major surgery requires stopping it
C) Switch to aspirin for the 2 weeks, since aspirin is gentler on the platelets
D) Stop the NSAID the morning of the procedure
Answer: A
Rationale: Because of the potential of NSAIDs to increase bleeding, patients undergoing elective surgery need to stop taking NSAIDs at least 1 week before surgery.
Trap: (C) is worse than doing nothing, and it is chosen because aspirin feels like the milder over-the-counter option. Aspirin's COX-1 inhibition inside the platelet is irreversible, so switching to it makes the bleeding risk larger, not smaller. The "only a minor procedure" framing in the stem is the bait.

Subtopic: interactions

Q: A patient taking aspirin 325 mg daily adds over-the-counter ibuprofen for back pain, reasoning that two mild drugs are safer than one strong one. What is the problem?
A) The combination produces increased gastrointestinal toxicity with no therapeutic advantage
B) The combination produces a therapeutic advantage but requires monitoring of liver enzymes
C) Ibuprofen blocks aspirin's absorption entirely, so the aspirin is simply wasted
D) The combination is safe as long as the doses are taken 4 hours apart
Answer: A
Rationale: Table 44.5 lists aspirin and other salicylates taken with other NSAIDs as producing reduction of NSAID absorption and additive gastrointestinal toxicities, with the result stated as increased gastrointestinal toxicity with no therapeutic advantage. The combination also undercuts aspirin's cardioprotection.
Trap: (C) takes the "reduction of NSAID absorption" half of the mechanism and turns it into a complete block, which makes the combination sound merely pointless rather than harmful. The table names doubled risk alongside the absent benefit.

Q: A patient whose hypertension has been well controlled on a thiazide diuretic and an ACE inhibitor begins daily naproxen, and the blood pressure rises over the next 2 weeks. What is the most likely explanation?
A) NSAIDs inhibit prostaglandin synthesis, which reduces the hypotensive and diuretic effects of these drugs
B) The antihypertensive regimen has stopped working and needs to be replaced
C) Naproxen has caused fluid retention through direct renal tubular damage
D) The rise is unrelated to the naproxen, since NSAIDs lower blood pressure
Answer: A
Rationale: Table 44.5 lists diuretics and ACE inhibitors with NSAIDs as producing inhibition of prostaglandin synthesis with the result of reduced hypotensive and diuretic effects.
Trap: (B) reads the situation as treatment failure and would lead to changing the wrong drug. This is a drug-interaction item, and the second layer is that hypertension also appears in Table 44.1 as a comorbidity with its own suggested NSAIDs, which are sulindac, nonacetylated salicylate, ibuprofen, and etodolac.

Q: Which combinations with an NSAID increase the patient's bleeding risk? (Select all that apply.)
A) Alcohol
B) Warfarin
C) Feverfew, garlic, ginger, and ginkgo
D) Bisphosphonates
E) Misoprostol
Answer: A, B, C, D
Rationale: Table 44.5 lists alcohol as additive for increased gastrointestinal bleeding, anticoagulants as producing increased bleeding tendencies through platelet inhibition and hypoprothrombinemia, the herbals feverfew, garlic, ginger, and ginkgo as interfering with platelet function for increased bleeding risk, and bisphosphonates as additive gastrointestinal toxicities with increased gastrointestinal bleeding risk.
Trap: (E) is the drug given to prevent the bleed rather than one that causes it, and it draws students who assume every drug in a long interaction list belongs on the same side of the ledger. Note that the herbal row exists at all, which is why the medication history must include herbals and supplements.

Topic: nsaid and antigout patient teaching
Subtopic: how to take it

Q: A patient is discharged on an oral NSAID for chronic arthritis. Which instruction is correct?
A) Take the drug with food, milk, or antacids to minimize gastrointestinal upset
B) Take the drug on an empty stomach to speed absorption
C) Take the drug at bedtime with nothing by mouth afterward
D) Take the drug with alcohol only if the dose is small
Answer: A
Rationale: Most oral NSAIDs are better tolerated if taken with food to minimize gastrointestinal upset, and the teaching instruction is to take NSAIDs with food, milk, or antacids. The Clinical Pearl pairs this with hydration: NSAIDs can cause GI bleed and renal failure, so take with food and stay well hydrated.
Trap: (D) reads alcohol as dose-dependent rather than as an interaction. Alcohol is listed in Table 44.5 as additive with NSAIDs for increased gastrointestinal bleeding.

Q: A patient with difficulty swallowing asks whether her enteric-coated aspirin and her sustained-release NSAID can be crushed and mixed into applesauce. Which response is correct?
A) These forms are not to be crushed or chewed, and the prescriber should be asked for an alternative dosage form
B) They may be crushed as long as they are taken with food
C) Only the enteric-coated form may be crushed, since the coating is for taste
D) They may be chewed but not crushed
Answer: A
Rationale: These drugs, if in sustained-release or enteric-coated dosage forms, are not to be crushed or chewed. Enteric-coated aspirin is designed to protect the stomach mucosa by dissolving in the duodenum, and sustained-release formulations exist to allow once- or twice-daily dosing, which is known to improve adherence.
Trap: (C) misreads the purpose of the enteric coating as cosmetic. The coating is what keeps the aspirin out of the stomach, so destroying it removes the only gastric protection that dosage form provides.

Subtopic: what to report

Q: Which findings should a patient on long-term NSAID therapy be taught to report? (Select all that apply.)
A) Ringing in the ears
B) Black or tarry stools
C) Bleeding around the gums
D) Petechiae or easy bruising
E) Relief of joint stiffness within the first week
Answer: A, B, C, D
Rationale: The teaching list names ringing in the ears, persistent gastrointestinal problems, black or tarry stools, bleeding around the gums, petechiae, ecchymosis, and purpura, along with unusual bleeding and blood in the stool or vomitus.
Trap: (E) is the therapeutic response rather than an adverse one: the expected outcome is a decrease in acute pain and a decrease in swelling, pain, stiffness, and tenderness of a joint or muscle area. It gets selected because it appears in the same teaching section as the warnings.

Subtopic: monitoring and age

Q: A patient will begin long-term NSAID therapy for rheumatoid arthritis. Which baseline laboratory work does the chapter indicate?
A) RBC count, hemoglobin, hematocrit, WBC count, platelet count, BUN, and liver enzymes such as ALP, AST, and LDH
B) No laboratory work, since NSAIDs are available over the counter
C) Serum uric acid and a Done nomogram plot
D) Coagulation studies only, since bleeding is the sole concern
Answer: A
Rationale: If long-term use is indicated, the chapter lists RBC count, hemoglobin level, hematocrit, WBC count, platelet count, BUN level, and liver enzyme levels such as ALP, AST, and LDH.
Trap: (B) states the chapter's own exception and applies it to the wrong patient. Laboratory studies are usually not indicated when NSAIDs are used short-term for fever or acute pain, because those drugs are available over the counter and often self-administered; this patient is on long-term therapy.

Q: A nurse is planning NSAID therapy for an 80-year-old patient. Which consideration applies?
A) Use these drugs very cautiously because of decreased liver and renal functioning, and note that older adults are at greater risk for serious gastrointestinal events
B) Older adults tolerate NSAIDs better than younger adults because of reduced inflammation
C) Age has no bearing on NSAID selection, only on dosing intervals
D) Older adults require doubled doses because of reduced absorption
Answer: A
Rationale: The chapter states that these drugs are to be used very cautiously in older adult patients because of their decreased liver and renal functioning, and Box 44.3 states that older adult patients are at greater risk for serious gastrointestinal events. Ketorolac makes the point numerically, with an intravenous or intramuscular maximum of 120 mg/day under age 65 and 60 mg/day at 65 or older.
Trap: (D) moves the dose in the wrong direction, which is the reflex whenever a stem mentions reduced organ function without specifying which process is reduced. Reduced clearance calls for less drug, and ketorolac's age-split maximum is the chapter's own example.

Subtopic: teaching in gout

Q: A patient with recurrent gout attacks says he takes his allopurinol every day without fail, drinks three or four beers most evenings, and starts each morning with several cups of coffee. Which teaching is indicated?
A) Alcohol and caffeine must be avoided because they will increase uric acid levels, and fluid intake should be increased to 3 L/day unless contraindicated
B) Adherence is the only issue, and the beverage habits are unrelated to the attacks
C) Fluid intake should be restricted to 1 L/day to concentrate the drug at the renal tubule
D) Coffee is acceptable because caffeine is a diuretic and increases uric acid excretion
Answer: A
Rationale: The gout teaching directs the patient to increase fluid intake to 3 L/day unless contraindicated and to avoid alcohol and caffeine because these will increase uric acid levels. Antigout assessment includes hydration status and baseline serum uric acid levels.
Trap: (B) addresses only medication adherence, which is where the stem's own wording points by emphasizing perfect dosing. The chapter names a dietary half to this problem, and skipping it leaves the attacks unexplained. Note that this chapter pushes fluids for two different reasons: on an NSAID, hydration protects the kidney; in gout, it helps clear uric acid.

Topic: reye's syndrome
Subtopic: the contraindication

Q: A parent asks for something to bring down the fever of a 5-year-old with chickenpox. Which recommendation is correct?
A) Give acetaminophen or ibuprofen, because aspirin and other salicylates are contraindicated in children with flulike symptoms
B) Give a children's aspirin, and switch to acetaminophen only if the fever does not break
C) Give aspirin, since the risk applies only to teenagers
D) Give nothing and allow the fever to break on its own
Answer: A
Rationale: Aspirin and other salicylates have one very specific contraindication: the class is contraindicated in children with flulike symptoms, because their use has been strongly associated with Reye's syndrome. The lifespan box names acetaminophen and ibuprofen as the drugs that may be used instead.
Trap: (B) is the most attractive wrong answer because it sounds cautious, using aspirin only as a fallback. There is no fallback here; the contraindication does not relax if the first drug underperforms.

Q: A parent asks how long aspirin must be avoided after a child receives a varicella vaccine. Which response reflects the chapter's teaching?
A) Avoid aspirin for several weeks after a child or teenager has received a varicella vaccine
B) Aspirin may be resumed as soon as the injection site heals
C) The precaution applies only to the live influenza vaccine
D) There is no precaution, since vaccines do not cause viral illness
Answer: A
Rationale: The prevention teaching states that aspirin or medications that contain aspirin must not be given to children or teenagers to treat viral illnesses or fever, and to avoid aspirin for several weeks after a child or teenager has received a varicella vaccine.
Trap: (D) reasons from the vaccine rather than from the teaching, and it is the option that sounds most scientific. The chapter's instruction is stated as a flat interval, not as a judgment about whether the vaccine produces an illness.

Q: A parent is taught to keep all salicylates away from a child recovering from influenza. Which products and label terms should the parent be taught to check for? (Select all that apply.)
A) Acetylsalicylic acid, acetylsalicylate, salicylic acid, or salicylate on the ingredient list
B) Alka-Seltzer
C) Pepto-Bismol
D) Products containing oil of wintergreen
E) Children's acetaminophen suspension
Answer: A, B, C, D
Rationale: Other names for aspirin include acetylsalicylic acid, acetylsalicylate, salicylic acid, and salicylate. Aspirin is contained in many over-the-counter drugs, including Alka-Seltzer, some Excedrin products, and Pepto-Bismol, and substances with oil of wintergreen may also contain salicylates.
Trap: (E) is one of the two drugs named as the substitute for aspirin in this situation, so selecting it inverts the teaching entirely. This is the same trade-name trap Ch 38 builds around drugs whose names do not announce their class.

Subtopic: recognizing it

Q: A 12-year-old who is recovering from influenza and received aspirin at home is brought in quiet, lethargic, and vomiting persistently. Which action is the priority?
A) Report the findings and anticipate emergency AST, ALT, and serum ammonia levels, because this presentation suggests Reye's syndrome
B) Treat the vomiting as gastroenteritis and offer oral rehydration
C) Give another dose of aspirin for the residual fever
D) Reassure the parent, since Reye's syndrome does not occur after the illness has resolved
Answer: A
Rationale: Reye's syndrome is suspected with unexpected vomiting after any viral illness or with signs of disturbed brain function. Emergency aspartate aminotransferase, alanine aminotransferase, and serum ammonia levels need to be drawn. Signs and symptoms typically develop within 12 hours to 3 weeks after recovery from a viral illness, upper respiratory tract infection, or gastroenteritis, and hypoglycemia and hepatomegaly may be present.
Trap: (D) uses recovery from the viral illness as reassurance, which is exactly backwards: the syndrome characteristically appears in the window after recovery, and more commonly in children and teenagers recovering from chickenpox or flu.

Q: A child with Reye's syndrome who was obtunded with decorticate posturing and intact pupillary light reflexes now has dilated pupils with minimal light reflexes and decerebrate posturing. What does this change represent?
A) Progression from stage 3 to stage 4
B) Progression from stage 1 to stage 2
C) Improvement from stage 4 to stage 3
D) No change in stage, since posturing is not part of the staging
Answer: A
Rationale: The staging box gives stage 3 as obtunded with possible seizures and decorticate posturing with intact pupillary light reflexes, and stage 4 as pupil dilation with minimal light reflexes, seizures, deepening coma, and decerebrate posturing. Stage 1 is quiet, lethargic, and sleepy with persistent copious vomiting, and stage 2 is stupor, disorientation, and combativeness with hyperventilation and hyperreflexia.
Trap: (C) reads the change as improvement because the posturing terms sound interchangeable. Decorticate comes before decerebrate, and the pupillary response is the discriminator the box supplies.

Topic: gout pathophysiology and drug targets
Subtopic: the defect

Q: A patient newly diagnosed with gout asks what went wrong in the body. Which explanation is correct?
A) Gout is caused by overproduction of uric acid, decreased uric acid excretion, or both, resulting in hyperuricemia and deposits of urate crystals in tissues and joints
B) Gout is caused by an autoimmune attack on the joint synovium
C) Gout is caused by excessive purine excretion, which strips the joints of protective crystals
D) Gout is caused by calcium deposits in the joints from excess dietary calcium
Answer: A
Rationale: Gout is caused by the overproduction of uric acid, decreased uric acid excretion, or both, and persons with gout either overproduce or underexcrete uric acid, which is the end product of purine metabolism. Deposits of uric acid crystals collect in tissues and joints, causing an inflammatory response and extreme pain, because the crystals are like small needles that jab and stick into sensitive tissues and joints.
Trap: (C) reverses the direction of the excretion defect and inverts the role of the crystals, and it is chosen by anyone who registers "purine" and "excretion" without noting which way each one runs.

Subtopic: which drug fixes which defect

Q: A patient's gout is attributed to excess production of uric acid. Which drug targets that defect, and how?
A) Allopurinol, which inhibits xanthine oxidase and thereby prevents uric acid production
B) Probenecid, which inhibits xanthine oxidase and thereby prevents uric acid production
C) Allopurinol, which inhibits reabsorption of uric acid in the kidney
D) Colchicine, which blocks the conversion of hypoxanthine to xanthine
Answer: A
Rationale: Allopurinol is used for patients whose gout is caused by the excess production of uric acid, and it inhibits the enzyme xanthine oxidase, which thereby prevents uric acid production. Fig. 44.2 shows allopurinol and its metabolite oxypurinol blocking both xanthine oxidase steps, hypoxanthine to xanthine and xanthine to uric acid. The same upstream action is why allopurinol is also used to prevent acute tumor lysis syndrome during chemotherapy that causes cell death.
Trap: (C) keeps the right drug and gives it the wrong site of action, which is the more dangerous half of this confusion because the drug name still looks correct. Cue: allopurinol works upstream at the enzyme, not downstream at the tubule.

Q: A different patient's gout is attributed to underexcretion of uric acid, and probenecid is prescribed. How does it work, and what limits its use?
A) It binds the transporter in the proximal convoluted renal tubule that returns uric acid to the blood, so uric acid stays in the urine; it is ineffective and not to be used in renal impairment
B) It inhibits xanthine oxidase, so less uric acid is made; it is ineffective in hepatic impairment
C) It dissolves urate crystals already deposited in the joint; it is ineffective once tophi have formed
D) It reduces the inflammatory response to urate crystals; it is ineffective for lowering uric acid
Answer: A
Rationale: Probenecid inhibits the reabsorption of uric acid in the kidney and thus increases its excretion, binding the special transporter protein in the proximal convoluted renal tubule that takes uric acid from the urine and places it back into the blood. The probenecid is then reabsorbed while the uric acid remains in the urine and is excreted. It is ineffective and is not to be used in patients with renal impairment.
Trap: (B) is the mirror error of the previous item, swapping the uricosuric and the xanthine oxidase inhibitor. The contraindication follows from the mechanism: a drug that works in the kidney needs a working kidney, which is why the limiting organ is renal, not hepatic.

Q: A patient in an acute gout attack is given colchicine. What does it do to the serum uric acid level?
A) Nothing directly; it reduces the inflammatory response to the deposits of urate crystals by inhibiting the metabolism, mobility, and chemotaxis of polymorphonuclear leukocytes
B) It lowers the level by inhibiting xanthine oxidase
C) It lowers the level by increasing renal excretion of uric acid
D) It raises the level temporarily by dissolving joint deposits into the blood
Answer: A
Rationale: Colchicine's mechanism is not clearly defined but is thought to inhibit the metabolism, mobility, and chemotaxis of polymorphonuclear leukocytes, reducing the inflammatory response to the deposits of urate crystals. Chemotaxis is the chemical attraction of leukocytes to the site of inflammation, which worsens an inflammatory response.
Trap: (B) assigns colchicine the mechanism of allopurinol and febuxostat, and it is the reflex answer to any class-level question phrased as "antigout drugs work by decreasing serum uric acid levels." Colchicine, strictly, does not satisfy that description.

Q: Which drugs act on uric acid levels themselves rather than on the inflammation? (Select all that apply.)
A) Allopurinol
B) Febuxostat
C) Probenecid
D) Lesinurad
E) Colchicine
Answer: A, B, C, D
Rationale: Allopurinol and febuxostat inhibit xanthine oxidase and block production, while probenecid and lesinurad act at the renal tubule to increase excretion. Febuxostat is a nonpurine selective inhibitor of xanthine oxidase that is more selective for the enzyme than allopurinol is, and lesinurad is a uric acid transporter inhibitor that blocks renal uric acid reabsorption.
Trap: (E) is the drug every student associates with gout, and it is the one that does nothing to uric acid. Colchicine reduces the inflammatory response to urate crystals and leaves the underlying defect untouched.

Subtopic: what comes first

Q: A patient has a first acute attack of gouty arthritis. Which therapy does the chapter identify as first-line?
A) An NSAID such as indomethacin, naproxen, or sulindac
B) Colchicine, because it is the oldest available therapy for acute gout
C) Allopurinol, to stop uric acid production immediately
D) Probenecid, to increase uric acid excretion immediately
Answer: A
Rationale: Although specific antigout drugs are available, the NSAIDs are considered first-line therapy for most patients with gout, and colchicine is considered second-line therapy, after the NSAIDs. Table 44.1 names indomethacin, naproxen, and sulindac as the suggested NSAIDs for gout, and acute gout and acute gouty arthritis head the FDA-approved indications in Box 44.2.
Trap: (B) is the trap this whole section sets, because colchicine is the drug students associate with gout and the chapter itself calls it the oldest available therapy for acute gout. Oldest is not first-line; it is explicitly second-line.

Topic: antigout drug safety and dosing
Subtopic: timing

Q: A patient is in the middle of an acute gout attack, and a new order reads allopurinol to relieve the pain. What is the problem?
A) Allopurinol's onset is 1 to 2 weeks, so it will not relieve an attack in progress
B) Allopurinol's onset is 12 hours, so a faster agent is needed
C) Allopurinol is contraindicated in gout caused by overproduction of uric acid
D) Allopurinol should be given only after the serum uric acid has normalized
Answer: A
Rationale: The pharmacokinetics table gives allopurinol an onset of 1 to 2 weeks and colchicine an onset of 12 hours. Neither is the rescue drug for an attack in progress in the way an NSAID is, and the goals of treatment are to decrease the symptoms of an acute attack and to prevent recurrent attacks. Allopurinol is dosed 200 to 600 mg/day to a maximum of 800 mg/day, and its serious adverse effects are agranulocytosis, aplastic anemia, and potentially fatal skin conditions including exfoliative dermatitis, Stevens-Johnson syndrome, and toxic epidermal necrolysis.
Trap: (B) attaches colchicine's 12-hour onset to allopurinol. The two numbers sit next to each other in the same table and differ by more than an order of magnitude.

Subtopic: colchicine dosing and endpoints

Q: A patient is taking colchicine 0.6 mg hourly for an acute attack and develops severe nausea and diarrhea after a cumulative dose of 3.6 mg. What does this indicate?
A) A stopping endpoint has been reached, and the drug should be stopped even though the 6-mg maximum has not been reached
B) An expected effect to be managed with an antiemetic while the dosing continues to 6 mg
C) An allergic reaction requiring epinephrine
D) Underdosing, since gastrointestinal symptoms indicate poor absorption
Answer: A
Rationale: Acute dosing is an initial dose of 0.6 to 1.2 mg followed by 0.6 mg/hr until pain is relieved, the patient develops severe nausea and diarrhea, or a total of 6 mg has been administered, and some practitioners limit the cumulative dose to 3 mg. Three days must pass before a second course of therapy is initiated, and there is no specific antidote for colchicine poisoning.
Trap: (B) treats the gastrointestinal symptoms as a side effect to be managed rather than as one of the three stated stopping endpoints, and the presence of an unreached numeric maximum makes continuing feel defensible. Any one endpoint stops the drug.

Q: A patient on colchicine reports blood in the urine, and the chart shows a creatinine clearance that has fallen since admission. Which actions does the chapter support?
A) Stop the drug for the bleeding and expect the dose to be adjusted for decreased renal function
B) Continue the drug, since urinary bleeding is an expected effect of urate crystals
C) Continue the drug at the same dose, since colchicine is not renally handled
D) Give the specific colchicine antidote and continue therapy
Answer: A
Rationale: Severe adverse effects include bleeding into the gastrointestinal or urinary tracts, and the drug needs to be stopped if such effects appear. The Clinical Pearl states that the colchicine dose must be adjusted for decreased renal function, and there is no specific antidote for colchicine poisoning.
Trap: (D) invents a rescue that does not exist, which is the assumption most drugs in a nursing course encourage. The chapter states flatly that there is no specific antidote for colchicine poisoning, which is part of why the injectable form was withdrawn in 2008.

Subtopic: the newer agents

Q: A prescriber is weighing febuxostat against allopurinol for a patient with coronary artery disease. Which fact is the discriminator?
A) In 2017 the FDA issued a warning regarding an increased risk for cardiac death with febuxostat compared with allopurinol
B) Febuxostat has no cardiovascular risk, while allopurinol carries a black box warning for cardiac death
C) Febuxostat acts on the renal tubule, so it is safer in cardiac disease
D) Febuxostat is contraindicated in gout caused by overproduction of uric acid
Answer: A
Rationale: Febuxostat is a nonpurine selective inhibitor of xanthine oxidase and the first new drug approved for gout since the 1960s. Clinical trials suggest it may pose a greater risk for cardiovascular events than allopurinol does, and in 2017 the FDA issued a warning regarding an increased risk for cardiac death when compared with allopurinol.
Trap: (B) reverses which drug carries the cardiac warning, and the reversal is easy because newer usually reads as safer in this chapter's other comparisons. Same mechanism and same target enzyme, so the cardiac risk is the discriminator.

Q: An order reads lesinurad 200 mg daily as the sole drug for a patient's gout. What should the nurse do?
A) Question the order, because lesinurad must be given in combination with a xanthine oxidase inhibitor, and its black box names the risk for acute renal failure
B) Administer it and monitor liver enzymes, since the black box concerns hepatotoxicity
C) Administer it and hold all fluids, since lesinurad causes fluid overload
D) Administer it only if the patient is also taking probenecid
Answer: A
Rationale: Lesinurad must be given in combination with a xanthine oxidase inhibitor, and its black box warning covers both the risk for acute renal failure and the requirement that it be taken in conjunction with a xanthine oxidase inhibitor. It is dosed 200 mg/day with food, and patients are advised to stay well hydrated with at least 2 L of fluid per day.
Trap: (C) inverts the hydration instruction that the drug's own labeling requires, and (D) pairs it with another uricosuric rather than with a xanthine oxidase inhibitor, which leaves the black box requirement unmet while looking like a combination.

Subtopic: cross-chapter interaction

Q: A patient taking probenecid for gout is prescribed an NSAID for the pain of an acute attack. What is the concern?
A) NSAIDs antagonize uricosurics, which decreases uric acid excretion
B) Probenecid delays the renal excretion of the NSAID, which raises NSAID levels to toxic range
C) The combination has no interaction, since the two drugs act at different sites
D) NSAIDs potentiate uricosurics, which risks precipitating hypouricemia
Answer: A
Rationale: Table 44.5 lists uricosurics with NSAIDs under the mechanism of antagonism and the result of decreased uric acid excretion. Two drugs aimed at the same disease work against each other.
Trap: (B) borrows probenecid's real and famous property and points it at the wrong drug. Probenecid delays the renal excretion of penicillin, which increases serum penicillin levels and prolongs its effect, and Ch 38 and Ch 39 carry the same effect for cephalosporins, quinolones, and nitrofurantoin. That is an antibiotic interaction, not an NSAID one.
