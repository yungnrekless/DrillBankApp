# Chapter 32 drills — Labor and Birth at Risk

Authored from `sources/Ch32_Fact_Bank.md` (Lowdermilk, Cashion, Alden, Olshansky & Perry, 13th ed., ch. 32, pp. 685–725).

Blueprint weight is **~8 questions of 200 (4.0%), depth `full`** — topics *Labor
Interventions, induction, forceps*. The chapter is the longest in the course at
41 pages, and the reading guide assigns six islands inside it: tocolysis, fetal
lung maturity, obesity, induction and cervical ripening, operative vaginal
birth, and cesarean birth.

That earns **the lower part of the `full` band**, well below Chapter 18's
ceiling: the weight is eight items, not eighteen, and the assigned pages are
drug-guide dense rather than concept dense.

The organizing insight the fact bank names is the spine of this set: **every
intervention in these pages is a dose-response problem with the same failure
mode — too much uterine activity.** Tocolytics suppress contractions,
prostaglandins and oxytocin create them, and the complication that closes each
medication guide is the same word: **tachysystole**. Learning the definition
once — *more than five contractions in 10 minutes averaged over a 30-minute
window* — and the standard response once resolves most of the drug content.

Every distinction the chapter states in both directions is drilled from both
ends: misoprostol and dinoprostone, the Cervidil insert and the Prepidil gel,
induction and augmentation, a category I and a category II or III response to
tachysystole, skin incision and uterine incision, low transverse and classical,
favorable and unfavorable VBAC predictors, and terbutaline as a tocolytic versus
terbutaline as a rescue drug.

**What is left out, and it is most of the chapter.** Preterm labor and birth
(pp. 685–691), prelabor rupture of membranes and chorioamnionitis, dystocia,
multifetal labor, post-term pregnancy, and the obstetric emergencies — shoulder
dystocia, cord prolapse, uterine rupture, and amniotic fluid embolism
(pp. 719–725) — are all outside the six assigned islands and are not drilled
here. They are substantial and testable topics; the reading guide simply does
not assign them under this chapter. The one item closest to the boundary is
**Box 32.4, the contraindications to tocolysis**, which pairs directly with the
assigned tocolytic pages and is the most likely place an exam would reach.

---

Topic: tocolytic therapy selection and contraindications
Subtopic: matching the drug to the woman

Q: A woman in preterm labor has a history of myasthenia gravis. Which tocolytic does the medication guide direct the nurse to question?
A) Magnesium sulfate, which is not given to women with myasthenia gravis, hypocalcemia, or renal failure
B) Terbutaline, which is not given to women with neuromuscular disease
C) Indomethacin, which is not given to women with neuromuscular disease
D) Nifedipine, which is not given to women with neuromuscular disease
Answer: A
Rationale: The magnesium sulfate guide states plainly: "Do not give to women with myasthenia gravis, hypocalcemia, or renal failure." Magnesium is a CNS depressant that relaxes smooth muscle, and it worsens neuromuscular blockade.
Trap: (B) is the tempting pick because terbutaline carries the longest contraindication list of the four, but that list is cardiac, metabolic, and neurologic in a different sense — heart disease, diabetes, severe preeclampsia or eclampsia, hyperthyroidism, glaucoma, and seizure disorder.

Q: A woman with pregestational diabetes and a history of hyperthyroidism is in preterm labor. Which tocolytic is contraindicated for her?
A) Magnesium sulfate
B) Terbutaline
C) Indomethacin
D) Nifedipine
Answer: B
Rationale: Terbutaline "should not be used in women with known or suspected heart disease, pregestational or gestational diabetes, preeclampsia with severe features or eclampsia, hyperthyroidism, glaucoma, or seizure disorder." It is a beta-2-adrenergic agonist, and the guide notes that "myocardial infarction leading to death has been reported after use."
Trap: (C) is contraindicated in renal or hepatic disease, active peptic ulcer disease, poorly controlled hypertension, asthma, and coagulation disorders — a different list entirely, and the one that catches an aspirin-sensitive woman rather than a diabetic one.

Q: A woman receiving magnesium sulfate for tocolysis has an order added for nifedipine. Why should the nurse question this?
A) Administering nifedipine and magnesium sulfate simultaneously can cause skeletal muscle blockade
B) The combination causes fetal ductal constriction
C) Nifedipine inactivates magnesium sulfate
D) The combination has no interaction and may be given together
Answer: A
Rationale: The Safety Alert is explicit: "Administering nifedipine and magnesium sulfate simultaneously can cause skeletal muscle blockade. In addition, nifedipine should not be given along with or immediately following a beta2-adrenergic agonist (e.g., terbutaline) because of effects on maternal heart rate and blood pressure."
Trap: (B) names indomethacin's fetal effect and attaches it to the wrong drug pair. Ductal constriction is a prostaglandin-inhibitor problem, not a calcium channel blocker one.

Subtopic: what each drug is and is not for

Q: A woman is discharged after an episode of preterm labor with a prescription for a terbutaline pump for maintenance tocolysis. What does the chapter say about this plan?
A) It is the standard of care for maintenance tocolysis after 34 weeks
B) Long-term oral or subcutaneous administration as maintenance therapy has not been proven effective and terbutaline should not be used for this purpose
C) It is acceptable if the dose does not exceed 0.25 mg every 4 hours
D) It is acceptable only with concurrent corticosteroids
Answer: B
Rationale: "Long-term oral or subcutaneous administration (e.g., terbutaline pump) as maintenance therapy to suppress preterm labor has not been proven to be effective in reducing prematurity or neonatal morbidity, so terbutaline should not be used for this purpose." It also "has the potential to cause serious maternal cardiac problems and death."
Trap: (C) supplies the correct acute dose — 0.25 mg subcutaneously every 4 hours, for no longer than 24 hours — and uses it to justify the wrong indication. The dose is right and the duration is what makes the plan wrong.

Topic: magnesium sulfate tocolysis monitoring
Subtopic: the stop-the-infusion findings

Q: A woman receiving magnesium sulfate for tocolysis has a respiratory rate of 10 breaths/min and absent deep tendon reflexes. What do these findings represent?
A) Expected adverse effects that subside once the loading dose is complete
B) Intolerable adverse effects, meaning the infusion must be stopped
C) Findings that require only an increase in the infusion rate
D) Signs of hypocalcemia requiring additional magnesium
Answer: B
Rationale: The medication guide lists among intolerable adverse effects a respiratory rate under 12 breaths/min, pulmonary edema, absent DTRs, chest pain, severe hypotension, altered level of consciousness, extreme muscle weakness, urine output under 25 to 30 mL/h or under 100 mL in 4 hours, and a serum magnesium of 10 mEq/L or greater.
Trap: (A) applies a real note from the same guide — "some reactions may subside when loading dose is completed" — to findings that are on the intolerable list. Hot flushes and nausea may subside; absent reflexes stop the drug.

Q: Which assessments does the Safety Alert direct the nurse to perform frequently during magnesium sulfate administration, and why?
A) Respiratory status, deep tendon reflexes, and level of consciousness, because magnesium sulfate depresses central nervous system function
B) Blood glucose and potassium, because magnesium causes hyperglycemia and hypokalemia
C) Amniotic fluid volume and fetal ductus arteriosus function
D) Maternal heart rate and blood pressure only
Answer: A
Rationale: "Because magnesium sulfate depresses the function of the central nervous system, the nurse must frequently assess the woman's respiratory status, deep tendon reflexes, and level of consciousness to identify signs that the serum level of magnesium sulfate is reaching toxic levels."
Trap: (B) belongs to terbutaline, whose guide directs assessment of glucose and potassium before treatment, and (C) belongs to indomethacin. Each of the four tocolytics has its own monitoring set, and they are not interchangeable.

Q: A woman's serum magnesium level returns at 9 mg/dL during tocolysis. How should the nurse interpret this value, and what must be available?
A) Within the therapeutic range of 5 to 8 mg/dL, with calcium gluconate available to reverse toxicity
B) Above the therapeutic range of 5 to 8 mg/dL, with calcium gluconate available to reverse toxicity
C) Below the therapeutic range, requiring a second loading dose
D) Above the therapeutic range, with propranolol available to reverse toxicity
Answer: B
Rationale: The guide gives the therapeutic range as "4–7.5 mEq/L or 5–8 mg/dL" and directs the nurse to "ensure that calcium gluconate is available for emergency administration to reverse magnesium sulfate toxicity."
Trap: (D) names terbutaline's antidote. Propranolol reverses the cardiovascular effects of a beta agonist; calcium gluconate reverses magnesium.

Subtopic: dosing and the other magnesium indications

Q: Magnesium sulfate is ordered for a woman at 28 weeks whose preterm birth now appears inevitable within 24 hours. What is the purpose of this order?
A) Tocolysis, to stop the labor
B) Neuroprotection, to reduce or prevent neonatal neurologic morbidity such as cerebral palsy
C) Seizure prophylaxis for preeclampsia
D) Promotion of fetal lung maturity
Answer: B
Rationale: "When preterm birth appears inevitable (i.e., is expected to occur within the next 24 hours), magnesium sulfate may be administered to reduce or prevent neonatal neurologic morbidity (e.g., cerebral palsy)." It is given at 24 to less than 32 weeks and "should not be continued longer than 24 hours if birth has not occurred."
Trap: (A) is the same drug at overlapping doses for a different purpose, which is what makes this a real bedside confusion. Tocolysis tries to stop labor; neuroprotection assumes labor will not be stopped.

Topic: indomethacin and nifedipine cautions
Subtopic: indomethacin's fetal effects and limits

Q: A woman at 30 weeks is started on indomethacin for tocolysis. Which fetal effects does the medication guide list? Select all that apply.
A) Constriction of the ductus arteriosus
B) Oligohydramnios caused by reduced fetal urine production
C) Neonatal pulmonary hypertension
D) Fetal tachycardia and decreased FHR variability
E) Decreased fetal breathing movements lasting 48 to 72 hours
Answer: A, B, C
Rationale: Indomethacin is a prostaglandin synthetase inhibitor, and its listed fetal effects are constriction of the ductus arteriosus, oligohydramnios from reduced fetal urine production, and neonatal pulmonary hypertension. These effects are why the drug is limited to under 32 weeks and 48 hours or less.
Trap: (D) is terbutaline's fetal effect and (E) is the antenatal glucocorticoid effect. All three drugs may be running in the same woman during the same admission, which is how these lists get merged.

Q: Which limits does the chapter place on indomethacin tocolysis?
A) Use only if gestational age is under 32 weeks, administer for 48 hours or less, and check amniotic fluid volume and ductus arteriosus function before starting and within 48 hours of stopping
B) Use only after 34 weeks and continue until birth
C) Use at any gestational age for up to 7 days
D) Use only in women with asthma, since it is a prostaglandin inhibitor
Answer: A
Rationale: The guide directs: "Use only if gestational age is <32 weeks," "administer for 48 h or less," and "determine amniotic fluid volume and function of fetal ductus arteriosus before initiating therapy and within 48 h of discontinuing therapy." The chapter adds that therapy should be limited "to no more than 2 to 3 days."
Trap: (D) inverts a contraindication into an indication. Indomethacin is not given in asthma, and specifically not to aspirin-sensitive clients. The guide also warns that the drug "can mask maternal fever," which matters when chorioamnionitis is in the differential.

Subtopic: nifedipine teaching

Q: A woman receiving nifedipine for tocolysis wants to get up to the bathroom. Which instruction does the Safety Alert support?
A) Rise quickly to minimize the time spent standing
B) Change position slowly from supine to upright, then sit until any dizziness disappears before standing, and maintain adequate fluid balance
C) Remain on bed rest for the duration of therapy
D) Take the next dose sublingually before ambulating
Answer: B
Rationale: "Because using a calcium channel blocker can result in orthostatic hypotension and dizziness, women must be instructed to change position slowly from supine to upright and then sit until any dizziness disappears before standing. In addition, maintaining adequate fluid balance is important to reduce the drop in blood pressure that can occur with the drug-related vasodilation."
Trap: (D) adds a route the guide forbids outright: "do not use sublingual route of administration." Nifedipine's contraindications are intrauterine infection, hypertension, and cardiac disease.

Topic: antenatal glucocorticoid therapy
Subtopic: dose, route, and window

Q: Betamethasone is ordered for a woman at 30 weeks at risk of preterm birth. What is the correct dosing, and what is the alternative regimen?
A) Betamethasone 12 mg IM for 2 doses 24 hours apart; dexamethasone 6 mg IM for 4 doses 12 hours apart
B) Betamethasone 6 mg IM for 4 doses 12 hours apart; dexamethasone 12 mg IM for 2 doses 24 hours apart
C) Betamethasone 12 mg orally for 2 doses 24 hours apart; dexamethasone 6 mg orally for 4 doses 12 hours apart
D) Betamethasone 12 mg IM for 2 doses 12 hours apart; dexamethasone 6 mg IM for 2 doses 24 hours apart
Answer: A
Rationale: The medication guide gives "betamethasone: 12 mg IM injection for 2 doses 24 h apart" and "dexamethasone: 6 mg IM injection for 4 doses 12 h apart."
Trap: (B) swaps the two regimens, which is the error worth catching because both numbers are real. The larger dose is given fewer times and farther apart.

Q: A woman asks whether she could take the steroid by mouth instead of by injection, since the injections are painful. What does the chapter direct?
A) Oral administration is acceptable if the woman cannot tolerate injections
B) The medication must be given by IM injection; oral administration is not an acceptable alternative
C) The medication may be given intravenously instead
D) The injection may be given subcutaneously to reduce pain
Answer: B
Rationale: The guide is absolute on route: "Medication must be given by IM injection; oral administration is not an acceptable alternative," and directs a "deep IM injection in ventral gluteal or vastus lateralis muscle." It acknowledges that the "injection is painful."
Trap: (A) trades an absolute rule for comfort, and the comfort premise is even conceded by the guide — which is what makes the option attractive.

Q: How long after the first injection does the fetus receive optimal benefit, and why does that timing matter?
A) 12 hours, which is why doses are given 12 hours apart
B) 48 hours from the time of the first injection, which is why timely administration is essential
C) 7 days, which is why the course is repeated weekly
D) 24 hours, matching the betamethasone dosing interval
Answer: B
Rationale: The Medication Alert states: "Because 48 h from the time of the first injection is required for the fetus to receive optimal benefit, timely administration of the medication is essential." This is also why tocolysis is used to buy a window rather than to prolong the pregnancy.
Trap: (D) borrows the betamethasone interval, which is the number the eye lands on. The interval between doses and the interval to benefit are different facts.

Subtopic: who gets a course, and how many

Q: Which statements about antenatal glucocorticoid administration does the chapter support? Select all that apply.
A) A single course is recommended for women at 24 to 34 weeks at risk of birth within 7 days
B) Betamethasone is recommended at 34 0/7 to 36 6/7 weeks for women at risk within 7 days who have not had a previous course
C) A rescue course may be given if the initial course was more than 2 weeks earlier and gestational age remains under 34 weeks
D) More than two courses of antenatal glucocorticoids are not recommended
E) A course should be repeated every 2 weeks until 37 weeks
Answer: A, B, C, D
Rationale: The chapter gives a single course at 24 to 34 weeks, betamethasone at 34 0/7 to 36 6/7 weeks when no previous course was given, a rescue course after more than 2 weeks if still under 34 weeks with birth likely within 7 days, and the ceiling: "More than two courses of antenatal glucocorticoids, however, are not recommended."
Trap: (E) turns the rescue provision into a schedule, which the two-course ceiling forbids.

Topic: obesity in labor and birth
Subtopic: classification and risk

Q: A woman's prepregnancy BMI is 41. How is this classified, and which labor and birth risks does the chapter associate with it?
A) Obese; an increased risk for cesarean birth but not for emergency cesarean birth
B) Severely obese; increased risks for induction, longer labor, cesarean birth in general, and emergency cesarean birth
C) Overweight; an increased risk for postterm pregnancy only
D) Severely obese; a decreased risk for postpartum hemorrhage because of larger blood volume
Answer: B
Rationale: "People with a BMI of 25 or greater are categorized as overweight, people with a BMI of 30 or greater are considered obese, and people with a BMI of 40 or greater are classified as severely obese." The chapter lists an increased incidence of postterm pregnancy and longer labor, a greater likelihood that labor will need to be induced, and increased risks for cesarean birth in general and emergency cesarean birth.
Trap: (D) reverses the hemorrhage risk, which the chapter states in the other direction and explains mechanically in the next item.

Q: Why does the chapter give obese women a higher risk for postpartum hemorrhage?
A) Because of an increased likelihood of induction, prolonged labor, birth of a macrosomic infant, and caregiver difficulty locating the uterine fundus to provide effective fundal massage
B) Because adipose tissue produces anticoagulants
C) Because oxytocin is less effective in obese women
D) Because obese women are more likely to refuse uterotonic medication
Answer: A
Rationale: The chapter gives a causal chain rather than a single factor: the higher risk follows from "an increased likelihood of induction, resulting in prolonged labor, birth of a macrosomic infant, and caregiver difficulty with locating the uterine fundus to provide effective fundal massage."
Trap: (B) invents a mechanism. The mechanical item at the end of the chain — difficulty finding the fundus to massage it — is the one most often missed and the one a nurse can act on.

Subtopic: care management

Q: What does the chapter identify as the central safety problem in caring for an obese woman in labor?
A) That routine procedures require more time and effort, which can slow essential interventions such as an emergency cesarean birth
B) That obese women cannot receive regional anesthesia
C) That external fetal monitoring is always impossible
D) That obese women should be delivered by scheduled cesarean birth
Answer: A
Rationale: "Even routine procedures require more time and effort.... This can slow essential interventions and increase risks to the mother and fetus, such as when an emergency cesarean birth is needed." Concrete examples include repeated IV attempts sometimes by multiple people, difficult transfers especially once regional anesthesia is in effect, and delays from difficulty establishing an epidural or spinal block or accomplishing intubation.
Trap: (C) overstates a real difficulty: continuous external FHR and contraction monitoring "can be extremely difficult if not impossible to perform," which is not the same as always impossible.

Q: Which measures does the chapter describe for an obese woman after cesarean birth? Select all that apply.
A) Antiembolism stockings and sequential compression device boots, with prophylactic heparin for some women
B) Early ambulation as soon as possible
C) Washing the incision with soap and water several times a day and drying it thoroughly, including with a handheld hair dryer on a low setting
D) Leaving sutures or staples in place longer than usual
E) Removing staples on postoperative day 1 to allow the pannus to drain
Answer: A, B, C, D
Rationale: The chapter describes TED hose and SCD boots with prophylactic heparin for some women, early ambulation, and pannus wound care — washing with soap and water several times a day and drying thoroughly, with a hair dryer on a low setting working well. "Sutures or staples used to close the skin incision are generally left in place longer than usual to avoid possible wound disruption when they are removed," and the incision is sometimes left open to heal by secondary intention.
Trap: (E) reverses the closure rule. The pannus keeps the area moist and encourages infection, which is the reason for longer closure, not earlier removal.

Topic: indications and timing of induction
Subtopic: elective induction and the 39-week rule

Q: A woman at 38 weeks asks to be induced because she is uncomfortable and her family is visiting. What does the chapter direct?
A) Elective induction is appropriate at any gestational age after 37 weeks
B) To prevent iatrogenic prematurity, elective induction should not be initiated until the woman reaches 39 completed weeks of gestation
C) Elective induction is never permitted for any reason
D) Elective induction is permitted at 38 weeks if the Bishop score is 8 or more
Answer: B
Rationale: "In an elective induction, labor is initiated without a medical indication," and the rule is stated directly: "To prevent iatrogenic prematurity, elective induction of labor should not be initiated until the woman reaches 39 completed weeks of gestation." The same floor applies to cesarean birth on maternal request.
Trap: (D) uses a real number from a different decision. The Bishop score predicts whether an induction will succeed; it says nothing about whether the induction is appropriate to schedule.

Q: Which assessments does the chapter require before an induction of labor begins? Select all that apply.
A) Cervical assessment including Bishop score and uterine activity
B) Assessment of the pelvis, fetal size, and presentation
C) Evaluation of maternal status and fetal well-being
D) Determination of gestational age
E) Documentation that the woman has requested the induction in writing
Answer: A, B, C, D
Rationale: The chapter requires assessment of "the cervix (Bishop score and uterine activity), pelvis, fetal size, and presentation," evaluation of "both maternal status and fetal well-being," and determination of gestational age, with any potential risks evaluated. Women "must be fully counseled regarding risks, benefits, and alternatives of labor stimulation methods as part of the process for informed consent."
Trap: (E) converts informed consent into a written request, which is not what the chapter describes. Counseling on risks, benefits, and alternatives is the requirement.

Subtopic: indications and contraindications

Q: Which findings does Box 32.7 list as contraindications to labor induction? Select all that apply.
A) Acute, severe fetal distress
B) Shoulder presentation, meaning a transverse lie
C) Floating fetal presenting part
D) Placenta previa and uncontrolled hemorrhage
E) Multiple gestation
Answer: A, B, C, D
Rationale: Box 32.7's contraindications are acute severe fetal distress, shoulder presentation, a floating presenting part, uncontrolled hemorrhage, placenta previa, and a previous uterine incision that prohibits a trial of labor.
Trap: (E) is on the relative contraindication list, along with grand multiparity, suspected cephalopelvic disproportion, breech presentation, and inability to monitor the FHR adequately. Relative and absolute are two separate lists in the same box.

Q: A woman with preeclampsia at 37 weeks is scheduled for induction. How does Box 32.7 classify this, and how is grand multiparity defined in the same box?
A) A maternal indication for induction; grand multiparity is 5 or more pregnancies that ended after 20 weeks of gestation
B) A relative contraindication; grand multiparity is 3 or more live births
C) A fetal indication; grand multiparity is 5 or more live births
D) An absolute contraindication; grand multiparity is 4 or more pregnancies
Answer: A
Rationale: Box 32.7 lists "hypertensive complications of pregnancy: gestational hypertension, preeclampsia, eclampsia" among the maternal indications for induction, along with fetal death and chorioamnionitis. It defines grand multiparity in the relative contraindication list as "5 or more pregnancies that ended after 20 weeks of gestation."
Trap: (C) is a real trap in this box because hypertensive complications appear on both the maternal indication list and the fetal indication list — the fetal entry covers conditions in which fetal testing demonstrates significant jeopardy.

Topic: bishop score and cervical ripeness
Subtopic: reading the score

Q: A woman's Bishop score is 9. What does this indicate about her induction?
A) That the cervix is unfavorable and ripening is required first
B) That the likelihood of vaginal birth is similar whether labor is spontaneous or induced
C) That induction is contraindicated
D) That the maximum possible score has been reached
Answer: B
Rationale: "A score of 8 or more on this 13-point scale indicates that the cervix is soft, anterior, 50% or more effaced, and dilated 2 cm or more and that the presenting part is engaged. When the Bishop score totals 8 or more, the likelihood of vaginal birth is similar whether labor is spontaneous or induced."
Trap: (D) assumes 9 is at the top. The maximum is 13, because cervical consistency and cervical position max out at 2 while dilation, effacement, and station each reach 3.

Q: What does the chapter identify as the most important predictor of successful induction, and what documentation rule follows?
A) Gestational age; the estimated date of birth must be documented before induction
B) Cervical ripeness; the Bishop score should be documented before the use of methods to ripen the cervix or induce labor
C) Parity; the gravidity and parity must be documented before induction
D) Fetal weight; an estimated fetal weight must be documented before induction
Answer: B
Rationale: "Success rates for induction of labor are higher when the condition of the cervix is favorable, or inducible. Cervical ripeness is the most important predictor of successful induction." And: "The Bishop score should be documented before the use of methods to ripen the cervix or induce labor."
Trap: (A) names a real prerequisite — gestational age must be determined before induction — and promotes it to the predictor role that belongs to the cervix.

Q: At which Bishop score do both prostaglandin medication guides indicate cervical ripening before oxytocin induction?
A) A score of 4 or less
B) A score of 8 or more
C) A score of 13
D) Any score, since ripening always precedes oxytocin
Answer: A
Rationale: Both the misoprostol and the dinoprostone guides give the same indication: ripen the cervix before oxytocin induction of labor "when the Bishop score is 4 or less."
Trap: (B) is the threshold for the opposite conclusion — a score of 8 or more means the cervix is already favorable, and ripening is what a low score calls for.

Topic: prostaglandin cervical ripening
Subtopic: misoprostol versus dinoprostone

Q: A provider is choosing between misoprostol and dinoprostone for cervical ripening. Which comparison does the chapter give?
A) Misoprostol is less expensive, easier to insert, and faster in onset, but carries a higher risk for uterine tachysystole with abnormal FHR changes
B) Misoprostol is the only FDA-approved agent for cervical ripening
C) Dinoprostone is less expensive and faster in onset than misoprostol
D) The two carry identical risks of tachysystole
Answer: A
Rationale: Misoprostol, a PGE1 analogue, is "less expensive, is easier to insert, and has a quicker onset of action than PGE2," but is "associated with a higher risk for uterine tachysystole with abnormal FHR and pattern changes," particularly with higher-dose protocols.
Trap: (B) states the fact that belongs to the other drug: "dinoprostone is the only FDA-approved medication for cervical ripening or labor induction." Misoprostol use for this purpose is off-label.

Q: A woman receiving a dinoprostone vaginal insert develops uterine tachysystole. What safety advantage does this form have over the gel and over misoprostol?
A) The insert can be removed by pulling its string, while there is no effective way to remove the gel from the vagina
B) The insert is absorbed more slowly, so no intervention is needed
C) The gel can be suctioned from the vagina if tachysystole occurs
D) The insert delivers a lower total dose, so tachysystole does not occur with it
Answer: A
Rationale: The insert "has the advantage of easy removal should adverse reactions, including uterine tachysystole, occur," and the nurse is directed to "prepare to pull the string to remove the insert and to administer terbutaline 0.25 mg subcutaneously if significant adverse effects occur." Of the gel, the guide states: "There is no effective way to remove the gel from the vagina if uterine tachysystole or abnormal FHR and pattern occur."
Trap: (C) invents a rescue for the gel that the guide explicitly denies, and it is the most consequential misconception in this comparison.

Subtopic: dosing, waiting, and storage

Q: Misoprostol 25 mcg was placed intravaginally 2 hours ago and the cervix remains unfavorable with minimal uterine activity and a category I tracing. What does the guide permit?
A) Redosing now, since the tracing is category I
B) Redosing only after at least 3 hours have passed since the last dose
C) Starting oxytocin now, since the cervix is unfavorable
D) Redosing at double the dose to speed ripening
Answer: B
Rationale: "Redosing is permissible if the cervix remains unfavorable, uterine activity is minimal, FHR is normal (category I), and at least 3 h have passed since the last dose." Risk is reduced by using 25 mcg "no more frequently than every 3–6 h."
Trap: (A) satisfies three of the four conditions and ignores the clock, which is the condition that limits total exposure. Continuous monitoring of FHR and uterine activity is indicated for 4 hours after each dose.

Q: How long must oxytocin be delayed after each of these ripening agents?
A) No sooner than 4 hours after the last misoprostol dose; 6 to 12 hours after the last dinoprostone gel instillation or 30 to 60 minutes after removal of the insert
B) No sooner than 30 minutes after misoprostol; 4 hours after any dinoprostone
C) Immediately after either agent, once the cervix is favorable
D) No sooner than 12 hours after misoprostol; 4 hours after the insert is removed
Answer: A
Rationale: The misoprostol guide directs: "Initiate oxytocin for induction of labor no sooner than 4 h after the last dose of misoprostol was administered." The dinoprostone guide directs: "Delay the initiation of oxytocin for induction of labor for 6–12 h after the last instillation of the gel or for 30–60 min after removal of the insert."
Trap: (D) reverses the two waiting periods. The removable insert allows the shortest wait, the gel the longest, and misoprostol sits between them — which tracks the removability of each form.

Q: A woman with a previous low transverse cesarean birth is scheduled for induction, and cervical ripening with a prostaglandin is proposed. What does the chapter say?
A) Misoprostol may be used but dinoprostone may not
B) Neither agent should be used, because both carry warnings against use after a previous cesarean birth or other major uterine surgery
C) Either agent may be used at a reduced dose
D) Dinoprostone may be used because it is FDA approved
Answer: B
Rationale: Misoprostol "should not be used if the woman has a history of previous cesarean birth or other major uterine surgery," and dinoprostone "is not recommended for use if the woman has a history of previous cesarean birth or other major uterine surgery."
Trap: (D) treats FDA approval as clearance for this particular woman. Approval settles the indication in general, not the contraindication in front of you.

Topic: mechanical and physical ripening methods
Subtopic: devices

Q: A balloon catheter is inserted for cervical ripening. How does it work, and what should the nurse expect?
A) The balloon is inflated above the internal os with 30 to 50 mL of sterile water, producing pressure, stretching, and release of endogenous prostaglandins, and it usually falls out within 8 to 12 hours at about 3 cm of dilation
B) The balloon is inflated below the external os with 10 mL of saline and is removed after 2 hours
C) The balloon delivers prostaglandin gel directly into the cervical canal
D) The balloon must be removed before the woman ambulates
Answer: A
Rationale: The catheter balloon "is inflated above the internal cervical os with 30 to 50 mL of sterile water," producing "pressure and stretching of the lower uterine segment and the cervix as well as the release of endogenous prostaglandins," and "usually falls out within 8 to 12 hours, when cervical dilation reaches approximately 3 cm." It is especially helpful for women who cannot receive exogenous prostaglandins and carries a reduced risk of tachysystole.
Trap: (C) confuses the mechanical device with a chemical one. The balloon releases the woman's own prostaglandins; nothing is instilled through it.

Q: Laminaria tents and synthetic dilators are placed for cervical ripening. Which statements are accurate? Select all that apply.
A) Laminaria tents are natural cervical dilators made from desiccated seaweed
B) They absorb fluid, expand, and cause cervical dilation and release of endogenous prostaglandins
C) They are left in place for 6 to 12 hours before removal
D) Compared with prostaglandins they cause less tachysystole but more discomfort and more maternal and newborn infections
E) They are inserted after the membranes are ruptured
Answer: A, B, C, D
Rationale: Hydroscopic dilators are inserted into the endocervix "without rupturing the membranes," absorb fluid, expand, and release endogenous prostaglandins. They are left 6 to 12 hours, produce less tachysystole than prostaglandins, and carry "increased discomfort at insertion and during expansion and a higher incidence of postpartum maternal and newborn infections."
Trap: (E) reverses an explicit condition. The nurse's documentation duty follows from the same concern for what is inside the uterus: record "the number of dilators and sponges inserted during the procedure as well as the number removed."

Subtopic: physical and alternative methods

Q: A woman at 40 weeks asks about using herbal preparations such as blue cohosh or evening primrose oil to start labor. What is the chapter's nursing concern?
A) That they are ineffective and therefore harmless
B) That they may potentiate the effect of pharmacologic methods, increasing the potential for tachysystole and precipitous labor and birth
C) That they interfere with fetal monitoring equipment
D) That they are contraindicated only in women with a previous cesarean birth
Answer: B
Rationale: Women sometimes use blue cohosh and castor oil for labor stimulation and black cohosh and evening primrose oil for cervical ripening, and the chapter's concern is concrete: these preparations "may potentiate the effect of pharmacologic methods ..., thereby increasing the potential for tachysystole and precipitous labor and birth."
Trap: (A) is the reflex dismissal of an unproven remedy, and it misses the interaction that makes disclosure matter at the bedside.

Topic: amniotomy
Subtopic: prerequisites and risks

Q: An amniotomy is planned. Which condition must be met to prevent cord prolapse?
A) The cervix must be fully dilated
B) The presenting part must be engaged and well applied to the cervix
C) The woman must be in the active phase of labor
D) An IUPC must be in place
Answer: B
Rationale: "The presenting part of the fetus should be engaged and well applied to the cervix before the procedure to prevent cord prolapse." The woman should also be free of active genital tract infection such as herpes and should be HIV negative.
Trap: (A) applies a forceps prerequisite to an amniotomy. Full dilation is required before forceps are placed, not before membranes are ruptured — amniotomy is used to induce labor when the cervix is favorable or to augment a labor that is slowing.

Q: The FHR is assessed immediately after an amniotomy. Which findings would the Safety Alert have the nurse treat as concerning rather than expected?
A) Transient tachycardia
B) Bradycardia or variable decelerations, which can indicate cord compression or prolapse
C) Moderate variability
D) A baseline of 145 beats/min
Answer: B
Rationale: "Fetal heart rate is assessed before and immediately after the amniotomy to detect any changes. Transient tachycardia is common. Bradycardia and variable decelerations can indicate cord compression or prolapse."
Trap: (A) is the finding the alert names as common, and calling it an emergency is the mirror error of missing the one that is not common.

Q: What are the two main risks of amniotomy named in the chapter, and what commitment does it create?
A) Chorioamnionitis from prolonged rupture if labor does not start, and variable decelerations from cord compression or prolapse; once performed, the woman is committed to labor with an unknown outcome
B) Postpartum hemorrhage and uterine rupture; the procedure can be reversed if labor does not begin
C) Maternal pain and fetal bradycardia; the membranes can reseal
D) Precipitous labor and uterine tachysystole; the procedure commits the woman to oxytocin
Answer: A
Rationale: If amniotomy does not stimulate labor, "the resulting prolonged rupture may lead to chorioamnionitis," and "variable FHR deceleration patterns can occur as a result of cord compression associated with umbilical cord prolapse or a decreased amount of amniotic fluid." The chapter adds: "Once an amniotomy is performed, the woman is committed to labor with an unknown outcome for how and when she will give birth."
Trap: (C) offers reassurance the chapter does not give and understates the procedure. The rupture itself "is painless for her and the fetus," but the commitment it creates is the point.

Subtopic: monitoring and scope of practice

Q: A nurse on a labor unit is asked by a provider to perform the amniotomy. What does the Legal Tip direct?
A) The nurse may perform it after demonstrating competence
B) Performing amniotomy is outside the scope of practice of nurses, and a policy consistent with professional standards that clearly explains the nurse's role should be in place
C) The nurse may perform it only if the cervix is fully dilated
D) The nurse may perform it with a verbal order documented afterward
Answer: B
Rationale: The Legal Tip states: "Performing amniotomy is outside the scope of practice of nurses. In some locations, however, nurses have been asked to perform this procedure. A policy that is consistent with professional standards of care and clearly explains the nurse's role in amniotomy should be in place in all labor and birth areas."
Trap: (A) substitutes demonstrated skill for scope of practice, which is the reasoning that gets nurses into exactly the situation this tip describes.

Topic: oxytocin administration for labor stimulation
Subtopic: why the protocol looks the way it does

Q: Why does the low-dose oxytocin protocol increase the dose no more frequently than every 30 to 60 minutes, when the uterus responds within 3 to 5 minutes?
A) Because the half-life is 10 to 12 minutes, so the drug is cleared before the next dose
B) Because approximately 40 minutes is required for the full effect of a dosage increment to be reflected in contractions
C) Because the infusion pump cannot be adjusted more often
D) Because contractions must be documented for 30 minutes before any change
Answer: B
Rationale: The uterus responds within 3 to 5 minutes and the half-life is 10 to 12 minutes, but "approximately 40 minutes" is required "for the full effect of a dosage increment to be reflected in more intense, frequent, and longer contractions." Increasing before steady state is how tachysystole is produced.
Trap: (A) uses a real pharmacokinetic value to argue the opposite conclusion. A short half-life does not mean the effect has peaked; steady state is what the interval is protecting.

Q: What does the chapter say about high-dose oxytocin protocols compared with low-dose protocols?
A) They shorten labor significantly with no increase in adverse effects
B) They result in more uterine tachysystole and more category II or category III FHR patterns without a clinically significant decrease in the length of labor
C) They are preferred for augmentation but not induction
D) They reduce the cesarean birth rate
Answer: B
Rationale: High-dose protocols "have been found to result in more uterine tachysystole and more indeterminate or abnormal (category II or category III) FHR patterns," and "did not result in a clinically significant decrease in length of labor." The stated goal is "to produce contractions of normal intensity, duration, and frequency using the lowest dose of medication possible."
Trap: (A) is the assumption behind high-dose protocols and the reason the comparison is worth knowing — more drug bought more tachysystole and no time.

Subtopic: safe administration

Q: Which statements about oxytocin administration match the medication guide? Select all that apply.
A) Oxytocin is on the Institute for Safe Medication Practices list of high-alert medications
B) It is administered through a secondary line connected to the main line at the port closest to the IV insertion site
C) It is always administered by infusion pump
D) Contractions should not occur more frequently than every 2 minutes
E) The dose should be increased steadily until contractions occur every 90 seconds
Answer: A, B, C, D
Rationale: The Safety Alert notes oxytocin's high-alert designation "because it has a heightened risk for causing significant client harm when used in error." The guide directs administration "through a secondary line connected to the main line at the proximal port (connection closest to the IV insertion site)," always by infusion pump, with the ceiling that "contractions should not occur more frequently than every 2 min."
Trap: (E) sets a target past the guide's own ceiling. Contractions every 90 seconds exceed five in 10 minutes, which is the definition of tachysystole.

Q: How often should the FHR tracing be evaluated during an oxytocin infusion?
A) Every 30 minutes in the first stage and every 15 minutes in the second stage
B) At least every 15 minutes during the first stage and the passive descent phase of the second stage, and every 5 minutes during active pushing
C) Every hour, since continuous EFM is in use
D) Every 15 minutes throughout labor regardless of stage
Answer: B
Rationale: The guide directs continuous EFM with evaluation of the tracing "at least every 15 min during the first stage of labor and during the passive fetal descent phase of the second stage," and "every 5 min during the active pushing phase of the second stage."
Trap: (A) gives the intervals for a low-risk woman without oxytocin. An oxytocin infusion is itself the risk factor that moves a woman to the tighter column.

Q: A woman's labor began spontaneously but the active phase has slowed, and augmentation is being considered. What does the chapter direct before invasive interventions?
A) Begin oxytocin immediately, since the diagnosis is hypotonic uterine dysfunction
B) Attempt noninvasive methods first, including emptying the bladder, ambulation and position changes, relaxation measures, nourishment and hydration, and hydrotherapy
C) Perform an amniotomy first in every case
D) Proceed directly to cesarean birth for failure to progress
Answer: B
Rationale: "Augmentation of labor is the stimulation of uterine contractions after labor has started spontaneously and progress is unsatisfactory," usually for hypotonic uterine dysfunction with a protracted active phase. "Noninvasive methods such as emptying the bladder, ambulation and position changes, relaxation measures, nourishment and hydration, and hydrotherapy should be attempted before initiating invasive interventions."
Trap: (A) skips the step that distinguishes augmentation from induction. Induction starts a labor that has not begun; augmentation adds to one that has, and it has a required noninvasive first phase.

Topic: uterine tachysystole management
Subtopic: recognizing it

Q: A woman receiving oxytocin has six contractions in 10 minutes, averaged over 30 minutes, with a category I tracing and no report of increased pain. How is this documented?
A) Uterine tachysystole
B) Normal uterine activity, since the tracing is category I
C) Uterine hyperstimulation
D) Normal uterine activity, since the woman is comfortable
Answer: A
Rationale: "This group defined uterine tachysystole as more than five contractions in 10 minutes averaged over a 30-minute window," and the definition deliberately "does not include an abnormal FHR or pattern or the woman's perception of pain." The term applies to both spontaneous and stimulated labor.
Trap: (C) uses a term the chapter retired: "use of the terms hyperstimulation and hyperactivity be abandoned because they are not defined." (B) and (D) each add a criterion the standardized definition excludes.

Q: Besides contraction frequency, which findings appear in the Emergency box as signs of uterine tachysystole? Select all that apply.
A) Contractions lasting 2 minutes or longer
B) Insufficient return of uterine resting tone between contractions by palpation
C) Intraamniotic pressure greater than 25 mm Hg between contractions by IUPC
D) Maternal report of severe pain
E) Late decelerations on the tracing
Answer: A, B, C
Rationale: The Emergency box lists more than five contractions in 10 minutes averaged over 30 minutes, contractions lasting 2 minutes or longer, and insufficient return of resting tone between contractions, either by palpation or as intraamniotic pressure greater than 25 mm Hg by IUPC.
Trap: (D) and (E) are both deliberately excluded from the standardized definition, which is what allows tachysystole to be recognized and treated before the tracing deteriorates.

Subtopic: the response, which splits on the tracing

Q: Tachysystole occurs during an oxytocin infusion and the FHR tracing is category I. What is the correct sequence?
A) Discontinue the oxytocin immediately, reposition, and give a fluid bolus
B) Reposition to a side-lying position, give at least a 500 mL lactated Ringer's bolus, decrease the oxytocin by at least half if activity has not normalized in 10 to 15 minutes, and discontinue it after another 10 to 15 minutes if needed
C) Give terbutaline 0.25 mg subcutaneously first
D) Continue the infusion and reassess in 30 minutes
Answer: B
Rationale: With a category I tracing, the Emergency box directs repositioning to side-lying, an IV fluid bolus of at least 500 mL of lactated Ringer's solution, halving the oxytocin dose if uterine activity has not returned to normal after 10 to 15 minutes, and discontinuing it after another 10 to 15 minutes if activity is still abnormal.
Trap: (A) applies the category II or III half of the box to a normal tracing. The tracing decides whether oxytocin is titrated down or stopped outright.

Q: Tachysystole occurs during an oxytocin infusion and the tracing is category III. What is the first action?
A) Decrease the oxytocin by half and reassess in 10 to 15 minutes
B) Discontinue the oxytocin infusion immediately
C) Administer terbutaline 0.25 mg subcutaneously
D) Give oxygen at 10 L/min by nonrebreather mask
Answer: B
Rationale: For a category II or category III tracing the box begins with "discontinue oxytocin infusion immediately," then repositioning, then a 500 mL lactated Ringer's bolus, then considering oxygen at 10 L/min by nonrebreather mask if those measures do not resolve the tracing, then considering terbutaline 0.25 mg subcutaneously, then notifying the provider.
Trap: (A) is the mirror error of the previous item and is the single most consequential mix-up in this box — halving the dose on an abnormal tracing leaves the stimulus running.

Q: Oxytocin was stopped 45 minutes ago for tachysystole and uterine activity is now normal with a reassuring tracing. At what rate should it be resumed?
A) At the rate that was running when the tachysystole occurred
B) At no more than half the rate that caused the tachysystole
C) At the initial starting dose
D) It may not be resumed during this labor
Answer: C
Rationale: The box gives two restart rules: "if oxytocin infusion has been discontinued for less than 20–30 min, resume at no more than one-half the rate that caused the tachysystole," and "if oxytocin infusion has been discontinued for more than 30-40 min, resume at the initial starting dose."
Trap: (B) is the correct answer for a shorter interruption and is the mirror error here. The longer the drug has been off, the further the restart drops — 45 minutes puts this woman back at the beginning.

Topic: operative vaginal birth
Subtopic: classification and prerequisites

Q: At a forceps-assisted birth, the fetal scalp is visible on the perineum without separating the labia. How is this classified?
A) Outlet
B) Low
C) Midpelvis
D) High
Answer: A
Rationale: Table 32.3 defines outlet as "fetal scalp is visible on the perineum without separating the labia," low as "fetal head is at least at +2 station," and midpelvis as "fetal head is above +2 station but is engaged."
Trap: (B) is the next category down and is chosen when the station is assumed rather than read. The classification is set by how far the head has descended, not by which instrument is used.

Q: Which prerequisites must be met before a forceps-assisted birth? Select all that apply.
A) The cervix must be fully dilated
B) The bladder should be empty
C) The presenting part must be engaged, with a vertex presentation desired
D) The membranes must be ruptured
E) An IUPC must be in place to measure contraction intensity
Answer: A, B, C, D
Rationale: "The woman's cervix must be fully dilated to prevent lacerations and hemorrhage. The bladder should be empty. The presenting part must be engaged—vertex presentation is desired. Membranes must be ruptured so that the position of the fetal head can be precisely determined and the forceps can firmly grasp the head." The pelvis must also be assessed as adequate for the estimated fetal size.
Trap: (E) adds a monitoring device that is not on the list. Each real prerequisite has a stated reason, and an option without one is usually the invented item.

Q: A nurse tells a student that forceps require conditions a vacuum extractor does not. How should this be corrected?
A) Indications and prerequisites for both instruments are identical, and the choice is based on the experience and personal preference of the physician
B) Vacuum extraction requires full dilation but forceps do not
C) Forceps may be used before 34 weeks but vacuum may not, which is the only difference
D) Vacuum extraction has no prerequisites
Answer: A
Rationale: "Indications and prerequisites for the use of both instruments are identical. The decision to use forceps or a vacuum extractor is based on the experience and personal preference of the physician." Vacuum-assisted birth carries one added restriction: "it is generally not used to assist birth before 34 weeks of gestation."
Trap: (C) states that one real difference and then calls it the only one, when the underlying claim in the stem — different prerequisites — is what needed correcting. Another scope fact from the same pages: "nurse midwives do not perform operative vaginal births."

Subtopic: complications and nursing response

Q: The FHR drops as forceps are applied. What is the cause, and what does the chapter direct?
A) Compression of the cord between the fetal head and the forceps; the forceps are removed and reapplied
B) Vagal stimulation from head compression; no action is needed
C) Uteroplacental insufficiency; oxytocin is discontinued
D) Maternal hypotension; the woman is repositioned
Answer: A
Rationale: The Safety Alert states that "compression of the cord between the fetal head and the forceps will cause a decrease in fetal heart rate (FHR)," so the FHR "is assessed, reported, and recorded before and after application of the forceps." The chapter adds: "If a decrease in the FHR occurs, the forceps are removed and reapplied."
Trap: (B) treats the deceleration as a benign early deceleration, which is a defensible reading of head compression in general and the wrong one here — the instrument is the cause and removing it is the intervention.

Q: Which findings would the nurse assess for in the newborn after a forceps-assisted birth? Select all that apply.
A) Bruising or abrasions at the site of the blade applications
B) Facial palsy from pressure of the blades on the facial nerve
C) Subdural hematoma
D) Urinary retention
E) Hematoma formation in the pelvic soft tissues
Answer: A, B, C
Rationale: The newborn assessments after forceps are bruising or abrasions at the blade sites, facial palsy from pressure on the facial nerve, and subdural hematoma. The chapter adds a handoff rule: "newborn and postpartum caregivers should be told that a forceps-assisted birth was performed."
Trap: (D) and (E) belong to the maternal list, along with vaginal or cervical lacerations. The two lists sit side by side in the text and sort cleanly by whose body was injured.

Topic: cesarean birth indications and technique
Subtopic: the incisions

Q: A woman with a low transverse skin scar from a previous cesarean birth asks whether she can attempt a vaginal birth. What does the nurse need to establish first?
A) Nothing further; the transverse skin scar establishes eligibility
B) The type of uterine incision, because the type of skin incision does not necessarily indicate the type of uterine incision
C) The surgeon's name, since technique varies
D) Whether the skin incision was closed with sutures or staples
Answer: B
Rationale: "The type of skin incision does not necessarily indicate the type of uterine incision." A low transverse uterine incision permits a trial of labor; a classical incision does not.
Trap: (A) is the assumption this sentence exists to break, and it is the most testable line in the cesarean section. A Pfannenstiel scar is visible and tells you nothing about the uterus underneath it.

Q: Which advantages does the chapter give for the low transverse uterine incision?
A) It is easier to perform and repair, is associated with less blood loss, and preserves the option of a trial of labor and vaginal birth in subsequent pregnancies
B) It allows faster access in an emergency than a vertical incision
C) It is preferred when the lower uterine segment is underdeveloped
D) It is preferred when there is an anterior placenta previa
Answer: A
Rationale: The low transverse incision is "performed in almost all cesarean births," is "easier to perform and repair and is associated with less blood loss," and "provides for the option of TOL and vaginal birth after cesarean (VBAC) in subsequent pregnancies."
Trap: (C) and (D) name the indications for the vertical or classical incision — an underdeveloped lower uterine segment, transverse lie, anterior placenta previa or accreta, or obstruction by fibroids — which is the other half of this pair.

Q: Why is vaginal birth contraindicated after a classical uterine incision?
A) Because the scar prevents cervical dilation
B) Because it is associated with a higher incidence of uterine rupture in subsequent pregnancies
C) Because it is always accompanied by a vertical skin incision
D) Because it indicates a prior placenta previa
Answer: B
Rationale: The classical incision is "only rarely performed, but occasionally it is necessary," and "because it is associated with a higher incidence of uterine rupture in subsequent pregnancies ..., vaginal birth after a classical uterine incision is contraindicated."
Trap: (C) reintroduces the skin-incision assumption from a different angle. Nothing about the skin tells you the uterine incision, in either direction.

Subtopic: indications and anesthesia

Q: A woman requests a primary cesarean birth without a medical indication because she fears labor pain. Which responses does the chapter support? Select all that apply.
A) She should be offered prenatal birth education, analgesia and anesthesia, and emotional support during labor and birth
B) Cesarean birth on request should not be performed before 39 weeks of gestation
C) She should be informed that the risks for placenta previa, placenta accreta, and cesarean hysterectomy increase with each subsequent cesarean birth
D) Insufficient evidence exists to recommend elective cesarean birth to prevent long-term urinary or anal incontinence
E) Elective cesarean birth prevents future pelvic support and incontinence problems
Answer: A, B, C, D
Rationale: The chapter attributes maternal requests to "fear of pain during labor and birth and the mistaken belief that the surgery will prevent future problems with pelvic support, bladder and bowel incontinence, or sexual dysfunction," and directs education, analgesia, and support; the 39-week floor; the counseling on placenta previa, accreta, and cesarean hysterectomy; and the finding that "currently, insufficient evidence exists to recommend elective cesarean birth to prevent long-term urinary or anal incontinence."
Trap: (E) restates the belief the chapter calls mistaken, which is the reason the counseling points exist.

Q: According to Box 32.10, at what estimated fetal weight should cesarean birth for suspected macrosomia be considered?
A) At least 4000 g in all women
B) At least 5000 g in women without diabetes and at least 4500 g in women with diabetes
C) At least 4500 g in women without diabetes and at least 5000 g in women with diabetes
D) At any weight above the 90th percentile
Answer: B
Rationale: Box 32.10 recommends avoiding "cesarean birth for suspected fetal macrosomia unless the estimated fetal weight is at least 5000 g (11 lb. 0 oz.) in women without diabetes and at least 4500 g (9 lb. 15 oz.) in women with diabetes."
Trap: (C) swaps the two thresholds. Diabetes lowers the threshold, because the shoulder-to-head disproportion of a diabetic macrosomic fetus makes the same weight riskier.

Topic: tolac and vbac candidacy
Subtopic: who is a candidate

Q: Which woman should be counseled regarding a trial of labor after cesarean?
A) A woman with a previous low transverse uterine incision and no contraindications to vaginal birth
B) A woman with a previous classical uterine incision
C) A woman with a prior uterine rupture
D) A woman with a complete placenta previa in this pregnancy
Answer: A
Rationale: "A woman who has had a previous cesarean birth with a low transverse uterine incision and who has no contraindications for vaginal birth should be counseled regarding TOLAC." Those excluded are women "with a previous classical or T-shaped incision, prior uterine rupture, or extensive transfundal uterine surgery," and women for whom vaginal birth is otherwise contraindicated.
Trap: (D) fails on the second clause rather than the first — her uterine scar might be low transverse, but vaginal birth is contraindicated for a separate reason, and TOLAC does not override it.

Q: Which factors make a successful VBAC more likely? Select all that apply.
A) A prior vaginal birth
B) Spontaneous rather than induced or augmented labor
C) A first cesarean performed for a nonrecurring indication such as breech presentation
D) Gestational age at or beyond 40 weeks
E) An interbirth interval of less than 19 months
Answer: A, B, C
Rationale: "The strongest predictors of a successful VBAC are a prior vaginal birth and spontaneous (rather than induced or augmented) labor," and a first cesarean for a nonrecurring indication is also favorable. Success overall is approximately 60% to 80%.
Trap: (D) and (E) are on the unfavorable list, along with a recurrent indication such as dystocia, increased maternal age, a BMI over 30, an estimated fetal weight over 4000 g, labor induction, and preeclampsia at the time of birth. The predictors run in both directions and the exam uses both.

Subtopic: risks and language

Q: What is the major risk associated with VBAC, and where should TOLAC be offered?
A) Postpartum hemorrhage; in any birth setting including home
B) Uterine rupture; in facilities that can provide cesarean birth for situations that are immediate threats to the life of the woman or the fetus
C) Placenta accreta; in facilities with a neonatal intensive care unit
D) Infection; in facilities with continuous anesthesia coverage only
Answer: B
Rationale: "The major risk associated with VBAC is uterine rupture," with other risks including operative injury, blood transfusion, hysterectomy, endometritis, and death. TOLAC and VBAC should "be offered in facilities that can provide cesarean birth for situations that are immediate threats to the life of the woman or the fetus."
Trap: (A) names a risk VBAC actually reduces — vaginal birth after cesarean carries "lower rates of hemorrhage, thromboembolism, and infection and a shorter recovery period" than repeat cesarean birth.

Q: A woman attempting TOLAC ultimately gives birth by cesarean. How should the nurse document and discuss this outcome?
A) As a failed VBAC, which is the standard term
B) Without labeling it a failed VBAC, which the chapter states is very important
C) As an elective repeat cesarean birth
D) As a contraindication to future trials of labor
Answer: B
Rationale: The chapter gives a language rule to take literally: "It is very important that this outcome not be labeled a failed VBAC."
Trap: (A) uses the phrase that is still common in practice, which is precisely why the chapter names it.

Q: A woman in TOLAC is visibly anxious and her labor is progressing slowly. What does the chapter say about the effect of anxiety, and what nursing care follows?
A) Anxiety has no physiologic effect on labor progress
B) Anxiety increases catecholamine release and can inhibit oxytocin release, delaying progress and possibly leading to a repeat cesarean birth, so the nurse encourages breathing and relaxation techniques and position changes
C) Anxiety increases oxytocin release and speeds labor
D) Anxiety should be treated with an anxiolytic before labor can progress
Answer: B
Rationale: "Anxiety increases the release of catecholamines and can inhibit the release of oxytocin, thus delaying the progress of labor and possibly leading to a repeat cesarean birth." The nurse encourages "breathing and relaxation techniques" and "position changes to promote labor progress."
Trap: (C) reverses the hormonal effect, and it is a plausible inversion because oxytocin and stress are both released under arousal. In labor the catecholamine surge works against oxytocin.
