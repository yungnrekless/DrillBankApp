# Chapter 14 drills — Nursing Care of the Family During Pregnancy

Authored from `sources/Ch14_Fact_Bank.md` (Lowdermilk, Cashion, Alden, Olshansky & Perry, 13th ed., ch. 14, pp. 265–294).

Blueprint weight is **~7 questions of 200 (3.5%), depth `standard`** — topic
*EDB, Early Complications, Concerning Signs/Symptoms, Vaccinations*. The Week 2
reading guide assigns six islands from a 30-page chapter, and the blueprint's
topic string lines up with them almost exactly: *EDB* is the Naegele rule,
*Concerning Signs/Symptoms* is Table 14.4, and *Early Complications* is that
table's first-trimester block. Only *Vaccinations* falls outside the assigned
pages, and it is carried here because the blueprint names it.

That earns **45 drills across 10 topics** — the top of the `standard` band of 30 to
45, which is where a 7-item chapter belongs, since 7 is the top of the 4-to-7
blueprint range that defines that band.

The organizing insight the fact bank names is the spine of this set: **this
chapter's testable content is almost entirely classification.**
Presumptive/probable/positive, gravida/para, GTPAL, live versus inactivated
vaccine, first-trimester versus second- and third-trimester warning signs. Each
is a set with sharp boundaries and one obvious way to write a wrong answer —
move an item across a boundary. These questions drill the boundary rule for each
set rather than the items one at a time.

This chapter also carries the **gravidity and parity** definitions that
Chapter 16's blueprint line names but Chapter 16 never defines. Boxes 14.2 and
14.3 are here, so that topic is drilled here.

**What is left out.** The chapter's emotional-adaptation material beyond p. 269,
the physical examination and laboratory battery, the health-promotion teaching
on hygiene, activity, travel and clothing, and **Table 14.3, Discomforts Related
to Pregnancy** — which is the chapter's largest table and the first place to
extend this bank if the exam reaches past the guide. Perinatal education,
birth plans, and choice of provider and setting are also outside the assignment.

Topic: classifying signs of pregnancy
Subtopic: who perceives the sign

Q: A woman reports amenorrhea, breast tenderness, nausea, and urinary frequency. How are these findings classified, and what do they establish?
A) Presumptive signs: subjective changes experienced by the woman that suggest the possibility of pregnancy
B) Probable signs: objective changes perceived by an examiner that strongly suggest pregnancy
C) Positive signs: objective changes that indicate proof of pregnancy
D) Presumptive signs, which confirm pregnancy when four or more are present
Answer: A
Rationale: Table 14.1 defines presumptive signs as "subjective changes experienced by the woman that suggest the possibility of pregnancy." Every one of these findings has another possible cause in the table's third column — premenstrual changes, stress, gastrointestinal virus, infection.
Trap: (D) treats accumulation as proof, which the classification does not allow. No number of presumptive signs becomes a positive sign; only detection of the fetus itself does.

Q: An examiner detects fetal heart tones with a Doppler ultrasound stethoscope at 10 weeks. How is this classified, and what makes it that class?
A) Probable, because Doppler findings can have other causes
B) Positive, because the finding can be attributed only to the presence of a fetus and has no other cause
C) Presumptive, because the woman has not yet felt movement
D) Probable, because positive signs require visualization rather than sound
Answer: B
Rationale: Positive signs are "objective changes observed/perceived by an examiner that indicate proof of pregnancy," and every positive row in Table 14.1 reads "no other causes" in its third column. Fetal heart tones by Doppler at 8 to 17 weeks is one of those rows.
Trap: (D) invents a requirement. Visualization by ultrasound, fetal heart tones by any of three methods, and fetal movements palpated or seen by the examiner are all positive — the common element is the fetus itself, not the modality.

Q: A woman brings in a positive home pregnancy test and says it confirms she is pregnant. How should the nurse classify this finding?
A) Positive, because a pregnancy test detects hCG produced only by a pregnancy
B) Probable, because a hydatidiform mole or choriocarcinoma can produce a positive test, and a urine test can be falsely positive from pelvic infection or tumors
C) Presumptive, because the woman performed the test herself
D) Positive, if the test is repeated and remains positive
Answer: B
Rationale: Table 14.1 lists both serum and urine pregnancy tests as probable signs, with "hydatidiform mole, choriocarcinoma" and "false-positive result may be caused by pelvic infection, tumors" in the other-possible-cause column.
Trap: (C) reclassifies by who ran the test rather than by what the finding proves. A serum test run in a clinic is also probable — the class is set by the possibility of another cause, not by the setting.

Q: A woman at 18 weeks reports feeling fluttering movements, and at her next visit the examiner palpates fetal movement. How are these two findings classified?
A) Both are presumptive
B) Both are positive
C) Quickening felt by the woman is presumptive; fetal movement palpated by the examiner is positive
D) Quickening felt by the woman is probable; fetal movement palpated by the examiner is positive
Answer: C
Rationale: Quickening at 16 to 20 weeks is a presumptive sign whose other possible causes are gas and peristalsis. Fetal movements palpated by an examiner at 19 to 22 weeks is a positive sign with no other causes.
Trap: (A) treats the phenomenon as fixed and the class as following from it. The same movement changes category depending on who perceives it, which is the rule this chapter is built on.

Subtopic: the probable signs by name

Q: An examiner documents softening of the cervix at 5 weeks in one woman, a bluish-purple discoloration of the vulva and vagina in another, and softening of the lower uterine segment in a third. Which signs are these?
A) Goodell, Chadwick, and Hegar
B) Chadwick, Goodell, and Hegar
C) Hegar, Chadwick, and Goodell
D) Goodell, Hegar, and Chadwick
Answer: A
Rationale: Goodell sign is cervical softening at about 5 weeks, Chadwick sign is the vulvar and vaginal color change at 6 to 8 weeks, and Hegar sign is softening of the lower uterine segment at 6 to 12 weeks. All three are probable signs, and all three list pelvic congestion as another possible cause.
Trap: (C) reverses the first and third, which is the usual mix-up because both are described as softening. The exam's own wording is the cue: "assessed by observing the cervix" is Goodell, "softening of the lower uterine segment" is Hegar.

Topic: estimating the date of birth
Subtopic: applying the naegele rule

Q: A woman's last menstrual period began on December 10, 2022. Using the Naegele rule, what is her estimated date of birth?
A) September 17, 2023
B) September 3, 2023
C) March 17, 2023
D) September 17, 2022
Answer: A
Rationale: Box 14.1's own worked example uses these dates: subtract 3 from the month, add 7 to the day, add 1 to the year. December 10, 2022 becomes September 17, 2023.
Trap: (B) performs the month subtraction and forgets the 7 days, which is the most common arithmetic slip. Fig. 14.1's caption gives a second pair to check against: a last period beginning February 21, 2022 gives an estimated date of birth of November 29, 2022.

Q: A woman with irregular cycles ranging from 35 to 45 days asks how reliable her Naegele-rule due date is. What assumption limits it?
A) It assumes a 28-day cycle with fertilization on the 14th day
B) It assumes conception occurred on the first day of the last menstrual period
C) It assumes a 30-day cycle with fertilization on the 16th day
D) It assumes the woman has been pregnant before
Answer: A
Rationale: "It assumes that the woman has a 28-day cycle and that fertilization occurred on the 14th day." A woman whose cycles run longer ovulates later, so the rule dates her pregnancy as further along than it is.
Trap: (B) confuses the starting point of the calculation with the assumed timing of conception. The rule counts from the last menstrual period precisely because conception is assumed to follow it by 14 days.

Subtopic: how accurate the estimate is

Q: Which method does the chapter identify as the most accurate assessment of the estimated date of birth?
A) The Naegele rule applied to an accurately recalled last menstrual period
B) Ultrasound measurement of the embryo or fetus during the first trimester
C) A gestational wheel
D) Fundal height measured at 20 weeks
Answer: B
Rationale: "The most accurate assessment of the EDB is based on ultrasound measurement of the embryo or fetus during the first trimester of pregnancy." The estimated date of birth is determined from both the last menstrual period and the first accurate ultrasound examination.
Trap: (A) is the method the chapter teaches in most detail, which is why it gets promoted to most accurate. Teaching emphasis and accuracy are different things here.

Q: A woman reaches her estimated date of birth and has not given birth. What should the nurse teach her about the meaning of that date?
A) Only about 5% of women give birth spontaneously on the estimated date, and most births occur from 7 days before to 7 days after it
B) Most women give birth within 24 hours of the estimated date
C) Passing the estimated date by any amount means the pregnancy is postterm
D) The estimated date is accurate to within 1 day when calculated by the Naegele rule
Answer: A
Rationale: "Only about 5% of women give birth spontaneously on the EDB as determined by the Naegele rule; most births occur within the time period from 7 days before to 7 days after the EDB."
Trap: (C) confuses the estimated date with the postterm boundary, which is 42 0/7 weeks — about 2 weeks past the estimated date, not a day past it.

Topic: gravidity parity and gtpal
Subtopic: what each term counts

Q: A woman pregnant for the second time gave birth to twins at 36 weeks in her first pregnancy; both children are living. What is her gravidity and parity using the 2-digit system?
A) Gravida 2, para 2, because she has given birth to two children
B) Gravida 2, para 1, because parity counts pregnancies reaching 20 weeks, not the number of fetuses born
C) Gravida 3, para 2, because a twin pregnancy counts twice
D) Gravida 2, para 0, because the birth was preterm
Answer: B
Rationale: "Parity: The number of pregnancies in which the fetus or fetuses have reached 20 weeks of gestation or more, not the number of fetuses (e.g., twins) born." Gravida counts pregnancies including the present one, so she is gravida 2, para 1.
Trap: (A) counts babies, which is the error this definition is written against, and (D) treats preterm birth as not counting toward parity — the boundary is 20 weeks, not 37.

Q: A woman at 24 weeks in her third pregnancy had a stillbirth at 22 weeks and a miscarriage at 10 weeks. How does the stillbirth affect her parity?
A) It does not count, because the fetus showed no signs of life
B) It counts toward parity, because parity is not affected by whether the fetus is born alive or is stillborn
C) It counts as an abortion in GTPAL because there is no living child
D) It counts toward parity only if the fetus weighed more than 500 g
Answer: B
Rationale: "Parity is not affected by whether the fetus is born alive or is stillborn (i.e., showing no signs of life at birth)." The 22-week stillbirth reached 20 weeks, so it counts toward parity and belongs in the preterm column of GTPAL; the 10-week loss is an abortion.
Trap: (C) uses the living-children column to decide the preterm column. Those are separate letters in GTPAL, and a pregnancy can count as preterm with no living child to show for it.

Subtopic: the five gtpal columns

Q: A woman is pregnant for the second time. In her first pregnancy she gave birth at 35 weeks, and that child is 3 years old, alive, and well. What is her GTPAL?
A) 2-0-1-0-1
B) 2-1-0-0-1
C) 1-0-1-0-1
D) 2-0-1-1-1
Answer: A
Rationale: This is Box 14.3's own worked example: gravida 2 including this pregnancy, term 0, preterm 1 because 35 weeks falls between 20 0/7 and 36 6/7, abortion 0, living 1.
Trap: (B) files a 35-week birth under term, which is the boundary error GTPAL is built to catch. Term begins at 37 0/7 weeks.

Q: A pregnancy ends at 38 weeks. In which GTPAL column does it belong, and why?
A) The preterm column, because 38 weeks is not full term
B) The term column, because term includes early-term, full-term, late-term, and postterm births at 37 weeks 0 days and beyond
C) The abortion column, because it did not reach 39 weeks
D) It is not counted until the child's status is known
Answer: B
Rationale: Box 14.3 defines the term column as "the number of pregnancies that ended in term births (37 weeks 0 days and beyond; including early-term, full-term, late-term, or postterm births)."
Trap: (A) applies the everyday meaning of full term to the GTPAL column. The frequent error is to reserve the term column for 39 weeks and beyond, when the box explicitly folds all four term categories into it.

Q: Which pregnancies are counted in the abortion column of GTPAL?
A) Only elective terminations
B) Pregnancies ending in miscarriage or elective termination before 20 weeks, or in which the fetus weighed less than 500 g at birth
C) Any pregnancy loss at any gestational age
D) Only pregnancies ending before 12 weeks
Answer: B
Rationale: Box 14.3 defines the abortion column as "the number of pregnancies that ended in miscarriage (spontaneous abortion) or elective termination (therapeutic abortion) before 20 weeks or weighed less than 500 g at birth."
Trap: (C) drops the 20-week boundary, which would move stillbirths out of parity and into this column. The same 20-week line separates abortion from preterm and non-parity from parity.

Topic: gestational age terminology
Subtopic: the ladder from preterm to postterm

Q: A pregnancy reaches 38 weeks 2 days. How is this gestational age classified?
A) Full term
B) Early term
C) Late preterm
D) Term, with no further subdivision
Answer: B
Rationale: The chapter's ladder is exact: early term is 37 weeks 0 days to 38 weeks 6 days, and full term begins at 39 weeks 0 days and runs to 40 weeks 6 days.
Trap: (A) uses the everyday meaning of full term, which covers everything past 37 weeks. A stem offering 38 weeks and the phrase "full term" is using ordinary usage against the book's definition — and this is also the boundary behind the 39-week rule for elective induction.

Q: Match the remaining gestational-age categories to their boundaries. Which statement is correct?
A) Late preterm is 34 weeks 0 days to 36 weeks 6 days; late term is 41 weeks 0 days to 41 weeks 6 days; postterm is 42 weeks 0 days and beyond
B) Late preterm is 32 weeks 0 days to 36 weeks 6 days; late term is 40 weeks; postterm is 41 weeks and beyond
C) Late preterm is 34 to 37 weeks; late term is 41 to 42 weeks; postterm is 43 weeks and beyond
D) Late preterm and late term are the same boundary applied before and after term
Answer: A
Rationale: Preterm is 20 weeks 0 days to before 37 weeks 0 days, late preterm is 34 weeks 0 days to 36 weeks 6 days, late term is 41 weeks 0 days to 41 weeks 6 days, and postterm is 42 weeks 0 days and beyond.
Trap: (C) rounds every boundary to whole weeks, which erases the "0 days" and "6 days" structure that makes these categories non-overlapping.

Q: Parents of an infant born at 23 weeks ask whether their baby was viable. What does the chapter say about viability?
A) Viability begins at 24 weeks 0 days
B) Viability is the capacity to live outside the uterus, with no clear limits of gestational age or weight; infants born at 22 to 25 weeks are considered to be on the threshold of viability
C) Viability begins at 20 weeks, matching the parity boundary
D) Viability is defined by a birth weight of 500 g
Answer: B
Rationale: "Viability: The capacity to live outside the uterus; there are no clear limits of gestational age or weight. Infants born at 22–25 weeks of gestation are considered to be on the threshold of viability and are especially vulnerable to brain injury if they survive."
Trap: (C) borrows the 20-week line that governs parity and applies it to viability. The two boundaries are near each other and answer different questions — one is a counting rule, the other a clinical threshold with no fixed value.

Topic: the prenatal interview and psychosocial screening
Subtopic: conducting the interview

Q: A nurse begins an initial prenatal interview. Which approach does the chapter recommend for eliciting the woman's concerns?
A) A checklist of closed questions to ensure nothing is missed
B) A broad open-ended question such as "How have you been feeling?", with the woman's chief concerns recorded in her own words
C) Questions directed to the woman's partner to reduce her anxiety
D) Deferring concerns to the follow-up visit, since the first visit is already long
Answer: B
Rationale: "When the woman is asked a broad open-ended question such as, 'How have you been feeling?' she may reveal problems that could otherwise be overlooked. The woman's chief concerns should be recorded in her own words to alert other personnel to the priority of needs as she identifies them."
Trap: (A) is efficient and is exactly what the chapter says misses problems. Recording her words verbatim is what carries her priorities to the rest of the team.

Q: What should the nurse tell a woman to expect at her first prenatal visit, and what does that visit include?
A) That it is longer and more detailed than future visits, including a comprehensive health history, physical assessment, diagnostic testing, and an overall risk assessment
B) That it is brief, with the detailed history deferred to the second trimester
C) That it consists only of laboratory testing
D) That family members may not be present at any point
Answer: A
Rationale: "The first prenatal visit is longer and more detailed than future visits," and the initial evaluation includes "a comprehensive health history emphasizing the current pregnancy, previous pregnancies, the family, a psychosocial profile, a physical assessment, diagnostic testing, and an overall risk assessment." Family members are included with the woman's permission.
Trap: (D) removes a choice that belongs to the woman. Her permission is the condition, not a prohibition — and the private-setting rule for violence screening is a separate matter handled in its own item.

Q: A woman's surgical history includes a spinal fusion and an appendectomy. Why does the chapter say surgical history matters in pregnancy?
A) Spinal surgery may contraindicate the use of spinal or epidural anesthesia, and appendectomy rules out appendicitis as a cause of right lower quadrant pain
B) Only uterine surgery is relevant to pregnancy
C) Prior surgery of any kind requires cesarean birth
D) Surgical history is collected for billing purposes only
Answer: A
Rationale: The chapter spells out the reasons: uterine surgery or extensive pelvic-floor repair may mean "a cesarean birth may be necessary," "appendectomy rules out appendicitis as a cause of right lower quadrant pain in pregnancy," and "spinal surgery may contraindicate the use of spinal or epidural anesthesia."
Trap: (C) generalizes the one surgical finding that can lead to cesarean birth — uterine surgery — to every operation the woman has ever had.

Subtopic: screening for violence and mood disorders

Q: A woman arrives for her first prenatal visit with her partner, who stays at her side throughout. How should the nurse conduct intimate partner violence screening?
A) Screen her with the partner present, since his responses may be informative
B) Arrange to screen her in a safe, private setting with the woman alone
C) Omit the screening and document that the partner was present
D) Give her a written questionnaire to complete in the waiting room with her partner
Answer: B
Rationale: "It is essential that the screening be done in a safe, private setting with the woman alone." Intimate partner violence "occurs in as many as 20% of pregnancies," and May's announcement phase notes that it "may occur for the first time during pregnancy or increase in frequency."
Trap: (A) and (D) both perform the motions of screening in a setting that makes an honest answer unsafe, which is why the chapter states the condition rather than assuming it.

Q: At minimum, when should pregnant women be screened for intimate partner violence?
A) At the first prenatal visit only
B) At the first prenatal visit, at least once every trimester, and at the postpartum visit
C) Once, at any point in pregnancy
D) Only when physical findings suggest injury
Answer: B
Rationale: "At a minimum, pregnant women should be screened for IPV at the first prenatal visit, at least once every trimester, and at the postpartum visit." In practice, "nurses typically assess for IPV at every prenatal visit during routine interviewing and physical examination."
Trap: (D) makes screening contingent on suspicion, which defeats the purpose of a universal schedule and misses the women whose injuries are not visible.

Q: What does the chapter say about perinatal depression and its screening?
A) It is uncommon and screening is optional
B) It is the most common complication of pregnancy, and screening for depression and anxiety symptoms is recommended at least once during the perinatal period using a validated instrument such as the PHQ-9 or the Edinburgh Postnatal Depression Scale
C) It should be screened for only after birth
D) Women reliably raise mental health concerns on their own, so screening adds little
Answer: B
Rationale: "Perinatal depression is the most common complication of pregnancy; if untreated, it can have serious adverse effects on the mother, her newborn, and her family." ACOG recommends screening "at least once during the perinatal period" with a standardized validated instrument.
Trap: (D) reverses the chapter's nursing insight: "women are usually unlikely to initiate discussions with health care professionals related to mental health concerns, but they may be more willing to disclose issues and concerns when they are informed that the screening is part of routine prenatal care."

Topic: immunizations in pregnancy
Subtopic: the live-virus rule

Q: A woman at 12 weeks is found to be nonimmune to rubella and varicella. When should she be vaccinated?
A) Immediately, since nonimmunity poses a fetal risk
B) As part of postpartum care, because immunization with live or attenuated live viruses is contraindicated during pregnancy due to potential teratogenicity
C) In the third trimester, when organogenesis is complete
D) Never, since these vaccines are contraindicated in women of childbearing age
Answer: B
Rationale: "Immunization with live or attenuated live viruses is contraindicated during pregnancy because of potential teratogenicity; recommended vaccination with these agents should be part of postpartum care." The live-virus vaccines named are measles (rubeola and rubella), varicella, mumps, and some influenza vaccines.
Trap: (C) treats the first trimester as the only vulnerable window. The contraindication runs through the whole pregnancy, and the postpartum visit is where these vaccines are given.

Q: Which vaccines may be given during pregnancy? Select all that apply.
A) Combined tetanus-diphtheria-acellular pertussis
B) Recombinant hepatitis B
C) COVID-19
D) Inactivated injectable influenza
E) Varicella
Answer: A, B, C, D
Rationale: The vaccines that can be given during pregnancy are Tdap, recombinant hepatitis B, COVID-19, and inactivated injectable influenza. ACOG, the CDC, and SMFM "agree that the benefits of COVID-19 vaccination outweigh any risks."
Trap: (E) is a live-virus vaccine and belongs to the postpartum list. Influenza is the one that appears on both lists and must be sorted by formulation: the injectable inactivated vaccine "is safe throughout pregnancy," while some influenza vaccines are live.

Subtopic: tdap timing and cocooning

Q: A woman who received Tdap during her last pregnancy 2 years ago asks whether she needs it again. What should the nurse tell her?
A) No, because immunity from the previous dose persists
B) Yes, because maternal antipertussis antibodies are short-lived, so Tdap is administered during each pregnancy regardless of prior vaccination history
C) Yes, but only if her partner is unvaccinated
D) No, unless she has had a known pertussis exposure
Answer: B
Rationale: "Maternal antipertussis antibodies are short-lived and antibody levels drop significantly during the first year after vaccination. As a result, it is unlikely that a Tdap vaccination in one pregnancy will transfer passive immunity from mother to infant in a subsequent pregnancy. Therefore the recommendation is to administer Tdap to a pregnant woman during each pregnancy regardless of her prior vaccination history."
Trap: (A) applies adult-booster logic to a strategy whose purpose is passive transfer to this infant. Her own protection is not the point of the dose.

Q: At what gestational age should Tdap ideally be given, and why?
A) Between 27 and 36 weeks, to provide a maximal maternal antibody response and transfer passive immunity to the infant
B) At the first prenatal visit, to protect the woman throughout pregnancy
C) Between 20 and 24 weeks, before the third trimester
D) Immediately postpartum in every case
Answer: A
Rationale: "To provide a maximal maternal antibody response and transfer passive immunity to the infant, Tdap should ideally be administered between 27 and 36 weeks of gestation." The chapter adds: "If Tdap is not administered during pregnancy, it should be given immediately postpartum."
Trap: (D) names the fallback and treats it as the plan. Postpartum administration protects the mother and future contacts but forfeits the passive transfer the timing window exists to achieve.

Q: A grandmother who will be caring for a newborn asks whether she needs anything before the baby comes home. What does the Safety Alert direct?
A) No vaccination is needed for household contacts
B) Adolescents and adults who will have close contact with an infant under 12 months and are not up to date with pertussis vaccination should receive a single dose of Tdap at least 2 weeks before contact with the infant
C) Household contacts should receive Tdap on the day of the birth
D) Only health care personnel need pertussis vaccination
Answer: B
Rationale: The Safety Alert states the cocooning rule: "Adolescents and adults (parents, grandparents, siblings, childcare workers, and health care personnel) who will have close contact with an infant less than 12 months of age and are not up to date with pertussis vaccination should receive a single dose of Tdap at least 2 weeks before contact with the infant." The chapter adds that everyone providing care to the infant should also receive the current influenza vaccine.
Trap: (C) gets the vaccine right and the timing wrong, which leaves the contact unprotected during the window that matters — the 2-week interval is what makes the dose useful.

Topic: rh immune globulin schedule
Subtopic: the routine doses

Q: An Rh-negative woman with a negative antibody screen is at 28 weeks. What does the chapter direct?
A) A dose of 300 mcg of Rh immune globulin, which is routinely administered at 26 to 30 weeks to all Rh-negative women without evidence of anti-D alloimmunization
B) No treatment unless the fetus is known to be Rh positive
C) A dose of 300 mcg only after birth
D) A dose at 36 weeks, closer to the anticipated birth
Answer: A
Rationale: "A dose of 300 mcg of Rh immune globulin is routinely administered at 26 to 30 weeks to all Rh-negative women without evidence of anti-D alloimmunization." Blood type is determined at the first prenatal visit, and Rh-negative women have an antibody screen drawn in the first and third trimesters.
Trap: (B) waits for information the antenatal dose does not require. The point is to prevent antibody formation before the immune system encounters the D antigen, which is why the dose is routine rather than conditional.

Q: The same woman gives birth to an Rh-negative infant. What does the chapter direct regarding the postpartum dose?
A) Repeat the 300 mcg dose within 72 hours after birth regardless of the infant's blood type
B) The repeat dose is indicated if the infant is Rh positive, so it is not given for an Rh-negative infant
C) Repeat the dose within 1 week after birth
D) Give a double dose because the antenatal dose has been cleared
Answer: B
Rationale: "If the woman gives birth to an Rh-positive infant, the dose of Rh immune globulin is repeated within 72 hours after birth." The infant's type is what triggers the postpartum dose.
Trap: (A) is the plausible-but-wrong option this pair exists to catch — the antenatal dose is unconditional and the postpartum dose is not, and holding both as unconditional is the usual simplification.

Q: Besides the routine antenatal and postpartum doses, which situations does the chapter list as indications for Rh immune globulin? Select all that apply.
A) Chorionic villus sampling and amniocentesis
B) Miscarriage and induced abortion
C) Ectopic pregnancy
D) External cephalic version and abdominal trauma
E) Every prenatal visit in the third trimester
Answer: A, B, C, D
Rationale: The listed indications are "chorionic villus sampling, amniocentesis, miscarriage, induced abortion, ectopic pregnancy, external cephalic version, and abdominal trauma." Each is an event that can introduce fetal red blood cells into the maternal circulation.
Trap: (E) turns a set of exposure events into a schedule. What the indications share is a breach that mixes fetal and maternal blood, not a point in time.

Topic: signs of potential complications by trimester
Subtopic: first trimester

Q: A woman at 9 weeks reports abdominal cramping with vaginal bleeding. According to Table 14.4, what are the possible causes?
A) Miscarriage or ectopic pregnancy
B) Placenta previa or placental abruption
C) Preterm labor
D) Normal implantation bleeding requiring no evaluation
Answer: A
Rationale: Table 14.4's first-trimester block pairs "abdominal cramping, vaginal bleeding" with "miscarriage, ectopic pregnancy."
Trap: (B) names the second- and third-trimester causes of the same two symptoms. Gestational age changes the differential, which is why this table is split by trimester rather than combined.

Q: Which findings appear in Table 14.4's first-trimester block? Select all that apply.
A) Severe vomiting, suggesting hyperemesis gravidarum
B) Chills and fever, suggesting infection
C) Burning on urination, suggesting infection
D) Abdominal cramping with vaginal bleeding
E) Sudden discharge of fluid from the vagina before 37 weeks
Answer: A, B, C, D
Rationale: The first-trimester rows are severe vomiting (hyperemesis gravidarum), chills and fever (infection), burning on urination (infection), diarrhea (infection), and abdominal cramping with vaginal bleeding (miscarriage, ectopic pregnancy).
Trap: (E) belongs to the second and third trimesters, where it signals preterm prelabor rupture of membranes. The sign is only meaningful once there is a 37-week deadline to be before.

Subtopic: the preeclampsia cluster

Q: A woman at 32 weeks reports a severe continuous headache, blurred vision with spots, and swelling of her face and fingers. What do these findings suggest, and what other symptom belongs with them?
A) Hypertensive conditions and preeclampsia; epigastric or abdominal pain, which the woman may describe as heartburn or a severe stomachache
B) Normal third-trimester discomforts; heartburn belongs with them as a normal finding
C) Kidney infection; flank pain belongs with them
D) Gestational diabetes; glycosuria belongs with them
Answer: A
Rationale: Table 14.4 groups five rows under hypertensive conditions and preeclampsia: visual disturbances, swelling of the face or fingers and over the sacrum, severe or continuous headaches, muscular irritability or seizures, and epigastric or abdominal pain "perceived as heartburn or severe stomachache."
Trap: (B) treats the cluster as ordinary late-pregnancy complaints, which is precisely how the epigastric row hides. The same row also names placental abruption as a possible cause.

Q: A woman at 34 weeks calls to report what she describes as bad heartburn and a severe stomachache that will not go away. What should the nurse recognize?
A) That this is a normal discomfort of the third trimester and antacids may be suggested
B) That epigastric or abdominal pain is a sign of potential complications associated with hypertensive conditions, preeclampsia, and placental abruption, and she should be evaluated
C) That this indicates gestational diabetes
D) That this is an expected effect of fetal position
Answer: B
Rationale: Table 14.4 lists "epigastric or abdominal pain (perceived as heartburn or severe stomachache)" with possible causes "hypertensive conditions, preeclampsia; placental abruption." The table's own wording anticipates that the woman will report it as a digestive complaint.
Trap: (A) is the reflex response to the words the woman uses, and it is the reason this row is worth drilling on its own. The chapter's language is the trap, and recognizing it is the skill.

Subtopic: fetal movement and what must be discussed

Q: A woman at 30 weeks reports that her baby has not moved as much as usual today and the pattern feels different. How should the nurse prioritize this report?
A) As low priority, since fetal movement varies normally
B) As high priority, because a change in fetal movements maps to fetal jeopardy or intrauterine fetal death
C) As a sign of impending labor
D) As a normal finding once quickening has been established
Answer: B
Rationale: Table 14.4 pairs "change in fetal movements: absence of fetal movements after quickening, any unusual change in pattern or amount of fetal movements" with "fetal jeopardy or intrauterine fetal death." That ranks it above nearly any comfort or teaching option in a prioritization stem.
Trap: (D) uses quickening as reassurance when the table uses it as the reference point: it is change after quickening that matters.

Q: Which signs does the chapter say must be discussed with the woman beyond handing her the printed list of warning signs? Select all that apply.
A) Vaginal bleeding
B) Alteration in fetal movements
C) Symptoms of preeclampsia
D) Rupture of membranes and preterm labor
E) Constipation and heartburn
Answer: A, B, C, D
Rationale: "These include vaginal bleeding, alteration in fetal movements, symptoms of preeclampsia, rupture of membranes, and preterm labor." The woman and family should also receive a printed list "written at the appropriate literacy level and in their language," with phone numbers for the provider or clinic.
Trap: (E) names discomforts from Table 14.3, which is a different table with a different purpose — self-management rather than warning signs.

Topic: maternal and partner adaptation to pregnancy
Subtopic: the woman's three phases

Q: A woman at 22 weeks has begun referring to the fetus by a nickname and talking about what the baby will be like. Which phase of accepting the pregnancy does this represent?
A) Phase 1, "I am pregnant," in which the child is viewed as part of herself
B) Phase 2, "I am going to have a baby," in which she accepts the growing fetus as distinct from herself, usually by the fifth month
C) Phase 3, "I am going to be a mother"
D) The moratorium phase
Answer: B
Rationale: In phase 2 "the woman accepts the growing fetus as distinct from herself. This is usually accomplished by the fifth month," and attachment "increases when ultrasound examination and quickening confirm the reality of the fetus."
Trap: (D) borrows a label from May's phases of the expectant father. The two sets overlap in sequence by design, which is why a distractor pairs one set's label with the other set's task.

Q: A woman at 24 weeks has become quiet and introspective, and her partner says he feels left out. How should the nurse interpret this?
A) As a sign of perinatal depression requiring immediate referral
B) As the expected introspection of phase 2, in which she withdraws and concentrates on the unborn child, and partners sometimes feel left out
C) As evidence of an unwanted pregnancy
D) As a sign that the couple's relationship is failing
Answer: B
Rationale: In phase 2 the woman "enters a quiet period and becomes more introspective," and "as she seems to withdraw and concentrate her interest on the unborn child, her partner sometimes feels left out." Other children "can become more demanding in their efforts to redirect the mother's attention to themselves."
Trap: (A) pathologizes a described normal phase. Perinatal depression is real and common, and it is screened for with a validated instrument rather than inferred from expected introspection.

Subtopic: the partner's phases

Q: An expectant father has become introspective and is talking at length about his philosophy of life, his religion, and his relationship with his own father. Which of May's phases does this represent?
A) Announcement
B) Moratorium
C) Focusing
D) Attachment
Answer: B
Rationale: In the moratorium phase the developmental task is "to accept the reality of pregnancy," and men "often become more introspective and engage in many discussions about their philosophy of life, religion, childbearing and child-rearing practices, and their relationships with family members, particularly with their own fathers."
Trap: (C) is the phase in which the man negotiates his role in labor and begins to think of himself as a father, which sounds like the culmination of this kind of reflection but is the phase after it. The announcement phase is where ambivalence is common and where intimate partner violence may first appear.

Q: A partner responds to the pregnancy by taking on extra work and spending more time away from home. How does the chapter frame this response?
A) As abnormal and requiring counseling referral
B) As one of a wide range of normal responses, which can include nurturing behavior at one end and loneliness or alienation at the other, with most men using the time to prepare for the parental role
C) As evidence that the pregnancy is unwanted
D) As a response seen only in first-time fathers
Answer: B
Rationale: The chapter describes the range without pathologizing it: "One man may engage in nurturing behavior, whereas another may feel lonely and alienated as the woman becomes physically and emotionally engrossed in the unborn child," and he may seek relationships outside the home, a new hobby, or absorption in work. "However, for most men, pregnancy is a time of preparation for the parental role and for intense learning."
Trap: (A) converts a described variation into a diagnosis. The chapter also states that these same positive and negative feelings exist in partners in LGBTQIA relationships.

Topic: multifetal pregnancy care and risks
Subtopic: risks of a multiple gestation

Q: Which risks does the chapter associate with multifetal gestation? Select all that apply.
A) Miscarriage, hyperemesis, and anemia
B) Gestational hypertension and preeclampsia
C) Postpartum hemorrhage and maternal death
D) Preterm birth, with risk rising as the number of fetuses increases
E) A reduced risk of congenital abnormalities
Answer: A, B, C, D
Rationale: Multifetal gestation increases the risk for "miscarriage, hyperemesis, anemia, gestational hypertension, preeclampsia, postpartum hemorrhage, and maternal death," and "preterm birth is more likely with multifetal gestation; the risk rises as the number of fetuses increases." Fetal and neonatal risks include growth restriction or discordant growth, low and very low birth weight, congenital abnormalities, neonatal death, and cerebral palsy.
Trap: (E) reverses one of the listed fetal risks. Congenital abnormalities are more common in multiples, not less.

Q: Twin-to-twin transfusion is identified. Which twin does the chapter say may develop congestive heart failure in the first 24 hours after birth?
A) The donor twin, who is small, pallid, dehydrated, and hypovolemic
B) The larger recipient twin
C) Neither; the complication occurs before birth
D) Both twins equally
Answer: B
Rationale: "There can be local shunting of blood between placentas (twin-to-twin transfusion); this causes the recipient twin to be larger and the donor twin to be small, pallid, dehydrated, malnourished, and hypovolemic." And: "the larger twin can develop congestive heart failure during the first 24 hours after birth."
Trap: (A) is the twin who looks sicker at birth, which is what makes this counterintuitive. Being the bigger twin is not being the safer twin.

Subtopic: prenatal care for a multiple gestation

Q: A woman with a twin pregnancy and no risk factors asks whether she must abstain from sexual activity. What does the chapter direct?
A) Abstinence from intercourse is recommended for all multiple gestations
B) Abstinence from orgasm and nipple stimulation in the last trimester is recommended only if risk factors such as premature dilation of the cervix or bleeding are present
C) No restriction applies at any time in a multifetal pregnancy
D) Abstinence is recommended from the second trimester onward
Answer: B
Rationale: The restriction is conditional: "If risk factors such as premature dilation of the cervix or bleeding are present, abstinence from orgasm and nipple stimulation during the last trimester is recommended to help avert preterm labor."
Trap: (A) over-reads the text by applying a conditional restriction to every multiple gestation, which is the more common error because multiples already feel high risk.

Q: How does prenatal care change for a woman with a multifetal pregnancy? Select all that apply.
A) Prenatal visits are scheduled more frequently
B) Frequent ultrasound examinations, nonstress tests, and fetal heart rate monitoring are performed
C) Teaching covers the risk of preterm labor and birth, including warning signs and when to call the provider
D) Maternal support hose may be worn to control leg varicosities, and backache commonly worsens with uterine distention
E) Caloric intake is restricted to limit fetal growth
Answer: A, B, C, D
Rationale: The chapter describes more frequent visits, frequent ultrasound examinations, nonstress tests and FHR monitoring, teaching on maternal adaptations, discomforts, and the risk of preterm labor with warning signs, and comfort measures for worsened backache and leg varicosities.
Trap: (E) reverses the nutritional guidance: the woman should consume "a well-balanced diet with adequate caloric intake" and gain weight appropriately. Multiple newborns also "place a strain on finances, space, workload, and coping capabilities," and Multiples of America is the referral the chapter names.
