# Chapter 22 drills — Transition to Parenthood

Authored from `sources/Ch22_Fact_Bank.md` (Lowdermilk, Cashion, Alden, Olshansky & Perry, 13th ed., ch. 22, pp. 441–457).

Blueprint weight is **~6 questions of 200 (3.0%), depth `standard`** — topic
*Bonding, Attachment, Social Influences*. The Week 1–4 midterm reading guide does
not name this chapter, so the midterm does not reach it; the blueprint weights it
at six, so the final does. Scope comes from the blueprint alone, and its one
compound topic maps onto three parts of the chapter: bonding and attachment, the
interaction processes that produce them, and the social influences on the
transition to parenthood. That earns **44 drills across 10 topics**, near the top of
the `standard` band.

The organizing insight the fact bank names is the spine of this set: **bonding
and attachment are not the same word, and the whole chapter turns on the
difference.** Bonding is what the parent feels, it starts in pregnancy, and it is
observable in the first hours and days. Attachment is a two-way relationship the
infant forms over the first year, and its quality "is not typically observable
before 9 months of age." Everything else — reciprocity, synchrony, serve and
return — describes how the first turns into the second.

Every distinction the chapter states in both directions is drilled from both
ends: bonding and attachment, signaling and executive behaviors, facilitating and
inhibiting behaviors in both tables, reciprocity and synchrony, infantilizing
speech and baby talk, and general versus situation-specific support.

**A cultural caution runs through the whole chapter and through these questions.**
The book states twice that "incorrect judgments may be made about parent-infant
bonding if nurses do not practice culturally sensitive care," and it builds the
caveat into Box 22.1 itself. Naming, eye contact, and touch all vary by culture,
and several items here turn on that.

**What is left out.** The diversity sections on sexual and gender minority
parents, adolescent parents, and midlife parenting; parental sensory impairment;
the extended Becoming a Father material; and the Community Focus box.

---

Topic: bonding versus attachment
Subtopic: the two definitions

Q: A nurse documents that a 2-day-old infant has "good attachment" with the mother. Why is this the wrong term?
A) Because attachment is a two-way interaction that develops during the first year of the infant's life, and the quality of attachment is not typically observable before 9 months of age
B) Because attachment applies only to fathers
C) Because attachment cannot be documented by nurses
D) Because attachment and bonding are identical terms
Answer: A
Rationale: "Attachment is a two-way interaction between the mother (or primary caregiver) and the infant that develops during the first year of the infant's life," and "the quality of attachment is not typically observable before 9 months of age." What is observable in the first days is bonding: "bonding behaviors may be observed in the early moments, days, weeks, and months after birth."
Trap: (D) is the usage the chapter opens by correcting: "the term bonding should be considered separately from the term attachment, as they refer to different but related processes."

Q: When does bonding begin, and how does it relate to attachment developmentally?
A) At birth, and it follows attachment
B) During pregnancy, continuing to develop into childhood; parent-infant bonding precedes attachment developmentally
C) At 9 months, along with attachment
D) Only once the infant can respond socially
Answer: B
Rationale: Bonding is "the emotions and feelings experienced by the mother (or parent) in relation to the infant," and "it begins during pregnancy and continues to develop into childhood." The chapter states the order: "Parent-infant bonding precedes attachment developmentally."
Trap: (A) starts the process at birth and reverses the order, which would make prenatal attachment-promoting care look pointless.

Subtopic: mutuality and the infant's contribution

Q: An infant cries, and the parent comes to the crib; the infant then roots and grasps the parent's finger. Which behaviors are these, and what does each accomplish?
A) Crying is a signaling behavior that initiates contact and brings the caregiver to the child; rooting and grasping are executive behaviors that maintain the contact
B) Crying is an executive behavior; rooting is a signaling behavior
C) Both are signaling behaviors
D) Both are reflexes with no role in bonding
Answer: A
Rationale: "The infant displays signaling behaviors such as crying, smiling, and cooing that initiate the contact and bring the caregiver to the child," and "executive behaviors such as rooting, grasping, and postural adjustments that maintain the contact."
Trap: (B) reverses the pair. The cue that survives is that signaling calls the parent over and executive behavior holds on once they arrive.

Q: What does the chapter say about how an infant's own characteristics affect bonding?
A) Infant characteristics have no effect
B) The infant's behaviors and characteristics elicit a corresponding set of parental behaviors, and bonding occurs more readily with an infant whose temperament, social capabilities, appearance, and sex fit the parent's expectations
C) Only the parent's characteristics matter
D) Irritable infants bond more strongly because they demand more care
Answer: B
Rationale: "The concept of bonding includes mutuality; that is, the infant's behaviors and characteristics elicit a corresponding set of parental behaviors and characteristics." And plainly: "Most caregivers are attracted to an alert, responsive, cuddly infant but find it less desirable to interact with an irritable, apparently disinterested infant. Bonding occurs more readily with the infant whose temperament, social capabilities, appearance, and sex fit the parent's expectations."
Trap: (A) makes bonding entirely a parental achievement, which both misplaces responsibility and misses why an irritable or sedated infant is a bonding risk factor.

Topic: infant and parental bonding behaviors
Subtopic: what facilitates and what inhibits

Q: Which infant behaviors does Table 22.1 list as facilitating bonding? Select all that apply.
A) Visually alert, with eye-to-eye contact and tracking of the parent's face
B) Smiling, and vocalizing with crying only when hungry or wet
C) Enjoying being cuddled and held, and being easily consolable
D) Activity and regularity that are somewhat predictable
E) Gaze aversion with eyes closed most of the time
Answer: A, B, C, D
Rationale: Table 22.1's facilitating column includes visual alertness and eye contact, an appealing facial appearance, smiles, vocalization with limited crying, the grasp reflex, feeding well, enjoying being held, easy consolability, predictable activity, sufficient attention span, and preference for the parents.
Trap: (E) is from the inhibiting column, along with hyperirritability, a bland facial expression, colicky crying for hours, resisting holding by stiffening, and being inconsolable.

Q: Which parental behaviors does Table 22.2 list as inhibiting bonding? Select all that apply.
A) Turning away from the infant or ignoring the infant's presence
B) Identifying the infant with someone the parent dislikes, or failing to recognize the infant's unique features
C) Failing to place the infant in a family context or having difficulty naming the infant
D) Viewing the infant's behavior as exploiting or deliberately uncooperative, and the appearance as distasteful
E) Assigning meaning to the infant's actions and sensitively interpreting the infant's needs
Answer: A, B, C, D
Rationale: Table 22.2's inhibiting column includes each of these, plus avoiding proximity, refusing to hold the infant, maintaining a bland countenance or frowning, expressing disappointment, waking a sleeping infant, handling roughly, and hurrying feedings.
Trap: (E) is squarely in the facilitating column — assigning meaning and interpreting needs is what synchrony is built from.

Subtopic: the touch progression

Q: A mother has been touching her newborn only with her fingertips for the first several minutes after birth. How should the nurse interpret this?
A) As an inhibiting behavior requiring intervention
B) As the normal beginning of the touch progression — fingertip to fingers to palms to encompassing contact — since the mother begins exploration with her fingertips, one of the most touch-sensitive areas of the body
C) As evidence of postpartum depression
D) As a cultural practice requiring no assessment
Answer: B
Rationale: Table 22.2 describes the facilitating progression: "touches; progresses from fingertip to fingers to palms to encompassing contact." The prose confirms that the mother "begins the exploration process with her fingertips, one of the most touch-sensitive areas of the body. Within a short time, she uses her palm to caress the infant's trunk and eventually enfolds the infant."
Trap: (A) misreads the first step of a normal sequence. The inhibiting version is in the same table row and is defined by failure to advance: "fails to move from fingertip touch to palmar contact and holding."

Q: Which sensory and comfort behaviors does the chapter describe parents using with a newborn, and what cultural variation does it name?
A) Gentle stroking to soothe and quiet, and patting or gently rubbing the back after feedings; minimal touching and cuddling is a traditional Southeast Asian practice thought to protect the infant from evil spirits, while women in India and Bali have practiced infant massage since ancient times
B) Vigorous rubbing is preferred in all cultures
C) Touch practices are universal and do not vary
D) Infant massage is discouraged in all cultures
Answer: A
Rationale: "Gentle stroking motions are used to soothe and quiet the infant; patting or gently rubbing the infant's back is a comfort after feedings." The chapter names both cultural variations explicitly.
Trap: (C) is the assumption behind misjudging a family whose touch practices differ, which the chapter warns about by name: "incorrect judgments may be made about parent-infant bonding if nurses do not practice culturally sensitive care."

Topic: acquaintance and claiming
Subtopic: how the infant is claimed

Q: In what order does the claiming process proceed?
A) Uniqueness first, then differences, then likeness
B) The child is first identified in terms of likeness to other family members, then in terms of differences, and finally in terms of uniqueness
C) Differences first, then likeness
D) There is no established order
Answer: B
Rationale: "The child is first identified in terms of likeness to other family members, then in terms of differences, and finally in terms of uniqueness. The unique infant newcomer is thus incorporated into the family." Parents become acquainted through "eye contact, touching, talking, and exploring."
Trap: (A) reverses the sequence, and the order matters because a family still at the likeness stage on day 1 is doing exactly what the chapter describes.

Q: Can adoptive parents engage in the claiming process?
A) No, because claiming depends on biologic likeness
B) Yes — claiming can be accomplished through things other than biologic likeness, and adoptive parents or parents not biologically related to the child may also engage in it, undergoing the same acquaintance process when they first meet their child
C) Only after a year of placement
D) Only if the child resembles them
Answer: B
Rationale: "Claiming can be accomplished through things other than biologic likeness, and therefore adoptive parents or parents who are, for other reasons, not biologically related to the child may also engage in this claiming process." And: "Adoptive parents undergo the same process when they first meet their new child."
Trap: (D) reads the first step of claiming — identifying likeness — as a requirement rather than one route among several.

Subtopic: when claiming goes wrong

Q: A mother says her newborn "cries just to spite me" and "has been nothing but pain since the day he was born," and she holds him at a distance. How should the nurse interpret this?
A) As normal postpartum fatigue
B) As negative claiming: some parents claim the infant in terms of the discomfort or pain the infant causes, interpret the infant's normal responses as being negative toward them, and react with dislike or indifference
C) As an expected first-day response in all mothers
D) As a cultural practice
Answer: B
Rationale: "Some parents react negatively to their newborns. They claim the infant in terms of the discomfort or pain the infant causes. A parent might interpret the infant's normal responses as being negative toward the parents, and the parent may react to the child with dislike or indifference, not holding the child close or touching the child to be comforting."
Trap: (A) is the explanation that lets an inhibiting pattern pass unremarked, and fatigue is a real risk factor for it — but the behavior described is an assessment finding in its own right.

Q: What is the fantasy infant, and why does it matter?
A) An imaginary sibling the older child invents
B) The image of the ideal infant that parents develop during pregnancy and often before conception; at birth the fantasy infant becomes the real infant, and how closely the dream child resembles the real child influences the bonding process
C) A term for the infant's own developing self-image
D) The infant as described on ultrasound
Answer: B
Rationale: "During pregnancy and often even before conception, parents develop an image of the ideal or fantasy infant. At birth, the fantasy infant becomes the real infant. How closely the dream child resembles the real child influences the bonding process." A parental task is to "reconcile the actual child with the fantasy and dream child," and "if the real child differs greatly from the fantasy child, parents may delay the acceptance of the child."
Trap: (D) narrows a psychological construct to an image on a screen, and misses why an infant's sex, temperament, or appearance can slow acceptance.

Topic: assessing bonding
Subtopic: how the assessment is done

Q: How does the chapter contrast assessment of parent-infant bonding with physical assessment of the neonate?
A) Bonding assessment has more concrete guidelines
B) In contrast to physical assessment, which has concrete guidelines to follow, assessment of parent-infant bonding relies more on skillful observation and interviewing
C) Bonding assessment is performed only by social workers
D) Bonding cannot be assessed at all
Answer: B
Rationale: "In contrast to physical assessment of the neonate, which has concrete guidelines to follow, assessment of parent-infant bonding relies more on skillful observation and interviewing." Rooming-in and liberal visiting provide the opportunities, and "bonding behaviors can be easily observed during infant feeding sessions."
Trap: (C) hands off an assessment the chapter assigns to the nurse — and the feeding session, which the nurse is present for, is the named observation window.

Q: Which observations does Box 22.1 direct the nurse to make when assessing bonding? Select all that apply.
A) Whether the parents reach out for the infant and call the infant by name when the infant is brought to them
B) Whether they speak about the infant in terms of who the infant resembles and what appears special about their infant
C) What kind of body contact is seen, including whether fingertips or whole hands are used and whether there are body parts they avoid touching
D) What kinds of stimulation they provide when the infant is awake, and how they look at the infant
E) Whether the infant's weight gain is adequate
Answer: A, B, C, D
Rationale: Box 22.1 covers reaching out and naming, identification talk, body contact and the fingertip-versus-whole-hand observation, stimulation and visual contact when the infant is awake, comfort with caregiving including expressed disgust at activities such as diaper changes, demonstrations of affection, and comforting techniques used when the infant is fussy.
Trap: (E) is a physical assessment parameter, and the point of the box is that bonding is assessed by watching interaction rather than by measuring outcomes.

Subtopic: the cultural caveats inside the assessment

Q: Parents from a culture in which infants are not named in the early newborn period have not named their baby by day 2. How should the nurse interpret this?
A) As an inhibiting bonding behavior requiring referral
B) As culturally appropriate — Box 22.1 carries the caution inside the question itself, noting that in some cultures parents may not name the infant in the early newborn period
C) As evidence of an unintended pregnancy
D) As grounds for a child protection report
Answer: B
Rationale: Box 22.1's first item includes the parenthetical caution: "(Recognize that in some cultures, parents may not name the infant in the early newborn period.)" The chapter adds that nurses "need to clarify with the client and family members or friends what cultural norms the parents may follow to avoid possible stereotyping."
Trap: (A) applies Table 22.2's "has difficulty naming" line without the cultural clarification the same chapter requires, and this is the specific misjudgment the book names.

Q: A father avoids sustained direct eye contact with his newborn. What should the nurse consider before interpreting this?
A) That eye contact is universally a bonding indicator
B) That in North American culture eye contact appears to reinforce the development of a trusting relationship, but in other cultures sustained direct eye contact is considered rude, immodest, and dangerous — a danger that may arise from the mal de ojo, or evil eye, resulting from excessive admiration
C) That avoidance of eye contact always indicates depression
D) That the father should be instructed to maintain eye contact continuously
Answer: B
Rationale: "In North American culture, eye contact appears to reinforce the development of a trusting relationship. In other cultures, eye contact is perceived differently. For example, in some cultures, sustained direct eye contact is considered to be rude, immodest, and dangerous for some. This danger may arise from the mal de ojo (evil eye), resulting from excessive admiration."
Trap: (D) prescribes a behavior the family may experience as harmful, which is how well-meant teaching becomes culturally insensitive care.

Topic: risk factors for impaired bonding
Subtopic: what puts bonding at risk

Q: Which maternal factors appear in Box 22.2 as risk factors for delayed or impaired maternal-infant bonding? Select all that apply.
A) Unintended or undesired pregnancy, and negative feelings about the pregnancy
B) Primiparity
C) Cesarean birth, traumatic or complicated birth, and maternal exhaustion or sedation
D) Depressive symptoms, anxiety, stress, and social isolation
E) Multiparity with short interpregnancy intervals
Answer: A, B, C, D
Rationale: Box 22.2's maternal list includes unintended or undesired pregnancy, negative feelings about the pregnancy, primiparity, posttraumatic stress, depressive symptoms, anxiety and stress, pregnancy complications, preterm birth, traumatic or complicated birth, cesarean birth, maternal exhaustion or sedation, social isolation, and substance use.
Trap: (E) is not on the list, and (B) is the one most often missed — it is the first-time mother, not the experienced one, whom the box identifies as at risk.

Q: Which newborn factors does Box 22.2 name as risks for impaired bonding?
A) Prematurity with separation from parents such as in the NICU, congenital anomaly or genetic syndrome, neonatal abstinence syndrome, and a difficult or irritable temperament
B) Large birth weight and rapid feeding
C) Male sex only
D) Only congenital anomalies
Answer: A
Rationale: Box 22.2's newborn list is prematurity with separation, congenital anomaly or genetic syndrome, neonatal abstinence syndrome, and a difficult, irritable temperament.
Trap: (D) narrows a four-item list to its most visible entry, and the temperament item is the one that connects back to mutuality — an irritable infant is harder to bond with, and that is stated rather than implied.

Q: How does the chapter say the labor process itself affects bonding?
A) It has no effect once the infant is born healthy
B) It significantly affects immediate bonding: long labor, feeling tired or sedated after birth, problems with breastfeeding, preterm birth, and being separated from the infant at birth can delay the development of initial positive feelings toward the newborn
C) It affects only fathers
D) It affects bonding only after cesarean birth
Answer: B
Rationale: "The labor process significantly affects the immediate bonding of mothers to their newborn infants. Factors such as long labor, feeling tired or sedated after birth, problems with breastfeeding, preterm birth, and being separated from the infant at birth can delay the development of initial positive feelings toward the newborn."
Trap: (A) treats a healthy outcome as erasing the experience, and the word "delay" is what makes this a nursing problem rather than a permanent one.

Q: Which nursing interventions does the chapter describe for facilitating bonding? Select all that apply.
A) Encouraging and assisting parents to hold the newborn skin-to-skin immediately after birth and at other times
B) Providing opportunities for parents to examine their newborn and ask questions about the infant's appearance
C) Providing privacy for parents with rooming-in to enhance interaction
D) Heightening parental awareness of the infant's responses and ability to communicate, and bolstering the parent's self-confidence
E) Limiting parental handling of the infant to reduce overstimulation
Answer: A, B, C, D
Rationale: The chapter names skin-to-skin holding, opportunities to examine the newborn and ask questions, privacy with rooming-in, heightening awareness of infant communication, and bolstering the parent's self-confidence and ego.
Trap: (E) reverses the entire intervention set. The chapter's concern about overstimulation is about reading the infant's cues within an interaction, not about limiting parental contact.

Topic: early and extended contact
Subtopic: what skin-to-skin does and does not do

Q: A mother whose infant was taken to the NICU immediately after birth is distressed that she missed the first hour. What should the nurse tell her?
A) That the critical bonding window has passed
B) That early contact may facilitate bonding but a delay does not necessarily mean bonding will be inhibited, that no scientific evidence has demonstrated immediate contact is essential for the human parent-child relationship, and that the parent-infant relationship is a process that develops over time
C) That bonding is now unlikely without intensive intervention
D) That she should not hold the infant until discharge
Answer: B
Rationale: "Although a delay in contact does not necessarily mean that bonding will be inhibited, additional psychologic energy may be necessary to achieve the same effect. To date, no scientific evidence has demonstrated that immediate contact after birth is essential for the human parent-child relationship." Parents who could not have early contact "can be reassured that such contact is not essential for optimal parent-infant interactions," and nurses "need to stress that the parent-infant relationship is a process that develops over time."
Trap: (A) is the message an over-enthusiastic reading of skin-to-skin teaching sends, and it adds guilt to a separation the mother did not choose.

Q: Given that immediate contact is not essential, why is early skin-to-skin contact still recommended as a standard of care?
A) Because it facilitates parental affectionate bonding behaviors and has many benefits and a lack of adverse effects
B) Because it is required for lactation to begin
C) Because it prevents hypothermia only
D) It is no longer recommended
Answer: A
Rationale: "Early skin-to-skin contact between the newborn and the mother immediately after birth and during the first hour facilitates parental affectionate bonding behaviors and is recommended as a standard of care owing to the many benefits and lack of adverse effects." Fathers or co-parents can also hold newborns skin-to-skin, and in many settings the initial bath is delayed to allow time for this.
Trap: (D) takes the honest caveat about the evidence and converts it into abandonment of the practice — the chapter holds both positions at once.

Subtopic: extended contact and breastfeeding

Q: What is rooming-in, and for whom does the chapter say extended contact is especially important?
A) The infant stays in the room with the mother; extended contact should be available for all parents but especially for those at risk for parenting challenges, such as adolescents
B) The infant stays in the nursery, with scheduled visits
C) Extended contact is reserved for low-risk mothers
D) Extended contact is discouraged after cesarean birth
Answer: A
Rationale: With rooming-in, "the infant stays in the room with the mother," and "extended contact with the infant should be available for all parents but especially for those at risk for parenting challenges such as adolescents."
Trap: (C) inverts the targeting: extended contact matters most for the families with the fewest advantages, which is the opposite of restricting it to the lowest-risk group.

Q: How does breastfeeding contribute to bonding?
A) It has no effect on bonding
B) Breastfeeding enhances bonding, because the release of oxytocin with infant suckling promotes bonding and the development of maternal behavior
C) It delays bonding by tiring the mother
D) It contributes only through nutrition
Answer: B
Rationale: "Breastfeeding enhances bonding; the release of oxytocin with infant suckling promotes bonding and the development of maternal behavior."
Trap: (D) reduces feeding to calories, and it is the same oxytocin release that contracts the uterus — one hormone doing two jobs at once.

Topic: the senses in parent-infant interaction
Subtopic: eye contact and the en face position

Q: What is the en face position, and what are its two defining features?
A) The parent's face and the infant's face approximately 8 inches apart and on the same plane
B) The infant held facing away from the parent
C) The parent's face 18 inches from the infant's, at any angle
D) The infant held over the parent's shoulder
Answer: A
Rationale: "In this position, the parent's face and the infant's face are approximately 8 inches apart and on the same plane." Table 22.2 lists assuming the en face position among the facilitating parental behaviors.
Trap: (C) keeps one feature and loses the other. Both the distance and the shared plane are part of the definition, and the same plane is what makes eye contact possible.

Q: Which nursing actions promote eye contact between parents and newborn? Select all that apply.
A) Positioning the infant on the mother's abdomen or chest with the mother's and the infant's faces on the same plane immediately after birth
B) Dimming the lights, which encourages the infant's eyes to open
C) Delaying instillation of prophylactic antibiotic eye ointment until the infant and parents have had some time together in the first hour after birth
D) Waking the infant for interaction whenever the parents are present
E) Omitting the eye prophylaxis entirely
Answer: A, B, C
Rationale: The chapter names all three actions: the positioning on the same plane, dimming the lights, and delaying the eye ointment "until the infant and parents have had some time together in the first hour after birth."
Trap: (E) turns a timed delay into an omission of required newborn care, and (D) is listed in Table 22.2 as an inhibiting parental behavior — "wakes infant when infant is sleeping."

Subtopic: voice, scent, and rhythm

Q: A newborn waves his arms, lifts his head, and kicks his legs while his mother speaks to him. What is this called?
A) Biorhythmicity
B) Entrainment — newborns move in time with the structure of adult speech, seemingly dancing in tune to a parent's voice
C) Reciprocity
D) An exaggerated motor reflex
Answer: B
Rationale: "Newborns move in time with the structure of adult speech, which is termed entrainment." They "wave their arms, lift their heads, and kick their legs, seemingly 'dancing in tune' to a parent's voice. Culturally determined rhythms of speech are ingrained in the infant long before they use spoken language to communicate."
Trap: (A) is the related but different concept: "the fetus is in tune with the mother's natural rhythms, referred to as biorhythmicity, such as her heartbeat," and after birth the mother's heartbeat or a recording of one can soothe a crying infant.

Q: Which statements about the newborn's senses and the parents' use of them does the chapter support? Select all that apply.
A) Parents wait tensely for the first cry, and once it has reassured them of the infant's health, they begin comforting behaviors
B) Infants respond to higher-pitched voices and can distinguish the mother's voice from others soon after birth
C) Mothers note that each infant has a unique scent
D) Infants learn rapidly to distinguish the scent of their own mother's breast milk
E) Newborns cannot distinguish one voice from another until about 3 months
Answer: A, B, C, D
Rationale: The chapter describes the tense wait for the first cry and the comforting that follows, the infant's response to higher-pitched voices and recognition of the mother's voice soon after birth, mothers' comments on each infant's unique scent, and infants' rapid learning of their own mother's milk scent.
Trap: (E) contradicts the voice finding directly, and it is the sort of claim that would discourage parents from talking to a newborn who is already listening.

Topic: reciprocity synchrony and responsivity
Subtopic: the exchange and the fit

Q: How does the chapter distinguish reciprocity from synchrony?
A) Reciprocity is a type of body movement or behavior that provides the observer with cues, and the exchange often takes several weeks to develop; synchrony refers to the fit between the infant's cues and the parent's response
B) Reciprocity is the fit; synchrony is the exchange
C) They are interchangeable terms
D) Reciprocity applies only to feeding and synchrony only to play
Answer: A
Rationale: "Reciprocity is a type of body movement or behavior that provides the observer with cues," and it "often takes several weeks to develop with a new infant." "Synchrony refers to the 'fit' between the infant's cues and the parent's response."
Trap: (B) swaps them. Using the chapter's own metaphor: reciprocity is that the ball comes back, and synchrony is that it lands well.

Q: The chapter uses two metaphors for reciprocal interaction. What are they, and what does synchrony achieve?
A) Contingency, because the parent's response is contingent on the infant's cue, and serve and return, as the infant serves a cue and the parent returns it; synchrony happens when parents effectively and appropriately return their infants' serves, resulting in more prolonged engagement linked to healthy brain development
B) Mirroring and modeling; synchrony produces faster weight gain
C) Attachment and bonding; synchrony produces earlier walking
D) Feedback and reinforcement; synchrony has no demonstrated outcome
Answer: A
Rationale: Reciprocity "has also been called 'contingency,' as the parent's response is contingent on the infant's cue," and "the metaphor of 'serve and return' has been used to describe the interactive behavior as the infant serves a cue and the parent responds by returning the cue, as in a tennis game." And: "synchrony happens when parents are effectively and appropriately returning their infants' serves, resulting in more prolonged engagement that has been linked to healthy brain development."
Trap: (D) strips the outcome, and the brain-development link is why this is taught as more than a pleasant interaction.

Subtopic: reading the infant's cues

Q: During face-to-face play, an infant averts her eyes and yawns. What should the parent do?
A) Increase stimulation to re-engage her
B) Decrease the active response, since the infant can alternate the gaze voluntarily and looks away when understimulated or overstimulated; if the parent continues to stimulate the infant, the infant may again become fussy
C) End the interaction permanently
D) Wake the infant if she falls asleep
Answer: B
Rationale: The chapter's worked example ends exactly here: "the infant then averts the eyes and yawns; the mother decreases her active response. If the parent continues to stimulate the infant, the infant may again become fussy." The infant "is able to alternate the gaze voluntarily, looking away from the parent's face when understimulated or overstimulated."
Trap: (A) reads gaze aversion as a failure to engage rather than as a message, and the chapter notes that this sensitivity "is especially important when interacting with preterm infants."

Q: A mother talks and smiles continuously while her infant is actively sucking at the breast. What should the nurse teach?
A) To continue, since stimulation during feeding promotes bonding
B) To reserve stimulation for pauses in sucking activity and not to talk or smile excessively while the infant is sucking, because the infant will stop feeding to interact with her
C) To feed in silence at all times
D) To feed only when the infant is drowsy
Answer: B
Rationale: "Mothers learn to reserve stimulation for pauses in sucking activity and not to talk or smile excessively while the infant is sucking because the infant will stop feeding to interact with her."
Trap: (A) is the intuitive praise a nurse would give, which is what makes this counterintuitive rule worth teaching. (C) overcorrects — the pauses are exactly when interaction belongs.

Q: In which infant state does the chapter say interaction is most successful, and what makes it difficult?
A) The quiet alert state, which is one of the most difficult of the sleep-wake states to maintain and occurs most often during a feeding or in face-to-face play
B) Deep sleep, which is the calmest state
C) Active crying, which shows the most engagement
D) Drowsiness, which is the easiest to sustain
Answer: A
Rationale: For modulation of rhythm, "the infant must be in the quiet alert state, one of the most difficult of the sleep-wake states to maintain. The alert state occurs most often during a feeding or in face-to-face play." Nurses "teach parents to recognize the alert state and to engage in interactions when the infant is alert and receptive."
Trap: (C) treats crying as communication to be answered with play, when the chapter's own cue list puts "pouting or crying, arching of the back, and general squirming" at the end of an interaction.

Q: What is infantilizing speech, and how does the chapter distinguish it from baby talk?
A) Infantilizing speech means slowing the tempo, speaking loudly and rhythmically, and emphasizing keywords with repeated phrases; it does not mean using baby talk, which involves distortion of sounds
B) They are the same thing
C) Infantilizing speech means distorting sounds to match the infant's babbling
D) Both are discouraged
Answer: A
Rationale: The parent's behavioral repertoire includes infantilizing speech — "slowing the tempo, speaking loudly and rhythmically, and emphasizing keywords," with phrases repeated — and the chapter draws the line: "infantilizing does not mean using baby talk, which involves distortion of sounds."
Trap: (B) collapses a distinction the chapter makes deliberately, and it is the difference between a speech pattern that supports language and one that distorts it.

Topic: attachment quality and transition to parenthood
Subtopic: what makes attachment secure

Q: Which statements about the quality of attachment does the chapter support? Select all that apply.
A) All infants attach to their parents, but the quality varies from secure to insecure and disorganized
B) Secure attachment is based on a solid foundation of reciprocal and synchronous serve and return interactions
C) The parent fosters secure attachment by staying close to the infant and responding consistently to the infant's bids for attention that indicate need
D) The quality of attachment can be assessed during infancy, beginning at about 9 months of age
E) Only infants with responsive parents form attachments at all
Answer: A, B, C, D
Rationale: "While all infants attach to their parents, the quality of attachment varies from secure to insecure and disorganized." Secure attachment is built on reciprocal and synchronous serve and return interactions, with the parent staying close and responding consistently, providing "a secure base for the infant's exploration and a safe haven from distress." Quality can be assessed from about 9 months, and "secure attachment is linked to a host of optimal physical and mental health outcomes over the lifespan."
Trap: (E) confuses whether attachment forms with how good it is — the attachment system "maintains the proximity of the infant to the caregiver, thus helping to ensure the survival of the otherwise helpless infant," and it operates regardless.

Q: How does the current perspective frame the transition to parenthood?
A) As a major life crisis
B) As a developmental transition — a time of disorder and disequilibrium as well as satisfaction, typically more challenging for first-time parents, and for the majority of new parents an opportunity rather than a time of danger
C) As a period of uniform joy
D) As a transition that affects only the mother
Answer: B
Rationale: "The current perspective is that parenthood is a developmental transition rather than a major life crisis," and it is "a time of disorder and disequilibrium, as well as satisfaction," "typically more challenging for first-time parents." The chapter's summary: "For the majority of new parents, the transition to parenthood is an opportunity rather than a time of danger."
Trap: (D) misses the chapter's note that "the transition can be more difficult for fathers or co-parents. They often feel deprived when the mothers, who are also experiencing stress, cannot provide their usual level of support."

Subtopic: mercer's stages

Q: A mother 6 weeks after birth is developing her own style of mothering as she learns to respond to her infant's cues. Which of Mercer's stages of becoming a mother is this?
A) Stage 1, during pregnancy
B) Stage 2, beginning with birth
C) Stage 3, from 2 weeks to 4 months after birth, in which the mother is individualizing the maternal role
D) Stage 4, from about 4 months and beyond
Answer: C
Rationale: In stage 3, "2 weeks to 4 months after birth," the mother "is individualizing the maternal role, developing her own unique style of mothering as she learns to respond to infant cues." Stage 4, from about 4 months and beyond, is "incorporating the maternal identity into her self-system and gaining competence in her role."
Trap: (B) is the stage of learning from others and professionals, which ends before the individualizing work begins. The chapter also notes that "the time frames ... vary based on each individual mother and family; the most influential factors are the mother's relationships with her partner and with her baby."

Q: What happens in stage 1 of becoming a mother, and what does this imply about when the process starts?
A) It occurs during pregnancy — adjusting to physical, psychologic, and social aspects, exploring expectations of the maternal role, seeking information from others, and visualizing herself as a mother — so the process starts before the birth
B) It begins at birth with the first feeding
C) It begins at discharge
D) It begins when the infant first smiles socially
Answer: A
Rationale: Stage 1 occurs "during pregnancy" and involves "adjusting to the physical, psychologic, and social aspects, exploring expectations of the maternal role, seeking information from others, and visualizing herself as a mother."
Trap: (B) starts the process at birth, which is the same error as dating bonding from birth — both begin in pregnancy, which is what makes prenatal preparation part of the work.

Topic: social influences on parenting
Subtopic: support

Q: A fatigued second-time mother is struggling in the first weeks. Which kind of support does the chapter say will help her more, and why?
A) General support, which addresses feeling loved, respected, and valued
B) Situation-specific support, which relates to practical concerns such as physical needs and childcare — moods and fatigue in the postpartum period can be helped more by situation-specific support from family and friends than by general support
C) Professional counseling only
D) Neither type has been shown to help
Answer: B
Rationale: "General support addresses feeling loved, respected, and valued. Situation-specific support relates to practical concerns such as physical needs and childcare," and the mother's "moods and fatigue in the postpartum period can be helped more by situation-specific support from family and friends than by general support." The chapter's example: "the practical support of a grandparent bathing the infant can help lessen a second-time mother's feelings of loss by providing her time to be with her firstborn child."
Trap: (A) is the reassurance a nurse offers naturally, and the chapter says the practical kind does more for exactly this problem.

Q: Which statements about social support networks does the chapter support? Select all that apply.
A) Social support is strongly related to positive adaptation by new parents, including adolescent parents
B) The type and satisfaction of support seem to be more important than the total number of support network members
C) Sometimes a large network can cause problems because it results in conflicting advice from numerous people
D) Grandparents or in-laws are most appreciated when they assist with household responsibilities and do not intrude into the parents' privacy or judge them critically
E) The larger the support network, the better the adaptation
Answer: A, B, C, D
Rationale: The chapter states each of these, and notes that support spans "both in-person and online settings," and that "through seeking help within the social network, new mothers learn culturally valued practices and develop role competency."
Trap: (E) is contradicted twice — by the statement that type and satisfaction matter more than number, and by the conflicting-advice problem.

Subtopic: siblings and grandparents

Q: A 3-year-old begins wetting the bed and hitting the new baby after the mother returns home. How should the nurse interpret this, and what should the parents be taught?
A) As regression and aggression, which are expected negative reactions, with sibling jealousy common once the initial excitement is over; parents should allow children to interact at their own pace rather than forcing them to interact
B) As a behavioral disorder requiring evaluation
C) As evidence of poor parenting
D) As a sign the child should be separated from the infant
Answer: A
Rationale: Negative sibling behaviors include "regression in toileting and sleep habits, aggression toward the infant, and increased seeking of attention and whining," and "jealousy (sibling rivalry) is common once the initial excitement of having a new infant in the home is over." The chapter's pacing rule: "the adjustment of older children to a newborn takes time, and parents should allow children to interact at their own pace rather than forcing them to interact. To expect a young child to accept and love a rival for the parents' affection assumes an unrealistic level of maturity."
Trap: (B) pathologizes a reaction the chapter describes as expected, and reactions arise from three sources it names: temporary separation from the mother, changes in the parents' behavior, and the infant coming home.

Q: Which strategies does the chapter's teaching box give for facilitating sibling acceptance of a new baby? Select all that apply.
A) Move the child to a bed, if still sleeping in a crib, at least 2 months before the infant is due
B) When the child arrives at the birth facility, make sure your arms are open to embrace the child, and do not force interaction between the child and the infant
C) Have someone else carry the infant from the car when going home so that you can hug the child first
D) Arrange a special time for the child to be alone with each parent, and praise the child for acting age appropriately
E) Keep the child away during infant feeding times to avoid jealousy
Answer: A, B, C, D
Rationale: The teaching box includes the prenatal crib-to-bed move at least 2 months ahead, taking the child on a prenatal visit, open arms at the birth facility without forcing interaction, a gift for the child, having someone else carry the infant into the house, special one-on-one time, and praising age-appropriate behavior "so that being an infant does not seem better than being older."
Trap: (E) reverses the box's own instruction: "do not exclude the child during infant feeding times." Excluding the child during feeds makes the infant's care look like the reason for the child's loss of access.

Q: What does the chapter describe as the primary role of grandparents?
A) To take over infant care so the parents can rest
B) To support, nurture, and empower their child in his or her parenting role, acknowledging that things have changed since they first became parents
C) To advise the parents on correct techniques
D) To remain uninvolved during the first months
Answer: B
Rationale: "A primary role of the grandparents is to support, nurture, and empower their child in his or her parenting role. Grandparents must acknowledge that things have changed since they first became parents." Becoming a grandparent is "a time of transition as roles and relationships change," with joy and anticipation "often intermingled with some degree of anxiety and uncertainty."
Trap: (C) is the behavior the support section identifies as unwelcome — grandparents are most appreciated when they help practically and do not judge critically.

Q: How do socioeconomic conditions and personal aspirations affect the transition to parenthood, according to the chapter?
A) Socioeconomic conditions often determine access to available resources, and financial concerns can interfere with parents' ability to focus attention on the infant; unresolved resentment when parenthood blocks personal or career plans may result in indifference and neglect or in excessive concerns with impossibly high standards
B) Financial stress has no effect on parenting behavior
C) Career ambitions always improve parenting
D) Only the mother's aspirations matter
Answer: A
Rationale: "Socioeconomic conditions often determine access to available resources," and "concerns over financial issues can interfere with the ability of parents to focus their attention on the infant." When parenthood "interferes with or blocks their plans for personal freedom or career advancement," unresolved resentment "may result in indifference and neglect of the infant or in excessive concerns," with impossibly high standards set.
Trap: (B) treats finances as outside the nurse's scope, when the chapter's response is concrete: give parents opportunities to express feelings to an objective listener, discuss measures to permit personal growth, refer to support groups, and advocate for family-friendly work policies.
