# Chapter 37 drills — Perinatal Loss, Bereavement, and Grief

Authored from `sources/Ch37_Fact_Bank.md` (Lowdermilk, Cashion, Alden, Olshansky & Perry, 13th ed., ch. 37, pp. 817–834).

Blueprint weight is **~1 question of 200 (0.5%), depth `light`** — the smallest
weight in the course, and the final touches this chapter once. The Week 1–4
midterm reading guide does not name it. That earns **21 drills across 4 topics** —
one past the `light` band of 12 to 20, which is as close to the ceiling as this
chapter should sit.

The organizing insight the fact bank names is the spine of this set: **at a
perinatal death the nurse's job is not to relieve the parents' pain but to keep
from interrupting it.** Almost every intervention here — listening, sitting
through silence, taking prints and photographs, asking permission before each
keepsake — exists to let the parents do the grieving themselves. The "what not to
say" list is long precisely because the reflex to comfort is what "can stifle the
further expression of emotion."

Four things are drilled: the **standardized terminology**, which is the only part
of the chapter with hard testable boundaries; the **types of loss** and **Miles'
three phases of parental grief**; the **communication rules** in Box 37.1; and the
concrete nursing actions — **memory-making, postmortem care, and the Nursing
Alert**.

**What is left out.** The grief theories, the perinatal care settings and
neonatal palliative care discussion, family aspects of grief, the cultural and
spiritual assessment, and the special circumstances section covering adolescent
grief, complicated grief, and posttraumatic growth. At one blueprint question,
this chapter cannot carry more without crowding out chapters that carry six.

Topic: standardized terminology for perinatal loss
Subtopic: the twenty-week line

Q: A pregnancy ends in utero at 17 weeks. What is the correct term, and what term should the nurse use with the family?
A) Miscarriage, defined as any in utero death before 20 weeks of gestation; miscarriage is the accepted term among providers, used to differentiate this loss from elective abortions
B) Stillbirth, because the fetus died before birth
C) Fetal death, because it occurred in utero
D) Early neonatal death
Answer: A
Rationale: Miscarriage is "any in utero death before 20 weeks of gestation," and although "providers sometimes refer to these losses as spontaneous abortions, miscarriage is the accepted term among providers to differentiate this form of loss from elective abortions."
Trap: (B) applies a term the chapter reserves for 20 weeks and later, and the word choice matters to a family who may hear "abortion" as something they chose.

Q: A fetus dies in utero at 26 weeks. Which terms apply?
A) Fetal death, which is any death before birth after 20 weeks; it is also a stillbirth, since a stillbirth is a fetal death occurring at 20 weeks or later; and it is an early fetal death, since fetal deaths are subdivided as early at 20 to 27 weeks and late at 28 weeks or more
B) Miscarriage
C) Late fetal death
D) Late neonatal death
Answer: A
Rationale: "Fetal death refers to any death before birth after 20 weeks of gestation," "a stillbirth is a fetal death occurring at 20 weeks or later," and "fetal deaths are subdivided as early (20 to 27 weeks of gestation) or late (≥28 weeks of gestation)."
Trap: (C) crosses the 28-week boundary that separates early from late fetal death, and (D) applies a neonatal term to a fetus who was never born alive.

Subtopic: the neonatal and infant boundaries

Q: A live-born infant dies on day 12 of life. How is this classified?
A) A late neonatal death, since deaths occurring between 7 and 27 days are late neonatal deaths
B) An early neonatal death
C) An infant death but not a neonatal death
D) A stillbirth
Answer: A
Rationale: "Death of a live-born infant fewer than 7 days old is an early neonatal death," and "deaths occurring between 7 and 27 days are late neonatal deaths." Any live birth of a child who dies within the first year is an infant death.
Trap: (C) is what the same death becomes on day 30 — past 27 days it is no longer a neonatal death, though it remains an infant death through the first year.

Q: Why does the chapter say standardized terminology for pregnancy-associated deaths matters?
A) Because health care practitioners are required to report fetal deaths, live births, and infant deaths, which are used collectively as data to determine perinatal and neonatal mortality rates at state and national levels, and policy makers use those rates to influence decisions about allocating resources to improve care and outcomes
B) Because it determines insurance coverage
C) Because families prefer technical language
D) Because the terms have no practical consequence
Answer: A
Rationale: The American Academy of Pediatrics "recommended standard terminology for deaths associated with pregnancy" in 2016, and "health care practitioners are required to report fetal deaths, live births, and infant deaths, which are used collectively as data to determine perinatal and neonatal mortality rates at state and national levels. Policy makers, in turn, use these rates to influence policy decisions related to allocation of resources to improve care and outcomes."
Trap: (D) makes the definitions academic, when the chapter ties them directly to how resources reach perinatal care.

Q: How does the chapter define perinatal loss as a general term?
A) A general term commonly used to describe losses associated with pregnancy, particularly losses occurring after conception and after the woman has recognized that she is pregnant
B) Only deaths occurring after 20 weeks
C) Only neonatal deaths
D) Only losses occurring during labor
Answer: A
Rationale: Perinatal loss is "a general term commonly used to describe losses associated with pregnancy, particularly losses occurring after conception and after the woman has recognized that she is pregnant."
Trap: (B) restricts a deliberately broad term to the subset with its own name, and the breadth is what lets the chapter include losses that involve no death at all.

Topic: types of perinatal loss and grief phases
Subtopic: which losses are grieved

Q: A woman who had a miscarriage at 9 weeks tells the nurse that no one in her family knew she was pregnant. What does the chapter say about this situation?
A) Grief from miscarriage is often suffered in isolation because other people in a woman's family and social network may not even know about the pregnancy and subsequent loss, and it may be disenfranchised — even when others know, their understanding of the meaning of the pregnancy or its importance to the woman may be underestimated or unappreciated
B) Early losses are grieved less intensely
C) Grief is easier when others do not know
D) Miscarriage grief resolves within days
Answer: A
Rationale: "Grief from miscarriage is often suffered in isolation because other people in a woman's family and social network may not even know about the pregnancy and subsequent loss," and even when they do know, "their understanding of the meaning of the pregnancy or its importance to the woman may be underestimated or unappreciated."
Trap: (B) is the assumption that makes early losses invisible, and the isolation the chapter describes is an assessment finding rather than a mitigating factor.

Q: Which losses does the chapter describe as producing grief even though no death occurs? Select all that apply.
A) Preterm birth, where the full-term healthy newborn parents expected is replaced by a small baby who is sick or requires lengthy hospitalization
B) A delayed or foregone opportunity to breastfeed, which is another loss
C) A cesarean birth, which some women grieve, compounded when it is emergent and under general anesthesia so that the woman and partner are separated and she does not hear her baby's first cries
D) Multifetal pregnancy reduction, after which expectant parents may feel deep grief and sadness despite the continued development of one or two fetuses
E) A planned vaginal birth at term with a healthy infant
Answer: A, B, C, D
Rationale: "Other losses associated with childbearing can result in distress or even grief, even if the loss does not involve death." The chapter names preterm birth and the delayed or foregone breastfeeding it causes, the birth experience itself — "the reality of the birth experience is inconsistent with the parents' hopes and expectations" — and multifetal pregnancy reduction, where grief follows even as the pregnancy continues.
Trap: (E) describes the expected outcome, and the chapter's point is that grief is measured against what the parents hoped for rather than against a clinical standard.

Q: Why does a serious fetal diagnosis create a particularly difficult situation for parents?
A) Because the standard anatomy ultrasound is typically performed between 18 and 20 weeks — later than the time most women would otherwise consider an abortion — so most pregnancies with a diagnosis of a severe fetal defect are pregnancies that are wanted
B) Because the diagnosis is usually made in the first trimester
C) Because the incidence of fetal defects has risen sharply
D) Because the diagnosis is always certain
Answer: A
Rationale: "Current standards of maternity care include at least one ultrasound examination, typically performed between 18 and 20 weeks of gestation," and "because these diagnoses are made midpregnancy—later than the time most women would otherwise consider an abortion—most pregnancies with diagnosis of a severe fetal defect are pregnancies that are wanted."
Trap: (C) misreads a real trend that the chapter corrects explicitly: increased ultrasound use "has resulted in increased diagnosis of fetal defects; note that this is an increase in the diagnosis of defects, not in the actual incidence of defects." And (D) overstates certainty the chapter also qualifies — the abundance of prenatal information "may lead to more, not less, uncertainty about long-term outcomes."

Subtopic: miles' phases

Q: Which three phases does Miles describe in parental grief, and what qualifier does the chapter attach?
A) Acute distress characterized by shock and numbness, a period of intense grief with emotional, cognitive, behavioral, and physical responses, and reorganization, when parents return to their usual level of functioning although the distress associated with the death remains; the phases are overlapping rather than sequential stages
B) Denial, anger, and acceptance, completed in order
C) Shock, resolution, and closure
D) A single undifferentiated period of grief
Answer: A
Rationale: "Miles hypothesized that parental grief responses occur in three overlapping phases": acute distress following the diagnosis or death, "a period of intense grief that includes emotional, cognitive, behavioral, and physical responses," and reorganization, which "occurs when they return to their usual level of functioning, although the distress associated with the death remains."
Trap: (B) imports a different model and adds a sequence the word "overlapping" denies — and the chapter's larger point is that "the goal of the grief process is not resolution but rather the incorporation of the person who has died into one's own life moving forward."

Q: A mother whose infant has just died appears calm and shows almost no emotion. How should the nurse interpret this?
A) As a possible reflection of numbness during acute distress — occasionally a lack of affect, calmness, or even euphoria can reflect numbness, and for some people a way of coping with a profound loss is to avoid emotional displays
B) As evidence she is not grieving
C) As denial requiring confrontation
D) As a sign that no follow-up is needed
Answer: A
Rationale: Acute distress brings "a sense of unreality, loss of innocence, and powerlessness ... and having a sense of disbelief. Profound sadness accompanied by intense outbursts of emotion and crying is common." But: "Occasionally a lack of affect, calmness, or even euphoria can reflect their numbness," and "for some persons, their way of coping with a profound loss is to avoid emotional displays." Expressions of grief "may be guided by deep sociocultural and religious/spiritual influences that are unfamiliar to the nurse, who is expected to provide nonjudgmental support."
Trap: (B) reads a coping style as an absence of grief, which is the judgment the chapter's nonjudgmental-support instruction is written against.

Topic: communicating with bereaved parents
Subtopic: why comforting is the error

Q: A nurse feels an urge to say something that will make a bereaved mother feel better. What does the chapter say about this impulse?
A) The initial impulse to reduce one's sense of helplessness is to say or do something to reduce the parent's pain, and although such a response seems supportive at the time, it can stifle the further expression of emotion; the nurse should resist the temptation to give advice or use clichés
B) The impulse should be followed, since reassurance is the priority
C) The nurse should leave the room until the parents compose themselves
D) The nurse should offer a factual explanation of the cause
Answer: A
Rationale: "The initial impulse to reduce one's sense of helplessness is to say or do something to reduce his or her pain. Although such a response seems supportive at the time, it can stifle the further expression of emotion," and "the nurse should resist the temptation to give advice or use clichés in offering support to the bereaved."
Trap: (C) removes the presence that is itself the intervention: "the nurse should have a presence of self and the willingness to be alongside, quietly supporting the bereaved person," and "this presence is comforting for parents."

Q: Which statements appear in Box 37.1's "what to say" list? Select all that apply.
A) I'm sad for you
B) This must be hard for you
C) What can I do for you?
D) I'm here, and I want to listen
E) I know how you feel
Answer: A, B, C, D
Rationale: Box 37.1's "what to say" list is: I'm sad for you; How are you doing with all of this?; This must be hard for you; What can I do for you?; I'm sorry; and I'm here, and I want to listen.
Trap: (E) is on the "what not to say" list, alongside God had a purpose for her; Be thankful you have another child; The living must go on; It's God's will; You're young, you can have others; Now you have an angel in heaven; This happened for the best; Better for this to happen now, before you knew the baby; and There was something wrong with the baby anyway.

Subtopic: the techniques

Q: Which communication techniques does the chapter endorse with bereaved parents? Select all that apply.
A) Presence — being alongside and quietly supporting whatever expressions of feeling are appropriate for them
B) Minimal encouragers such as leaning forward and nodding, and inviting the person to say more
C) Sitting through the silence, which gives the bereaved person an opportunity to collect thoughts and process what they are sharing
D) Naming the feeling, such as observing aloud that the person sounds or looks angry
E) Using touch automatically with every bereaved parent
Answer: A, B, C, D
Rationale: The chapter endorses presence, minimal encouragers — "leaning forward, nodding the head" is "often encouragement enough for the bereaved person to tell his or her story" — silence, which "can be therapeutic," and identifying the feeling when a person expresses anger.
Trap: (E) skips the assessment the chapter requires: "careful assessment is important before using touch as a therapeutic technique. For some, touch is a meaningful expression of concern, but for others it is an invasion of privacy or contrary to their cultural or religious beliefs."

Q: A bereaved mother repeatedly asks the nurse whether something she did caused the loss. How should the nurse respond?
A) Recognize that the answers to these questions must be answered by the bereaved themselves as part of their healing; trying to give answers when there are no clear answers, or trying to assuage their feelings by telling them they should not feel guilty, does not help them process their grief
B) Provide a definitive answer to relieve her guilt
C) Tell her she should not feel guilty
D) Redirect the conversation to the infant's care
Answer: A
Rationale: "A sense of guilt can pervade the experience of bereaved parents, who may raise many questions surrounding their loss that can leave them feeling guilty. This is particularly true for mothers," and "the nurse should recognize that the answers to these questions must be answered by the bereaved themselves; it is part of their healing." Both failure modes are named: giving answers where there are none, and telling parents they should not feel guilty.
Trap: (C) is the kindest-sounding response and one of the two the chapter identifies as unhelpful — and statistics do not help either, since "for many people, understanding that something happens frequently is no consolation when it happens to them."

Q: Whose grief does the chapter say is most often overlooked, and what should the nurse do about it?
A) The partner's, since much of the attention at the time of a loss is on the woman and the partner is usually expected to be her main support; nurses may need to be intentional in addressing the partner's grief and helping them realize that they too need support, asking the father or partner directly about their views of what happened and their feelings of loss
B) The grandparents'
C) The nurse's own
D) No one's grief is overlooked
Answer: A
Rationale: "Much of the attention at the time of a loss is on the woman, and her partner is usually expected to be her main support. The partner's grief is sometimes not recognized or acknowledged," and "nurses may need to be intentional in addressing their grief." Because nurses "tend to be very focused on the physical and emotional needs of the mother, it is especially important to ask the father or partner directly about their views of what happened and the associated feelings of loss." Partners "may hide their feelings from each other to protect the other during times of loss."
Trap: (D) contradicts a gap the chapter names explicitly, and the direct question to the partner is the intervention that closes it.

Q: What should the nurse tell parents about what lies ahead in their grief?
A) That most parents are not prepared for the raw feelings they experience or for the fact that these painful, complex feelings and related behavioral reactions continue for many weeks or months, and that reassuring them their responses are normal and preparing them for a potentially lengthy process can be useful
B) That the worst will be over within a week
C) That grief resolves completely with time
D) That they should avoid discussing the loss
Answer: A
Rationale: "Most parents are not prepared for the raw feelings that they experience or the fact that these painful, complex feelings and related behavioral reactions continue for many weeks or months. Reassuring them that their responses are normal and preparing them for the potentially lengthy process of grief can be useful." Grief "is a long-term process that can extend for months and years; some aspects of grief endure throughout life."
Trap: (C) promises an endpoint the chapter replaces with continuing bonds — incorporating the person who died into one's ongoing life rather than resolving the loss.

Topic: memory making and postmortem care
Subtopic: mementos

Q: Which mementos can the nurse offer bereaved parents, and what rule governs offering them?
A) The baby's weight, length, and head circumference, footprints and handprints on a special card or in a memory book, and everyday articles such as the tape measure, baby lotions, combs or hairbrushes, clothing, hats, blankets, crib cards, and identification bands; the nurse should ask parents whether they wish to have these articles before giving them
B) All available items should be sent home without asking
C) Mementos should be offered only if parents request them first
D) Mementos are not appropriate after a perinatal death
Answer: A
Rationale: "The nurse can provide information about the baby's weight, length, and head circumference to the family. Footprints and handprints can be taken and placed with the other information on a special card or in a memory or baby book." Everyday articles listed include the tape measure, lotions, combs or hairbrushes, clothing, hats, blankets, crib cards, and identification bands, and "the identification band helps the parents remember the size of the baby and personalizes the mementos." The rule: "The nurse should ask parents if they wish to have these articles before giving them to the parents."
Trap: (B) removes the parents' choice from an object they may or may not want to carry home, and the same permission rule applies again to hair: "the nurse must ask the parents for permission before cutting a lock of hair, which can be removed from the nape of the neck, where it is not noticeable."

Q: What technique improves the quality of a newborn's footprints or handprints?
A) Applying alcohol or acetone to the palms or soles to help the ink adhere, especially for small babies, placing the heel or palm down first and rolling the foot or hand forward while keeping the toes or fingers extended
B) Pressing the whole foot down flat at once
C) Warming the skin with a heat lamp
D) Tracing the outline, which produces the most accurate record
Answer: A
Rationale: "Alcohol or acetone applied to the palms or soles can help the ink adhere and improve the quality of the prints, especially for small babies," and "the baby's heel or palm is placed down first, and the foot or hand is rolled forward, keeping the toes or fingers extended."
Trap: (D) names the fallback rather than the technique, and the chapter notes its limitation: tracing is possible "although this distorts the actual size."

Q: What does the chapter say about photographs after a perinatal death?
A) Photographs are generally taken when it has been determined to be culturally acceptable to the family, and the decision for photography is up to the parents; close-ups of the baby's face, hands, and feet are included, with the baby both clothed and unclothed, and objects placed nearby to contextualize the photo and give an idea of the baby's approximate size
B) Photographs should always be taken without asking
C) Photographs should never be taken
D) Only clothed photographs are appropriate
Answer: A
Rationale: "Photographs are generally taken when it has been determined to be culturally acceptable to the family—the decision for photography is up to the parents," and they include "close-ups of the baby's face, hands, and feet, with the baby both clothed and unclothed," with nearby objects that "serve to contextualize the photo and give an idea of the baby's approximate size."
Trap: (B) and (C) sit at opposite extremes of a decision the chapter assigns to the parents rather than to the nurse or the unit.

Subtopic: postmortem care

Q: A nurse is preparing a deceased infant's body for transfer to the morgue, and the infant takes an agonal respiration. What does the Nursing Alert direct?
A) Dying neonates may respond reflexively to the stimulation of cleaning and preparing for the morgue, sometimes for several hours after being pronounced dead; if this happens the nurse should notify a physician for confirmation of death
B) Resuscitation should be started immediately
C) The nurse should document the movement and continue
D) The parents should be told the infant is alive
Answer: A
Rationale: The Nursing Alert states that "dying neonates may respond reflexively to the stimulation of cleaning and preparing for the morgue and sometimes will do this for several hours after being pronounced dead. This is extremely startling and upsetting when a baby who has been pronounced dead takes an agonal respiration or an extremity twitches. If this happens, the nurse should notify a physician for confirmation of death."
Trap: (D) would give parents a moment of hope the chapter is preparing the nurse to handle, and the physician's confirmation is what settles it before anything is said.

Q: What is a burial cradle, and what other point does the chapter make about postmortem care?
A) A miniature casket usually made of Styrofoam or wood, used as a sensitive and respectful way to take the fetus or infant to the morgue, making the process more dignified for parents and nursing staff; the Joint Commission requires that appropriate care be offered to the body after death, and nurses can find that providing postmortem care helps them in their own grief, particularly NICU nurses who have cared for an infant for hours, days, or weeks
B) A bassinet used for viewing only, with no role in transport
C) A required legal document
D) A device with no relevance to staff
Answer: A
Rationale: "A sensitive and respectful approach for taking the fetus or infant to the morgue is the use of a burial cradle, which makes the process more dignified for parents and the nursing staff. A burial cradle is a miniature casket usually made of Styrofoam or wood." The Joint Commission "requires that appropriate care be offered to the body after death," and "nurses can find that providing postmortem care helps them in their own grief related to a perinatal loss."
Trap: (D) omits the staff half of the point, which is the chapter's acknowledgment that the nurses who provided the care are grieving too.
