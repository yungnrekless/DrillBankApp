# Chapter 27 drills — Hypertensive Disorders

Authored from `sources/Ch27_Fact_Bank.md` (Lowdermilk, Cashion, Alden, Olshansky & Perry, 13th ed., ch. 27, pp. 585–599).

Blueprint weight is **~10 questions of 200 (5.0%), depth `full`** — topics
*Hypertensive Disorders, HELLP, abruption, chronic HTN*. The chapter is 15 pages
and the Week 3 reading guide assigns about five of them, so the sampling rate on
the assigned pages is very dense and they are drilled close to exhaustively.

The organizing insight the fact bank names is the spine of this set:
**preeclampsia is not a blood-pressure disease, and the book says so outright —
"The main pathogenic factor is not an increase in BP but poor perfusion as a
result of vasospasm and reduced plasma volume."** Proteinuria, epigastric pain,
low platelets, elevated liver enzymes, headache, pulmonary edema and oliguria are
one lesion — endothelial dysfunction and vasospasm — showing up in different
organs. That is also why the definitions changed: proteinuria is no longer
required, because it was never the disease.

Every distinction the chapter states in both directions is drilled from both
ends: before and after 20 weeks, gestational hypertension and preeclampsia,
preeclampsia and preeclampsia with severe features, preeclampsia and HELLP on the
hematocrit, magnesium side effects and magnesium toxicity, the severe-range
treatment threshold and the chronic-hypertension treatment threshold, and the
pre- and post-CHAP approach to mild chronic hypertension.

**A note on the blueprint's fourth topic.** *Abruption* is listed under this
chapter, but placental abruption is not a Chapter 27 section — this chapter names
it only as a consequence of preeclampsia, HELLP, and chronic hypertension. Those
links are drilled here; the disorder itself, its grading, and its management are
Chapter 28's and are drilled there.

**What is left out.** The full care-management assessment sequence (pp. 590–593),
including **deep tendon reflex and ankle clonus grading**, blood pressure
technique, and Table 27.4. That is the most likely place an exam would reach
outside the guide, since reflex grading is done at the bedside alongside every
magnesium infusion. Also omitted: the Evidence-Based Practice box, the
Next-Generation case study, and the Community Focus exercise.

Topic: classifying hypertensive disorders of pregnancy
Subtopic: the twenty-week line

Q: A woman at her first prenatal visit at 15 weeks has a blood pressure of 150/95 mm Hg on two readings. She has never had her blood pressure checked before. How is this classified?
A) Gestational hypertension, because the hypertension is newly discovered
B) Chronic hypertension, because it is present before 20 weeks of gestation
C) Preeclampsia, pending a protein measurement
D) Superimposed preeclampsia
Answer: B
Rationale: "Chronic hypertension is defined as hypertension that is present before the pregnancy or diagnosed before 20 weeks of gestation." The 20-week line does the sorting, regardless of how new the finding is to the chart.
Trap: (A) treats "new to us" as "new-onset," which is the error this date exists to prevent. The chapter adds a second route to the diagnosis: women may be diagnosed with chronic hypertension once it is evident that hypertension persists after the postpartum period.

Q: A previously normotensive woman develops a blood pressure of 146/92 mm Hg at 32 weeks, confirmed 4 hours later, with no proteinuria and no other findings. How is this classified?
A) Gestational hypertension
B) Preeclampsia
C) Chronic hypertension
D) Preeclampsia with severe features
Answer: A
Rationale: Gestational hypertension is "development of hypertension after week 20 of pregnancy in a woman with a previously normal BP," recorded "on two occasions at least 4 hours apart." Hypertension alone, after 20 weeks, with nothing accompanying it, is gestational hypertension.
Trap: (B) is what the same reading becomes the moment proteinuria or any severe-feature finding appears. The blood pressure definitions are identical between the two diagnoses; what accompanies the reading is what separates them.

Q: A woman with chronic hypertension that has been well controlled on medication has a sudden increase in blood pressure and new-onset proteinuria at 30 weeks. What is this called, and why does it matter?
A) Gestational hypertension, which resolves after birth
B) Superimposed preeclampsia, which increases morbidity for both the pregnant woman and the fetus
C) Eclampsia
D) Preeclampsia with severe features, by definition
Answer: B
Rationale: Superimposed preeclampsia is "chronic hypertension in association with preeclampsia," diagnosed on "(1) a sudden increase in BP that was previously well controlled or (2) new-onset proteinuria or a sudden and sustained increase in proteinuria," and it "increases morbidity for both the pregnant woman and the fetus."
Trap: (D) jumps to the severe-features label without the blood pressure that defines it. Severe features require readings of 160/110 or the severe qualifiers, not simply a change from baseline.

Q: A nurse is reviewing the classification of hypertensive states in pregnancy. Which statements are accurate? Select all that apply.
A) Pregnancy-associated hypertensive disorders develop during pregnancy, during labor, or after birth
B) Chronic hypertensive disorders precede pregnancy
C) Eclampsia is the development of seizures or coma in a woman with preeclampsia who has no preexisting pathology that can cause seizures
D) Hypertensive disorders occur in approximately 5% to 10% of all pregnancies
E) Hypertensive disorders are rarely a source of maternal morbidity
Answer: A, B, C, D
Rationale: The chapter's opening split is that pregnancy-associated disorders — gestational hypertension, preeclampsia, and eclampsia — develop during pregnancy, labor, or after birth, while chronic hypertensive disorders precede pregnancy. Hypertensive disorders occur in approximately 5% to 10% of pregnancies.
Trap: (E) reverses one of the chapter's framing statements: "hypertensive disorders are a major cause of maternal and perinatal morbidity and mortality."

Subtopic: the course of gestational hypertension

Q: A woman diagnosed with gestational hypertension at 34 weeks asks how long it will last. What does the chapter say?
A) It resolves after giving birth, although it may require 6 to 12 months to do so
B) It resolves within 48 hours of birth in all cases
C) It is permanent once diagnosed
D) It resolves only if antihypertensive therapy is continued for a year
Answer: A
Rationale: "Gestational hypertension resolves after giving birth, although it may require 6 to 12 months to do so." The chapter adds that most women with gestational hypertension become normotensive during the first week after giving birth, while hypertension may take longer to resolve in women with preeclampsia.
Trap: (C) forecloses a diagnosis that is defined by its resolution. A woman whose hypertension persists beyond the postpartum period is reclassified as having chronic hypertension instead.

Q: Why does a diagnosis of gestational hypertension require ongoing surveillance rather than reassurance?
A) Because up to 50% of women with gestational hypertension go on to develop preeclampsia
B) Because gestational hypertension always progresses to eclampsia
C) Because gestational hypertension requires magnesium sulfate prophylaxis
D) Because the diagnosis cannot be confirmed until after birth
Answer: A
Rationale: "Up to 50% of women with gestational hypertension go on to develop preeclampsia," and "some women who are initially thought to have gestational hypertension are eventually diagnosed with chronic hypertension instead." It is a moving diagnosis in both directions.
Trap: (B) converts a 50% risk of progression to preeclampsia into certain progression to eclampsia, skipping a diagnosis and overstating the risk.

Topic: preeclampsia diagnostic criteria
Subtopic: the blood pressure thresholds

Q: A woman at 33 weeks has two blood pressure readings of 164/112 mm Hg taken 4 hours apart, with proteinuria of 400 mg in a 24-hour specimen. How is this classified?
A) Preeclampsia
B) Preeclampsia with severe features
C) Gestational hypertension
D) Chronic hypertension with superimposed preeclampsia
Answer: B
Rationale: Table 27.2 sets preeclampsia at readings of 140/90 or higher twice, at least 4 hours apart after 20 weeks in a previously normotensive woman, and preeclampsia with severe features at readings of 160/110 or higher twice, at least 4 hours apart.
Trap: (A) is correct up to the blood pressure. Four of the table's seven rows are identical between the two columns; what separates them is the pressure threshold plus the qualifiers severe, persistent, progressive, and unresponsive.

Q: A woman at 35 weeks has a blood pressure of 148/94 mm Hg twice, 4 hours apart, with no protein on dipstick but a platelet count of 88,000 and liver enzymes twice the upper limit of normal. How should the nurse interpret this?
A) She does not have preeclampsia, because proteinuria is required for the diagnosis
B) She meets criteria for preeclampsia, because in the absence of proteinuria new-onset hypertension with thrombocytopenia or impaired liver function establishes the diagnosis
C) She has gestational hypertension with an unrelated hematologic problem
D) She has HELLP syndrome by definition
Answer: B
Rationale: "In the absence of proteinuria, preeclampsia may be defined as hypertension along with thrombocytopenia, impaired liver function, or persistent right upper quadrant or epigastric pain ..., progressive renal insufficiency, pulmonary edema, new-onset cerebral symptoms ... not responding to analgesia, or visual disturbances." Thrombocytopenia is a platelet count under 100,000.
Trap: (A) applies the pre-2013 definition. The Task Force eliminated the amount of proteinuria, the presence of oliguria, and intrauterine growth restriction as diagnostic requirements.

Q: A woman with preeclampsia has 6 g of protein in a 24-hour specimen. How does this affect the classification of severity?
A) It establishes preeclampsia with severe features
B) It does not, because massive proteinuria of more than 5 g in a 24-hour specimen is no longer used as a diagnostic criterion
C) It rules out preeclampsia and suggests primary renal disease
D) It doubles the magnesium sulfate dose required
Answer: B
Rationale: Table 27.2 states directly: "Massive proteinuria (>5 g in a 24-h specimen) is no longer used as a diagnostic criterion." Proteinuria of 300 mg or more in 24 hours, a protein/creatinine ratio of 0.3 or more, or 2+ on dipstick establishes the finding, but the quantity does not grade the disease.
Trap: (A) grades severity by grams of protein, which is exactly the practice the 2013 changes removed — and the deeper reason is that proteinuria was never the disease.

Q: Which findings meet criteria for preeclampsia in a woman with new-onset hypertension after 20 weeks? Select all that apply.
A) Platelet count below 100,000
B) Liver enzymes elevated to twice the upper limit of normal
C) Serum creatinine above 1.1 mg/dL or a doubling of the creatinine in the absence of other renal disease
D) Pulmonary edema, new-onset headache unresponsive to medication, or visual symptoms
E) Intrauterine growth restriction
Answer: A, B, C, D
Rationale: Table 27.2 lists thrombocytopenia, impaired liver function, renal insufficiency, pulmonary edema, and cerebral or visual disturbances alongside proteinuria as the findings that establish preeclampsia with new-onset hypertension.
Trap: (E) was removed as a criterion by the 2013 Task Force, together with the amount of proteinuria and the presence of oliguria. Growth restriction still matters clinically; it no longer makes the diagnosis.

Q: A woman with preeclampsia reports a severe headache that has not responded to acetaminophen, along with spots in her visual field. How should the nurse classify and act on these findings?
A) As expected discomforts of late pregnancy, to be monitored at the next visit
B) As cerebral and visual disturbances meeting criteria for preeclampsia and reportable immediately
C) As side effects of magnesium sulfate
D) As evidence of migraine, unrelated to the preeclampsia
Answer: B
Rationale: New-onset headache unresponsive to medication and not accounted for by alternative diagnoses, and visual disturbances such as scotoma — "dim vision or blind or dark spots in the visual field" — are diagnostic findings and are on Box 27.3's reportable list. They arise from cerebral edema, CNS irritability, and retinal arteriolar vasospasm.
Trap: (C) attributes a preeclampsia finding to the drug used to treat it. Magnesium's expected effects are warmth, flushing, diaphoresis, burning at the IV site, and sedation — not headache with visual spots.

Topic: preeclampsia pathophysiology
Subtopic: the lesion and its consequences

Q: A student asks why a woman with preeclampsia can be seriously ill at a blood pressure of 150/95. Which response reflects the chapter?
A) The main pathogenic factor is not an increase in blood pressure but poor perfusion as a result of vasospasm and reduced plasma volume
B) The blood pressure reading is inaccurate in preeclampsia
C) The severity is determined by the amount of proteinuria rather than the pressure
D) Illness at that pressure indicates a second, unrelated disease
Answer: A
Rationale: The chapter states it plainly: "The main pathogenic factor is not an increase in BP but poor perfusion as a result of vasospasm and reduced plasma volume." Every organ finding in preeclampsia is that one lesion appearing in a different system.
Trap: (C) resurrects proteinuria as the severity measure, which the same chapter removed. The pressure is a marker of the vasospasm, not the injury itself.

Q: What placental change does the chapter identify as the root of preeclampsia, and when does it occur?
A) Premature separation of the placenta in the third trimester
B) Failure of the spiral arteries to remodel into thinner, larger-diameter vessels, developing early in pregnancy long before signs appear
C) Placental calcification after 34 weeks
D) Implantation in the lower uterine segment
Answer: B
Rationale: Normally the spiral arteries "widen from thick-walled muscular vessels to thinner, saclike vessels with much larger diameters," and "because this vascular remodeling does not occur or only partially develops in women with preeclampsia, decreased placental perfusion and endothelial dysfunction result." These changes "develop early in pregnancy, long before the signs and symptoms of preeclampsia become evident."
Trap: (A) names placental abruption, which is a complication of preeclampsia rather than its cause. The chapter is explicit that "the placenta is the root cause" and that "the disease begins to resolve after the placenta has been expelled."

Q: A woman with preeclampsia has a rising hematocrit and worsening edema at the same time. How does the chapter explain this combination?
A) She is dehydrated and needs aggressive fluid replacement
B) Endothelial permeability lets fluid leave the intravascular compartment, producing tissue edema and hemoconcentration at once
C) The hematocrit rise indicates hemolysis
D) The findings are unrelated to preeclampsia
Answer: B
Rationale: "Plasma colloid osmotic pressure decreases as serum albumin levels decrease. Intravascular volume is reduced as fluid moves out of the intravascular compartment, resulting in hemoconcentration, increased blood viscosity, and tissue edema. The hematocrit value increases as fluid leaves the intravascular space."
Trap: (A) is the intuitive response to a high hematocrit and is dangerous here, because arteriolar vasospasm with increased capillary permeability predisposes these women to pulmonary edema — which is why total fluids are capped at 125 mL/h.

Q: A woman with preeclampsia reports severe right upper quadrant pain. What does this finding represent?
A) Gastroesophageal reflux, to be treated with an antacid
B) Hepatic involvement from decreased liver perfusion with hepatic edema and subcapsular hemorrhage, which is a reportable finding
C) Gallbladder disease unrelated to the preeclampsia
D) Referred pain from uterine contractions
Answer: B
Rationale: "Decreased liver perfusion can lead to impaired liver function and elevated liver enzyme levels. If hepatic edema and subcapsular hemorrhage develop, the woman may experience epigastric or right upper quadrant abdominal pain." Rupture of a subcapsular hematoma "is a life-threatening complication and a surgical emergency."
Trap: (A) is the trap this sign sets in every preeclampsia stem. Epigastric pain here is a liver finding, a diagnostic criterion, a reportable condition, and an indication for immediate birth — not a digestive complaint.

Q: Which manifestations does the chapter attribute to central nervous system involvement in preeclampsia? Select all that apply.
A) Headaches
B) Hyperreflexia
C) Positive ankle clonus
D) Seizures
E) Burning at the intravenous site
Answer: A, B, C, D
Rationale: Neurologic effects include "cerebral edema and hemorrhage and increased central nervous system (CNS) irritability. CNS irritability manifests as headaches, hyperreflexia, positive ankle clonus, and seizures."
Trap: (E) is an expected side effect of magnesium sulfate, not a manifestation of the disease. Sorting the drug's effects from the disease's findings is what the whole magnesium assessment depends on.

Topic: preeclampsia risk factors and prevention
Subtopic: who is at risk

Q: Which factors appear in Box 27.1 as risk factors for preeclampsia? Select all that apply.
A) Nulliparity and multifetal gestation
B) Preeclampsia in a previous pregnancy
C) Chronic hypertension, pregestational diabetes, and gestational diabetes
D) Prepregnancy BMI over 30 and maternal age 35 years or older
E) Grand multiparity with a history of rapid labors
Answer: A, B, C, D
Rationale: Box 27.1 lists nulliparity, multifetal gestations, preeclampsia in a previous pregnancy, chronic hypertension, pregestational and gestational diabetes, thrombophilia, systemic lupus erythematosus, prepregnancy BMI over 30, antiphospholipid antibody syndrome, maternal age 35 or older, kidney disease, assisted reproductive technology, and obstructive sleep apnea.
Trap: (E) sounds like an obstetric risk factor and is not on the list. Nulliparity — not multiparity — is the parity-related risk here.

Q: A woman asks whether her new partner changes her risk of preeclampsia, since her partner's previous partner had it. What does the chapter say about paternal contribution?
A) Paternal factors have no bearing on preeclampsia risk
B) Men who have fathered a preeclamptic pregnancy are nearly twice as likely to father another preeclamptic pregnancy with a different woman, regardless of the new partner's history
C) Risk is determined entirely by the woman's previous pregnancies
D) Risk is lower with a new partner because of prior sperm exposure
Answer: B
Rationale: "Men who have fathered a preeclamptic pregnancy are nearly twice as likely to father another preeclamptic pregnancy with a different woman, regardless of whether the new partner has a history of a preeclamptic pregnancy." The chapter also notes that "women with limited sperm exposure with the same partner before conception also have a greater risk."
Trap: (D) inverts the sperm-exposure finding. Limited exposure before conception raises risk; a new partner means less exposure, not more.

Subtopic: what prevents it and what does not

Q: A woman with chronic hypertension and a prior preeclamptic pregnancy is seen at 13 weeks. What preventive therapy does the chapter recommend?
A) Daily low-dose aspirin, 81 mg/day, begun between 12 and 28 weeks and ideally before 16 weeks
B) Salt and protein restriction
C) Vitamin C and E supplementation
D) Prophylactic magnesium sulfate in the second trimester
Answer: A
Rationale: "Low-dose aspirin has been found to reduce preeclampsia and adverse outcomes in selected high-risk women," with the recommendation that high-risk women "begin daily low-dose (81 mg/day) aspirin therapy between 12 and 28 weeks of gestation (ideally before 16 weeks)." One high-risk factor is enough; moderate-risk factors prompt consideration when more than one is present.
Trap: (B) and (C) are on the list of interventions that "demonstrated minimal to no benefit," along with zinc, magnesium, fish oil, diuretics, other antihypertensives, and heparin.

Q: A woman asks whether a blood test early in pregnancy can tell her whether she will develop preeclampsia. What does the chapter say?
A) First-trimester screening tests are recommended in routine practice
B) No reliable routine screening test has been developed; biomarkers such as PlGF and sFlt-1 precede the disease by weeks to months but remain investigational
C) Doppler studies reliably predict preeclampsia and are recommended for screening
D) A 24-hour urine protein at 12 weeks predicts later preeclampsia
Answer: B
Rationale: "No reliable test that can be used as a routine screening tool for predicting preeclampsia has yet been developed." Decreased VEGF and PlGF and increased sFlt-1 and sEng "have been found to precede the development of preeclampsia by weeks or months," but adding them to risk assessment "remains investigational," and "the use of first-trimester screening tests for predicting preeclampsia in clinical practice is not recommended."
Trap: (C) is specifically rejected: "data do not support the use of Doppler studies for routine screening."

Topic: hellp syndrome
Subtopic: what it is and how it presents

Q: What does the acronym HELLP stand for, and what kind of diagnosis is it?
A) Hemolysis, elevated liver enzymes, and low platelet count; a laboratory diagnosis for a variant of preeclampsia involving hepatic dysfunction
B) Hypertension, edema, liver failure, and low pressure; a clinical diagnosis
C) Hemorrhage, eclampsia, low lipids, and proteinuria; a radiologic diagnosis
D) Hemolysis, edema, low liver perfusion, and pain; a diagnosis of exclusion
Answer: A
Rationale: "HELLP syndrome is a laboratory diagnosis for a variant of preeclampsia that involves hepatic dysfunction, characterized by hemolysis, elevated liver enzymes, and low platelet count." The chapter stresses that "specific laboratory findings are needed to diagnose HELLP syndrome and distinguish it from other serious diseases that share the same signs and symptoms."
Trap: (D) calls it a diagnosis of exclusion. It is the opposite: a set of specific laboratory findings is what establishes it, which is why a woman with the symptoms and no labs drawn gets misdiagnosed.

Q: A woman at 34 weeks reports nausea, malaise, and epigastric pain. Her blood pressure is 138/88 mm Hg and her urine dipstick shows trace protein. Why should the nurse still consider HELLP syndrome?
A) Because HELLP cannot occur without severe hypertension
B) Because HELLP syndrome can develop in women who do not have hypertension or proteinuria, with blood pressure only mildly elevated in 15% to 50% of cases and proteinuria absent in more than 10%
C) Because trace proteinuria is diagnostic of HELLP
D) Because epigastric pain alone establishes the diagnosis
Answer: B
Rationale: The Nursing Alert states that "many women with HELLP syndrome may not have signs or symptoms of preeclampsia with severe features," that blood pressure "may be only mildly elevated in 15% to 50% of cases," and that proteinuria "has been reported to be absent in more than 10% of cases." As a result these women "are often misdiagnosed with a variety of other medical or surgical disorders."
Trap: (A) is the assumption that produces those misdiagnoses, and this stem is built exactly as an exam would build it — near-normal pressure, vague symptoms, late third trimester.

Subtopic: the laboratory picture

Q: How do the hemoglobin and hematocrit differ between preeclampsia and HELLP syndrome, and why?
A) They rise in preeclampsia from hemoconcentration and fall in HELLP from hemolysis
B) They fall in preeclampsia and rise in HELLP
C) They rise in both conditions
D) They are unchanged in both conditions
Answer: A
Rationale: Table 27.3 shows hemoglobin and hematocrit as "may increase" in preeclampsia — fluid leaves the intravascular space and concentrates the blood — and as decreased in HELLP, where red cells are being destroyed.
Trap: (B) is the mirror error, and it matters because a rising hematocrit reported as evidence of HELLP has the physiology exactly backwards.

Q: Which laboratory findings distinguish HELLP syndrome from preeclampsia on Table 27.3? Select all that apply.
A) Burr cells or schistocytes present
B) Total bilirubin above 1.2 mg/dL
C) Decreased fibrinogen with fibrin split products present
D) LDH above 600 U/L, and AST and ALT at least twice the upper limit of normal
E) Platelet count below 100,000
Answer: A, B, C, D
Rationale: The rows that separate HELLP are the hemolysis and consumption findings: falling hemoglobin and hematocrit, burr cells or schistocytes, bilirubin above 1.2 mg/dL, falling fibrinogen, present fibrin split products, and the markedly elevated LDH, AST, and ALT.
Trap: (E) is common to both. A platelet count under 100,000 is thrombocytopenia in either diagnosis, so it cannot be what tells them apart.

Q: Which outcomes does the chapter associate with HELLP syndrome?
A) A maternal mortality rate of approximately 1% and a preterm birth rate of approximately 70%, with most perinatal deaths before 28 weeks in association with placental abruption or severe fetal growth restriction
B) No increase in maternal mortality
C) A perinatal mortality rate under 1%
D) Term birth in most cases
Answer: A
Rationale: HELLP carries an increased risk for maternal death, a maternal mortality rate of approximately 1%, a perinatal mortality rate ranging from 7.4% to 34%, and a preterm birth rate of approximately 70% with 15% of those births before 28 weeks. Complications include pulmonary edema, acute renal failure, DIC, placental abruption, liver hemorrhage or failure, acute respiratory distress syndrome, sepsis, and stroke.
Trap: (D) contradicts the 70% preterm rate, which is what makes HELLP a neonatal problem as much as a maternal one.

Topic: magnesium sulfate administration
Subtopic: purpose and dosing

Q: A woman with preeclampsia with severe features is started on magnesium sulfate, and her blood pressure remains 162/108 mm Hg an hour later. How should the nurse interpret this?
A) The magnesium is failing and the dose should be increased
B) The magnesium is working as intended, because it prevents seizures rather than lowering blood pressure, and an antihypertensive is what is indicated
C) The blood pressure should be rechecked in 4 hours before acting
D) The magnesium should be discontinued as ineffective
Answer: B
Rationale: "Magnesium sulfate is the medication of choice for preventing and treating seizure activity (eclampsia)," and "contrary to popular belief, magnesium sulfate has little effect on maternal BP when administered in this manner." Box 27.3's teaching point says it directly: magnesium "is used to prevent seizures, not to decrease blood pressure."
Trap: (A) treats an unchanged pressure as treatment failure, which is the misconception the chapter names twice. A pressure at or above 160/110 calls for hydralazine, labetalol, or nifedipine.

Q: What is the standard intravenous regimen for magnesium sulfate in preeclampsia, and what is the therapeutic serum level?
A) A loading dose of 4 to 6 g over 15 to 30 minutes, then 1 to 2 g/h, with a therapeutic level of 4 to 7 mEq/L
B) A loading dose of 10 g intramuscularly, then 1 g/h, with a therapeutic level of 10 mEq/L
C) A loading dose of 2 g over 5 minutes, then 4 g/h, with a therapeutic level of 1 to 3 mEq/L
D) A continuous infusion of 1 g/h with no loading dose, titrated to blood pressure
Answer: A
Rationale: Magnesium is given "as a secondary infusion (piggyback) by a volumetric infusion pump," with "an initial loading dose of 4 to 6 g ... infused over 15 to 30 minutes" and maintenance at 1 to 2 g/h from 40 g in 1000 mL of lactated Ringer's solution. The therapeutic serum level is 4 to 7 mEq/L.
Trap: (B) describes the intramuscular route, which is "rarely given ... because the absorption rate cannot be controlled, injections are painful, and tissue necrosis may occur" — reserved for low-resource settings or transport.

Q: Why is magnesium sulfate designated a high-alert medication, and what documentation rule follows?
A) Because it has a heightened risk for causing significant client harm when administered in error; it should never be abbreviated anywhere in the medical record
B) Because it is a controlled substance requiring witness counts
C) Because it must be refrigerated
D) Because it interacts with all antihypertensives
Answer: A
Rationale: The Safety Alert states that magnesium sulfate "is considered a high-alert medication because it has a heightened risk for causing significant client harm when administered in error," with safety measures including detailed policies, protocols, standing orders, and thorough assessment and documentation, plus the rule to "never abbreviate magnesium sulfate" anywhere in the medical record.
Trap: (D) overstates one real interaction into a general rule. The specific interaction named is with nifedipine, where concurrent use can cause skeletal muscle blockade.

Q: A fetal tracing during a magnesium sulfate infusion shows minimal baseline variability. How should the nurse interpret this?
A) As an expected effect of magnesium that requires no further evaluation, since fetal levels approximate maternal levels
B) As a finding that should not be assumed to result from magnesium until other causes of fetal hypoxemia have been ruled out
C) As proof of fetal hypoxemia requiring immediate birth
D) As unrelated to magnesium, since the drug does not cross the placenta
Answer: B
Rationale: The Nursing Alert states: "Because fetal levels of magnesium approximate those of the mother, fetal sedation is possible. However, absent or minimal baseline variability should not be assumed to be the result of magnesium sulfate therapy until other causes of fetal hypoxemia have been ruled out."
Trap: (A) is built from a true premise — the drug does reach the fetus — and reaches the reassuring conclusion the alert forbids. (D) fails on the same premise in the other direction.

Topic: magnesium toxicity and monitoring
Subtopic: side effects versus toxicity

Q: A woman receiving magnesium sulfate reports feeling warm and flushed, is sweating, and complains of burning at the IV site. What should the nurse do?
A) Stop the infusion and notify the provider
B) Recognize these as expected side effects and continue the infusion while continuing to monitor
C) Administer calcium gluconate
D) Slow the infusion by half
Answer: B
Rationale: The expected side effects are "a feeling of warmth, flushing, diaphoresis, and burning at the IV site," along with sedation and nausea, which Box 27.3 tells the nurse to describe to the woman in advance. The signs of toxicity are different: "absent deep tendon reflexes, decreased respiratory rate, and decreased level of consciousness."
Trap: (A) treats discomfort as danger and stops a seizure-prevention drug for a predicted effect. The teaching in Box 27.3 exists precisely so these sensations are not mistaken for an emergency.

Q: A woman receiving magnesium sulfate has a respiratory rate of 10 breaths/min, absent deep tendon reflexes, and is difficult to rouse. What is the nurse's priority action?
A) Notify the provider and continue the infusion while awaiting orders
B) Discontinue the magnesium infusion immediately, then administer calcium gluconate intravenously
C) Administer calcium gluconate and continue the infusion
D) Increase the IV fluid rate to promote magnesium excretion
Answer: B
Rationale: The Medication Alert states: "If magnesium toxicity is suspected, prompt actions are needed to prevent respiratory or cardiac arrest. The magnesium infusion should be discontinued immediately. Calcium gluconate (antidote for magnesium sulfate) can be given intravenously."
Trap: (C) gives the antidote while the cause keeps infusing, and (A) is too slow for a drug whose next step is respiratory or cardiac arrest. Both halves of the action are required, in that order.

Subtopic: the monitoring parameters

Q: Why is urine output a central assessment during magnesium sulfate therapy, and what is the floor?
A) Because magnesium is excreted in the urine, so declining renal function causes toxicity; output should be at least 25 to 30 mL/h
B) Because magnesium causes diuresis; output above 200 mL/h is expected
C) Because urine output measures blood pressure control; output should exceed 100 mL/h
D) Because proteinuria is quantified hourly; any output is acceptable
Answer: A
Rationale: "Because magnesium is excreted in the urine, accurate measurements of maternal urine output must be obtained. If renal function declines, excretion of magnesium sulfate is inadequate, resulting in magnesium toxicity." Box 27.3 sets output at "at least 25–30 mL/h" and makes less than that reportable.
Trap: (B) reverses the relationship. Oliguria in a woman on magnesium is both a preeclampsia finding and the mechanism by which she becomes toxic.

Q: Why does Box 27.3 restrict total intravenous and oral fluids to no more than 125 mL/h?
A) To reduce the risk of pulmonary edema
B) To concentrate the magnesium and improve its anticonvulsant effect
C) To prevent dilution of the antihypertensive medications
D) To limit proteinuria
Answer: A
Rationale: The chapter states the ceiling twice: "To reduce the risk of pulmonary edema, total intravenous (IV) and oral fluids should not exceed 125 mL/h." Increased capillary permeability from arteriolar vasospasm is what makes these women vulnerable to fluid overload.
Trap: (B) invents a pharmacologic rationale for what is a fluid-balance precaution. The same physiology that concentrates the blood also makes the lungs leak.

Q: Which findings are on Box 27.3's list of conditions to report during magnesium sulfate therapy? Select all that apply.
A) Systolic blood pressure 160 mm Hg or higher, or diastolic 110 mm Hg or higher
B) Respiratory rate below 12 breaths/min
C) Urinary output below 25 to 30 mL/h
D) Headache, visual disturbances, decreased level of consciousness, or epigastric pain
E) Flushing and a sensation of warmth during the loading dose
Answer: A, B, C, D
Rationale: Box 27.3's reportable conditions are severe-range blood pressures, a respiratory rate under 12, urine output under 25 to 30 mL/h, headache, visual disturbances, decreased level of consciousness or epigastric pain, increasing severity or loss of deep tendon reflexes, increasing edema and proteinuria, and any abnormal laboratory values.
Trap: (E) is the expected reaction the same box tells the nurse to warn the woman about in advance, which is what makes it the plausible extra selection here.

Topic: pharmacologic control of blood pressure
Subtopic: when to treat and to what target

Q: At what blood pressure are antihypertensive medications indicated in preeclampsia, and what is the treatment goal?
A) Indicated when systolic exceeds 160 or diastolic exceeds 110; goal is a systolic of 140 to 150 and a diastolic of 90 to 100
B) Indicated when systolic exceeds 140 or diastolic exceeds 90; goal is a normal blood pressure of 120/80
C) Indicated at any elevation; goal is the lowest pressure achievable
D) Indicated only after a seizure has occurred
Answer: A
Rationale: "Antihypertensive medications are indicated when systolic BP exceeds 160 mm Hg or diastolic BP exceeds 110 mm Hg," and "the goal of antihypertensive therapy is to achieve a systolic pressure of 140 to 150 mm Hg and a diastolic pressure of 90 to 100 mm Hg."
Trap: (B) and (C) both aim at a normal pressure, which the chapter warns against: "antihypertensive therapy must not decrease the arterial pressure too much or too rapidly" because uteroplacental perfusion depends on maternal pressure. Hydralazine's own fetal-effect column names the consequence: late decelerations and bradycardia if the maternal diastolic falls below 90.

Subtopic: the three drugs and their specific cautions

Q: Hydralazine is ordered for a woman whose preeclampsia has produced a persistent headache and a pulse of 120 beats/min. Why should the nurse question this order?
A) Hydralazine is avoided in women with persistent headaches and used with caution in the presence of maternal tachycardia
B) Hydralazine cannot be given intravenously
C) Hydralazine is contraindicated in all women with preeclampsia
D) Hydralazine must not be given with magnesium sulfate
Answer: A
Rationale: Table 27.5's nursing actions for hydralazine include "use with caution in the presence of maternal tachycardia" and "avoid in women with persistent headaches." Its maternal effects include headache, flushing, palpitations, and tachycardia, so it would both worsen the tachycardia and mask a preeclampsia sign. Doses are spaced at least 20 minutes apart, with a maximum of 25 mg in 60 minutes.
Trap: (D) names nifedipine's interaction. Nifedipine and magnesium together can cause skeletal muscle blockade; hydralazine carries no such warning here.

Q: A woman with preeclampsia and a history of asthma has a blood pressure of 168/112 mm Hg, with a pulse of 56 beats/min. Which order should the nurse question?
A) Hydralazine intravenously
B) Labetalol intravenously
C) Oral nifedipine
D) Magnesium sulfate infusion
Answer: B
Rationale: Table 27.5 directs the nurse to "hold dose (if giving IV) if maternal pulse is <60 beats/min" and to "do not use in women with asthma, heart disease, or congestive heart failure." This woman fails both checks. Labetalol's other limits are 80 mg maximum per single dose and 300 mg total in 24 hours.
Trap: (D) is the seizure prophylaxis she also needs, and it is unaffected by asthma or bradycardia — questioning it would leave her unprotected against the outcome magnesium exists to prevent.

Q: Nifedipine is ordered for a woman with severe-range hypertension and no IV access. Which statements about this order are accurate? Select all that apply.
A) Nifedipine is recommended for treating a hypertensive emergency when IV access is not available
B) It should be given by the oral route only
C) The sublingual form is avoided because of the risk for profound maternal hypotension
D) Concurrent use with magnesium sulfate is avoided because skeletal muscle blockade can result
E) The maximum dose is 300 mg within 24 hours
Answer: A, B, C, D
Rationale: Table 27.5 recommends nifedipine when IV access is not available, by the oral route only, avoiding the sublingual form and concurrent magnesium, and avoiding it in women with tachycardia and palpitations. Its maximum dose is 50 mg within 60 minutes.
Trap: (E) is labetalol's 24-hour ceiling. Each of the three drugs has its own maximum, and they are easy to reassign: hydralazine 25 mg in 60 minutes, nifedipine 50 mg in 60 minutes, labetalol 80 mg per dose and 300 mg in 24 hours.

Topic: expectant management and precautionary measures
Subtopic: managing before 34 weeks

Q: A woman at 31 weeks has preeclampsia with severe features and no indication for immediate birth. What does expectant management involve?
A) Outpatient management with weekly visits
B) Hospitalization at a tertiary or quaternary care facility, with oral antihypertensives to maintain a systolic of 140 to 155 and a diastolic of 90 to 105, commonly labetalol or nifedipine
C) Immediate cesarean birth regardless of fetal status
D) Magnesium sulfate alone, with no antihypertensive therapy
Answer: B
Rationale: Women "less than 34 0/7 weeks of gestation and have no indication for giving birth immediately" are hospitalized at a facility able to provide both maternal and neonatal intensive care, on "oral antihypertensive medications to maintain a systolic BP between 140 and 155 mm Hg and a diastolic BP between 90 and 105 mm Hg. Labetalol and nifedipine are medications commonly prescribed for this purpose."
Trap: (A) misses the level of care the chapter specifies, and it is worth knowing that "most women who are managed expectantly develop a maternal or fetal indication for giving birth within 2 weeks."

Q: Which findings are indications for immediate birth in a woman being managed expectantly? Select all that apply.
A) Uncontrolled severe-range blood pressures
B) Persistent headaches, epigastric or right upper quadrant pain, or visual disturbances
C) HELLP syndrome, eclampsia, or pulmonary edema
D) Suspected acute placental abruption or vaginal bleeding in the absence of placenta previa
E) A single blood pressure reading of 142/92 mm Hg
Answer: A, B, C, D
Rationale: The chapter's list includes uncontrolled severe-range blood pressures, persistent headaches, epigastric or right upper quadrant pain, visual disturbances or altered sensorium, stroke, myocardial infarction, HELLP syndrome, new or worsening renal dysfunction, eclampsia, pulmonary edema, suspected abruption or bleeding without previa, abnormal fetal testing, fetal death, and a fetus without expectation for survival.
Trap: (E) is a diagnostic-threshold reading, not a severe one, and expectant management exists precisely to carry a pregnancy through readings like it.

Q: Corticosteroids are ordered for a woman at 30 weeks with preeclampsia. Which regimen and rationale match the chapter?
A) Betamethasone 12 mg intramuscularly repeated in 24 hours, or dexamethasone 6 mg intramuscularly in four doses 12 hours apart, with neonatal benefit maximized when the interval between the first dose and birth exceeds 48 hours
B) Betamethasone 6 mg orally daily for 4 days
C) Dexamethasone 12 mg intravenously once
D) Corticosteroids are withheld in preeclampsia
Answer: A
Rationale: Corticosteroids are given to enhance fetal lung maturation at 34 weeks or less: "the dose of betamethasone is 12 mg intramuscularly, repeated in 24 hours, while dexamethasone is given intramuscularly as four doses of 6 mg each, 12 hours apart. Neonatal benefit is maximized when the interval between the first dose and birth is longer than 48 hours."
Trap: (D) contradicts the plan, but the more useful caution is the chapter's own: "delaying birth to reach maximum corticosteroid benefit may not be advisable if the maternal or fetal condition is worsening."

Q: Which precautionary measures does Box 27.2 specify for a hospitalized woman with preeclampsia? Select all that apply.
A) A quiet, nonstimulating environment with subdued lighting
B) Suction and oxygen equipment tested and ready to use, with the call button within reach
C) Hydralazine, labetalol, nifedipine, magnesium sulfate, and calcium gluconate available on the unit
D) An emergency birth pack easily accessible
E) Ambulation in the hallway every 2 hours to prevent thromboembolism
Answer: A, B, C, D
Rationale: Box 27.2 lists a quiet, nonstimulating environment with subdued lighting; seizure precautions including tested suction and oxygen equipment and a call button within reach; the five emergency medications; and an accessible emergency birth pack.
Trap: (E) contradicts the environment the rest of the box is built to create. A woman with preeclampsia with severe features is "maintained on bed rest with the side rails up in a quiet, darkened environment."

Q: What is the focus of intrapartum nursing care for a woman with preeclampsia, and what specific complication is assessed for?
A) Early identification of fetal heart rate abnormalities and prevention of maternal complications, with assessment for signs of placental abruption such as a tense, tender uterus
B) Maximizing the woman's mobility during labor
C) Restricting fetal monitoring to intermittent auscultation
D) Preparing for cesarean birth in every case
Answer: A
Rationale: Intrapartum nursing is directed toward "the early identification of fetal heart rate (FHR) abnormalities and the prevention of maternal complications." Continuous FHR and contraction monitoring is initiated, "and the woman is assessed for signs of placental abruption such as a tense, tender uterus."
Trap: (C) reverses the monitoring plan, and the abruption link in the correct answer is the chapter's connection to the blueprint's abruption topic: Chapter 28 names maternal hypertension as the most consistently identified risk factor for abruption.

Topic: eclampsia seizure management
Subtopic: recognizing and responding

Q: Which premonitory signs of eclampsia does the chapter name, and what caution accompanies them?
A) Persistent occipital or frontal headache, blurred vision, photophobia, epigastric or right upper quadrant pain, and altered mental status; but eclampsia can develop in women with absent or minimal hypertension, no proteinuria, and no edema
B) Only severe hypertension reliably precedes a seizure
C) Proteinuria of 5 g or more always precedes a seizure
D) Premonitory signs are absent in all cases
Answer: A
Rationale: The premonitory signs are "persistent occipital or frontal headache, blurred vision, photophobia, epigastric or right upper quadrant abdominal pain, and altered mental status," and the chapter warns that "eclampsia can develop in women with absent or minimal hypertension, no proteinuria, and no edema."
Trap: (B) is the assumption that makes an eclamptic seizure a surprise. The symptom cluster, not the pressure, is the warning.

Q: A woman with preeclampsia begins a tonic-clonic seizure. What are the nurse's immediate actions?
A) Leave to summon the provider, then return with an oral airway to insert
B) Keep the airway patent by turning her head to one side, call for assistance without leaving the bedside, raise and pad the side rails, and observe and record the convulsion activity including its time of onset and duration
C) Restrain her arms and legs to prevent injury
D) Administer lorazepam immediately
Answer: B
Rationale: Nursing actions during a seizure are "directed toward ensuring a patent airway and client safety": turn the head to one side, place a pillow under one shoulder or back if possible, "call for assistance. Do not leave bedside," raise and pad the side rails, and observe and record the activity. "It is important to note the time of onset and duration of the seizure."
Trap: (C) is the action that belongs to a different moment: restraints "may be necessary temporarily" in the postictal phase, when a woman "may be very confused and combative." Padding the rails is the seizure-phase safety measure, because "women with eclampsia have been known to sustain fractures from falling out of bed."

Q: The seizure has ended. What should the nurse do first, and why?
A) Lower the head of the bed and turn the woman onto her side, which helps prevent aspiration of vomitus
B) Sit her upright to improve ventilation
C) Insert an indwelling urinary catheter
D) Obtain a blood pressure before positioning her
Answer: A
Rationale: Immediately after the seizure "the nurse should lower the head of the bed and turn the woman onto her side. This helps prevent aspiration of vomitus." Airway, breathing, and pulse are then assessed; if apnea is present the nurse opens the airway, begins bag-mask ventilation, and activates the code, and if respirations are present, secretions are suctioned and oxygen is given at 10 L/min by face mask.
Trap: (C) and (D) are both on the post-convulsion list and both come after positioning. The woman has just vomited or may vomit, and the position is what protects her airway while the rest is done.

Subtopic: drugs and decisions after the seizure

Q: A woman has an eclamptic seizure while already receiving magnesium sulfate. What does the chapter direct?
A) Discontinue the magnesium and switch to diazepam
B) Administer additional magnesium sulfate, since magnesium is the drug of choice for treating eclamptic seizures and preventing repeated seizures
C) Administer phenytoin as first-line therapy
D) Withhold all anticonvulsants until a serum magnesium level returns
Answer: B
Rationale: "If eclampsia develops after initiating magnesium sulfate therapy, additional magnesium sulfate should be administered," and "magnesium sulfate is the drug of choice for treating eclamptic seizures and preventing repeated seizures." One of its advantages over diazepam "is that it reduces the risk for aspiration because it does not depress the gag reflex."
Trap: (A) switches to the drug the chapter compares unfavorably with magnesium, and for a specific reason — the gag reflex, in a woman who may vomit.

Q: A woman continues to have seizures despite adequate and therapeutic doses of magnesium sulfate. What is the named second-line drug?
A) Diazepam 10 mg intravenously
B) Lorazepam 2 mg intravenously over 3 to 5 minutes
C) Phenobarbital 60 mg intramuscularly
D) Calcium gluconate 1 g intravenously
Answer: B
Rationale: "Occasionally a woman will experience recurrent eclamptic seizures while receiving adequate and therapeutic doses of magnesium sulfate. If this occurs, lorazepam (Ativan) 2 mg, given intravenously over 3 to 5 minutes, may be administered."
Trap: (D) is the antidote for magnesium toxicity, not an anticonvulsant, and giving it to a seizing woman on therapeutic magnesium removes the protection she has.

Q: After an eclamptic seizure at 34 weeks, the fetal tracing shows bradycardia and late decelerations, and the uterus is hypertonic. How should the nurse interpret this, and what does it mean for the route of birth?
A) These findings mandate immediate cesarean birth, since eclampsia is an indication for it
B) These findings usually resolve soon after the seizure ends and the woman's hypoxia is corrected; eclampsia alone is not an indication for immediate cesarean birth, and the route is determined partly by gestational age, fetal presentation, and cervical findings
C) The tracing changes indicate fetal death
D) The hypertonic uterus rules out vaginal birth
Answer: B
Rationale: "During a seizure the uterus becomes hypercontractile and hypertonic," and the tracing may show "bradycardia, late decelerations, absent or minimal baseline variability, or compensatory tachycardia. These findings usually resolve soon after the seizure ends and the woman's hypoxia is corrected." And: "Eclampsia alone is not an indication for immediate cesarean birth."
Trap: (A) is the reflex response to a frightening tracing in a frightening situation, and it is the specific claim the chapter denies. The chapter also notes that membranes may have ruptured or the cervix dilated rapidly during the seizure, so birth may be imminent by the vaginal route.

Q: A woman with eclampsia and a platelet count of 62,000 requires cesarean birth. What anesthetic considerations apply?
A) Regional anesthesia is preferred at any platelet count
B) Regional anesthesia is not recommended with coagulopathy because of hemorrhagic complications and epidural hematoma risk; it is considered acceptable with an exceptionally low hematoma risk at a platelet count of 70,000 or greater, so general anesthesia will be used here
C) Spinal anesthesia is safe below 70,000 but epidural anesthesia is not
D) No anesthetic is required if magnesium sulfate is infusing
Answer: B
Rationale: "Regional anesthesia is not recommended for eclamptic women with coagulopathy because of the potential for hemorrhagic complications and the risk of epidural hematoma," and "epidural or spinal anesthesia is considered acceptable and the risk of epidural hematoma exceptionally low when the platelet count is 70 × 109/L or greater." Below that threshold, "general anesthesia will be used for cesarean birth."
Trap: (C) splits the two regional techniques, which the chapter treats together against the same platelet threshold.

Topic: chronic hypertension in pregnancy
Subtopic: epidemiology and risks

Q: Which statements about chronic hypertension in pregnancy does the chapter support? Select all that apply.
A) It affects approximately 1% to 5% of all pregnancies
B) More than 90% of affected women have primary or essential hypertension
C) Black women are much more likely to have a pregnancy complicated by chronic hypertension than women of other races or ethnicities
D) Maternal risks include superimposed preeclampsia, stroke, acute kidney injury, heart failure, placental abruption, and death
E) The incidence is expected to fall as more women delay childbearing
Answer: A, B, C, D
Rationale: Chronic hypertension affects approximately 1% to 5% of pregnancies, with more than 90% primary or essential and the remaining 10% secondary to renal or collagen vascular disease, an endocrine disorder, or arteriosclerosis. Maternal risks are as listed, and fetal risks are growth restriction, death, and preterm birth.
Trap: (E) reverses the projection: the number is expected to rise "as more women delay childbearing and are obese."

Subtopic: the chap reversal

Q: A woman with mild chronic hypertension enters pregnancy taking labetalol. Based on the CHAP trial and current recommendations, what should happen to her therapy?
A) The medication should be stopped and restarted only if the blood pressure becomes severely elevated
B) She should remain on the medication, with a treatment threshold of 140/90 mm Hg and a treatment goal below 140/90 mm Hg
C) The medication should be changed to an ACE inhibitor
D) Therapy should be deferred until after 20 weeks
Answer: B
Rationale: Following CHAP, ACOG and SMFM "now recommend that a BP of 140/90 mm Hg be used as the treatment threshold for chronic hypertension in pregnant women, rather than the previously recommended threshold of 160/110 mm Hg," that women "taking anti-hypertensive medication when pregnancy begins remain on the medication," and that "the treatment goal for pregnant women with mild chronic hypertension be <140/90 mmHg."
Trap: (A) is exactly the practice CHAP changed, and it is still the answer in older texts — which is what makes it the distractor to recognize.

Q: What did the CHAP trial find for women with mild chronic hypertension who received antihypertensive therapy during pregnancy?
A) No difference in outcomes, which is why treatment remains optional
B) Less preeclampsia with severe features, less placental abruption, fewer fetal or neonatal deaths, and fewer medically indicated preterm births before 35 weeks
C) More small-for-gestational-age infants with no maternal benefit
D) A reduction in cesarean birth rates only
Answer: B
Rationale: Women treated for mild chronic hypertension "reduced their risk for maternal and perinatal morbidity. They experienced less preeclampsia with severe features, placental abruption, and fetal or neonatal death and fewer medically indicated preterm births at less than 35 weeks gestation."
Trap: (A) states the pre-CHAP rationale — that "evidence of maternal or perinatal treatment benefit was lacking" — as though it still stood. The chapter says outright that "this practice is changing."

Q: Which medication does the chapter name as most often recommended for treating chronic hypertension in pregnancy, and what may be added if control is inadequate?
A) Labetalol, with a thiazide diuretic or nifedipine added if the blood pressure is not adequately controlled on the maximum dose
B) Hydralazine, with magnesium sulfate added
C) An ACE inhibitor, with a beta blocker added
D) Nifedipine sublingually, with labetalol added
Answer: A
Rationale: "Labetalol is the medication most often recommended for treating chronic hypertension in pregnancy. A thiazide diuretic or nifedipine may be added if the BP is not adequately controlled on the maximum dose of labetalol."
Trap: (D) reaches for the sublingual route that Table 27.5 forbids outright because of the risk for profound maternal hypotension.

Q: A woman with chronic hypertension asks whether she can breastfeed while taking her medication. What does the chapter say?
A) Breastfeeding is contraindicated with any antihypertensive
B) Women with chronic hypertension can breastfeed if they desire; all antihypertensives appear in breast milk to some degree, labetalol has a low concentration and is considered safe, and diuretics may decrease milk production
C) Only diuretics are safe during breastfeeding
D) Breastfeeding is permitted only after antihypertensives are discontinued
Answer: B
Rationale: "Women with chronic hypertension can breastfeed if they desire," and "all antihypertensive medications are present to some degree in breast milk. Labetalol has a low concentration in breast milk and is considered safe." Little is known about calcium channel blockers, but no apparent infant side effects have been noted, and "concentrations of diuretic agents in breast milk are low, but their use may cause a decrease in milk production."
Trap: (C) singles out the one class with a stated drawback for lactation. Presence in milk and safety in milk are different questions, and the chapter answers both.

Topic: postpartum care after a hypertensive pregnancy
Subtopic: the first days

Q: A woman with preeclampsia with severe features gives birth. What happens to the magnesium sulfate infusion, and when do signs typically resolve?
A) The infusion is stopped at birth, since the placenta has been delivered; signs resolve within 6 hours
B) The infusion is continued after birth for seizure prophylaxis, usually for 24 hours; signs and symptoms usually resolve within 48 hours, with diuresis and decreased edema as clinical signs of resolution
C) The infusion is continued for 7 days
D) The infusion is replaced by oral magnesium at discharge
Answer: B
Rationale: "The magnesium sulfate infusion is continued after birth for seizure prophylaxis as ordered, usually for 24 hours," and "the signs and symptoms of preeclampsia usually resolve within 48 hours after birth. Clinical signs that demonstrate resolution of preeclampsia include diuresis and decreased edema."
Trap: (A) applies "the disease begins to resolve after the placenta has been expelled" as though resolution were instantaneous. It is also why eclamptic seizures still occur postpartum — many cases occur more than 48 hours after birth.

Q: A woman with no history of hypertension develops a blood pressure of 168/110 mm Hg with a severe headache 3 days after giving birth. How should this be managed?
A) As a tension headache, treated with analgesia and reassurance
B) As new-onset postpartum preeclampsia, with IV magnesium sulfate for seizure prophylaxis
C) As an expected postpartum blood pressure fluctuation
D) As a contraindication to breastfeeding
Answer: B
Rationale: "Some women develop signs and symptoms of preeclampsia for the first time after giving birth. Women who develop hypertension and severe features of preeclampsia such as headaches or blurred vision or severe hypertension should be placed on IV magnesium sulfate for seizure prophylaxis." The chapter also teaches women to report persistent severe headaches unresponsive to maximum analgesic doses, visual disturbances, or epigastric pain after discharge.
Trap: (A) is the reading a woman without a hypertensive history invites, and it is why the discharge teaching names the specific symptoms rather than trusting recognition.

Q: A woman's blood pressure remains elevated 2 days after birth and she requests ibuprofen for afterpains. What caution applies?
A) None; NSAIDs are preferred in hypertensive women
B) Nonsteroidal antiinflammatory medications should be used with caution when hypertension persists more than 1 day after birth, because they can contribute to an increase in blood pressure
C) NSAIDs are contraindicated in all postpartum women
D) NSAIDs interact with magnesium sulfate
Answer: B
Rationale: "Nonsteroidal antiinflammatory pain medications should be used with caution when hypertension persists for more than 1 day after birth because these agents can contribute to an increase in BP."
Trap: (C) overstates a caution into a prohibition. The condition is persistent hypertension, not the postpartum state.

Q: What blood pressure follow-up does the chapter recommend after a hypertensive pregnancy?
A) Monitoring in the hospital or equivalent outpatient surveillance for at least 72 hours after birth, a recheck at 7 to 10 days postpartum, and antihypertensive therapy for a pressure of 150/100 or higher on two occasions 4 to 6 hours apart
B) A single recheck at the 6-week postpartum visit
C) Daily home monitoring for 6 months in all women
D) No follow-up if the pressure is normal at discharge
Answer: A
Rationale: "It is recommended that BP be monitored in the hospital or that equivalent outpatient surveillance be performed for at least 72 hours after birth. The BP should then be rechecked at 7 to 10 days postpartum. Women with a BP of 150/100 mm Hg or higher (on two occasions that are 4 to 6 hours apart) should be placed on an antihypertensive medication."
Trap: (B) is the ordinary postpartum schedule, and it is too late for a window in which eclampsia still occurs and new preeclampsia still appears.

Q: A woman who had early-onset preeclampsia asks about her long-term health. What should the nurse teach?
A) That preeclampsia has no long-term implications once the blood pressure normalizes
B) That she has a significantly increased risk of preeclampsia in a future pregnancy and an increased risk of chronic hypertension and cardiovascular disease later in life, and that lifestyle changes — maintaining a healthy weight, increasing physical activity, and avoiding smoking — are recommended
C) That future pregnancies are contraindicated
D) That the risk applies only to women who had eclampsia
Answer: B
Rationale: Women with preeclampsia with severe features "have a significantly increased risk for developing preeclampsia in a future pregnancy, especially women who had early-onset (diagnosed during the second trimester) preeclampsia," and an "increased risk for developing chronic hypertension and cardiovascular disease later in life," so they "should be educated about lifestyle changes." Even when normotensive in a later pregnancy, they face greater risk of preterm birth, a small-for-gestational-age infant, and perinatal death.
Trap: (A) takes "the disease resolves after birth" as the whole story. It resolves as an acute illness and leaves a lifetime risk marker behind.
