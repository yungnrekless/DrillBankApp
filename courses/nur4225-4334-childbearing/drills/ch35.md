# Chapter 35 drills — Acquired Problems of the Newborn

Authored from `sources/Ch35_Fact_Bank.md` (Lowdermilk, Cashion, Alden, Olshansky & Perry, 13th ed., ch. 35, pp. 771–792).

Blueprint weight is **~6 questions of 200 (3.0%), depth `standard`** — topics
*Newborn Complications, RDS, GBS, NAS, sepsis, medications*. The Week 1–4 midterm
reading guide does not name this chapter, so the midterm does not reach it; the
blueprint weights it at six, so the final does. That earns **41 drills across 9
topics**.

The organizing insight the fact bank names is the spine of this set: **the
newborn's immune system is not merely weak, it is missing specific parts, and
every infection in this chapter exploits one of them.** IgG crosses the placenta
and then falls to a nadir at 3 to 4 months; IgA and IgM do not cross at all;
phagocytosis is inefficient and complement is inadequate. That is why sepsis
presents without localizing signs, why breast milk — which supplies IgA — is
treated as an intervention, and why hand hygiene is called "the single most
important measure."

Every distinction the chapter states in both directions is drilled from both
ends: early-onset and late-onset sepsis, early-onset and late-onset group B
streptococcal disease, the acute and subacute signs of neonatal abstinence
syndrome, and the fetal alcohol spectrum diagnoses that do and do not include
facial features.

**A scope note on respiratory distress syndrome.** The disease itself is
Chapter 34's; this chapter's RDS section is about **RDS in the infant of a
diabetic mother**, and that is what is drilled here.

**What is left out.** Birth trauma — soft tissue, skeletal, peripheral, and
central nervous system injuries — the infant-of-a-diabetic-mother complications
other than RDS, the viral infection catalogue including CMV, hepatitis, HIV, and
HSV, the other bacterial, protozoal, and fungal infections, and the
substance-effects table that the chapter floats across four pages.

Topic: newborn susceptibility to infection
Subtopic: what the immune system is missing

Q: Which immunoglobulin crosses the placenta, and when does it reach its nadir?
A) IgG, reaching a nadir at 3 to 4 months of age for term newborns and at 3 months for preterm infants, with virtually all of the infant's IgG endogenous by 10 to 12 months
B) IgA, reaching a nadir at 6 months
C) IgM, reaching a nadir at 1 month
D) All immunoglobulins cross equally
Answer: A
Rationale: "Immunoglobulin G (IgG) crosses the placenta to provide protection to the newborn. IgG values typically reach a nadir at 3 to 4 months of age for term newborns and at 3 months for preterm infants. By 10 to 12 months of age, virtually all of the infant's IgG is endogenous."
Trap: (D) is contradicted by the next sentence in the chapter: "IgA and IgM do not cross the placenta, and both require time to reach optimal levels after birth." That gap is what colostrum partly fills.

Q: Besides immunoglobulin deficits, what other immune limitations does the chapter name in the newborn?
A) Phagocytosis is less efficient and serum complement levels are inadequate, with complement involved in immunologic reactions that kill or lyse bacteria and enhance phagocytosis; dysmaturity from growth restriction and preterm or postterm birth further compromises the immune system
B) The newborn has no white blood cells
C) Only the spleen is immature
D) The newborn's immune system is fully mature at birth
Answer: A
Rationale: "Phagocytosis is less efficient. Serum complement levels are inadequate," with complement "involved in immunologic reactions, some of which kill or lyse bacteria and enhance phagocytosis." And: "Dysmaturity seen with intrauterine growth restriction (IUGR) and preterm and postterm birth further compromises the neonate's immune system."
Trap: (D) would make the rest of the chapter inexplicable — the specific missing pieces are what determine how infection presents and how it is prevented.

Subtopic: how infection is acquired

Q: Which routes of infection acquisition does the chapter describe, and what characterizes each? Select all that apply.
A) Prenatal or congenital, with organisms placentally transferred or transmitted from infected amniotic fluid, or ascending from the vagina through the cervix into the uterus
B) Intrapartum, through contact with an infected birth canal, with the upper airway and gastrointestinal tract as the principal pathways for generalized infections and the conjunctiva and oral cavity as the usual sites of local infection
C) Postnatal, acquired during resuscitation, through foreign objects such as indwelling catheters or endotracheal tubes, or through breastfeeding
D) Health care–associated, transferred by the hands of parents or health care personnel or spread from contaminated equipment, with the umbilicus a receptive site for cutaneous infection leading to sepsis
E) Through the placenta only
Answer: A, B, C, D
Rationale: The chapter describes prenatal, intrapartum, and postnatal routes with exactly these features, and names health care–associated transfer through hands and equipment, with the umbilicus as "a receptive site for cutaneous infection leading to sepsis."
Trap: (E) reduces four routes to one, and it would miss the two — intrapartum and postnatal — that nursing care most directly affects.

Q: What consequences does the chapter attribute to prenatally acquired infection?
A) Miscarriage, preterm birth, stillbirth, and neonatal infection at birth or in the postnatal period
B) Only neonatal conjunctivitis
C) No fetal consequences
D) Only postnatal infection
Answer: A
Rationale: Prenatal infection with organisms such as herpes simplex virus, cytomegalovirus, or rubella can cause "miscarriage, preterm birth, stillbirth, and neonatal infection at birth or in the postnatal period."
Trap: (B) names a local intrapartum infection — the conjunctiva and oral cavity are "the usual sites of local infection" from contact with an infected birth canal, a different route with a different consequence.

Topic: early versus late onset sepsis
Subtopic: the two patterns

Q: How does the chapter define early-onset and late-onset neonatal sepsis?
A) Early-onset is invasive bacterial infection of the blood or cerebrospinal fluid occurring within the first 72 hours of life; late-onset occurs after 72 hours and up to 30 days of life
B) Early-onset is within 24 hours; late-onset is after 24 hours
C) Early-onset is within the first week; late-onset after the first month
D) The two terms are interchangeable
Answer: A
Rationale: Early-onset sepsis is "invasive bacterial infection of the blood and/or cerebrospinal fluid (CSF) that occurs within the first 72 hours of life," and late-onset sepsis occurs "after 72 hours and up to 30 days of life."
Trap: (B) moves the boundary to the first day, which would reclassify most early-onset cases and obscure the fact that the 72-hour line separates maternally transmitted infection from postnatally acquired infection.

Q: Which organisms are associated with early-onset versus late-onset sepsis?
A) Early-onset: group B streptococci, Escherichia coli, and coagulase-negative staphylococci; late-onset: coagulase-negative staphylococci, Klebsiella organisms, enterobacter, E. coli, or Candida species
B) Early-onset: Candida only; late-onset: group B streptococci
C) Both are caused only by group B streptococci
D) Organisms are identical in both
Answer: A
Rationale: Early-onset sepsis is associated with "group B streptococci (GBS), Escherichia coli, and coagulase-negative staphylococci," and late-onset with "coagulase-negative staphylococci, Klebsiella organisms, enterobacter, E. coli, or Candida species." Early-onset is "more often associated with maternal intrapartum transmission of invasive organisms," while late-onset "can include maternally derived infection or health care–acquired infection."
Trap: (D) collapses a distinction that determines the empiric antibiotic choice, which differs between the two.

Q: What does the chapter identify as the most significant risk factor for late-onset sepsis, and what else contributes?
A) Prematurity is the most significant risk factor; invasive procedures performed on a newborn such as intubation, catheterization, and surgery also contribute
B) Maternal fever is the most significant risk factor
C) Male sex is the most significant risk factor
D) There are no identified risk factors for late-onset sepsis
Answer: A
Rationale: "Prematurity is the most significant risk factor for late-onset sepsis," along with "invasive procedures that are performed on a newborn (e.g., intubation, catheterization, and surgery)." Entry sites include "the umbilical stump; the skin; mucous membranes of the eye, nose, pharynx, and ear; and internal systems such as the respiratory, nervous, urinary, and GI systems."
Trap: (B) is an intrapartum risk factor for early-onset disease, and the two lists sort by whether the exposure came from the mother or from the nursery.

Q: Which intrapartum factors appear in Table 35.2 as risk factors for neonatal sepsis? Select all that apply.
A) Prelabor rupture of membranes and rupture of membranes for more than 18 hours
B) Maternal group B streptococcus colonization and maternal fever
C) Chorioamnionitis and prolonged labor
D) Preterm labor and maternal urinary tract infection
E) Maternal age over 35
Answer: A, B, C, D
Rationale: Table 35.2's intrapartum column lists prelabor rupture of membranes, maternal GBS colonization, maternal fever, chorioamnionitis, prolonged labor, rupture of membranes over 18 hours, preterm labor, and maternal urinary tract infection.
Trap: (E) is not in the table. The maternal column names low socioeconomic status, poor prenatal care, poor nutrition, and substance abuse, and the neonatal column names multiple gestation, male sex, birth asphyxia, meconium aspiration, congenital anomalies of skin or mucous membranes, galactosemia, absence of spleen, low birth weight or preterm birth, malnourishment, prolonged hospitalization, and invasive procedures.

Q: Why has early-onset group B streptococcal sepsis declined?
A) Because of the use of intrapartum antibiotics for mothers who tested positive for GBS, which has significantly reduced the number of infants born with this life-threatening infection
B) Because GBS no longer colonizes pregnant women
C) Because of newborn antibiotic prophylaxis at birth
D) Because of routine cesarean birth for colonized women
Answer: A
Rationale: "Due to the use of intrapartum antibiotics for mothers who tested positive for GBS, there has been a significant reduction in the number of infants born with this life-threatening infection."
Trap: (C) moves the intervention from mother to infant. The prophylaxis works because it is given in labor, before the organism is transmitted.

Topic: recognizing neonatal sepsis
Subtopic: the assessment problem

Q: What does the chapter say about the signs of neonatal sepsis?
A) As a general rule, the signs of early-onset sepsis are nonspecific and nonlocalizing, differential diagnosis can be difficult because the signs resemble noninfectious problems such as anemia or hypoglycemia, and late-onset signs are also nonspecific and similar
B) The signs are highly specific and localize to the site of infection
C) Fever is always present
D) Signs appear only after 72 hours
Answer: A
Rationale: "As a general rule, the signs of early-onset sepsis are nonspecific and nonlocalizing," and "a differential diagnosis can be difficult because signs of sepsis are similar to signs of noninfectious neonatal problems such as anemia or hypoglycemia." Late-onset signs "are also nonspecific and similar to the signs of early-onset sepsis."
Trap: (C) expects an adult presentation. The chapter's general list names "temperature instability (usually hypothermia)" — the newborn is more likely to be cold than febrile.

Q: Which findings appear in Box 35.1 as clinical signs of neonatal sepsis? Select all that apply.
A) Respiratory: apnea, tachypnea, grunting, nasal flaring, retractions, cyanosis, decreased oxygen saturation, and metabolic acidosis
B) Cardiovascular: decreased cardiac output, tachycardia or bradycardia, arrhythmias, hypotension, and decreased perfusion with poor peripheral pulses, delayed capillary refill, and cold, clammy, or mottled skin
C) Neurologic: temperature instability, hypothermia or fever, lethargy, hypotonia, jitteriness, irritability, seizures, bulging fontanels, and a high-pitched or abnormal cry
D) Gastrointestinal: feeding intolerance with decreased suck strength and increasing residuals, vomiting, diarrhea, abdominal distention, and hypoactive bowel sounds
E) Localized erythema and purulent drainage at a single site
Answer: A, B, C, D
Rationale: Box 35.1 organizes the signs by system exactly as listed, and its footnote adds the laboratory findings: "neutropenia, increased bands, hypoglycemia or hyperglycemia, metabolic acidosis, and thrombocytopenia."
Trap: (E) describes a localized infection, and the chapter's point is that systemic sepsis in a newborn does not localize — which is why a subtle change across several systems is what raises suspicion.

Subtopic: laboratory evaluation

Q: What does the chapter say about the leukocyte count in diagnosing neonatal sepsis?
A) It is not a reliable indicator; the total neutrophil count, immature to total neutrophil ratio, absolute neutrophil count, platelet count, procalcitonin, and C-reactive protein can be used along with maternal and infant history and clinical examination
B) An elevated leukocyte count confirms sepsis
C) A normal leukocyte count rules out sepsis
D) No laboratory studies are useful
Answer: A
Rationale: "The leukocyte count is not a reliable indicator of sepsis; however, the total neutrophil count, immature to total neutrophil ratio, absolute neutrophil count, platelet count, procalcitonin, and C-reactive protein can be used, along with maternal and infant history and infant clinical examination, to diagnose sepsis." These are "likely adjuncts for the confirmation of neonatal sepsis."
Trap: (C) is the more dangerous of the two wrong readings, because it would stop a workup in an infant whose only reliable evidence is the clinical picture. Cultures of blood, cerebrospinal fluid, and urine are obtained.

Q: What is systemic inflammatory response syndrome as the chapter defines it for the newborn?
A) A syndrome recognized by the presence of two or more of the following: hypothermia or fever, tachypnea or hyperventilation, tachycardia, bradycardia, proven or suspected sepsis, or a high or low white blood cell count
B) A syndrome requiring a positive blood culture
C) A syndrome defined by fever alone
D) A syndrome seen only in preterm infants
Answer: A
Rationale: Neonatal sepsis "can be defined as a systemic inflammatory response syndrome that may occur secondary to infection," and SIRS is "recognized by the presence of two or more of the following signs and symptoms: hypothermia or fever, tachypnea or hyperventilation, tachycardia, bradycardia, proven or suspected sepsis, or a high or low white blood cell count."
Trap: (B) requires a confirmation that often arrives after treatment must begin, and the definition deliberately includes "proven or suspected sepsis" as one criterion among several.

Q: What complications should the nurse continue to watch for in an infant with sepsis?
A) Septicemia, meningitis, disseminated intravascular coagulation, necrotizing enterocolitis, pneumonia, and septic shock
B) Only pneumonia
C) Only jaundice
D) No complications are described
Answer: A
Rationale: The chapter names "septicemia, meningitis, disseminated intravascular coagulation, necrotizing enterocolitis, pneumonia, and septic shock," and identifies the sequelae of septicemia as "meningitis, disseminated intravascular coagulation, and septic shock." Pneumonia "is the most common form of neonatal infection" and "one of the leading causes of perinatal death."
Trap: (B) names the most common form and drops the rest, including necrotizing enterocolitis — which links this chapter's infection content directly to Chapter 34's.

Topic: treating and preventing neonatal sepsis
Subtopic: empiric antibiotics

Q: Which empiric antibiotic regimens does the chapter give for early-onset and late-onset sepsis?
A) Early-onset: ampicillin and an aminoglycoside; late-onset: vancomycin and an aminoglycoside
B) Early-onset: vancomycin alone; late-onset: ampicillin alone
C) Both: penicillin G alone
D) Both: a third-generation cephalosporin alone
Answer: A
Rationale: The chapter gives "ampicillin and an aminoglycoside" for early-onset sepsis and "vancomycin and an aminoglycoside" for late-onset. "Results of blood cultures determine the need for specific antimicrobial therapy. Duration of treatment depends on the specific organism, site of infection, and the neonate's clinical response." Acyclovir and ganciclovir are the antivirals named for neonates.
Trap: (B) swaps the two regimens, and the difference tracks the organisms: coagulase-negative staphylococci acquired in the nursery are what vancomycin covers.

Q: Which directions does the Medication Alert give for administering antibiotics to a newborn? Select all that apply.
A) Administer the prescribed dose as soon as possible after it is prepared, to avoid loss of drug stability
B) If the infant's IV fluid contains electrolytes, vitamins, or other medications, check with the hospital pharmacy before adding antibiotics
C) The antibiotic can be deactivated or can form a precipitate when combined with other substances
D) A secondary line of the prescribed solution may be attached with a needleless connector at the infusion site to prevent this
E) Antibiotics may be added directly to any running infusion without checking
Answer: A, B, C, D
Rationale: The Medication Alert gives each of these directions.
Trap: (E) is the practice the alert exists to prevent, and the consequence is either an inactivated antibiotic or a precipitate in the line of an infant already fighting sepsis.

Subtopic: breast milk and prevention

Q: Why is breastfeeding or human milk feeding encouraged in an infant at risk for infection?
A) Colostrum contains IgA, which offers protection against infection in the gastrointestinal tract; human milk contains an iron-binding protein that exerts a bacteriostatic effect on E. coli, as well as macrophages and lymphocytes, and passive transfer of maternal immunity reduces vulnerability to pathogens such as respiratory syncytial virus
B) Because human milk is easier to digest, with no immune effect
C) Because breastfeeding prevents all neonatal infections
D) Because breast milk is sterile
Answer: A
Rationale: "Colostrum contains IgA, which offers protection against infection in the GI tract. Human milk contains iron-binding protein that exerts a bacteriostatic effect on E. coli. Human milk also contains macrophages and lymphocytes. The vulnerability of infants to common mucosal pathogens such as respiratory syncytial virus can be reduced by passive transfer of maternal immunity in the colostrum and breast milk."
Trap: (B) treats milk as nutrition only, and the IgA point is exactly what fills the gap left by the immunoglobulin that does not cross the placenta.

Q: What does the chapter identify as the single most important measure in preventing the spread of infection, and what other measures accompany it?
A) Hand hygiene; along with standard precautions, careful and thorough cleaning of the environment and equipment, frequently replacing used equipment, appropriate disposal of excrement and linens, and avoiding overcrowding in nurseries
B) Prophylactic antibiotics for all newborns
C) Isolation of all newborns
D) Routine suctioning of secretions
Answer: A
Rationale: "Hand hygiene is the single most important measure in preventing the spread of infection," with the other measures as listed. Newborn care procedures intended to prevent infection include "instilling antibiotic ointment in newborns' eyes 1 to 2 hours after birth, bathing, and cord care."
Trap: (D) is specifically cautioned against: "routine suctioning is not recommended and can further compromise the infant's immune status as well as cause hypoxia and increase ICP," and secretions being suctioned "may be infected."

Topic: group b streptococcus
Subtopic: colonization and risk

Q: What does the chapter give as the vaginal and rectal colonization rate for group B streptococcus among pregnant women, and what does GBS cause?
A) 15% to 35%; it is a leading cause of perinatal infections including bacteremia, endometritis, chorioamnionitis, and urinary tract infection, and in the newborn it can cause focal or systemic disease
B) 5%; it causes only urinary tract infections
C) 50%; it causes only neonatal disease
D) Under 1%; it is rarely significant
Answer: A
Rationale: "Among pregnant women, the vaginal/rectal colonization rate is 15% to 35%." GBS "is a leading cause of perinatal infections including bacteremia, endometritis, chorioamnionitis, and urinary tract infection. In the newborn, it can cause focal or systemic disease. GBS commonly lives in the human GI and genitourinary tracts."
Trap: (D) would make universal screening pointless, and the colonization rate is what justifies screening every pregnancy.

Q: A mother tested negative for GBS at 36 weeks, and her newborn develops signs of infection. How should the nurse interpret this?
A) As impossible, since the screen was negative
B) As possible: infants born to mothers who tested negative can develop GBS infection, and GBS can also be transmitted in the birth facility from colonized health care staff or visitors, or in the community from family members or caregivers who are colonized
C) As evidence of a laboratory error
D) As late-onset disease only
Answer: B
Rationale: "Infants born to mothers who tested negative for GBS can develop GBS infection. GBS can also be transmitted in the birth facility from colonized health care staff or visitors or in the community from family members or caregivers who are colonized."
Trap: (A) is the reasoning that delays evaluation of a septic-appearing infant, and the chapter states the exception directly because the screen is a good test rather than a perfect one.

Q: Which factors increase the risk for early-onset GBS disease? Select all that apply.
A) Preterm birth and rupture of membranes more than 18 hours before birth
B) Intrapartum maternal fever above 38°C, and a previous infant with invasive GBS disease
C) Maternal GBS bacteriuria during the current pregnancy
D) Frequent vaginal examinations, intrauterine fetal monitoring, membrane sweeping, and intraamniotic infection
E) Maternal age over 35
Answer: A, B, C, D
Rationale: The chapter lists preterm birth, rupture of membranes more than 18 hours, intrapartum maternal fever above 38°C, a previous infant with invasive GBS disease, maternal GBS bacteriuria in the current pregnancy, frequent vaginal examinations, intrauterine fetal monitoring, membrane sweeping, and intraamniotic infection. It also notes "an increased risk among infants born to Black women and to women less than 20 years of age."
Trap: (E) inverts the age association the chapter names — the increased risk is among women under 20, not over 35.

Subtopic: presentation and treatment

Q: How do early-onset and late-onset GBS disease differ in timing and presentation?
A) Early-onset develops during the first 7 days, most often within 12 to 24 hours after birth, usually from vertical transmission from the birth canal, presenting as systemic infection or respiratory illness that mimics severe respiratory distress with rapid development of pneumonia, shock, and less often meningitis; late-onset typically occurs at 3 to 4 weeks of age and commonly manifests as bacteremia or meningitis
B) Early-onset occurs at 3 to 4 weeks; late-onset within 24 hours
C) Both present identically as meningitis
D) Early-onset presents only as a skin infection
Answer: A
Rationale: Early-onset GBS "develops during the first 7 days, most often within 12 to 24 hours after birth" and "usually results from vertical transmission from the birth canal," presenting as "systemic infection or respiratory illness that mimics the symptoms of severe respiratory distress," with rapid development of "pneumonia, shock, and, less often, meningitis." Late-onset disease occurs "at 3 to 4 weeks of age" and manifests as "bacteremia or meningitis," sometimes with osteomyelitis and septic arthritis.
Trap: (B) reverses the two windows, and the early presentation matters clinically because it looks like respiratory distress syndrome rather than like infection.

Q: What is the usual treatment for a newborn with suspected GBS infection, and how does it change once GBS is identified?
A) Ampicillin in combination with an aminoglycoside for suspected infection; if GBS is identified as the cause, penicillin G alone may be given, with dosage and length of treatment depending on whether bacteremia or meningitis is being treated
B) Vancomycin alone throughout
C) No antibiotics until cultures return
D) Penicillin G alone from the start in all cases
Answer: A
Rationale: "The usual treatment for newborns with suspected GBS infection is ampicillin in combination with an aminoglycoside. If GBS is identified as the cause of infection, penicillin G alone may be given," and "the dosage of ampicillin and/or penicillin G and length of treatment depend on whether bacteremia or meningitis is being treated."
Trap: (C) waits for a result that takes longer than the infant has — the mortality rate for early-onset infection is 1% to 3%, with higher rates among preterm infants, and about 20% of survivors of GBS meningitis have moderate to severe neurodevelopmental impairment.

Topic: respiratory distress in the infant of a diabetic mother
Subtopic: the mechanism

Q: Why is the infant of a diabetic mother at increased risk for respiratory distress syndrome?
A) Fetal hyperinsulinism is thought to inhibit surfactant production, leading to a delay in the maturation of the lungs; in a fetus exposed to high maternal glucose, synthesis of surfactant can be delayed because of high fetal serum levels of insulin and glucose
B) Because these infants are always preterm
C) Because maternal insulin crosses the placenta and damages the lungs
D) Because of meconium aspiration
Answer: A
Rationale: "Fetal hyperinsulinism is thought to inhibit surfactant production, leading to a delay in the maturation of the lungs." And: "In the fetus exposed to high levels of maternal glucose, synthesis of surfactant can be delayed because of the high fetal serum levels of insulin and/or glucose."
Trap: (C) has maternal insulin crossing the placenta, which it does not — the fetus makes its own in response to maternal glucose, and that is what delays lung maturation.

Q: At what gestational age does the risk of respiratory distress syndrome in the infant of a well-controlled diabetic mother become similar to the general population's?
A) At term; however, until the infant reaches 38 weeks' gestation, there is a significant increase in the risk of RDS
B) At 34 weeks
C) At 36 weeks
D) The risk is never comparable
Answer: A
Rationale: "Among infants born to women with well-controlled diabetes who give birth at term, the risk of RDS is similar to that of the general population. However, until the infant reaches 38 weeks' gestation, there is a significant increase in the risk of RDS."
Trap: (C) sets the threshold two weeks early, and the 38-week line is what makes an elective early-term birth in a diabetic pregnancy a respiratory decision as well as a scheduling one.

Q: What does the chapter say about the current frequency of respiratory distress syndrome in infants of diabetic mothers, and when is earlier birth still indicated?
A) It is much less common than in the past because of improved protocols to manage maternal glucose levels and enhanced antepartum fetal surveillance to assess lung maturity; late preterm birth may be indicated when maternal glycemic control cannot be achieved or when antepartum fetal testing is abnormal
B) It is more common than in the past
C) It is unchanged
D) Early birth is never indicated
Answer: A
Rationale: "RDS in infants of mothers with diabetes is much less common than in the past because of improved protocols to manage maternal glucose levels and enhanced antepartum fetal surveillance techniques to assess lung maturity," and "late preterm birth may be indicated in cases where maternal glycemic control cannot be achieved or for mothers who have abnormal antepartum fetal testing."
Trap: (D) removes a real indication, and the trade-off is the point: the same 38-week threshold that argues against early birth is knowingly crossed when the intrauterine environment is worse than the risk.

Topic: tobacco and alcohol exposure
Subtopic: tobacco

Q: What does the chapter say about tobacco use in pregnancy and its effects?
A) Tobacco remains the most used substance during pregnancy, and low birth weight is its most widely recognized effect, attributed to the vasoconstrictive effects of nicotine and to carbon monoxide crossing the placenta and causing fetal hypoxemia; other outcomes include miscarriage, preterm birth, placental abruption, and placenta previa
B) Tobacco use has no measurable fetal effect
C) Macrosomia is the most widely recognized effect
D) Tobacco affects only the third trimester
Answer: A
Rationale: Tobacco "remains the most used substance during pregnancy," and "low birth weight is the most widely recognized effect," attributed to "the vasoconstrictive effects of nicotine" and carbon monoxide crossing the placenta "which can cause fetal hypoxemia." Other outcomes are "miscarriage, preterm birth, placental abruption, and placenta previa."
Trap: (C) reverses the growth effect. Nicotine constricts vessels and reduces delivery, which makes infants smaller rather than larger.

Q: What risks does the chapter associate with secondhand smoke exposure for the infant?
A) Ear infections, respiratory illnesses such as asthma and bronchitis, and sudden infant death syndrome, with mothers and all others advised to refrain from smoking near the infant
B) Only mild respiratory irritation
C) No risks after birth
D) Only risks in the first week
Answer: A
Rationale: Secondhand smoke "increases the risk of ear infections, respiratory illnesses such as asthma and bronchitis, and sudden infant death syndrome," and "mothers and all others should refrain from smoking near the infant."
Trap: (C) ends the exposure at birth, when the postnatal risks the chapter names are among the most modifiable in newborn teaching.

Subtopic: alcohol and the spectrum disorders

Q: How much alcohol is required to produce fetal effects, and what determines the outcome?
A) The quantity required is unclear; effects relate to the gestational age at exposure, maternal age, the amount consumed, the pattern of consumption, and maternal alcohol metabolism, and ethanol easily crosses the placenta
B) A specific safe threshold has been established
C) Only binge drinking causes effects
D) Only first-trimester exposure matters
Answer: A
Rationale: "Ethanol easily crosses the placenta. The quantity of alcohol required to produce fetal effects is unclear," with effects relating to "the gestational age at exposure, maternal age, the amount of alcohol consumed, the pattern of consumption, and maternal alcohol metabolism."
Trap: (B) supplies a threshold the chapter says does not exist, and it is the claim that makes "just a little" sound safe.

Q: Which criteria define fetal alcohol syndrome, and how does alcohol-related neurodevelopmental disorder differ?
A) FAS requires specific dysmorphic facial features — short palpebral fissures, thin upper lip, smooth philtrum — plus growth deficiency and CNS abnormalities; alcohol-related neurodevelopmental disorder describes children with confirmed prenatal alcohol exposure who lack the facial features or growth deficits yet have structural CNS and cognitive abnormalities
B) Both require the facial features
C) FAS requires only the facial features
D) The two are the same diagnosis
Answer: A
Rationale: Fetal alcohol syndrome is "the most severe FASD and can result in fetal death," with criteria of "specific dysmorphic facial features (short palpebral fissures, thin upper lip, smooth philtrum), growth deficiency, and CNS abnormalities." Alcohol-related neurodevelopmental disorder describes children "who lack facial features or growth deficits associated with FAS, yet have structural CNS and cognitive abnormalities."
Trap: (B) would make every affected child recognizable on sight, and the chapter's closing point about this group is the opposite: "often these problems are not detected until the child goes to school and learning problems become evident."

Q: What does alcohol-related birth defects describe, and what is the prevalence of fetal alcohol syndrome?
A) Confirmed prenatal alcohol exposure with congenital abnormalities most often including cardiac and renal anomalies, orthopedic defects, and anomalies of the eyes and ears; FAS prevalence is about 1 infant per 1000 live births in certain areas of the United States
B) Only neurologic abnormalities; prevalence is 1 in 100,000
C) Only facial features; prevalence is 1 in 10
D) The term is not used
Answer: A
Rationale: Alcohol-related birth defects describe "confirmed PAE and congenital abnormalities that most often include cardiac and renal anomalies, orthopedic defects, and anomalies of the eyes and ears." FAS prevalence is "1 infant for every 1000 live births in certain areas of the United States." The fetal alcohol spectrum disorder umbrella includes FAS, alcohol-related neurodevelopmental disorder, alcohol-related birth defects, and neurobehavioral disorder associated with prenatal alcohol exposure.
Trap: (B) reduces a multi-organ pattern to the nervous system, which is what the neurodevelopmental category covers instead.

Topic: neonatal abstinence syndrome
Subtopic: why the signs cluster as they do

Q: Why do the most common signs of neonatal abstinence syndrome reflect central nervous system irritability, increased autonomic activity, and gastrointestinal abnormalities?
A) Because opioid receptors are located in the CNS and gastrointestinal tract
B) Because opioids damage the liver
C) Because withdrawal affects only the brain
D) Because the infant is hypoglycemic
Answer: A
Rationale: "Opioid receptors are located in the CNS and GI tract. This explains why the most common signs of NAS reflect CNS irritability, increased autonomic activity, and GI abnormalities."
Trap: (C) drops the gastrointestinal half, which is where the feeding problems that drive length of stay come from.

Q: In what proportion of neonates exposed in utero to opioids do signs of withdrawal develop, and what determines severity?
A) 55% to 94%; withdrawal symptoms are more severe in newborns exposed to larger amounts of drugs for longer periods, and severity is also related to the timing of maternal drug use in relation to birth
B) Fewer than 10%; severity is unrelated to dose
C) All exposed infants, with uniform severity
D) About 25%, determined only by the specific drug
Answer: A
Rationale: "Signs of withdrawal develop in 55% to 94%" of neonates exposed in utero to opioids, and "withdrawal symptoms are more severe in newborns exposed to larger amounts of drugs for longer periods of time. The severity of withdrawal is also related to the timing of maternal drug use in relation to birth."
Trap: (C) removes the variability that makes assessment necessary, and Box 35.2 adds the timing rule: "the closer to birth that a mother takes the drug, the more severe the symptoms and the greater the delay in onset."

Subtopic: recognizing withdrawal

Q: When may signs of neonatal abstinence syndrome first appear, according to Box 35.2?
A) Signs and symptoms may not be exhibited for up to 72 hours
B) Always within the first hour
C) Always within 12 hours
D) Not until after discharge in every case
Answer: A
Rationale: Box 35.2's first framing statement is that "signs and symptoms may not be exhibited for up to 72 hours." The most common signs are "CNS hyperirritability, GI dysfunction, respiratory distress, and autonomic instability."
Trap: (B) would allow a quiet infant at 6 hours to be considered unaffected, which is exactly the observation window the 72-hour statement extends.

Q: Which acute signs of neonatal abstinence syndrome does Box 35.2 list as possibly persisting for several weeks? Select all that apply.
A) Restlessness and tremors, disturbed at first and later undisturbed
B) A high-pitched cry, increased muscle tone, and irritability with inconsolability
C) Increased deep tendon reflexes and an exaggerated Moro reflex
D) Seizures in approximately 1% to 2% of heroin-exposed neonates and approximately 7% of methadone-exposed neonates
E) Hypotonia and lethargy
Answer: A, B, C, D
Rationale: Box 35.2's acute signs are restlessness, tremors progressing from disturbed to undisturbed, a high-pitched cry, increased muscle tone, irritability and inconsolability, increased deep tendon reflexes, an exaggerated Moro reflex, and seizures at the stated rates.
Trap: (E) describes the opposite neurologic state and belongs to sepsis or hypothermia — the withdrawing infant is hypertonic and irritable, not floppy and quiet.

Q: Which subacute signs may persist for 4 to 6 months?
A) Irritability, sleep pattern disturbance, hyperactivity, feeding problems, and hypertonia
B) Seizures and an exaggerated Moro reflex
C) Jaundice and poor weight gain only
D) No signs persist beyond the hospital stay
Answer: A
Rationale: Box 35.2 lists the subacute signs persisting 4 to 6 months as irritability, sleep pattern disturbance, hyperactivity, feeding problems, and hypertonia.
Trap: (D) ends the syndrome at discharge, and the months-long course is what makes parent teaching and follow-up part of the treatment rather than an afterthought.

Subtopic: scoring and treatment

Q: Which scoring approaches does the chapter describe for neonatal abstinence syndrome, and what is the AAP's position?
A) The Neonatal Abstinence Scoring System and the newer Eat, Sleep, Console tool, which many users say decreases length of stay, need for pharmacologic intervention, and maternal-newborn separation; there is not sufficient evidence to support one tool over another, so the AAP does not endorse one scoring system, but there is clear evidence that a consistent protocol improves outcomes and every facility should have a written protocol
B) The Finnegan tool only, which the AAP endorses
C) Eat, Sleep, Console only, which the AAP endorses
D) No scoring tools exist
Answer: A
Rationale: "Scoring tools such as the Neonatal Abstinence Scoring System (Finnegan, 1990) ... are used to objectively review the infant's signs of withdrawal," and the newer Eat, Sleep, Console tool "is touted by many of its users to decrease hospital length of stay, need for pharmacologic intervention, and maternal-newborn separation." The AAP position: "There is not sufficient evidence to support one tool's use over another; therefore, AAP does not endorse one scoring system over another. There is clear evidence that establishment of a consistent protocol and approach to the care of these infants improves outcomes, so it is recommended that every facility has a written protocol."
Trap: (B) and (C) each endorse a tool the AAP declines to endorse, and the chapter's actual recommendation is about consistency rather than about which instrument.

Q: What treatment principle does the chapter give for infants with neonatal abstinence syndrome?
A) All infants showing signs of withdrawal should receive nonpharmacologic care; infants requiring medication have longer stays, and length of stay varies but is often several weeks
B) All infants should receive medication immediately
C) No treatment is needed if the mother was in a treatment program
D) Infants should be discharged as soon as scoring begins
Answer: A
Rationale: "The length of hospital stay varies for infants with NAS and is often several weeks. All infants showing signs of withdrawal should receive nonpharmacologic care. Infants requiring medication have longer stays."
Trap: (B) reverses the order of treatment, and the Eat, Sleep, Console approach exists precisely because nonpharmacologic care first reduces how many infants need medication at all.

Topic: perinatal substance use framing
Subtopic: what determines the effect

Q: What does the chapter identify as the critical determinants of a drug's effect on the fetus?
A) The specific drug, the dosage, the route of administration, the genotype of the mother or fetus, and the timing of the drug exposure
B) Only the specific drug
C) Only the trimester of exposure
D) Only the mother's age
Answer: A
Rationale: "Critical determinants of the effect of the drug on the fetus include the specific drug, the dosage, the route of administration, the genotype of the mother or fetus, and the timing of the drug exposure."
Trap: (B) treats the substance as the whole answer, which would make every exposure to a given drug equivalent — and the genotype and timing entries are what explain why outcomes vary so widely.

Q: Why does the chapter say attributing outcomes to a specific substance is difficult?
A) Because of polydrug use, errors or omissions in reporting drug use, and variations in the strength, purity, and types of additives found in illicit drugs, compounded by poverty and malnutrition and comorbid conditions such as sexually transmitted infections
B) Because substance use is rare in pregnancy
C) Because laboratory testing is unavailable
D) Because effects are always immediate and obvious
Answer: A
Rationale: The chapter names "polydrug use; errors or omissions in reporting drug use; and variations in the strength, purity, and types of additives found in illicit drugs," compounded by "poverty and malnutrition and comorbid conditions such as STIs."
Trap: (D) contradicts both the 72-hour delay in withdrawal signs and the alcohol-related deficits that surface only at school age.

Q: Which obstetric complications does the chapter associate with opioid dependence?
A) Miscarriage, preterm labor, preterm birth, placental abruption, chorioamnionitis, intrauterine growth restriction, and fetal distress
B) Only preterm birth
C) Only postterm pregnancy
D) No obstetric complications
Answer: A
Rationale: The chapter lists "miscarriage, preterm labor, preterm birth, placental abruption, chorioamnionitis, IUGR, and fetal distress" as obstetric complications of opioid dependence.
Trap: (B) narrows a list that spans the whole pregnancy, and several of its entries — abruption, growth restriction, chorioamnionitis — also appear as risk factors for the neonatal sepsis drilled earlier in this chapter.
