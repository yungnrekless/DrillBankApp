# Chapter 15 drills — Maternal Nutrition

Authored from `sources/Ch15_Fact_Bank.md` (Lowdermilk, Cashion, Alden,
Olshansky & Perry, *Maternity & Women's Health Care*, 13th ed.).

Blueprint weight is **7 questions of 200 (3.5%), depth `standard`** — the top of
that band — topic *"Nutrition in Pregnancy, iron, folate, protein, adolescent
risk."* That earns **45 questions across 10 topics**.

**Scope.** Almost nothing needed trimming. The blueprint's named sub-topics are
exactly the reading guide's five assignments (*Nutritional Considerations*
pp. 298–309, *Macronutrients* pp. 301–305, *Minerals* pp. 306–307 with Table
15.3, *Water-soluble vitamins* p. 306 with the iron supplementation teaching box,
*Adolescent pregnancy* p. 313), so the fact bank curated all of them at depth.
The chapter's Care Management half — the food intake questionnaire, physical
assessment of nutritional status, dietary planning, food safety — is outside the
assigned pages and is not in the bank, so it is not drilled here.

This is a chapter of **numbers**, and nearly every testable item is a quantity
attached to a nutrient. Four topics are therefore organized around the numbers
that decide something: the three folic acid doses, the iron teaching box, the
four weight-gain ranges, and the energy and protein increments.

The organizing idea the fact bank names: **only two nutrients here genuinely
cannot be met by diet alone** — the book says the needs of pregnant women "with
perhaps the exception of folate and iron, can be met through dietary sources."
Everything else in the micronutrient sections is either a food-source list or a
warning against over-supplementation, and that asymmetry is what most of the
vitamin questions below turn on.

Every invertible pair the fact bank flags is drilled from both ends: folate and
folic acid, the enhancers and inhibitors of iron absorption, physiologic and
iron deficiency anemia, too little and too much weight gain, fat-soluble and
water-soluble vitamin toxicity, isotretinoin and topical tretinoin, and the
adolescent's mechanical and nutritional risks.

**Two source problems worth knowing about.** First, **the book contradicts
itself on the underweight BMI cutoff**: Table 15.1 prints "less than 18," while
the prose on p. 298 defines the category as "less than 18.5." The fact bank
confirmed both readings against the publisher's e-reader, so this is a typo in
the printed table rather than an extraction error. Items below use **18.5**, the
prose value and the standard category boundary. Second, the fact bank's reference
sections carry page numbers in the 805–818 range, outside the chapter's printed
range of pp. 297–313. Those are **labelled `PDF` in the bank's outline** rather
than stale cites — they mark the unassigned Care Management half, which was never
re-cited to printed pages. Nothing here is cited to them.

**What the band could not buy.** Cut, in this order: Table 15.4's entire
calorie-level grid (six columns of serving amounts the reading guide does not
name); Table 15.2's tissue-by-tissue breakdown of where the weight goes; Box
15.1's five-tier folate food list and Box 15.3's calcium-equivalent list, of
which only the teaching points are kept; iodine and choline beyond their
existence; the potassium section; alcohol, caffeine, and artificial sweeteners;
and the vegetarian and gluten-free diet sections beyond the vegan referral rule.
Kept without exception: all three folic acid doses, the full iron teaching box,
all four weight-gain rows, the energy and protein increments, the mercury Safety
Alert, the vitamin A teratogen warning, and every adolescent-specific rule,
because those are the items the seven blueprint questions will be built from.

Topic: folic acid dosing and neural tube defects

Q: A 24-year-old with no history of a pregnancy affected by a neural tube defect asks how much folic acid she should take while she is trying to conceive. What is the recommended daily amount?
A) 0.4 mg, which is 400 mcg, along with dietary sources of folate
B) 4 mg daily, beginning 1 month before conception
C) 0.6 mg daily, which is the pregnancy requirement
D) No supplement is needed until pregnancy is confirmed
Answer: A
Rationale: All adolescents and women capable of becoming pregnant are advised to take "0.4 mg (400 mcg) of folic acid every day" and to "consume dietary sources of folate" (p. 297).
Trap: (B) is a tenfold overdose, and it is chosen by anyone who remembers the higher number without the condition attached to it. The 4 mg dose belongs only to a woman with a previous neural-tube-defect-affected pregnancy, and the units switch between milligrams and micrograms in the source, which is what makes these doses easy to trade.

Q: A patient whose first child was born with spina bifida is planning another pregnancy. What folic acid regimen should the nurse expect to be prescribed?
A) 4 mg daily, beginning at least 1 month before attempting to conceive and continuing throughout the first trimester
B) 0.4 mg daily, beginning when pregnancy is confirmed
C) 0.6 mg daily throughout pregnancy only
D) 4 mg daily, beginning in the second trimester when fetal growth accelerates
Answer: A
Rationale: A woman who has had a pregnancy "that resulted in the birth of a child with an NTD" should take "4 mg of folic acid daily," beginning "at least 1 month before attempting to conceive and continuing throughout the first trimester" (p. 297).
Trap: (B) is the mirror error of the previous item and delivers a tenfold underdose to the one patient who needs the high dose. (D) gets the dose right and the timing catastrophically wrong: the neural tube has closed long before the second trimester, so a regimen starting then cannot prevent anything.

Q: A patient at her first prenatal visit at 10 weeks says she began taking folic acid last week. How should the nurse respond?
A) Continuing is still worthwhile, but the neural tube begins to close within the first month of gestation, often before a woman knows she is pregnant, which is why supplementation is recommended before conception
B) Starting at 10 weeks provides full protection against neural tube defects
C) Folic acid is only useful in the third trimester, so she has started too early
D) She should stop the supplement now that pregnancy is confirmed
Answer: A
Rationale: "Proper closure of the neural tube is required for normal formation of the spinal cord, and the neural tube begins to close within the first month of gestation, often before the woman realizes that she is pregnant" (p. 297). Folate remains needed throughout pregnancy for increased red blood cell production and for the rapidly growing cells of the fetus and placenta.
Trap: (B) is the reassuring answer that is not true, and the timing is the whole reason the recommendation targets all women capable of becoming pregnant rather than pregnant women. Folate is still needed for other purposes, so the honest response keeps the supplement and corrects the expectation.

Q: A patient asks about the difference between the folate on a food label and the folic acid in her prenatal vitamin. Which explanation is accurate?
A) Folate is the form found naturally in foods, and folic acid is the form used in fortification of grain products and in vitamin supplements
B) Folic acid is the natural food form, and folate is the synthetic supplement form
C) The two are unrelated nutrients with different functions
D) Folate is active only in pregnancy, and folic acid only before conception
Answer: A
Rationale: "Folate (vitamin B9) is the form in which this vitamin is found naturally in foods, and folic acid is the form used in fortification of grain products and other foods and in vitamin supplements" (p. 297).
Trap: (B) reverses the two terms, which is the mirror of the same distinction. It matters at the counseling level because a patient told to "get more folate" from food and a patient told to "take folic acid" are being told two different things, and only one of them is a supplement instruction.

Q: What is the recommended daily folate intake during pregnancy itself?
A) 600 mcg, which is 0.6 mg
B) 400 mcg, the same as before conception
C) 4 mg, the same as for a woman with a previous affected pregnancy
D) 1000 mcg, double the preconception amount
Answer: A
Rationale: Table 15.3 gives a pregnancy folate value of 600 mcg against a nonpregnant 400 mcg and a lactation value of 500 mcg, and the water-soluble vitamins section states the pregnancy recommendation as "0.6 mg (600 mcg) of folic acid daily" (p. 306). The increase reflects "the increase in RBC production during pregnancy as well as the nutritional requirements of the rapidly growing cells in the fetus and placenta."
Trap: (B) carries the preconception dose into pregnancy unchanged, which is the middle error of a three-value set. Holding all three together is what prevents it: 400 mcg before, 600 mcg during, and 4 mg only with a previous affected pregnancy.

Topic: iron requirements and supplementation teaching

Q: What is the recommended daily iron intake during pregnancy, and how does it compare with lactation?
A) 27 mg of elemental iron during pregnancy, which falls to 10 or 9 mg during lactation
B) 27 mg during pregnancy, rising to 40 mg during lactation
C) 18 mg during pregnancy, the same as for nonpregnant adult women
D) 15 mg during pregnancy, rising through lactation as milk production increases
Answer: A
Rationale: "The recommended daily allowance for iron during pregnancy is 27 mg; this is the amount of elemental iron in most supplements" (p. 306). Table 15.3 gives nonpregnant values of 15 mg for adolescents and 18 mg for adult women, a pregnancy value of 27 mg, and lactation values of 10 and 9 mg. The pregnancy increase allows "transfer of adequate iron to the fetus" and "expansion of the maternal RBC mass."
Trap: (B) assumes that if pregnancy raises a requirement, breastfeeding must raise it further. Iron is the counterexample: the requirement is highest in pregnancy and falls below the nonpregnant value in lactation, so it is one of the few nutrients whose curve goes back down.

Q: A patient is starting an iron supplement. Select all the instructions that are accurate.
A) A diet rich in vitamin C and in heme iron from meats increases absorption of the supplement
B) Bran, tea, coffee, milk, oxalates in spinach and Swiss chard, and egg yolk decrease absorption and should not be consumed at the same time as the supplement
C) Iron is absorbed best on an empty stomach, taken between meals with a beverage other than tea, coffee, or milk
D) Black or dark green stools are an expected effect of the supplement
E) Taking the supplement with a glass of milk improves both absorption and tolerance
Answer: A, B, C, D
Rationale: The Teaching for Self-Management box states that vitamin C and heme iron increase absorption; that "bran, tea, coffee, milk, oxalates (in spinach and Swiss chard) and egg yolk decrease iron absorption"; that "iron is absorbed best if it is taken when the stomach is empty; that is, take it between meals with a beverage other than tea, coffee, or milk"; and that "iron supplements can cause stools to become black or dark green" (p. 306).
Trap: (E) is the single wrong option and it is the one most likely to be checked, because milk is the beverage people reach for to settle a stomach. Milk is on the inhibitor list twice over — named directly and named again as a beverage to avoid — so it is exactly backwards. Spinach is the other counterintuitive entry: it is a folate source and an iron-absorption inhibitor at the same time.

Q: A patient reports abdominal discomfort when she takes her iron supplement between meals. What should the nurse advise?
A) Take the iron at bedtime, since it can be taken then if abdominal discomfort occurs when it is taken between meals
B) Take the iron with milk to buffer the stomach
C) Stop the iron until the discomfort resolves
D) Take a double dose every other day to reduce the frequency of discomfort
Answer: A
Rationale: "Iron is absorbed best if it is taken when the stomach is empty; that is, take it between meals ... Iron can be taken at bedtime if abdominal discomfort occurs when it is taken between meals" (p. 306).
Trap: (B) sacrifices the whole point of the dose to comfort, since milk is a named absorption inhibitor. The teaching box supplies a solution that preserves absorption, which is why bedtime dosing is the answer rather than any strategy that pairs iron with food that blocks it.

Q: A patient realizes 10 hours after her scheduled dose that she forgot her iron. What should she do?
A) Take it now, since a missed dose should be taken as soon as it is remembered if that is within 13 hours of the scheduled dose, and doses should not be doubled
B) Skip it and take two tomorrow
C) Skip it entirely, since a missed dose cannot be made up
D) Take it now and also take tomorrow's dose early to catch up
Answer: A
Rationale: "If an iron dose is missed, take it as soon as it is remembered if that is within 13 hours of the scheduled dose. Do not double up on the dose" (p. 306).
Trap: (B) and (D) both double a dose, which the box prohibits outright. The 13-hour figure is unusual enough to be worth memorizing as printed, and the prohibition on doubling connects to the box's other safety line — iron is kept in a childproof container because it is a common pediatric poisoning.

Q: A pregnant patient taking iron reports constipation. What should the nurse recommend?
A) A diet high in fiber with adequate fluid intake, since constipation is common with iron supplementation
B) Discontinuing the iron supplement
C) A stimulant laxative daily for the remainder of the pregnancy
D) Switching the iron to the morning with coffee
Answer: A
Rationale: "Constipation is common with iron supplementation. A diet high in fiber with adequate fluid intake is recommended" (p. 306).
Trap: (D) would relocate the dose to a time paired with a named absorption inhibitor and would not address the constipation. This is the one iron side effect the box responds to with an intervention rather than reassurance; black or dark green stools, by contrast, are simply expected.

Q: A patient's hemoglobin is low and she is told she has iron deficiency anemia. She asks whether this is the same normal anemia of pregnancy she read about. Which response is accurate?
A) They are different; physiologic anemia of pregnancy is a normal dilutional change as blood volume increases, while iron deficiency anemia reflects poor iron status
B) They are the same condition described in two ways
C) Physiologic anemia is the more serious of the two and requires supplementation
D) Iron deficiency anemia occurs only after birth, so the diagnosis is likely an error
Answer: A
Rationale: "Poor iron status, which can result in iron deficiency anemia, is relatively common among women in the childbearing years. This is not the same as physiologic anemia of pregnancy, which is a normal occurrence as the maternal blood volume increases" (p. 306). If a woman is already anemic, "increased dosages may be required and must be discussed with the woman's health care provider."
Trap: (B) is the conflation the chapter explicitly forecloses, and the consequence runs in both directions: treating a dilutional finding as a deficiency leads to unnecessary supplementation, and dismissing a real deficiency as physiologic leaves it untreated. The two are distinguished by the trimester-specific thresholds from Chapter 13, not by the hemoglobin value alone.

Topic: weight gain by body mass index

Q: A patient with a prepregnancy body mass index of 32 asks how much weight she should gain. What is the recommended total gain?
A) 5.0 to 9.1 kg, or 11 to 20 lb
B) 11.5 to 16 kg, or 25 to 35 lb
C) 12.7 to 18.1 kg, or 28 to 40 lb
D) She should aim to lose weight during the pregnancy
Answer: A
Rationale: Table 15.1 gives recommended total gain by prepregnancy category: underweight, 12.7 to 18.1 kg (28 to 40 lb); normal, 11.5 to 16 kg (25 to 35 lb); overweight, 6.8 to 11.3 kg (15 to 25 lb); and obese, 5.0 to 9.1 kg (11 to 20 lb). The obese category is a body mass index of 30 or greater (p. 298).
Trap: (D) is the intuitive recommendation and the chapter forbids it in as many words. The four ranges run inversely to body mass index, so the heavier the starting point the smaller the recommended gain, but the recommendation never becomes a loss.

Q: An obese patient asks why she cannot simply diet during pregnancy, since she has weight to lose. Which explanation reflects the chapter?
A) Even overweight or obese pregnant women need to gain at least enough weight to equal the weight of the products of conception, and dietary restriction causes catabolism of fat stores that augments production of ketones
B) Dieting is acceptable in pregnancy as long as protein intake is maintained
C) Dieting is discouraged only because the patient will feel hungry
D) Dieting is acceptable in the first trimester, when the fetus is small
Answer: A
Rationale: "Pregnancy is not a time for a diet for weight loss. Even overweight or obese pregnant women need to gain at least enough weight to equal the weight of the products of conception (fetus, placenta, and amniotic fluid)." Limiting energy intake may also "excessively limit their intake of important nutrients. Moreover, dietary restriction results in catabolism of fat stores, which in turn augments the production of ketones" (pp. 298–299).
Trap: (C) is the incomplete answer that a nurse gives when the mechanism has not been learned. The ketone production is the reason the source supplies, and it is what makes the prohibition physiologic rather than merely a matter of comfort or adherence.

Q: A patient of normal prepregnancy weight is at 10 weeks and has gained 3 lb. How should the nurse interpret this?
A) It is appropriate; recommended total first-trimester gain is 2 to 4 lb, after which about 1 lb per week is recommended
B) It is excessive; no weight gain is expected in the first trimester
C) It is inadequate; 1 lb per week is expected from conception onward
D) It cannot be assessed without a body mass index recalculation
Answer: A
Rationale: "During the first trimester of singleton pregnancy, the recommended total weight gain is 2 to 4 lb. (0.9 to 1.8 kg) for a woman with normal prepregnancy weight. Thereafter the recommended weight gain increases to approximately 1 lb. (0.45 kg) per week for an underweight woman and a woman of normal weight" (p. 298).
Trap: (C) applies the weekly rate to the first trimester, which would set an expectation more than three times the recommended total. The rate changes at the end of the first trimester because growth changes location: in the first and second trimesters growth is primarily in maternal tissues, and in the third it is primarily fetal.

Q: A patient at 26 weeks has gained 8 lb since her visit 4 weeks ago. Her weight was recorded on the same scale in similar clothing. What should the nurse do first?
A) Assess for signs of preeclampsia, since a gain of more than 6.6 lb in a month, especially after the 20th week, can be associated with its development
B) Provide dietary counseling about portion sizes
C) Reassure her that weight gain accelerates in the third trimester
D) Recommend that she restrict sodium to limit fluid retention
Answer: A
Rationale: "An exceptionally high gain is likely to be caused by an accumulation of fluids," and "a gain of more than 6.6 lb. (3 kg) in a month, especially after the 20th week of gestation, can be associated with the development of preeclampsia" (p. 298). The source also names benign explanations to exclude first, including "measurement or recording errors or differences in weight of clothing or time of day," which this stem has already addressed.
Trap: (B) treats a possible complication as a dietary problem, which is the error the fact bank names: sudden excessive gain after 20 weeks is a preeclampsia flag, not a counseling opportunity. (D) is separately wrong, since sodium is not routinely restricted in pregnancy and restriction has not proved effective in preventing hypertensive disorders.

Q: A severely underweight patient is not gaining adequately. Which outcomes are most associated with this pattern, as distinct from those associated with excessive gain?
A) Preterm labor, low birth weight, intrauterine growth restriction, small-for-gestational-age infants, and increased perinatal mortality
B) Gestational diabetes, hypertensive disorders, and cesarean birth
C) Stillbirth, birth defects, and abnormal fetal growth
D) Surgical site infection and venous thromboembolism
Answer: A
Rationale: "Severely underweight women are more likely to have preterm labor and to give birth to LBW infants," and both normal-weight and underweight women with inadequate gain have increased risk of intrauterine growth restriction. Low prepregnancy weight and inadequate gain "increase the risk of preterm birth and small-for-gestational-age (SGA) infants. Perinatal mortality is also increased" (pp. 298–299).
Trap: (B), (C), and (D) all list real risks that belong to obesity and excessive gain rather than to inadequate gain. Note that preterm birth appears on both lists, so it is not a discriminator between them; the growth-restriction and small-for-gestational-age outcomes are what mark the underweight pattern. Inadequate gain should also prompt screening for an eating disorder and for lack of financial resources to buy food.

Topic: energy and protein needs

Q: A patient in her first trimester says she has been "eating for two." What should the nurse teach about caloric needs?
A) During the first trimester the recommended daily caloric intake is the same as before pregnancy; an increase of 340 kcal per day begins in the second trimester
B) Caloric needs double from the moment of conception
C) An additional 340 kcal per day is needed from the first trimester onward
D) Caloric needs decrease in the first trimester because of nausea
Answer: A
Rationale: "During the first trimester the recommended daily caloric intake is the same as in the nonpregnant state. An increase of 340 kcal/day during the second trimester and 452 kcal/day during the third trimester is recommended" (pp. 300–301).
Trap: (B) is the belief this teaching exists to correct, and it is wrong by an order of magnitude. The source quantifies how small the real increase is: the second-trimester addition is "one additional serving" from a single food group, and the third-trimester addition is about one-third of a serving beyond that.

Q: A patient in her third trimester asks how much extra food the additional calories represent. Which response is accurate?
A) About 452 kcal per day above nonpregnant needs, which is roughly one additional serving plus one-third of a serving from a basic food group
B) About 1000 kcal per day, roughly one additional meal
C) About 100 kcal per day, roughly one piece of fruit
D) No increase is needed in the third trimester, since activity typically decreases
Answer: A
Rationale: The third-trimester increase is 452 kcal per day. "The additional kilocalories needed during the second trimester can be provided by one additional serving" from milk, yogurt or cheese, fruits, vegetables, or grains, and "in the third trimester, an additional one-third of a serving will provide the needed kilocalories" (p. 301). "Longitudinal assessment of weight gain during pregnancy is the best way to determine whether the kilocalorie intake is adequate."
Trap: (B) overshoots by more than double, which is how the "eating for two" idea survives even after the number is learned. The check the source recommends is not calorie counting at all but the weight-gain trend over time, which is what a prenatal visit actually measures.

Q: What is the recommended protein intake during pregnancy, and when does the increase begin?
A) 71 g per day, up from 46 g, with the increase applying to the second and third trimesters
B) 71 g per day beginning at conception
C) 46 g per day throughout, since American protein intake is already adequate
D) 96 g per day, double the nonpregnant requirement
Answer: A
Rationale: "The recommended protein intake during pregnancy is 71 g/day; this is an increase over the 46 g/day recommended for nonpregnant adults" (p. 301). Table 15.3 specifies "first trimester, same as nonpregnant; second and third trimesters, nonpregnant needs + 25 g," with a footnote to "add an additional 25 g in twin pregnancies."
Trap: (B) starts the increase a trimester early, which mirrors the same error in the energy row — both nutrients hold at nonpregnant levels through the first trimester. (C) misuses a true framing sentence: the source does say many women may not need to increase protein at all because American intake is relatively high, but that is an observation about actual diets, not a lowering of the recommendation.

Q: A pregnant patient following a macrobiotic diet asks about taking a high-protein supplement to be safe. Which response is accurate?
A) High-protein supplements are not recommended because of potentially harmful effects on the fetus; her intake should be addressed through food sources
B) High-protein supplements are recommended for all women on restricted diets
C) High-protein supplements are safe but unnecessary, since protein needs do not rise in pregnancy
D) She should double the recommended protein intake to compensate for her diet
Answer: A
Rationale: "High-protein supplements are not recommended because of potentially harmful effects on the fetus" (p. 301). Women adhering to a macrobiotic, or highly restricted vegetarian, diet are named alongside pregnant adolescents and women from impoverished backgrounds as those whose protein intake is most likely to be inadequate.
Trap: (B) is the reflex response to a patient in a named risk group — the risk is real, and the proposed remedy is the one the source rules out. This patient needs attention to food sources and dietitian involvement, which is also the chapter's guidance for vegan patients generally.

Q: A patient asks what combination of foods would supply most of her recommended protein. Which response reflects the chapter?
A) Three servings of milk, yogurt, or cheese, and two servings of 5 to 6 oz of meat, poultry, or fish
B) One serving of dairy and one 3-oz serving of meat daily
C) Protein powder added to two meals daily
D) Six servings of whole grains daily, which are complete protein foods
Answer: A
Rationale: "Three servings of milk, yogurt, or cheese (four for adolescents) and two servings (5 to 6 oz. [140 to 168 g]) of meat, poultry, or fish would supply most of the recommended protein" (p. 301). Milk, meat, eggs, and cheese are complete protein foods of high biologic value; legumes, whole grains, and nuts are also valuable sources.
Trap: (D) promotes whole grains from valuable sources to complete proteins, which the source reserves for the animal-derived foods. Note also the parenthetical this item does not test but the adolescent topic does: adolescents need four dairy servings rather than three.

Topic: fats carbohydrates and mercury safety

Q: A pregnant patient asks whether she should avoid all fish because of mercury. Which response reflects the chapter?
A) She should avoid shark, swordfish, king mackerel, and tilefish, but may eat up to 12 oz per week of low-mercury fish such as shrimp, salmon, pollock, catfish, and canned light tuna
B) She should avoid all fish and shellfish throughout pregnancy
C) She may eat any fish in unlimited quantities as long as it is thoroughly cooked
D) She should eat only albacore tuna, which is lowest in mercury
Answer: A
Rationale: The Safety Alert directs pregnant women to "avoid eating shark, swordfish, king mackerel, and tilefish" and to "eat as much as 12 oz. (340 g) per week of a variety of commercially caught fish and shellfish low in mercury, such as shrimp, salmon, pollock, catfish, and canned light tuna," while limiting "albacore or white tuna and tuna steaks, which contain more mercury, to 6 oz. (170 g) per week" (p. 305).
Trap: (D) inverts the tuna rule, which is the confusable pair inside this alert: canned light tuna is the permitted one and albacore or white tuna is the limited one. (B) is the overcautious answer that contradicts the chapter's own recommendation of one to two seafood servings per week for docosahexaenoic acid.

Q: A patient asks about fish caught by her brother in a local lake, for which she can find no state advisory. What should the nurse advise?
A) Limit intake of that fish to 6 oz and eat no other fish that week
B) Avoid all locally caught fish under every circumstance
C) Treat it as a low-mercury fish and eat up to 12 oz that week
D) No limit is needed for freshwater fish
Answer: A
Rationale: The Safety Alert directs women to "check local advisories about the safety of fish caught by family and friends in local bodies of water, but if no advisory is available, limit intake of these fish to 6 oz. (170 g), and eat no other fish that week" (p. 305).
Trap: (C) applies the commercial low-mercury allowance to a fish of unknown mercury content. The clause most often dropped is the second one — the 6-oz limit comes with a prohibition on other fish that week, so it is not simply a smaller portion but a weekly cap.

Q: Why is docosahexaenoic acid recommended during pregnancy, and how much?
A) It is essential to fetal brain development and neurologic function; many providers recommend at least 300 mg per day, obtainable from one to two servings of seafood per week
B) It prevents preeclampsia; 1000 mg per day is recommended
C) It replaces the need for folic acid supplementation in the third trimester
D) It is a saturated fat and should be limited to 10% of daily calories
Answer: A
Rationale: The long-chain polyunsaturated fatty acids docosahexaenoic acid and arachidonic acid "are considered essential to fetal brain development and neurologic function," and supplementation with omega-3 forms "has been associated with reduced risk for preterm birth and improved neurologic and visual development in offspring." "Many health care providers recommend at least 300 mg/day of DHA," and adequate amounts come from "one to two servings (8 to 12 oz.) of seafood per week" (pp. 301–305).
Trap: (D) confuses this fatty acid with the saturated fat limit from the same section. Saturated fat should be no more than 10% of total daily calories and trans fatty acids should be avoided entirely, but docosahexaenoic acid is a fat the chapter wants increased rather than restricted.

Q: What are the recommended carbohydrate intake and its proportion of daily calories during pregnancy?
A) 175 g per day, constituting no more than 45% to 64% of daily caloric intake, ideally from fresh fruits and vegetables and whole grains
B) 300 g per day, constituting at least 70% of daily caloric intake
C) 100 g per day, with carbohydrates restricted to prevent gestational diabetes
D) No specific recommendation exists, since carbohydrate needs do not change in pregnancy
Answer: A
Rationale: "Carbohydrates are the primary source of energy, and carbohydrate needs increase during pregnancy to 175 g/day. Carbohydrates should constitute no more than 45% to 64% of daily caloric intake." The ideal sources "are fresh fruits and vegetables and whole grains instead of processed foods" (p. 305).
Trap: (C) applies a diabetes-management instinct to a healthy pregnancy, and it collides with the chapter's broader rule that pregnancy is not a time for restriction. Carbohydrate needs rise in pregnancy; what the source constrains is the proportion of calories and the quality of the source.

Topic: fat-soluble vitamin safety

Q: A patient asks whether she can take extra vitamin supplements to be thorough. Which principle should guide the nurse's response about vitamins A, D, E, and K?
A) These are fat-soluble and stored in body tissues, so prolonged overdoses can reach toxic levels; supplements should be taken only as prescribed, although toxicity from dietary sources is very unlikely
B) These are water-soluble and readily excreted in urine, so excess intake is harmless
C) These cannot reach toxic levels in pregnancy because maternal metabolism accelerates
D) These should all be supplemented at double the usual dose during pregnancy
Answer: A
Rationale: The fat-soluble vitamins are "stored in the body tissues; in the event of prolonged overdoses, these vitamins can reach toxic levels. Because of the high potential for toxicity, pregnant women are advised to take fat-soluble vitamin supplements only as prescribed. Toxicity from dietary sources is very unlikely" (p. 305).
Trap: The tempting error is applying the water-soluble rule to this class. Water-soluble vitamins have much smaller body stores, are readily excreted in the urine, and must be consumed frequently, with toxicity less likely — the opposite profile, which is why the class a vitamin belongs to decides the answer to any supplement question about it.

Q: A patient asks whether she should take a vitamin A supplement for fetal eye development. Which response is accurate?
A) Vitamin A in excessive amounts is a known teratogen, so supplements are not routinely recommended and it is best obtained from dietary sources
B) Vitamin A supplements are recommended for all pregnant women to support fetal eye development
C) Vitamin A has no role in fetal development, so supplementation is simply unnecessary
D) Vitamin A supplements are safe at any dose because excess is excreted in urine
Answer: A
Rationale: Vitamin A is "essential for cell differentiation and proliferation and for development of the heart, spine, eyes, and ears in the fetus," but "vitamin A in excessive amounts is a known teratogen. Congenital malformations of the heart, lungs, skull, and eye have occurred in infants of mothers who took excessive amounts. Vitamin A supplements are not recommended routinely" and it is "best obtained from dietary sources" (p. 305).
Trap: The trap here is that the nutrient's importance argues for supplementing it. Both halves are true at once: the vitamin is essential to the very organs that excess damages, so the answer is dietary sources rather than a supplement. Deep yellow and deep green vegetables and fruits are the sources named.

Q: A patient taking isotretinoin for cystic acne is planning a pregnancy, and her sister uses topical tretinoin. What should the nurse teach about each?
A) Isotretinoin in early pregnancy is associated with heart malformations, facial abnormalities, cleft palate, hydrocephalus, deafness and blindness, and increased miscarriage risk; topical tretinoin does not appear to enter the circulation in substantial amounts, but its safety in pregnancy has not been confirmed
B) Both are equally dangerous and both must be stopped
C) Both are safe in pregnancy because they are vitamin A analogs rather than vitamin A
D) Isotretinoin is safe once pregnancy is confirmed; only preconception use is a concern
Answer: A
Rationale: Isotretinoin use during early pregnancy "has been associated with an increased incidence of heart malformations, facial abnormalities, cleft palate, hydrocephalus, and deafness and blindness in the infant as well as an increased risk for miscarriage. Topical agents such as tretinoin (Retin-A) do not appear to enter the circulation in substantial amounts, but their safety in pregnancy has not been confirmed" (p. 306).
Trap: The two wrong directions are equally available here, and the source takes neither. The topical agent is not established as safe and is not established as dangerous; the honest answer is that its safety has not been confirmed, which is what the patient should be told rather than a reassurance or a warning the source does not support.

Q: A patient asks whether vitamin E supplementation will help prevent preeclampsia. Which response is accurate?
A) There is no evidence that supplementation improves outcomes, it does not prevent preeclampsia, and vitamin E supplementation is not recommended during pregnancy
B) Vitamin E supplementation is recommended specifically to prevent preeclampsia
C) Vitamin E requirements double in pregnancy, so supplementation is necessary
D) Vitamin E is water-soluble, so supplementation carries no risk
Answer: A
Rationale: Vitamin E is an antioxidant that helps protect against oxidative stress, but "there is no evidence that supplementation ... improves outcomes; it does not prevent problems such as preeclampsia. In fact, supplementation with vitamin E is not recommended during pregnancy" (p. 305). Table 15.3 gives the same value of 15 mg for nonpregnant and pregnant women.
Trap: (C) assumes a pregnancy increase where the table shows none. Vitamin E is one of the nutrients whose requirement does not rise, alongside vitamin K, whose pregnancy requirement is stated to be the same as for nonpregnant women, and calcium, whose requirement is unchanged in both pregnancy and lactation.

Topic: water-soluble vitamins in pregnancy

Q: A patient with persistent nausea and vomiting asks whether any vitamin might help. Which vitamin does the chapter identify, and what is its pregnancy requirement?
A) Pyridoxine, vitamin B6, with a requirement of 1.9 mg per day, though larger doses are effective for some women in reducing nausea and vomiting
B) Vitamin B12, with a requirement of 2.6 mcg per day
C) Vitamin C, with a requirement of 85 mg per day
D) Folic acid, with a requirement of 600 mcg per day
Answer: A
Rationale: Pyridoxine is "essential for carbohydrate, protein, and fat metabolism and is involved in the synthesis of RBCs, antibodies, and neurotransmitters," with a pregnancy requirement of 1.9 mg per day, and "there is evidence that larger doses are effective for some women in reducing nausea and vomiting" (p. 306).
Trap: The distinction worth holding is that the daily requirement and the therapeutic dose are different numbers for this vitamin. A stem asking what she should take for nausea is not answered by the 1.9 mg requirement, which is a baseline intake rather than a treatment.

Q: A patient following a vegan diet asks which vitamin she is most at risk of lacking, given her food choices. Which response is accurate?
A) Vitamin B12, since its food sources are milk and milk products, eggs, meats, and liver, with fortified soy milk the only plant source named
B) Vitamin C, since plant diets are low in ascorbic acid
C) Folate, since plant foods contain no folate
D) Vitamin E, since vegetable oils are the only source
Answer: A
Rationale: Vitamin B12 is "involved in production of nucleic acids and protein; especially important in the formation of RBCs and neural functioning," and its sources are "milk and milk products, eggs, meats, liver, and fortified soy milk" (p. 306). Strict vegetarians are likely to be deficient in iron, zinc, vitamins D, E, and B12, choline, calcium, and essential fatty acids, and every vegan patient warrants a registered dietitian referral, ideally before conception.
Trap: (C) inverts folate's food profile entirely — dark green leafy vegetables, legumes, oranges, broccoli, and asparagus are among its richest sources, so a plant-based diet is generally strong in folate. The distinction that matters clinically is between lacto-ovo-vegetarians, who are unlikely to have significant problems, and vegans, who need the referral.

Q: A patient who smokes asks whether her vitamin C needs differ. Which response is accurate?
A) Women who smoke and women exposed to secondhand smoke need more vitamin C, which is important for tissue formation and enhances the absorption of iron
B) Smoking does not affect vitamin C requirements
C) Smokers need less vitamin C because of slowed metabolism
D) Vitamin C is fat-soluble, so supplementation risks toxicity in smokers
Answer: A
Rationale: Vitamin C "plays an important role in tissue formation and enhances the absorption of iron." The requirement is met by "at least one or two daily servings of citrus fruit or juice," but "women who smoke and women who are exposed to secondhand smoke need more" (p. 306).
Trap: (D) misclassifies the vitamin, which matters because the class determines the toxicity risk. Vitamin C is water-soluble, so body stores are small and it is readily excreted. Its second listed role is the one that connects this topic to the iron box: enhancing iron absorption is why the teaching there names citrus fruits, tomatoes, melons, and strawberries.

Topic: calcium magnesium and zinc

Q: A pregnant patient asks how much more calcium she needs now that she is pregnant. Which response is accurate?
A) There is no increase in the calcium requirement during pregnancy or lactation compared with the nonpregnant recommendation
B) Calcium requirements double during pregnancy to support fetal skeletal formation
C) Calcium requirements rise in pregnancy and rise further during lactation
D) Calcium requirements fall in pregnancy because absorption improves
Answer: A
Rationale: "There is no increase in the DRI of calcium during pregnancy and lactation compared with the recommendation for a nonpregnant woman." The nonpregnant amount "appears to provide sufficient calcium for fetal bone and tooth development to proceed while maintaining maternal bone mass" (pp. 306–307). Table 15.3 shows the same values across all three columns.
Trap: (B) and (C) are both versions of the error the fact bank calls the single most common one on this table. Because the fetal skeleton clearly requires calcium, an increase feels obligatory; the source's position is that the existing recommendation already covers it. Note also that the table's slashed values mean adolescents need more calcium than adults at every stage, which is a separate distinction from any pregnancy increase.

Q: A patient with lactose intolerance is concerned about her calcium intake. What should the nurse suggest first?
A) Try yogurt, sweet acidophilus milk, buttermilk, cheese, chocolate milk, or cocoa, which may be tolerated even when fresh fluid milk is not, along with lactase supplements or lactase-treated milk
B) Begin a calcium supplement immediately
C) Eliminate all dairy products and rely on dark green leafy vegetables including spinach
D) Accept that her calcium intake will be inadequate throughout pregnancy
Answer: A
Rationale: Milk consumption in lactose intolerance can cause "abdominal cramping, bloating, and diarrhea," although "many affected individuals can tolerate small amounts of milk without symptoms." The named workarounds are that "yogurt, sweet acidophilus milk, buttermilk, cheese, chocolate milk, and cocoa may be tolerated even when fresh fluid milk is not," plus commercial lactase supplements and lactase-treated milk (pp. 306–307). A calcium supplement is considered only if intake appears low and the woman does not change her dietary habits despite counseling, and it is determined by her provider.
Trap: (C) names spinach, which Table 15.3 specifically excludes — the calcium food source entry reads "dark green leafy vegetables except spinach or Swiss chard," the same two vegetables the iron box names as absorption inhibitors for their oxalates. (B) reaches for the supplement before the dietary strategies the source puts first.

Q: A patient with iron deficiency anemia is prescribed more than 60 mg of elemental iron daily. What additional supplement does the chapter indicate she needs, and why?
A) Zinc, because when large amounts of iron and folic acid are consumed, the absorption of zinc is inhibited
B) Calcium, because iron and calcium compete for the same transporter
C) Vitamin E, because high-dose iron increases oxidative stress
D) No additional supplement, since a prenatal vitamin covers all needs
Answer: A
Rationale: "When large amounts of iron and folic acid are consumed, the absorption of zinc is inhibited ... If a woman is taking more than 60 mg of elemental iron, she also needs a zinc supplement" (p. 307). Zinc is a constituent of numerous enzymes in major metabolic pathways, and "zinc deficiency is associated with fetal malformations of the central nervous system."
Trap: (D) treats the prenatal vitamin as a complete answer, which the chapter separately warns against — a multiple micronutrient supplement "does not lessen the need to consume a nutritious, well-balanced diet," and it is not formulated for the interaction created by high-dose therapeutic iron. The threshold of 60 mg is what converts this from a theoretical interaction into an instruction.

Q: Which statement about magnesium in pregnancy reflects the chapter?
A) Diets of women in the childbearing years are likely to be low in magnesium, with adolescents and low-income women especially at risk
B) Magnesium deficiency is rare because it is present in all foods
C) Magnesium requirements fall during pregnancy
D) Magnesium is a fat-soluble nutrient with a high risk of toxicity
Answer: A
Rationale: Magnesium is involved in "protein synthesis, muscle and nerve function, glycolysis, and regulation of blood pressure." "Diets of women in the childbearing years are likely to be low in magnesium. Adolescents and low-income women are especially at risk." Sources are "dairy products, nuts, whole grains, and green leafy vegetables" (p. 307).
Trap: (C) reverses the direction shown in Table 15.3, where the pregnancy value rises above the nonpregnant one. The at-risk groups named here overlap with the two groups the chapter flags repeatedly — adolescents and women with limited financial resources — which is a pattern worth noticing across the whole chapter rather than a fact about magnesium alone.

Topic: fluids sodium pica and nutritional risk

Q: A patient at 36 weeks reports irregular contractions and leg cramping. Her fluid intake has been poor. Which teaching is indicated?
A) Recommended fluid intake is about 8 to 12 cups daily, with food supplying an additional 700 mL or more for a total of 3 L per day; dehydration may increase the risk for cramping, contractions, and preterm labor
B) Fluid intake should be restricted to reduce peripheral edema
C) Fluid needs do not change in pregnancy
D) She should drink only when thirsty, since overhydration is the greater risk
Answer: A
Rationale: "The recommended daily intake of fluid is about 8 to 12 cups a day. ... Foods in the diet should supply an additional 700 mL or more of fluid for a total intake of 3 L/day," from water, milk, and decaffeinated tea. "Dehydration may increase the risk for cramping, contractions, and preterm labor" (pp. 307–308).
Trap: (B) applies an outdated edema strategy that this chapter explicitly reverses, and it would worsen exactly the symptoms the patient reports. The link between dehydration and contractions is what makes hydration an assessment priority rather than general advice in a patient reporting irregular contractions near term.

Q: A patient with mild ankle edema at 32 weeks asks whether she should cut salt out of her diet. Which response reflects the chapter?
A) Sodium is not routinely restricted in pregnancy; moderate peripheral edema is normal, and restriction has not proved effective in preventing hypertensive disorders such as preeclampsia
B) Strict sodium restriction is standard care for peripheral edema in pregnancy
C) Sodium should be eliminated entirely after 20 weeks
D) Sodium needs decrease in pregnancy because body water expands
Answer: A
Rationale: "In the past, dietary sodium was routinely restricted in an effort to control the peripheral edema that commonly occurs during pregnancy. It is now recognized that moderate peripheral edema is normal in pregnancy ... Sodium is not routinely restricted in pregnancy, and restriction has not proved to be effective in preventing hypertensive disorders of pregnancy." The need for sodium in fact "increases slightly," and "severe sodium restriction may make it difficult for pregnant women to achieve an adequate diet" (p. 308).
Trap: (D) gets the direction backwards for the very reason the source gives for the increase — body water is expanding, which raises the sodium requirement rather than lowering it. Restriction is warranted only for a medical condition such as renal or liver failure or cardiovascular disease; the practical target is light salting during cooking, none at the table, and avoiding low-nutrient high-sodium foods.

Q: A patient with anemia mentions that she has been chewing ice constantly. What is the nurse's most appropriate action?
A) Ask open, nonjudgmental questions about cravings, such as whether she has had cravings for things that are not food
B) Explain immediately why eating nonfood substances is harmful
C) Document the finding and take no further action
D) Tell her that ice is harmless and requires no discussion
Answer: A
Rationale: Pica is "likely to be discovered only by the sensitive interviewer who has developed a relationship of trust with the woman. In a nonjudgmental manner," the interviewer may ask "Have you had any cravings?" and "Have you had cravings for things that are not food, such as ice, clay, or dirt?" and "How much of these things have you eaten and how often?" Pica is "strongly associated with iron deficiency during pregnancy, although the cause is unclear," and screening occurs "at the first prenatal visit, every trimester, and anytime anemia is present" (p. 308).
Trap: (B) is the well-intentioned response that ends the conversation. Education before assessment is premature when the behavior has not yet been characterized, and a judgmental opening is what prevents disclosure of the substances that actually matter. (D) treats ice as trivial, but the chapter names ice and freezer frost within its own definition of pica.

Q: Which harms does the chapter attribute to pica?
A) Displacement of more nutritious foods from the diet, interference with absorption of nutrients especially minerals, and risk that nonfood items are contaminated with heavy metals or other toxic substances
B) Only the risk of intestinal obstruction from ingested material
C) Only nutritional displacement; contamination has not been documented
D) Pica is harmless and is a normal variant of food craving
Answer: A
Rationale: The chapter names three harms: "regular and heavy consumption of low-nutrient products may cause more nutritious foods to be displaced from the diet"; "the pica items consumed may interfere with the absorption of nutrients, especially minerals"; and "there is a risk that nonfood items are contaminated with heavy metals or other toxic substances" (p. 308). Lead contamination of soils and soil-based products "has caused high levels of lead in pregnant women and their newborns," and clay "can be a source of parasites or other infectious agents."
Trap: (D) conflates pica with ordinary food cravings, which the chapter treats differently — satisfying ordinary cravings "is not harmful," with concern only about dietary imbalance. The line between the two is whether the substance is food, and that line is why the screening question asks specifically about cravings for things that are not food.

Q: A nurse is screening a new prenatal patient. Select all the findings the chapter lists as indicators of nutritional risk in pregnancy.
A) Adolescence
B) An interpregnancy interval of less than 3 months
C) A restrictive diet such as vegetarian, vegan, macrobiotic, or raw food
D) Limited access to food sources, such as poverty
E) A prepregnancy body mass index in the normal range
Answer: A, B, C, D
Rationale: Box 15.2 lists adolescence; frequent gestations with an interpregnancy interval of less than 3 months; history of low-birth-weight infants or obstetric complications; history of or current eating disorder; use of tobacco, alcohol, or drugs; weight at conception under or over normal weight; weight loss during pregnancy; restrictive diet; low hemoglobin or hematocrit; food allergies or intolerances; history of or current gestational diabetes; chronic illness affecting intake, absorption, or metabolism; and limited access to food sources (p. 305).
Trap: (E) is the single wrong option, and the box in fact flags weight at conception that is under *or over* normal, so a normal body mass index is the one weight finding that is not a risk indicator. (C) is the option most likely to be left unchecked, because the chapter elsewhere says lacto-ovo-vegetarians are unlikely to have significant problems — the box and the vegetarian section answer different questions, and ordinary vegetarianism does appear on this list.

Topic: adolescent pregnancy nutrition

Q: A pregnant 16-year-old with a normal body mass index asks how much weight she should gain. Which response reflects the chapter?
A) The same range as for adult women, 25 to 35 lb, but she is encouraged to choose a goal at the upper end of that range
B) A higher range than for adult women, because she is still growing
C) A lower range than for adult women, to reduce the risk of cephalopelvic disproportion
D) A range determined from adolescent body mass index growth charts rather than adult categories
Answer: A
Rationale: "Recommended weight gain goals for pregnant adolescents are the same as those for pregnant women. Pregnant adolescents are encouraged to choose a weight gain goal at the upper end of the range for their BMI. BMI is calculated the same as for adult women rather than by using the adolescent BMI growth charts" (p. 313).
Trap: All three wrong options are live errors the fact bank names: a higher range, a lower range, and pediatric growth charts. The distinction is subtle and worth holding precisely — the range is identical, and only the target within it moves upward.

Q: Why do young adolescents have a higher incidence of cephalopelvic disproportion and other mechanical problems in labor?
A) Growth of the pelvis is delayed in comparison with growth in stature
B) Adolescent pelvic ligaments are less responsive to relaxin
C) Adolescents have larger infants than adult women
D) The mechanism is nutritional rather than anatomic
Answer: A
Rationale: "Growth of the pelvis is delayed in comparison with growth in stature, and this helps explain why cephalopelvic disproportion and other mechanical problems associated with labor are common among young adolescents" (p. 313).
Trap: (D) reaches for the nutritional explanation in a nutrition chapter, and the chapter gives two separate mechanisms rather than one. The anatomic mechanism is delayed pelvic growth; the nutritional mechanism, stated separately, is competition for nutrients between the growing adolescent and the fetus. Attributing everything to nutrition drops half of what the source says.

Q: A nurse is assessing risk in a pregnant adolescent. Select all the statements the chapter supports.
A) Growth of the pelvis is delayed relative to growth in stature, contributing to mechanical problems in labor
B) Competition for nutrients between the growing adolescent and the fetus may contribute to poor outcomes
C) Many adolescent girls have diets providing less than recommended intakes of calcium, iron, and folic acid
D) Approximately 80% of teen pregnancies are unintentional, meaning there is no opportunity for preconception nutrition counseling
E) Obesity is rare among adolescent girls, so excessive gestational weight gain is not a concern in this group
Answer: A, B, C, D
Rationale: The chapter names delayed pelvic growth and nutrient competition as the two mechanisms behind poorer adolescent outcomes. "Many adolescent girls have diets that provide less than the recommended intakes of key nutrients including calcium, iron, and folic acid," and "approximately 80%" of teen pregnancies are unintentional (p. 313).
Trap: (E) is the single wrong option and it contradicts the chapter directly: "nearly 20%" of teen girls in the United States are obese, "and when they become pregnant, they are prone to the same obstetric risks as obese women." The adolescent is simultaneously at risk for undernutrition and for obesity, which is why the assessment cannot assume either direction.

Q: How many servings of milk, yogurt, or cheese does the chapter recommend for a pregnant adolescent, and how does this compare with an adult woman?
A) Four servings for an adolescent, compared with three for an adult woman
B) Three servings for both, since dairy needs do not vary by age
C) Two servings for an adolescent, since adolescents tolerate dairy poorly
D) Six servings for an adolescent, double the adult recommendation
Answer: A
Rationale: "Three servings of milk, yogurt, or cheese (four for adolescents) and two servings (5 to 6 oz. [140 to 168 g]) of meat, poultry, or fish would supply most of the recommended protein" (p. 301). Adolescents are also named among those whose protein intake is most likely to be inadequate, and as especially at risk for low magnesium.
Trap: (B) misses a pattern that runs through Table 15.3 as well: where a value is given as two numbers separated by a slash, the first is for adolescents younger than 19 and the second for women 19 to 50. Calcium is the clearest instance, where adolescents need 1300 mg against an adult 1000 mg, so the adolescent requirement can be higher even where the pregnancy requirement does not rise at all.
