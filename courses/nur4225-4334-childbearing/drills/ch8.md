# Chapter 8 drills — Contraception and Abortion

Authored from `sources/Ch8_Fact_Bank.md` (Lowdermilk, Cashion, Alden, Olshansky
& Perry, *Maternity & Women's Health Care*, 13th ed.).

Blueprint weight is **5 questions of 200 (2.5%), depth `standard`**, topic
*"Contraception, Family Planning, Abortion Risks."* That earns **45 questions
across 10 topics**, the count this course uses for a `standard` chapter.

**Scope, and how the reference layer is rationed.** The Week 1 reading guide
assigns two islands out of a 21-page chapter: the counseling and consent
framework at the front (pp. 143–144, with the BRAIDED Legal Tip and the
backup-method Safety Alert) and induced abortion at the back (pp. 161–163, with
the postabortion Safety Alert). The fact bank curates those at full depth and
keeps the method-by-method middle as an explicitly labelled **reference layer**,
because the blueprint topic line names "Contraception" and "Family Planning"
generally even though the reading guide does not assign those pages.

That split is carried through here on purpose: **41 of the 45 questions come
from the two assigned islands** (22 from the counseling and consent front,
19 from induced abortion), and only 4 come from the reference layer — the
warning-signs mnemonic and the contraindication cutoffs, which are the two
reference objects a contraception exam item is most likely to be built from.
The reference layer is deliberately not drilled method by method; a chapter that
spent 20 questions on failure-rate tables the reading guide never assigned would
be trading assigned depth for unassigned breadth.

Topics split the front island three ways rather than calling it all
"contraceptive counseling," because the chapter draws three different lines
through it — what the terms mean, how the counseling visit is run, and what
informed consent requires — and a learner can be solid on one and lost in
another. Splitting them means a red topic says which.

Every invertible pair the fact bank flags is drilled from both ends: family
planning and contraception and birth control as a trio, theoretic and typical
effectiveness, elective and therapeutic abortion, misoprostol and mifepristone,
and the surgical-versus-medical contraception restart rule.

**What the band could not buy.** Cut, in this order: the typical-use failure
rate table method by method (13 rows of numbers the reading guide does not
assign, and whose page cites are still PDF-era — see the note below); Table
8.1's duration-of-effect column by route; the full 20-item contraindication list
beyond the smoking, migraine, and thromboembolic entries; the specific
discharge-instruction variations the source itself flags as differing among
providers, of which only the settled items are drilled; the ectopic-risk figure
after tubal reanastomosis; and the reasons-for-abortion list. Kept without
exception: BRAIDED in full, the CDC definition's four elements, both Safety
Alerts, the misoprostol and mifepristone mechanisms, the Rh prophylaxis window
and its two conditions, and the retained-products reasoning chain, because those
are the items that decide an action.

**A citation note.** The fact bank's section 4 reference table carries page
numbers in the 389–433 range, which are outside this chapter's printed range of
pp. 143–163. Those are **deliberately labelled `PDF` in the bank's outline**, not
survivors of the 2026-09-02 re-citation — they mark the unassigned method-by-method
pages, for which no printed-page cite was established. Nothing below is cited to
them. Every page cite in this file is a printed page from a re-cited section.

Topic: family planning terminology and framing

Q: A patient tells the nurse that she and her partner have decided they want to wait four years before trying to conceive, and are weighing what to do in the meantime. Which term names the decision she has just described?
A) Family planning
B) Contraception
C) Birth control
D) Fertility awareness
Answer: A
Rationale: Family planning is "the conscious decision on when to conceive or to avoid pregnancy throughout the reproductive years" (p. 143). The patient has described a decision about timing across her reproductive years, not a method.
Trap: (C) is the reflex pick because the three terms are used interchangeably in ordinary speech. The fact bank gives the cue that separates them: if the option describes a choice or a plan it is family planning, and if it describes a thing you use it is birth control.

Q: The same patient then asks the nurse to explain what the phrase "birth control" specifically refers to. Which response is accurate?
A) The device or practice used to decrease the risk of conceiving or bearing offspring
B) The conscious decision on when to conceive or to avoid pregnancy
C) The intentional prevention of pregnancy during sexual intercourse
D) The percentage of users expected to conceive during the first year of use
Answer: A
Rationale: Birth control is "the device and/or practice used to decrease the risk of conceiving or bearing offspring" (p. 143). It names the thing used, not the decision behind it or the outcome sought.
Trap: (B) is the mirror error of the previous item, handing back the decision when the device was asked for. (D) defines the contraceptive failure rate, which is a separate term from the same page cluster.

Q: A nursing student is asked which term names the intentional prevention of pregnancy during sexual intercourse. Which term is it?
A) Contraception
B) Family planning
C) Birth control
D) Therapeutic abortion
Answer: A
Rationale: Contraception is "the intentional prevention of pregnancy during sexual intercourse" (p. 143). The three terms are a graded set rather than synonyms: family planning names the decision, contraception the intent and outcome, and birth control the device or practice.
Trap: This is the third angle on the same trio, and it is deliberate. A learner who has the set backwards will get one of the three right by elimination; asking from all three ends is what exposes it.

Q: A nurse is preparing a community education session and wants to open with the scale of the problem. Which statement reflects the chapter's framing?
A) Nearly half of all US pregnancies are not planned, and Healthy People 2030 aims to reduce unintended pregnancies from 43.0% to 36.5%
B) Nearly one in ten US pregnancies is unplanned, and the rate has been rising steadily
C) Unintended pregnancy is now rare in the US, which is why modern contraception is called a public health achievement
D) Healthy People 2030 aims to eliminate unintended pregnancy entirely by increasing access to sterilization
Answer: A
Rationale: Modern contraception "is considered one of the 10 greatest public health achievements of the 20th century," and "nevertheless, nearly half of all US pregnancies are not planned." The Healthy People 2030 goal is to "decrease the percentage of unintended pregnancies from 43.0% to 36.5% by increasing the use of effective birth control" (p. 143).
Trap: (C) is the seductive misreading of the achievement sentence — the chapter names contraception an achievement and then immediately says nearly half of pregnancies are still unplanned. The two statements sit next to each other precisely because the second qualifies the first.

Topic: contraceptive counseling and assessment

Q: A nurse is preparing to counsel a patient about contraceptive options. Which arrangement of the visit best reflects the chapter's guidance?
A) A private setting with distractions minimized and samples of birth control devices available for interactive teaching
B) A group session in the waiting area, so that patients can learn from one another's questions
C) The examination room during the pelvic examination, so that anatomy can be pointed out directly
D) A telephone call after discharge, so that the patient can consult her partner first
Answer: A
Rationale: Contraceptive counseling is "best conducted in a private setting. The woman should feel safe and free to communicate. Distractions are minimized, and samples of birth control devices for interactive teaching are available" (pp. 143–144).
Trap: (C) sounds efficient and clinically grounded, which is what makes it attractive. A patient in the lithotomy position mid-examination is not in a setting where she is free to communicate, which is the condition the source actually specifies.

Q: A patient has chosen a diaphragm. After explaining how it is used, what should the nurse do next?
A) Ask her to perform a return demonstration and assess her understanding
B) Document that teaching was completed and schedule her annual visit
C) Provide written instructions and end the session, since repetition can overwhelm
D) Refer her to the physician to confirm that she has understood
Answer: A
Rationale: "Education is the cornerstone of the plan of care." After the method is taught, the nurse asks "the woman to perform a return demonstration of how a device is used, if appropriate, and assess[es] her understanding," then provides written instructions, a list of reliable websites, and contact information (pp. 143–144).
Trap: (B) is the reflex pick for anyone who treats documentation as the closing step of teaching. Documenting that teaching occurred is not the same as verifying that it worked, and for a device method the source names the return demonstration specifically.

Q: A patient is leaving with her first prescription for combined oral contraceptives. The chapter singles out one piece of information as very important to provide. Which is it?
A) Information about backup methods of birth control and emergency contraception
B) The theoretic effectiveness rate of the method to the nearest tenth of a percent
C) A schedule of the long-term cancer risks associated with hormonal methods
D) Instructions to discontinue the method if any side effect occurs
Answer: A
Rationale: "It is very important to provide the woman and her partner with information about backup methods of birth control and emergency contraception (EC)" (pp. 143–144). This pairs with the chapter's Safety Alert, which is that a woman using a new method needs a backup method readily available during the initial learning phase.
Trap: (D) is the dangerous distractor because it sounds cautious. Blanket discontinuation on any side effect is not what the chapter teaches; the specific stop-and-report list is the warning-signs mnemonic, and stopping for a minor effect without a backup is how an unintended pregnancy happens.

Q: While assessing a patient who is considering a fertility-awareness-based method or a diaphragm, the nurse includes an appraisal that is not part of a routine history. Which assessment does the chapter name?
A) The woman's level of comfort and willingness to touch her genitals and cervical mucus
B) The woman's household income and insurance status
C) The partner's contraceptive history with previous partners
D) The woman's willingness to undergo laboratory testing for hormone levels
Answer: A
Rationale: Assessment "begins with the following appraisals," among them "assessing the woman's level of comfort and willingness to touch her genitals and cervical mucus," alongside her knowledge about contraception, her partner's commitment, frequency of coitus, number of partners, objections to any methods, misconceptions, religious and cultural factors, and her reproductive life plan (pp. 143–144).
Trap: (B) and (C) are plausible-sounding intake items that the source does not name, and the second is not the nurse's to collect from this patient. The appraisal that distinguishes this assessment is the one that decides whether a method requiring self-touch is workable at all.

Topic: informed consent and the braided framework

Q: A nurse counseling a patient about an intrauterine device explains the advantages of the method and its success rates. Which element of the BRAIDED framework has the nurse addressed?
A) Benefits
B) Risks
C) Explanations
D) Alternatives
Answer: A
Rationale: In BRAIDED, B is Benefits — "information about advantages and success rates" — while R is Risks, "information about disadvantages and failure rates" (p. 144).
Trap: (C) is the reflex pick for anyone who reads any act of explaining as the Explanations element. Explanations is narrower than that: it is information about the method and how it is used. Success rates belong to Benefits, and failure rates to Risks.

Q: A nurse documents that a patient was told she could take more time and could change her mind about sterilization at any point before the procedure. Which BRAIDED element does this satisfy, and which one does it not?
A) It satisfies Decisions; it does not satisfy Documentation, which requires recording the information given and her understanding of it
B) It satisfies Documentation; Decisions refers to the provider's decision about candidacy
C) It satisfies both, since recording the conversation is what Decisions means
D) It satisfies neither, since BRAIDED does not apply to sterilization
Answer: A
Rationale: BRAIDED contains two different D elements: Decisions is the "opportunity to decide or to change her mind," and Documentation is "information given and client's understanding" (p. 144). The nurse "often has the responsibility to document information provided and the understanding of that information by the client."
Trap: (C) collapses the two D elements into one, which is the standard corruption of this mnemonic. Giving a patient room to change her mind and recording what she understood are separate obligations, and a chart entry can satisfy one without touching the other.

Q: A nursing student lists the BRAIDED elements as Benefits, Risks, Alternatives, Inquiries, Decisions, Effectiveness, and Documentation. Which correction is needed?
A) The E is Explanations, information about the method and how it is used; effectiveness is covered under Benefits and Risks
B) The E is Effectiveness, and the student is correct
C) The E is Evaluation, meaning assessment of the client's understanding after teaching
D) The E is Emergency contraception, which must be offered with every method
Answer: A
Rationale: E in BRAIDED stands for Explanations — "information about method and how it is used" (p. 144). Success rates sit under Benefits and failure rates under Risks, so effectiveness is already accounted for elsewhere in the mnemonic.
Trap: (B) is the error the fact bank names, and it is attractive because effectiveness genuinely is something a patient must be told. The point is that it is told twice already, under two other letters, which is why the E is free to mean something else.

Q: A patient is scheduled for a tubal ligation. Which statement about informed consent for this procedure is accurate?
A) The BRAIDED framework applies, since the source names informed consent as vital in educating a client about contraception or sterilization
B) BRAIDED applies only to reversible methods, so a separate surgical consent framework is used
C) BRAIDED applies, but the Alternatives element is omitted for permanent methods
D) Informed consent for sterilization is the surgeon's responsibility alone, and the nurse has no documentation role
Answer: A
Rationale: "Informed consent is a vital component in educating a client about contraception or sterilization," and the nurse "often has the responsibility to document information provided and the understanding of that information by the client" (p. 144).
Trap: (C) is the subtle version of the error and it inverts the priority. Alternatives matter most, not least, when the method under discussion is intended to be permanent — a patient consenting to sterilization is precisely the one who needs to hear what the reversible options are.

Q: A nurse is auditing a contraception counseling note against the BRAIDED framework. Select all the elements that belong to BRAIDED as the source defines it.
A) Alternatives, meaning information about other available methods
B) Inquiries, meaning the opportunity to ask questions
C) Risks, meaning information about disadvantages and failure rates
D) Decisions, meaning the opportunity to decide or to change her mind
E) Duration, meaning how long the method remains effective
Answer: A, B, C, D
Rationale: BRAIDED is Benefits, Risks, Alternatives, Inquiries, Decisions, Explanations, and Documentation (p. 144).
Trap: (E) is the single wrong option and it is the standard corruption the fact bank warns about — a plausible clinical concept fitted to a letter that already means something else. Duration of effect is real content from the hormonal contraception table, but it is not a consent element.

Topic: contraceptive effectiveness and failure rates

Q: A package insert reports how effective a method is when it is used exactly as directed every time. Which term names this figure?
A) Theoretic effectiveness
B) Typical effectiveness
C) The contraceptive failure rate
D) The method's safety profile
Answer: A
Rationale: Theoretic effectiveness is "how effective the method is with perfect use," and typical effectiveness is "how effective the method is with typical use" (p. 144).
Trap: (C) is the pick for anyone who treats every effectiveness number as a failure rate. The failure rate is a distinct measure with a defined denominator and a one-year window, not a restatement of effectiveness under ideal conditions.

Q: A counselor wants to quote the number that reflects how the method performs in the hands of real users, including inconsistent ones. Which term names this figure?
A) Typical effectiveness
B) Theoretic effectiveness
C) Perfect-use effectiveness
D) The ideal contraceptive standard
Answer: A
Rationale: Typical effectiveness is "how effective the method is with typical use," as against theoretic effectiveness, which is perfect use (p. 144).
Trap: (B) is the mirror error of the previous item, and (C) is the same error wearing the correct term's own definition as its name — perfect use is the theoretic figure, so an option calling it perfect-use effectiveness is naming theoretic effectiveness in different words.

Q: A patient asks what the "7% failure rate" printed in her pill pamphlet actually counts. Which explanation is accurate?
A) The percentage of users expected to have an unplanned pregnancy during the first year of use, even when they use the method consistently and correctly
B) The percentage of users who will misuse the method at some point during the first year
C) The percentage of pills in a pack that fail to suppress ovulation
D) The lifetime percentage of users who will eventually conceive while using the method
Answer: A
Rationale: The contraceptive failure rate is "the percentage of contraceptive users expected to have an unplanned pregnancy during the first year of use, even when they use a method consistently and correctly" (p. 144).
Trap: (B) inverts the definition in the way the fact bank flags: the failure rate is not a misuse rate. The "even when they use a method consistently and correctly" clause is the whole point — these are pregnancies that occur despite correct use, which is why no method is quoted at zero.

Q: A nurse notes that a method's failure rate is lower in the second year of use than in the first, and a student asks why. Which explanation does the chapter give?
A) Users gain experience and use the method more appropriately, and less effective users stop using the method
B) The method becomes more potent with continued exposure
C) Fertility declines measurably over a single year of contraceptive use
D) Second-year figures exclude any user who has become pregnant
Answer: A
Rationale: "Failure rates decrease over time, either because a user gains experience and uses a method more appropriately or because less effective users stop using the method" (p. 144).
Trap: (B) is the error the fact bank names — attributing the decline to the method rather than to the population using it. Both stated reasons are about users: one improves, and the other leaves the denominator.

Q: A nurse is explaining why two patients using the same method may have different real-world results. Select all the factors the chapter names as affecting contraceptive method effectiveness.
A) Frequency of intercourse
B) Motivation to prevent pregnancy
C) Understanding of how to use the method
D) Consistent use of the method
E) The manufacturer's brand of the method chosen
Answer: A, B, C, D
Rationale: Box 8.1 lists frequency of intercourse, motivation to prevent pregnancy, understanding of how to use the method, adherence to correct use, provision of short- or long-term protection, likelihood of pregnancy for the individual woman, and consistent use of the method (p. 144). Effectiveness "depends on the properties of the method and the characteristics of the user."
Trap: (E) is the single wrong option, and it represents the framing error the fact bank flags — treating effectiveness as a property of the product. Five of the seven listed factors are user variables, which is why two people using an identical method get different results.

Topic: contraceptive safety and risk weighing

Q: A patient with well-controlled type 1 diabetes asks whether contraception is simply too risky for someone with a chronic illness. Which response reflects the chapter's position?
A) In most instances pregnancy would be more dangerous for a woman with medical problems than a particular contraceptive method would be
B) Women with any chronic medical condition should use only barrier methods
C) Contraceptive risk always exceeds pregnancy risk when a chronic illness is present
D) Contraceptive safety is unaffected by medical history, so her condition is not relevant
Answer: A
Rationale: Safety "may be affected by a woman's medical history (e.g., thromboembolic problems and contraceptive methods containing estrogen)," but "in most instances, pregnancy would be more dangerous for a woman with medical problems than a particular contraceptive method" (p. 144).
Trap: (D) overcorrects a true reassurance into a false one — medical history does matter, and the chapter names the thromboembolic-plus-estrogen combination as its example. The answer is that risk is weighed against the risk of pregnancy, not that risk is absent.

Q: A patient asks whether any contraceptive method offers a health benefit beyond preventing pregnancy. Which response reflects the chapter?
A) Barrier methods such as the male condom offer some protection from acquiring sexually transmitted infections, and oral contraceptives lower the incidence of ovarian and endometrial cancer
B) Oral contraceptives offer protection from sexually transmitted infections, and condoms lower cancer risk
C) No contraceptive method has effects beyond preventing pregnancy
D) All hormonal methods lower the incidence of every reproductive cancer, including cervical cancer
Answer: A
Rationale: "Barrier methods, such as the male condom, offer some protection from acquiring STIs, and oral contraceptives lower the incidence of ovarian and endometrial cancer" (p. 144).
Trap: (B) swaps the two benefits between the two method classes, which is the version most likely to be chosen quickly. (D) overstates a real benefit by extending it to every reproductive cancer, when the source names exactly two.

Q: A patient is starting a new contraceptive method today. Which teaching point should the nurse prioritize?
A) She needs a backup method of birth control readily available during the initial learning phase
B) She should watch for long-term metabolic side effects over the coming year
C) She should return in twelve months to reassess the method's typical effectiveness
D) She should discontinue the method if she experiences any breakthrough bleeding
Answer: A
Rationale: The chapter's Safety Alert states that when a woman is using a new method, "it is important that she understands the need to have a backup method of birth control readily available during the initial learning phase" (p. 144).
Trap: (B) and (C) are plausible-but-adjacent, and the fact bank names exactly this pattern: on a "what does the nurse teach first" item about a newly prescribed method, the backup-method answer beats options about long-term effects. The risk that a new method addresses is immediate, and the learning phase is when it is highest.

Q: A patient asks the nurse to describe what an ideal contraceptive would look like. Which response reflects the chapter's list?
A) Effective, safe, easily available, economical, acceptable, simple to use, and promptly reversible, although no method may ever have all of these characteristics
B) Effective and permanently reversible, with all other characteristics being matters of preference
C) Effective and inexpensive, since those two characteristics determine adherence
D) Effective and free of side effects, a standard several current methods already meet
Answer: A
Rationale: The ideal contraceptive "should be effective, safe, easily available, economical, acceptable, simple to use, and promptly reversible. Although no method may ever have all of these characteristics..." (p. 144).
Trap: (D) is attractive because it flatters current practice, and the source says the opposite in the same sentence that lists the criteria — the list is explicitly framed as a standard no method fully meets, which is why method choice is a matter of trade-offs.

Topic: oral contraceptive warning signs and contraindications

Q: A patient taking combined oral contraceptives is taught the ACHES warning signs. Select all the symptoms for which she should stop taking the pill and report immediately.
A) Abdominal pain
B) Chest pain or shortness of breath
C) Sudden or persistent headaches
D) Severe leg pain
E) Mild nausea during the first cycle of use
Answer: A, B, C, D
Rationale: The instruction is to "stop taking the pill and to report" any ACHES symptom "immediately": abdominal pain, suggesting a liver or gallbladder problem; chest pain or shortness of breath, suggesting a clot in the lungs or heart; headaches, suggesting cerebrovascular accident or hypertension; eye problems, with the same two explanations; and severe leg pain, suggesting a thromboembolic process (pp. 155–156).
Trap: (E) is the single wrong option and it is the one a cautious learner adds anyway. The mnemonic exists to separate the symptoms that mean stop the pill now from the ordinary early side effects that do not, and treating every symptom as an emergency defeats the sorting the list was written to do.

Q: A patient taking combined oral contraceptives calls to report new visual disturbances. Which explanation should the nurse associate with this symptom?
A) A vascular accident or hypertension
B) A liver or gallbladder problem
C) A thromboembolic process in the leg
D) A clot within the lungs or heart
Answer: A
Rationale: In ACHES, eye problems indicate a "vascular accident or hypertension," the same two explanations given for headaches. Abdominal pain points to the liver or gallbladder, chest pain or shortness of breath to a clot in the lungs or heart, and severe leg pain to a thromboembolic process (pp. 155–156).
Trap: (B) is the error the fact bank names — pairing eye problems with gallbladder is drawing the explanation from the wrong letter. The structural cue worth holding is that headaches and eye problems share their two explanations, which is why they can be learned as a pair.

Q: Four patients request combined oral contraceptives. Which one meets a contraindication exactly as the chapter states it?
A) A 36-year-old who smokes one pack per day
B) A 30-year-old who smokes one pack per day
C) A 38-year-old with migraine headaches without aura
D) A 25-year-old with irritable bowel syndrome in remission
Answer: A
Rationale: Box 8.3 lists "smoking 15 or more cigarettes per day at age 35 or older" as a contraindication, along with a history of thromboembolic disorders, cerebrovascular or cardiovascular disease, and migraine with aura (p. 155).
Trap: (B) and (C) each satisfy one half of a two-part criterion, which is why both are attractive. The smoking contraindication requires both the quantity and the age, so a 30-year-old smoking the same amount does not meet it; and it is migraine with aura that is contraindicated, not migraine generally.

Q: A patient taking combined oral contraceptives reports that she recently started an herbal supplement and has had breakthrough bleeding. Which supplement does the chapter name as able to alter the effectiveness of these pills?
A) St. John's wort
B) Echinacea
C) Ginkgo biloba
D) Saw palmetto
Answer: A
Rationale: The Medication Alert states that "over-the-counter medications as well as some herbal supplements (such as St. John's wort) can alter the effectiveness of COCs," and women should be asked about their use when combined oral contraceptives are prescribed (p. 155).
Trap: The other three are real supplements with real interactions elsewhere, which makes them plausible, but St. John's wort is the one this chapter names. The clinical reason the question matters is that the patient's presenting complaint, breakthrough bleeding, is also an early sign that contraceptive coverage may have been lost.

Topic: induced abortion definitions and law

Q: A nursing student is asked what makes a procedure a legal induced abortion under the CDC definition the chapter quotes. Select all the required elements.
A) It is performed by a licensed clinician
B) It is performed within the limits of state regulations
C) It is intended to terminate a suspected or known ongoing intrauterine pregnancy
D) It does not result in a live birth
E) It is performed before 12 weeks of gestation
Answer: A, B, C, D
Rationale: The CDC defines legal induced abortion as "an intervention performed by a licensed clinician (e.g., a physician, certified nurse-midwife, nurse practitioner, physician assistant) within the limits of state regulations that is intended to terminate a suspected or known ongoing intrauterine pregnancy and that does not result in a live birth" (p. 161).
Trap: (E) is the single wrong option and it imports a gestational limit the definition does not contain. It is attractive because roughly 90% of abortions are in fact first-trimester, so a true statistic gets mistaken for a defining criterion. The word "intrauterine" is the element most often overlooked, and it is what separates this from an ectopic pregnancy.

Q: A patient requests termination of an unintended pregnancy. There is no maternal or fetal health indication. Which term applies?
A) Elective or induced abortion
B) Therapeutic abortion
C) Spontaneous abortion
D) Second-trimester abortion
Answer: A
Rationale: "If the abortion is performed at the woman's request, the term elective or induced abortion is usually used" (p. 161).
Trap: (C) is the boundary the fact bank flags: spontaneous abortion is miscarriage, and this chapter explicitly sends it to Chapter 28. A distractor pulling miscarriage content into a Chapter 8 item is testing whether the reader knows the chapter's own scope.

Q: A pregnancy is terminated because continuing it would endanger the patient's life. Which term applies?
A) Therapeutic abortion
B) Elective abortion
C) Induced abortion at the woman's request
D) Spontaneous abortion
Answer: A
Rationale: "If performed for reasons of maternal or fetal health or disease, the term therapeutic abortion applies" (p. 161), as distinct from elective or induced abortion, which is performed at the woman's request.
Trap: (B) is the mirror error of the previous item. Both procedures may be identical technically, so the term is decided entirely by the indication, and a stem that supplies a medical indication has already answered the question.

Q: A patient asks the nurse what the legal status of abortion is now. Which response reflects the chapter?
A) The 2022 Dobbs vs. Jackson Women's Health decision overturned Roe vs. Wade, so each state can determine its own abortion legislation
B) The 2022 Dobbs decision made abortion illegal nationwide
C) Roe vs. Wade, decided in 1973, remains the controlling federal precedent
D) Abortion law has been determined by individual states continuously since before 1970
Answer: A
Rationale: "In January 1973, the US Supreme Court set aside previous antiabortion laws and legalized abortion as a result of the Roe vs. Wade decision." "In June 2022, the US Supreme Court overturned Roe vs. Wade by its decision in favor of Dobbs vs. Jackson Women's Health. As a result of this decision, each state can determine legislation regarding abortion," and about half the states have moved to outlaw it (p. 161).
Trap: (B) overstates the holding in the direction most people assume, and the fact bank flags it: the decision returned the question to the states rather than banning anything nationally. The practical consequence for the nurse is the Legal Tip that follows — know the law in your own state of practice before offering abortion counseling or care.

Q: A nurse is correcting a misconception that most abortions occur late in pregnancy. Which data from the chapter should the nurse cite?
A) Approximately 90% are performed in the first trimester and fewer than 1% after 20 weeks
B) Approximately 60% are performed in the first trimester and about 10% after 20 weeks
C) Approximately half are performed in each trimester, with the rate rising
D) Approximately 40% are performed in the first trimester, which is why medical abortion is uncommon
Answer: A
Rationale: Approximately 90% of abortions are performed in the first trimester and fewer than 1% after 20 weeks, and "with improved contraceptive methods available, the rate of abortion has been decreasing in the US" (p. 161).
Trap: (B) borrows a real number from the wrong place — 60% is aspiration's share of first-trimester procedures, not the first trimester's share of all abortions. Numbers from the same page cluster attached to the wrong denominator are the reliable way to miss this item.

Topic: nursing role and ethics in abortion care

Q: A nurse holds a personal moral objection to abortion and is assigned a patient seeking one. What is the nurse's ethical responsibility as the chapter states it?
A) To counsel the woman about her options and to make appropriate referrals
B) To decline the assignment and leave the unit until another nurse is available
C) To provide care while explaining the personal objection to the patient
D) To ask the patient to transfer to another facility
Answer: A
Rationale: "Nurses who provide care to women seeking abortion have an ethical responsibility to counsel women about their options and to make appropriate referrals" (pp. 161–163), regardless of personal views.
Trap: (B) fails the client-abandonment clause the chapter raises separately. The nurse's beliefs are accommodated through institutional policy, not by stepping away from a patient mid-care, and the two questions are handled in different places for that reason. (C) shifts the nurse's moral position onto a patient who did not ask for it.

Q: A nurse wants to understand how her institution handles a nurse's ethical or moral objection to participating in abortion care. What should she look for?
A) Policies that describe how the institution will accommodate the nurse's beliefs and what the nurse should do to avoid client abandonment
B) A state statute, since institutions cannot set policy on this question
C) An individual agreement negotiated with each patient at the time of care
D) Nothing; a nurse's objection is a private matter with no institutional dimension
Answer: A
Rationale: The Legal Tip on institutional policies states that nurses' rights "should be protected through policies that describe how the institution will accommodate the nurse's ethical or moral beliefs and what the nurse should do to avoid client abandonment in such situations. Nurses should know what policies are in place in their institutions and encourage such policies to be written" (p. 161).
Trap: (D) misses that the source treats this as a two-sided policy question: the policy protects the nurse and protects the patient from abandonment in the same document. A distractor that protects only one side is incomplete in the direction the exam cares about.

Q: A patient who has just learned she is pregnant is undecided about what to do. Which nursing approach reflects the chapter's guidance?
A) A calm, matter-of-fact approach that offers information about all options, including abortion, referral to adoption agencies, and support services if she chooses to keep her baby
B) Presenting abortion as the option most consistent with her stated circumstances
C) Deferring all discussion until she has decided, to avoid influencing her
D) Providing information about adoption and parenting only, unless she raises abortion herself
Answer: A
Rationale: "A calm, matter-of-fact approach on the part of the nurse can be helpful," using clarifying, restating, and reflecting statements, open-ended questions, and feedback. "Information about all options, including abortion, referral to adoption agencies, or to support services if a woman chooses to keep her baby, should be provided," and "the decision to have an abortion should be the woman's, based on her own values and situation" (pp. 161–163).
Trap: (D) is the version that feels respectful and is not — withholding one option until the patient names it is not neutrality, because a patient cannot ask about what she has not been told exists. (C) makes the same error by deferring the information she needs in order to decide.

Q: A patient asks whether having an abortion will cause lasting psychological harm. Which response reflects the chapter?
A) Psychologic sequelae are rare, with no evidence of a correlation between abortion and mental health issues; most people report relief, though some have temporary distress or mixed emotions
B) Depression following abortion is well established and affects most patients within the first year
C) Emotional responses cannot be predicted, so no information can be offered
D) Any distress after the procedure indicates that the decision was made in error
Answer: A
Rationale: "Psychologic sequelae of induced abortion are rare with no evidence of a correlation between abortion and mental health issues," and afterward "most people report relief, but some have temporary distress or mixed emotions" (pp. 161–163).
Trap: (B) contradicts the source directly, and the fact bank names it as the trap in this section. (D) is the more damaging error clinically: it converts the temporary distress the source explicitly describes as possible into evidence of a wrong decision, which is neither accurate nor supportive.

Topic: abortion procedures by gestational age

Q: A patient at 9 weeks since her last menstrual period is scheduled for a first-trimester aspiration abortion. Which statement about the procedure is accurate?
A) It is the most common first-trimester procedure at 60% of all procedures, is usually done under local anesthesia, and generally requires less than 5 minutes
B) It requires general anesthesia and an overnight admission
C) It is ideally performed after 14 weeks since the last menstrual period
D) It accounts for almost all second-trimester procedures
Answer: A
Rationale: Aspiration, or vacuum or suction curettage, is "the most common procedure in the first trimester, with 60% of all procedures being performed by this method," is "usually performed under local anesthesia in the health care provider's office, the clinic, or the hospital," is ideally done at "less than 14 weeks since the last menstrual period," and "usually requires less than 5 minutes" (pp. 161–162). Misoprostol is given 1 to 3 hours before the start of the procedure.
Trap: (C) inverts the ideal window, turning a ceiling into a floor. (D) borrows dilation and evacuation's defining statistic, which is the swap this section invites because both procedures share the same basic technique.

Q: Immediately after an aspiration procedure, which action does the chapter identify as necessary before the patient is moved to recovery?
A) The aspirated uterine contents must be carefully inspected to ascertain whether all fetal parts and adequate placental tissue have been evacuated
B) A serum pregnancy test must be drawn to confirm termination
C) An ultrasound must be repeated to confirm an empty uterus
D) Oxytocin must be administered routinely to all patients
Answer: A
Rationale: "The aspirated uterine contents must be carefully inspected by the health care provider to ascertain whether all fetal parts and adequate placental tissue have been evacuated" (pp. 161–162). The patient then remains in the recovery area for 1 to 3 hours to detect excessive cramping or bleeding.
Trap: (B) describes something the chapter does place in the pathway, but at the follow-up visit rather than immediately, so the timing is what makes it wrong. The inspection matters right now because retained products of conception are the primary cause of excessive or prolonged bleeding, which is what the recovery period is watching for.

Q: A patient undergoing medical abortion receives a drug that is a prostaglandin analog, acting directly on the cervix to soften and dilate it and on the uterine muscle to stimulate contractions. Which drug is this?
A) Misoprostol
B) Mifepristone
C) Methotrexate
D) Methylergonovine
Answer: A
Rationale: Misoprostol (Cytotec) is "a prostaglandin analog" that acts "directly on the cervix to soften and dilate" and "on the uterine muscle to stimulate contractions" (p. 162).
Trap: (B) is the pick for anyone matching on the first three letters, which is exactly why the pair is confusable. The two drugs are given together and do entirely different things, so the mechanism in the stem is the only reliable discriminator.

Q: The second drug in the same regimen works by binding to progesterone receptors and blocking the action of progesterone. Which drug is this, and how is the regimen sequenced?
A) Mifepristone, formerly known as RU 486, given orally first, followed by buccal or vaginal misoprostol
B) Misoprostol, formerly known as RU 486, given first, followed by oral mifepristone
C) Mifepristone, given after misoprostol has softened the cervix
D) Methotrexate, given as a single agent without a second drug
Answer: A
Rationale: Mifepristone, "formerly known as RU 486, works by binding to progesterone receptors and blocking the action of progesterone, which is necessary for maintaining pregnancy." The FDA-approved regimen is "mifepristone orally, followed by buccal (in the pouch of the cheek) or vaginal misoprostol," and the combination is 95% effective in resulting in a complete abortion over a 24- to 72-hour period (p. 162).
Trap: (C) is the mirror error of the previous item, and it is attractive because softening the cervix first sounds like the logical order. The sequence runs the other way: the progesterone blockade comes first, and the prostaglandin follows. Note also that the side effects of nausea, vomiting, diarrhea, headache, dizziness, fever, and chills are attributed to misoprostol, not to mifepristone.

Q: A patient at 15 weeks of gestation is scheduled for a dilation and evacuation. Which statements about this procedure are accurate?
A) It accounts for almost all second-trimester abortions, requires more cervical dilation because the products of conception are larger, and is preceded by osmotic dilators or misoprostol applied to the cervix
B) It is performed most commonly between 17 and 20 weeks and requires no cervical preparation
C) It carries fewer complications and lower costs than a first-trimester procedure
D) It uses a smaller cannula than aspiration because the cervix is already dilated
Answer: A
Rationale: "D&E accounts for almost all second-trimester abortions performed in the United States," is most commonly performed between 13 and 16 weeks, and "the cervix requires more dilation because the products of conception are larger." Osmotic dilators such as laminaria "are inserted several hours or several days before the procedure, or misoprostol can be applied to the cervix," and a larger cannula is used. An ultrasound examination should be performed before a second-trimester abortion is done (p. 162).
Trap: (C) inverts the risk gradient, and it is the inversion that matters for counseling: second-trimester abortion is associated with more complications and higher costs, which is the reason timing is discussed at all. (D) reverses the cannula size, which follows from the same fact that drives the extra dilation.

Topic: postabortion care and complications

Q: A patient is being discharged after a first-trimester aspiration abortion. Which discharge instruction is accurate?
A) She may shower daily, should avoid douches of any type, and may expect her menstrual period to resume 4 to 6 weeks from the day of the procedure
B) She should expect her menstrual period to resume within 2 weeks, and douching is encouraged to reduce infection risk
C) She should avoid showering until bleeding stops, and menses will resume at the next expected date
D) She should use tampons rather than pads so that blood loss can be measured accurately
Answer: A
Rationale: "The woman may shower daily," and she should "watch for excessive bleeding and other signs of complications and to avoid douches of any type." "The woman may expect her menstrual period to resume 4 to 6 weeks from the day of the procedure" (pp. 161–162). Tampons are restricted for at least 3 days and in some protocols avoided for up to 3 weeks.
Trap: (D) inverts the tampon guidance and dresses it up with a clinical-sounding reason. (B) contains the specific numeric error the fact bank flags — menses return at 4 to 6 weeks, not 2 weeks — which is also why the follow-up visit and its pregnancy test matter.

Q: A patient is given the postabortion warning signs before discharge. Select all the findings for which she should return immediately to the health care facility or emergency department.
A) Fever greater than 38°C (100.4 °F)
B) Bleeding greater than two saturated pads in 2 hours
C) Foul-smelling vaginal discharge
D) Abdominal tenderness when pressure is applied
E) Bleeding about the equivalent of a heavy menstrual period on the first day
Answer: A, B, C, D
Rationale: The Safety Alert directs the woman to "return immediately to the health care facility or emergency department" for fever greater than 38°C (100.4 °F), chills, bleeding greater than two saturated pads in 2 hours or heavy bleeding lasting a few days, foul-smelling vaginal discharge, severe abdominal pain, cramping, or backache, and abdominal tenderness when pressure is applied (p. 162).
Trap: (E) is the single wrong option and it is drawn from the other side of the same boundary: bleeding after the procedure is "normally about the equivalent of a heavy menstrual period," and cramps are "rarely severe." The list is only useful if the normal comparator is held alongside it, which is why the near-miss thresholds — a fever cutoff of 37.5°C, or a bleeding cutoff of one pad an hour — are the usual way to get this wrong.

Q: A patient returns 4 days after an aspiration abortion with prolonged heavy vaginal bleeding. What is the most likely cause, and what management should the nurse anticipate?
A) Retained products of conception, managed with evacuation of the uterus, uterine massage, and administration of oxytocin or methylergonovine or both
B) Normal postprocedure bleeding, managed with reassurance and iron supplementation
C) Endometritis, managed with prophylactic antibiotics alone
D) A coagulation disorder, managed with transfusion and hematology referral
Answer: A
Rationale: "Excessive vaginal bleeding and infection, such as endometritis or salpingitis, are the most common complications of elective abortion. Retained products of conception are the primary cause of excessive or prolonged vaginal bleeding." Management may require "evacuation of the uterus, uterine massage, and administration of oxytocin or methylergonovine (Methergine) or both" (pp. 161–162).
Trap: (C) names the other common complication and attaches it to the wrong presentation — infection is common, but retained tissue is what the source names as the primary cause of this specific finding. The reasoning chain the fact bank draws is heavy bleeding to retained tissue to evacuation, not simply observe and reassure.

Q: A patient who is D negative with negative Coombs test results has just undergone an induced abortion. Which intervention is indicated?
A) Administer Rh D immune globulin within 72 hours after the abortion
B) Administer Rh D immune globulin only if she has had a previous pregnancy
C) No prophylaxis is needed, since isoimmunization occurs only after a term birth
D) Administer Rh D immune globulin within 2 weeks, once Coombs results are repeated
Answer: A
Rationale: "If the woman is Rh negative, she is a candidate for prophylaxis against Rh isoimmunization. She should receive Rh D immune globulin within 72 hours after the abortion if she is D negative and if Coombs test results are negative (if the woman is unsensitized or isoimmunization has not developed)" (pp. 162–163).
Trap: (D) misses the window, which is the error with real consequences — prophylaxis given late does not prevent sensitization. The two conditions are worth holding together: a patient with a positive Coombs result is already sensitized and is not a candidate for prophylaxis at all, so the negative result is part of the indication rather than a formality.

Q: Two patients ask when they can start contraception: one had a surgical abortion today, the other took the first pill of a medical abortion regimen today. Select all the statements that are accurate.
A) After a surgical abortion, all methods of contraception may be started immediately
B) After a medical abortion, oral and other hormonal methods may be started the day of the first pill
C) After a medical abortion, insertion of an intrauterine device may occur once it is known the woman is no longer pregnant
D) After a medical abortion, tubal ligation may occur once it is known the woman is no longer pregnant
E) After a medical abortion, all methods may be started immediately, as with surgical abortion
Answer: A, B, C, D
Rationale: "All methods of contraception may be started immediately after surgical abortion." After medical abortion, oral and other hormonal methods are started "the day of the first pill," while "insertion of an IUD and tubal ligation may occur once it is known the woman is no longer pregnant" (p. 162).
Trap: (E) is the single wrong option and it is the swap the fact bank names — applying the surgical rule to the medical case. The distinction has a mechanism behind it: after a surgical procedure the uterus is known to be empty, and after a medical one that is not yet established, which is why the two methods that involve the uterus or permanence are the two that wait.
