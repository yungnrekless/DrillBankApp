# Chapter 29 drills — Endocrine and Metabolic Disorders

Authored from `sources/Ch29_Fact_Bank.md` (Lowdermilk, Cashion, Alden, Olshansky & Perry, 13th ed., ch. 29, pp. 618–637).

Blueprint weight is **~7 questions of 200 (3.5%), depth `standard`** — topics
*Endocrine Disorders, GDM, hyperemesis, thyroid disorders*. The Week 3 reading
guide assigns pp. 620–637 across three rows, which is eighteen of the chapter's
twenty pages, so the source is broad rather than narrow and the cutting here is
real: **45 drills across 11 topics**, the top of the `standard` band of 30 to 45.

The organizing insight the fact bank names is the spine of this set: **pregnancy
changes insulin requirements on a predictable curve, and almost every diabetes
question in this chapter is asking where on that curve the woman is.** First
trimester the requirement falls; after 14 to 16 weeks it climbs and peaks at 36
weeks; in the first 24 hours postpartum it collapses, because the placenta is
gone. Hyperemesis and thyroid disease sit alongside diabetes for the same reason
— all three are metabolic states pregnancy itself distorts, so the ordinary
reference values do not apply.

Every distinction the chapter states in both directions is drilled from both
ends: hypoglycemia and ketoacidosis, first-trimester and third-trimester insulin
needs, White's class A1 and A2, pregestational anomalies and gestational
macrosomia, the two-step and one-step screening methods, propylthiouracil and
methimazole by trimester, and hyperthyroidism and hypothyroidism.

**What is left out.** The chapter's opening section on the metabolic changes of
normal pregnancy (pp. 618–619), maternal phenylalanine hydroxylase deficiency
(p. 637), and the long procedural passages on insulin self-injection technique,
foot care, and exercise logging, which the fact bank itself compresses. Several
numeric sets are drilled through their rules rather than exhaustively — the full
carbohydrate-counting allocations and the complete Table 29.4 duration column are
in the fact bank for reference.

Topic: the insulin requirement curve in pregnancy
Subtopic: which way the dose moves

Q: A woman with type 1 diabetes at 8 weeks of gestation reports frequent hypoglycemia, especially during sleep. How should this be interpreted?
A) As an error in her technique requiring retraining
B) As the expected first-trimester pattern, in which maternal glucose is normally reduced and the insulin response enhanced, so the dose should be reduced by 10% to 25% at weeks 6 to 10
C) As early diabetic ketoacidosis
D) As a sign that she should stop insulin entirely
Answer: B
Rationale: In the first trimester "maternal blood glucose levels are normally reduced and the insulin response to glucose is enhanced," so "the insulin dose for the woman with well-controlled diabetes may have to be reduced to prevent hypoglycemia." Specifically: "in the first trimester, from weeks 6 to 10 of gestation, the insulin dose should be reduced by 10% to 25% to avoid hypoglycemia." Early hypoglycemia "occurs frequently, often during sleep."
Trap: (A) treats a physiologic shift as a teaching failure, and it is the response that leaves the dose unchanged. Nausea, vomiting, and food cravings add dietary fluctuations on top of the hormonal change.

Q: What happens to insulin requirements after the first trimester, and when do they peak?
A) Insulin resistance begins as early as 14 to 16 weeks and continues to increase, with requirements peaking at 36 weeks and decreasing significantly after that
B) Requirements continue to fall through the third trimester
C) Requirements stay constant after 20 weeks
D) Requirements peak at 24 weeks and then fall steadily
Answer: A
Rationale: "Because insulin requirements steadily increase after the first trimester, the insulin dose must be adjusted accordingly to prevent hyperglycemia. Insulin resistance begins as early as 14 to 16 weeks of gestation and continues to increase until it stabilizes during the last few weeks of pregnancy." And: "insulin requirements normally peak at 36 weeks of gestation and decrease significantly after that."
Trap: (B) carries the first-trimester direction forward, which is the error that produces third-trimester hyperglycemia in a woman whose dose was never raised.

Subtopic: after the birth

Q: A woman with type 1 diabetes gave birth 6 hours ago and is eating a regular diet. What insulin dose should the nurse expect?
A) Her full pregnancy dose, to prevent rebound hyperglycemia
B) One-third to one-half of her most recent pregnancy dose, because the placental hormones that created the insulin resistance are gone
C) Double her prepregnancy dose
D) No insulin for the first week
Answer: B
Rationale: "During the first 24 hours postpartum, insulin requirements decrease substantially because the major sources of insulin resistance, the placental hormones, are greatly reduced," and "women with preexisting diabetes usually require only one-third to one-half of their most recent pregnancy insulin dose on the first postpartum day." A subcutaneous dose should be given at least 1 hour before discontinuing an IV insulin infusion.
Trap: (A) is the clinically dangerous half of this curve, because continuing the pregnancy dose after the placenta is delivered produces severe hypoglycemia.

Q: A breastfeeding woman with type 1 diabetes checks her glucose before a late-night feeding and it is 88 mg/dL. What should she do?
A) Take her usual insulin dose and feed the baby
B) Consume 15 g of carbohydrates without taking insulin, since insulin requirements decrease during lactation and hypoglycemia risk is highest after late-night feedings
C) Skip the feeding and pump instead
D) Wait until the glucose falls below 70 mg/dL before acting
Answer: B
Rationale: "Insulin requirements in breastfeeding women decrease because of the carbohydrate used in human milk production," and they "are at increased risk for hypoglycemia after breastfeeding, particularly after late-night feeding sessions. Women should check their blood glucose level just before breastfeeding. If it is less than 100 mg/dL, they should consume 15 g of carbohydrates ... without taking insulin."
Trap: (D) waits for the treatment threshold when this is a prevention threshold. The chapter also notes that frequent hypoglycemia can reduce milk production, and that the lactation dose must be recalculated at weaning.

Topic: classifying diabetes in pregnancy
Subtopic: type 1, type 2, and gdm

Q: Which statements distinguish type 1 from type 2 diabetes as the chapter defines them? Select all that apply.
A) Type 1 accounts for 5% to 10% of cases and is caused primarily by pancreatic islet beta-cell destruction, with absolute insulin deficiency
B) Type 2 accounts for 90% to 95% of cases and involves insulin resistance with usually relative rather than absolute insulin deficiency
C) Type 1 is prone to ketoacidosis
D) Type 2 often goes undiagnosed for years because hyperglycemia develops gradually
E) Type 2 usually has an abrupt onset at a young age
Answer: A, B, C, D
Rationale: The chapter gives type 1 at 5% to 10% of all diabetes from beta-cell destruction with a tendency to ketoacidosis and an abrupt onset at a young age, and type 2 at 90% to 95% with insulin resistance and relative insulin deficiency, often undiagnosed for years because hyperglycemia develops gradually and is "often not severe enough for the person to recognize the classic signs of polyuria, polydipsia, and polyphagia."
Trap: (E) assigns type 1's onset pattern to type 2, which is what makes a gradually developing case easy to miss.

Q: How does the chapter define gestational diabetes mellitus, and what does the definition exclude?
A) Carbohydrate intolerance that develops during pregnancy, identified during the second or third trimester, excluding women with preexisting type 1 or type 2 diabetes diagnosed during pregnancy
B) Any elevated glucose value at any point in pregnancy
C) Diabetes diagnosed at any time that resolves after birth
D) Insulin resistance present before conception
Answer: A
Rationale: "Gestational diabetes mellitus (GDM) is defined as carbohydrate intolerance that develops during pregnancy," and "it also requires that carbohydrate intolerance be identified during the second or third trimester of pregnancy." The ADA definition "excludes women with preexisting diabetes (type 1 or type 2) that is diagnosed during pregnancy" — GDM is "simply diabetes diagnosed during pregnancy that is clearly not overt (preexisting) diabetes."
Trap: (B) drops the timing clause, and the timing is what separates a woman whose fetus was exposed during organogenesis from one whose fetus was not.

Subtopic: white's classification

Q: A woman with no diabetes before pregnancy has two abnormal values on her oral glucose tolerance test, and her glucose is controlled by diet alone. Which White's class applies, and what would change it?
A) Class A1; requiring insulin or an oral hypoglycemic medication would make her class A2
B) Class A2; adding diet control would make her A1
C) Class B, based on her age at onset
D) Class F, if she develops proteinuria
Answer: A
Rationale: White's class A1 is a woman with "two or more abnormal values on OGTT, but fasting and postprandial glucose values are diet controlled," while class A2 is a woman "not known to have diabetes before pregnancy but requires either insulin or oral hypoglycemic medication for blood glucose control."
Trap: (C) reaches into the pregestational classes. Classes B, C, and D grade by age at onset and duration of illness, while F, R, and T name which organ has failed — nephropathy, retinitis proliferans, and renal transplant. Pregestational women fall into classes B through T.

Topic: hypoglycemia versus ketoacidosis
Subtopic: telling them apart

Q: A pregnant woman with type 1 diabetes is pale, clammy, irritable, and shaky, with shallow respirations and a rapid pulse. Her urine is negative for sugar and acetone. What is occurring?
A) Diabetic ketoacidosis
B) Hypoglycemia, or insulin shock
C) Normal pregnancy fatigue
D) Thyroid storm
Answer: B
Rationale: Table 29.2 gives hypoglycemia a rapid onset with irritability, hunger, sweating, nervousness, weakness, blurred vision, pallor, clammy skin, shallow respirations, and a rapid pulse, with urine negative for sugar and acetone and a blood glucose under 70 mg/dL.
Trap: (A) shares one symptom — headache appears in both columns — but nothing else. The cue that survives a blank moment is cold and clammy versus hot and dry: ketoacidosis brings flushed dry skin, rapid breathing, and a fruity acetone breath odor, with urine positive for sugar and acetone.

Q: A pregnant woman with type 1 diabetes has a blood glucose of 240 mg/dL with nausea, vomiting, abdominal pain, and fruity breath. A colleague says the glucose is not high enough for ketoacidosis. How should the nurse respond?
A) Agree, since DKA requires a glucose above 300 mg/dL
B) Disagree: in pregnancy, DKA may occur with blood glucose levels barely exceeding 200 mg/dL, compared with 300 to 350 mg/dL in the nonpregnant state
C) Agree, but recheck in 2 hours
D) Disagree, because DKA is diagnosed by glucose alone
Answer: B
Rationale: "DKA may occur with blood glucose levels barely exceeding 200 mg/dL compared with 300 to 350 mg/dL in the nonpregnant state." It occurs most often in the second and third trimesters, "when the diabetogenic effect of pregnancy is greatest," and "DKA is a medical emergency ... Ketoacidosis occurring at any time during pregnancy can lead to intrauterine fetal death."
Trap: (A) applies the nonpregnant threshold, which is precisely the delay this fact exists to prevent.

Q: A woman with type 1 diabetes in preterm labor is receiving terbutaline and has been given betamethasone. What complication should the nurse anticipate?
A) Hypoglycemia from increased glucose uptake
B) Diabetic ketoacidosis, because beta-mimetic drugs for tocolysis and corticosteroids for fetal lung maturity can precipitate hyperglycemia and DKA
C) Thyroid storm
D) No metabolic effect from either drug
Answer: B
Rationale: The chapter names among DKA triggers "beta-mimetic drugs such as terbutaline (Brethine) for tocolysis ... or corticosteroids given to enhance fetal lung maturation," alongside illness or infection and poor adherence to treatment.
Trap: (A) reverses the direction of both drugs' metabolic effects. Both are given for good obstetric reasons, which is what makes the interaction worth anticipating rather than avoiding.

Subtopic: treating hypoglycemia

Q: A pregnant woman's blood glucose is 62 mg/dL and she is alert. What does Table 29.2 direct?
A) Consume 2 to 4 glucose tablets or gel, 8 to 16 g of carbohydrate, immediately, and recheck the blood glucose in 15 minutes
B) Give 50% dextrose IV push
C) Administer 1 mg of glucagon intramuscularly
D) Give a full meal and recheck in 1 hour
Answer: A
Rationale: "If blood glucose is <70 mg/dL, consume 2–4 glucose tablets or gel (8–16 g carbohydrate) immediately," then "recheck blood glucose level in 15 min." If still under 70, repeat, recheck again in 15 minutes, and if still low "notify health care provider immediately."
Trap: (B) and (C) are the interventions for an unconscious woman, where oral glucose would be an aspiration risk. The parenteral options are 50% dextrose IV push, 5% to 10% dextrose IV drip, or 1 mg glucagon intramuscularly.

Topic: maternal and fetal risks of diabetes
Subtopic: the macrosomic infant

Q: A woman with pregestational diabetes is carrying a fetus with an estimated weight of 4300 g. Why is the risk of shoulder dystocia greater than for another macrosomic infant of the same weight?
A) Because infants of women with diabetes tend to have a disproportionate increase in shoulder, trunk, and chest size
B) Because the maternal pelvis is smaller in diabetes
C) Because diabetic pregnancies are always postterm
D) There is no difference in risk at the same weight
Answer: A
Rationale: "Infants born to women with diabetes tend to have a disproportionate increase in shoulder, trunk, and chest size. Because of this tendency, the risk for shoulder dystocia is greater in these infants than in other macrosomic infants." Macrosomia occurs in approximately 40% of pregestational diabetic pregnancies and up to 50% of pregnancies complicated by GDM.
Trap: (D) treats weight as the whole story. The asymmetry between head and shoulders, not the number on the scale, is what makes the birth difficult.

Q: What is the mechanism by which maternal hyperglycemia produces a macrosomic fetus?
A) Maternal insulin crosses the placenta and acts as a growth hormone
B) The fetal pancreas begins secreting insulin at 10 to 14 weeks and responds to maternal hyperglycemia with hyperinsulinism; insulin acts as a growth hormone, producing excess glycogen, protein, and adipose tissue
C) Excess maternal glucose is stored directly as fetal fat without fetal insulin
D) Placental hormones directly stimulate fetal growth
Answer: B
Rationale: "The fetal pancreas begins to secrete insulin at 10 to 14 weeks of gestation. The fetus responds to maternal hyperglycemia by secreting large amounts of insulin (hyperinsulinism). Insulin acts as a growth hormone, causing the fetus to produce excess stores of glycogen, protein, and adipose tissue and leading to increased fetal size, or macrosomia."
Trap: (A) has maternal insulin crossing the placenta, which it does not — the fetus makes its own, and that is also why the newborn becomes hypoglycemic when the maternal glucose supply stops at birth.

Subtopic: anomalies, fluid, and infection

Q: A woman diagnosed with gestational diabetes at 26 weeks asks whether her baby is at risk for birth defects. What does the chapter support?
A) That no increase in birth defects has been found among infants of women who develop GDM after the first trimester, because the critical period of organ formation has already passed
B) That cardiac and neural tube defects are equally likely as in pregestational diabetes
C) That the risk is higher than in pregestational diabetes
D) That all diabetic pregnancies carry the same anomaly risk regardless of timing
Answer: A
Rationale: "No increase in the incidence of birth defects has been found among infants of women who develop GDM after the first trimester because the critical period of organ formation has already passed." In pregestational diabetes, by contrast, "hyperglycemia during the first trimester of pregnancy, when organs and organ systems are forming, is the main cause of diabetes-associated birth defects," affecting primarily the cardiovascular system and the CNS.
Trap: (B) applies the pregestational risk to a woman whose hyperglycemia began after organogenesis. Both groups still face macrosomia and neonatal hypoglycemia, which come from later fetal hyperinsulinism.

Q: Why does the chapter treat infection as more than a local problem in a pregnant woman with diabetes?
A) Because infection causes increased insulin resistance and may result in ketoacidosis
B) Because antibiotics are contraindicated in diabetic pregnancy
C) Because infection lowers blood glucose dangerously
D) Because infection only affects the urinary tract in these women
Answer: A
Rationale: "Infections are more common and more serious in pregnant women with diabetes," because carbohydrate metabolism disorders alter resistance to infection — "the inflammatory response, leukocyte function, and vaginal pH are all affected." And: "Infection is serious because it causes increased insulin resistance and may result in ketoacidosis."
Trap: (C) reverses the metabolic direction, which would make a nurse watch for the wrong complication entirely.

Topic: glycemic targets diet and exercise
Subtopic: the numbers

Q: Which target plasma glucose values does Table 29.3 give for pregnancy? Select all that apply.
A) Before breakfast, 60 to less than 95 mg/dL
B) Before lunch or dinner, 60 to 105 mg/dL
C) One hour after a meal, less than 140 mg/dL
D) Two hours after a meal, less than 120 mg/dL
E) Two a.m. to 6 a.m., less than 60 mg/dL
Answer: A, B, C, D
Rationale: Table 29.3 gives before breakfast 60 to under 95, before lunch or dinner 60 to 105, 1-hour postmeal under 140, 2-hour postmeal under 120, and 2 a.m. to 6 a.m. greater than 60.
Trap: (E) inverts the overnight target. The overnight value has a floor rather than a ceiling, because the danger in those hours is hypoglycemia during sleep.

Q: A woman with diabetes asks whether she should test her urine to guide her diet. What does the chapter direct, and what is the hemoglobin A1c caution?
A) Dietary management must be based on blood, not urine, glucose levels; and iron deficiency anemia falsely increases the A1c level
B) Urine glucose testing is the basis of dietary management; A1c is unaffected by anemia
C) Both are equally valid; A1c reflects 3 months of control in pregnancy
D) Neither blood nor urine testing is needed if she follows her diet
Answer: A
Rationale: "Dietary management during diabetic pregnancy must be based on blood (not urine) glucose levels." The Nursing Alert states: "Iron deficiency anemia falsely increases the A1c level." Because red cells turn over more rapidly in pregnancy, the A1c reflects "only the previous 2 to 6 weeks," and levels under 6% to 6.5% in early pregnancy are associated with the lowest rates of adverse fetal outcomes.
Trap: (C) carries the nonpregnant 3-month window into pregnancy, which would make a nurse over-read a single result.

Subtopic: meals and activity

Q: Which dietary instructions match the chapter's teaching for a pregnant woman with diabetes? Select all that apply.
A) Divide daily food intake among three meals and two or three snacks
B) Eat a substantial bedtime snack of at least 25 g of complex carbohydrate with some protein or fat
C) Never go more than 4 hours without food intake
D) Eat consistently each day and never skip meals or snacks
E) Restrict all carbohydrates to avoid postmeal spikes
Answer: A, B, C, D
Rationale: Intake is "best distributed among three meals and three snacks," with a bedtime snack of "at least 25 g of complex carbohydrate with some protein or fat ... to help prevent hypoglycemia and starvation ketosis during the night." "Going more than 4 hours without food intake increases the risk for episodes of hypoglycemia," and the teaching box directs eating consistently and never skipping meals or snacks.
Trap: (E) contradicts the prescribed macronutrient distribution of 33% to 40% carbohydrates, and complex carbohydrates are specifically chosen because "the starch and protein in such foods help to regulate the blood glucose level by producing a more gradual release of glucose."

Q: A woman with diabetes plans to walk after dinner. Her glucose is 210 mg/dL and her urine is positive for ketones. What should the nurse advise?
A) Walk as planned, since exercise will lower the glucose
B) Avoid exercise, because hyperglycemia and ketosis can worsen with physical activity
C) Walk after taking 15 to 30 g of carbohydrates
D) Walk twice as long to clear the ketones
Answer: B
Rationale: "Women should avoid exercise if they have positive urine ketones or a blood glucose greater than 200 mg/dL because hyperglycemia and ketosis can worsen with physical activity." The other numeric rule runs the opposite way: "if the blood glucose is less than 100 mg/dL, women should consume 15 to 30 g of carbohydrates to prevent hypoglycemia."
Trap: (A) applies ordinary diabetes teaching — activity lowers glucose — to the one situation where the chapter forbids it. (C) applies the low-glucose rule to a high-glucose reading.

Topic: insulin therapy
Subtopic: action times

Q: A woman is prescribed lispro insulin. When should it be given, and what is its onset?
A) Immediately before mealtime, with an onset of about 15 minutes
B) Thirty minutes before meals, with an onset of 2 hours
C) At bedtime, with no peak
D) Twice daily regardless of meals, with a peak at 6 to 12 hours
Answer: A
Rationale: Table 29.4 gives lispro and aspart an onset of 15 minutes, and the chapter notes that these rapid-acting analogues "are convenient to use because they are injected immediately before mealtime." Because their effects last only 3 to 5 hours, "most clients require a longer-acting insulin in addition."
Trap: (D) describes intermediate-acting NPH, whose 6- to 12-hour peak is when an unexpected drop occurs. Matching the action time to the meal is what prevents hypoglycemia from a delayed or missed meal.

Q: A woman takes glargine insulin. Which statements are accurate? Select all that apply.
A) It is a long-acting analogue with no pronounced peak and a 24-hour duration
B) It is usually administered at bedtime
C) It cannot be mixed with any other insulin in the same syringe
D) Prepared syringes are stable for 2 weeks in the refrigerator
E) It may be combined with rapid-acting insulin in one syringe to reduce injections
Answer: A, B, C, D
Rationale: Glargine and detemir "are long-acting insulin analogues ... released slowly, with no pronounced peak," both appear safe in pregnancy, and Box 29.1 states that "glargine insulin is usually administered at bedtime. It cannot be mixed with any other insulin in the same syringe. Prepared syringes are stable for 2 weeks in the refrigerator."
Trap: (E) is attractive because glargine and a rapid-acting insulin are often given together — as separate injections. The box's rule is explicit.

Subtopic: preparing and giving the dose

Q: A woman is drawing up NPH and rapid-acting insulin in one syringe. What is the correct sequence?
A) Inject air into the NPH vial, inject air into the rapid-acting vial, withdraw the rapid-acting dose, then withdraw the NPH dose without adding more air to the NPH vial
B) Inject air into the rapid-acting vial first, then withdraw NPH first
C) Withdraw NPH first, then rapid-acting
D) Draw each into a separate syringe, since they cannot be mixed
Answer: A
Rationale: The teaching box gives the order: air into the NPH vial first, then air into the rapid-acting vial, then withdraw the rapid-acting dose, then "without adding more air to the NPH vial, carefully withdraw NPH dose." Box 29.1 confirms that regular insulin can be mixed with NPH in the same syringe, as can lispro.
Trap: (C) is the most common reversal and would contaminate the clear insulin with the cloudy one. If a mixed syringe is stored for later use, it should be rotated 20 times before injection.

Q: Which statements about insulin injection sites and storage match Box 29.1? Select all that apply.
A) The abdomen is the preferred site because insulin is best absorbed there
B) Other sites include the upper outer arm — not the deltoid area — the thighs, and the buttocks
C) Each injection should be given 2 inches from the previous injection in one quadrant before moving to another quadrant
D) Unopened vials are refrigerated until their expiration date and are never frozen, while a vial in use may be kept at room temperature for up to 1 month
E) Premixed insulins are preferred in pregnancy for convenience
Answer: A, B, C, D
Rationale: Box 29.1 gives the abdomen as preferred, the upper outer arm excluding the deltoid, thighs, and buttocks as alternatives, 2 inches between injections within a quadrant, and the storage rules for unopened and in-use vials.
Trap: (E) reverses the chapter's position: premixed insulins "usually are not in the correct percentages to be effective in pregnancy. Therefore they are rarely used."

Topic: gestational diabetes screening and diagnosis
Subtopic: when to screen

Q: A woman with severe obesity, a strong family history of type 2 diabetes, and GDM in a previous pregnancy presents for her first prenatal visit at 10 weeks. When should she be screened?
A) At 24 to 28 weeks, like all other women
B) At the initial prenatal visit, by one of the methods used to diagnose diabetes in the nonpregnant population, and rescreened at 24 to 28 weeks if that result is negative
C) Only if she develops glycosuria
D) At 36 weeks
Answer: B
Rationale: "Although most women are screened for GDM between 24 and 28 weeks of gestation, women with strong risk factors should be screened earlier in pregnancy" — those with "severe obesity, a strong family history of type 2 diabetes, and a history of GDM in a previous pregnancy" are tested at the initial visit using nonpregnant diagnostic methods, and "if negative, they should be rescreened at 24 to 28 weeks of gestation for GDM."
Trap: (C) waits for a sign that arrives late or not at all, and "more than half of all women diagnosed with GDM do not have these risk factors" — which is why screening is universal rather than risk-based.

Q: A woman is given a 50-g oral glucose load without fasting and has a 1-hour plasma glucose of 152 mg/dL. What happens next?
A) She is diagnosed with GDM
B) This is a positive screen in the two-step method, so she returns on another day for a 3-hour 100-g oral glucose tolerance test, and GDM is diagnosed if two or more values are met or exceeded
C) The test is repeated fasting
D) No further testing is needed if she has no risk factors
Answer: B
Rationale: The two-step approach begins with "a 50-g oral glucose load followed by a plasma glucose measurement 1 hour later. The woman need not be fasting. A glucose value of 130 to 140 mg/dL or higher is considered a positive screen," followed by "a 3-hour (100-g) oral glucose tolerance test (OGTT) on another day." GDM is diagnosed "if two or more values are met or exceeded."
Trap: (A) treats a screen as a diagnosis. Approximately 95% of United States obstetricians continue to use the two-step approach.

Q: How does the one-step method differ from the two-step method?
A) It uses a 75-g OGTT between 24 and 28 weeks and diagnoses GDM if only one glucose value is exceeded, significantly increasing the incidence of GDM — likely double that found with the two-step method
B) It uses a 100-g load and requires two abnormal values
C) It is a nonfasting screen only
D) It is performed only in the first trimester
Answer: A
Rationale: The one-step method is "a 75-g OGTT diagnostic test ... between 24 and 28 weeks" with fasting, 1-hour, and 2-hour draws, and "a diagnosis of GDM is made if only one glucose value is exceeded." It "significantly increases the incidence of GDM, likely double that found using the two-step method." ACOG keeps the two-step method because there is no evidence the one-step method improves outcomes while it does increase costs.
Trap: (B) swaps the two methods' glucose loads and diagnostic rules. The cue is to count the values needed: two for the two-step, one for the one-step.

Q: How should a woman prepare for an oral glucose tolerance test?
A) After an overnight fast and at least 3 days of unrestricted diet with at least 150 g of carbohydrates and normal physical activity, avoiding caffeine and abstaining from smoking for 12 hours beforehand
B) After fasting for 24 hours
C) After 3 days of carbohydrate restriction
D) With no special preparation
Answer: A
Rationale: The OGTT is "administered after an overnight fast and at least 3 days of unrestricted diet (at least 150 g of carbohydrates) and physical activity. The woman is instructed to avoid caffeine because it increases glucose levels and to abstain from smoking for 12 hours before the test."
Trap: (C) is the intuitive preparation — cut carbohydrates before a glucose test — and it invalidates the result, because the test measures how she handles a normal intake.

Topic: gestational diabetes management and follow-up
Subtopic: when medication starts

Q: A woman with GDM has fasting glucose values persistently above 95 mg/dL despite diet and exercise. What happens next, and which medication is preferred?
A) Pharmacologic therapy is begun, and insulin is the preferred medication recommended by the ADA
B) Diet therapy is intensified for another month before any medication
C) Glyburide is started first because it is least likely to cross the placenta
D) No treatment is needed unless postmeal values are also elevated
Answer: A
Rationale: "If fasting plasma glucose levels are persistently greater than 95 mg/dL, 1-hour postmeal levels are persistently greater than 140 mg/dL, or 2-hour postmeal levels are persistently greater than 120 mg/dL despite nutritional therapy and exercise, pharmacologic therapy is begun," and "insulin is recommended by the ADA as the preferred medication for women with GDM." About one-third of women with GDM require medication.
Trap: (C) inverts the placental transfer comparison and the recommendation. Metformin "readily crosses the placenta in levels as high as maternal levels," glyburide crosses "in smaller amounts," and yet "current recommendations suggest the use of metformin over glyburide" — while insulin remains preferred because the oral agents cross the placenta and lack long-term neonatal safety data.

Q: A woman with GDM has excellent diet-controlled glucose levels, normal fetal growth, no hypertension, and no prior stillbirth. What antepartum fetal testing does the chapter recommend?
A) Twice-weekly nonstress tests beginning at 32 weeks
B) Routine antepartum fetal testing is not performed, because these women are at low risk for intrauterine fetal death, unless she also has hypertension, a prior stillbirth, or suspected macrosomia
C) Weekly biophysical profiles from 28 weeks
D) Daily kick counts and nothing else, throughout pregnancy
Answer: B
Rationale: "Women with GDM whose blood glucose levels are well controlled by diet are at low risk for IUFD. Therefore antepartum fetal testing is not performed routinely in these women unless they also have hypertension, a history of a prior stillbirth, or suspected macrosomia."
Trap: (A) is the schedule for women with those complications or on medication, and applying it to every GDM patient over-treats a low-risk group.

Q: During labor for a woman with GDM, what glucose range is targeted and why, and how is it usually achieved?
A) 80 to 110 mg/dL, because levels in this range decrease the incidence of neonatal hypoglycemia; excellent control is usually possible by avoiding IV fluids containing dextrose
B) 140 to 180 mg/dL, to provide energy for labor
C) Below 60 mg/dL, to prevent fetal hyperinsulinism
D) Any level, since labor is too short to matter
Answer: A
Rationale: In GDM, "blood glucose levels are monitored every 1 to 2 hours to maintain levels between 80 and 110 mg/dL. Levels within this range decrease the incidence of neonatal hypoglycemia," and "it is usually possible to maintain excellent glucose control in women with GDM during labor by avoiding the use of IV fluids containing dextrose."
Trap: (B) borrows the rationale that applies to pregestational diabetes, where a dextrose-containing solution is piggybacked once active labor begins or glucose falls below 70 — a different plan for a different metabolic starting point.

Q: What postpartum follow-up does the chapter recommend after a pregnancy complicated by GDM?
A) A 75-g 2-hour OGTT or fasting plasma glucose at 6 to 12 weeks postpartum, then lifelong repeat screening at least every 3 years
B) No follow-up, since GDM resolves at birth
C) A single fasting glucose at the 6-week visit and nothing further
D) Annual A1c testing beginning at age 45
Answer: A
Rationale: ACOG recommends "assessing all women who had GDM for carbohydrate intolerance with a 75-g, 2-hour OGTT or a fasting plasma glucose level at 6 to 12 weeks postpartum," then "lifelong repeat screening at least every 3 years." Up to one-third are found to have diabetes or impaired glucose metabolism at that screening, and the chance of developing type 2 diabetes within 10 to 20 years is as high as 70%.
Trap: (B) treats resolution of the pregnancy as resolution of the risk. The chapter's counterweight is that "progression to type 2 diabetes is not inevitable" for women who exercise, become less sedentary, and eat a healthy diet.

Topic: intrapartum and postpartum care in pregestational diabetes
Subtopic: labor and cesarean birth

Q: A woman with pregestational diabetes is in active labor. How often is her glucose checked, what is the target, and why?
A) Hourly, with short-acting insulin titrated to keep the level under 110 mg/dL, because hyperglycemia during labor can cause metabolic problems in the neonate, particularly hypoglycemia
B) Every 4 hours, with a target of 140 mg/dL
C) Once on admission, since labor is brief
D) Hourly, with a target above 140 mg/dL to fuel labor
Answer: A
Rationale: "During active labor, blood glucose levels should be checked hourly," with short-acting insulin titrated to maintain levels "less than 110 mg/dL," because "hyperglycemia during labor can cause metabolic problems in the neonate, particularly hypoglycemia." The infusion is normal saline or lactated Ringer's initially, with 5% dextrose piggybacked once active labor begins or glucose falls below 70 mg/dL.
Trap: (D) confuses the woman's energy needs with her glucose target. The dextrose infusion supplies the calories; the insulin keeps the level down.

Q: A woman with pregestational diabetes has a cesarean birth scheduled. What does the chapter direct about timing and insulin?
A) Schedule it in the early morning to facilitate glycemic control; she takes her full dose of insulin the night before, no morning insulin is given on the day of surgery, and she has nothing by mouth after midnight
B) Schedule it in the afternoon and give her usual morning insulin
C) Hold the evening insulin and give the morning dose
D) Give a double dose the night before to cover the fasting period
Answer: A
Rationale: A scheduled cesarean "should be scheduled in the early morning to facilitate glycemic control. Women should take their full dose of insulin the night before surgery. No morning insulin is given on the day of surgery," and she is given nothing by mouth after midnight. Regional anesthesia "is recommended because hypoglycemia can be detected earlier if the woman is awake."
Trap: (B) gives insulin to a fasting woman, which is the combination that produces intraoperative hypoglycemia.

Subtopic: after the birth

Q: Which postpartum complications are more likely in a woman with pregestational diabetes? Select all that apply.
A) Preeclampsia or eclampsia
B) Hemorrhage, particularly if the uterus was overdistended by hydramnios or a macrosomic fetus, or overstimulated by oxytocin induction
C) Infection such as endometritis
D) Mastitis if she breastfeeds
E) Hypertension caused by the fall in insulin requirements
Answer: A, B, C, D
Rationale: The chapter names preeclampsia or eclampsia, hemorrhage, and infection, with hemorrhage tied to a uterus "overdistended (hydramnios, macrosomic fetus) or overstimulated (oxytocin induction)" and endometritis more likely. Breastfeeding mothers with diabetes are also "at increased risk for mastitis," and poor metabolic control "may delay lactogenesis and contribute to decreased milk production."
Trap: (E) invents a hemodynamic consequence for a metabolic change. The fall in insulin requirement causes hypoglycemia if the dose is not reduced, not hypertension.

Q: Which contraceptive approach does the chapter describe as often recommended for a woman with diabetes, and what is said about the transdermal patch?
A) Long-acting reversible contraception, an IUD or implant, which does not appear to affect glycemic control postpartum; the transdermal patch is contraindicated in obese women, with a higher failure rate above 90 kg
B) Combined oral contraceptives for all women with diabetes; the patch is preferred in obesity
C) Barrier methods only, because hormones are contraindicated
D) Sterilization for every woman with diabetes after one birth
Answer: A
Rationale: LARC is "considered quite effective and is often recommended. These forms of contraception do not appear to affect glycemic control postpartum." The transdermal patch is "contraindicated in obese women," with a higher failure rate above 90 kg and possible increased thromboembolic risk. Combined oral contraceptives are controversial and may be prescribed to nonsmoking women without vascular disease, while progestin-only pills may be used by women with vascular disease.
Trap: (D) generalizes a recommendation the chapter reserves for a woman "who has completed her family, or who has significant vascular disease or other end-organ complications" — and it notes that vasectomy for the partner is safer than surgical sterilization in a woman with diabetes.

Topic: hyperemesis gravidarum
Subtopic: what makes it hyperemesis

Q: Which findings distinguish hyperemesis gravidarum from ordinary nausea and vomiting of pregnancy?
A) Weight loss, electrolyte imbalance, nutritional deficiencies, and ketonuria
B) The number of emesis episodes per day
C) Onset before 10 weeks of gestation
D) Nausea that persists past 12 weeks
Answer: A
Rationale: "When vomiting during pregnancy becomes excessive enough to cause weight loss, electrolyte imbalance, nutritional deficiencies, and ketonuria, the disorder is termed hyperemesis gravidarum." Ordinary nausea and vomiting complicates 50% to 80% of pregnancies, typically begins at 4 to 10 weeks, and is "typically benign, with no significant metabolic alterations or risks to the mother or fetus."
Trap: (B) counts episodes instead of consequences, which is how a woman with frequent but tolerated vomiting gets over-treated and a woman with fewer episodes and ketonuria gets missed.

Q: What does the chapter identify as the most important initial laboratory test in suspected hyperemesis gravidarum, and what other test is often added?
A) A determination of ketonuria; and because of the recognized association between hyperemesis and hyperthyroidism, thyroid levels may also be measured
B) A complete blood count; and a fasting glucose
C) Liver function tests; and a urine culture
D) Serum hCG; and a coagulation panel
Answer: A
Rationale: "The most important initial laboratory test to be obtained is a determination of ketonuria," and "because of the recognized association between hyperemesis gravidarum and hyperthyroidism, thyroid levels may also be measured." Other tests help rule out gastroenteritis, pyelonephritis, pancreatitis, cholecystitis, peptic ulcer, and hepatitis.
Trap: (D) reaches for a hormone that is a proposed cause rather than a diagnostic test, and it misses the thyroid link that can change the treatment entirely.

Subtopic: treatment and teaching

Q: A woman with nausea and vomiting of pregnancy has not responded to dietary measures. What does the chapter give as initial medical management?
A) Pyridoxine, vitamin B6, either alone or in combination with doxylamine
B) Ondansetron, because it is best studied in pregnancy
C) A corticosteroid taper
D) Promethazine as first-line therapy
Answer: A
Rationale: The chapter names "pyridoxine (vitamin B6), either alone or in combination with doxylamine (Unisom) as initial medical management." Diclegis is the single-tablet delayed-release combination, with cost as its disadvantage, and Bonjesta is a second extended-release brand.
Trap: (B) names the drug most familiar from practice, and the chapter says its "use in pregnancy has not been well studied." Dopamine antagonists such as promethazine "have been found to be effective, but their safety in pregnancy has not been proven," and corticosteroids are reserved for nonresponders and "avoided if possible during the first trimester" because of a possible facial clefting risk.

Q: A woman hospitalized with hyperemesis has stopped vomiting. How should feedings resume, and what does the chapter emphasize about her care?
A) Small amounts at frequent intervals — limited oral fluids and bland foods such as crackers, toast, or baked chicken — with intake and output including emesis measured accurately, and calm, compassionate care in a quiet, odor-free environment
B) A full regular diet immediately to restore nutrition
C) Nothing by mouth for another 48 hours
D) High-fat foods to replace lost calories
Answer: A
Rationale: "Once the vomiting has stopped, feedings are started in small amounts at frequent intervals" — "limited amounts of oral fluids and bland foods such as crackers, toast, or baked chicken." Intake and output including the amount of emesis are measured accurately, and the woman "needs calm, compassionate, and sympathetic care," with irritability, tearfulness, and mood changes recognized as consistent with the disorder.
Trap: (D) is the opposite of the dietary teaching, which calls for "dry, bland, low-fat, and high-protein foods," with cold foods often better tolerated than warm.

Q: Which instructions appear in the Teaching for Self-Management box on diet for hyperemesis gravidarum? Select all that apply.
A) Avoid an empty stomach; eat at least every 2 to 3 hours, separating liquids from solids and alternating every 2 to 3 hours
B) Eat a high-protein snack at bedtime
C) In general, eat what sounds good rather than trying to balance meals, following the salty and sweet approach
D) Eat protein after sweets, and try ginger tea or warm ginger ale made with sugar rather than artificial sweetener
E) Maintain a strictly balanced diet at every meal to protect the fetus
Answer: A, B, C, D
Rationale: The box lists avoiding an empty stomach with frequent small intake and separated liquids and solids, a high-protein bedtime snack, eating what sounds good rather than balancing meals, the salty and sweet approach with junk foods permitted, protein after sweets, dairy products, a Popsicle for vomiting on an empty stomach, ginger tea, warm ginger ale with sugar, water with lemon, and drinking from a cup with a lid.
Trap: (E) is the instinct of good prenatal nutrition teaching and is exactly what the box suspends — for this woman, getting anything down is the goal.

Topic: thyroid disorders in pregnancy
Subtopic: recognizing thyroid disease

Q: Which findings help distinguish hyperthyroidism from the normal changes of pregnancy?
A) Weight loss, goiter, and a pulse rate greater than 100 beats/min, with elevated T3 and T4 and greatly suppressed TSH
B) Heat intolerance and fatigue alone
C) Weight gain and cold intolerance
D) Nausea and anxiety alone
Answer: A
Rationale: Hyperthyroidism presents with heat intolerance, diaphoresis, fatigue, anxiety, emotional lability, and tachycardia, but the three findings that separate it from normal pregnancy are "weight loss, goiter, and a pulse rate greater than 100 beats/min," with laboratory findings of "elevated T3 and T4 levels and greatly suppressed TSH levels." Graves disease causes 90% to 95% of cases in pregnancy.
Trap: (C) describes hypothyroidism, whose symptoms are weight gain, lethargy, decreased exercise capacity, and cold intolerance, with elevated TSH with or without low T4.

Subtopic: antithyroid drugs

Q: A woman with Graves disease is 8 weeks pregnant and taking methimazole. What does the chapter direct?
A) Continue methimazole throughout pregnancy
B) Use propylthiouracil in the first trimester only, because methimazole taken during the first trimester can cause choanal or esophageal atresia, facial anomalies, hearing loss, developmental delay, and congenital cardiac malformations, then switch to methimazole after the first trimester
C) Stop all antithyroid medication until after birth
D) Switch to radioactive iodine therapy
Answer: B
Rationale: The chapter directs that "PTU be used only in the first trimester of pregnancy," and "after the first trimester, women requiring drug therapy ... should be switched to MMI for the remainder of pregnancy," because propylthiouracil "can cause hepatic toxicity serious enough to require liver transplantation" while methimazole in the first trimester causes the listed anomalies.
Trap: (D) names the one treatment that is absolutely excluded: "radioactive iodine must not be used in diagnosis or treatment of hyperthyroidism in pregnancy because therapeutic doses given to treat maternal thyroid disease may also destroy the fetal thyroid."

Q: A woman taking an antithyroid medication reports fever and an unexpected sore throat. What should the nurse tell her?
A) These are expected side effects requiring no action
B) These suggest agranulocytosis: she should report them to the health care provider and immediately stop taking the medication
C) She should double the dose to control her symptoms
D) These indicate thyroid storm
Answer: B
Rationale: The most common side effects of antithyroid drugs are pruritus and skin rash; the severe one is "agranulocytosis, which occurs rarely and usually develops only in older women and in those taking high doses. Symptoms ... are fever and unexpected sore throat, which should be reported to the health care provider; in addition, the woman should immediately stop taking the medication."
Trap: (D) is the other febrile emergency in this chapter, and the discriminator is the rest of the picture: thyroid storm brings restlessness, tachycardia, vomiting, or stupor in response to stress such as labor, infection, preeclampsia, or surgery.

Q: A woman with partially treated hyperthyroidism develops fever, restlessness, tachycardia, and vomiting during labor. What is the treatment sequence?
A) IV fluids and oxygen, along with high doses of propylthiouracil, and after the PTU is administered, iodide; other medications include antipyretics, dexamethasone, and beta-blockers
B) Iodide first, followed by propylthiouracil
C) Beta-blockers alone
D) Methimazole and antipyretics only
Answer: A
Rationale: The Nursing Alert on thyroid storm directs: "Intravenous fluids and oxygen are administered, along with high doses of propylthiouracil (PTU). After administration of PTU, iodide is given. Other medications include antipyretics, dexamethasone, and beta-blockers."
Trap: (B) reverses the order the alert states explicitly, which is the detail this emergency turns on.

Subtopic: hypothyroidism

Q: A woman with hypothyroidism takes levothyroxine and a prenatal iron supplement. What teaching applies, and what happens to her dose as pregnancy progresses?
A) Take levothyroxine at a different time of day, at least 4 hours apart from the iron, because ferrous sulfate decreases absorption of T4; and increased doses of thyroid hormone are usually required as pregnancy progresses
B) Take them together to improve adherence; the dose stays constant
C) Stop the iron while taking levothyroxine; the dose decreases through pregnancy
D) Take levothyroxine only in the third trimester
Answer: A
Rationale: The Medication Alert states: "If taking iron supplementation, pregnant women should be told to take levothyroxine at a different time of day, at least 4 h apart, from their iron tablets because ferrous sulfate decreases absorption of T4." The usual starting dose is 0.1 to 0.15 mg/day, adjusted by 25 to 50 mcg every 4 weeks based on maternal TSH, and "as pregnancy progresses, increased doses of thyroid hormone are usually required."
Trap: (C) solves the interaction by stopping a supplement most pregnant women need. Separating the doses in time is the fix, and levothyroxine is considered safe while breastfeeding.
