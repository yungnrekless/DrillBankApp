# Chapter 13 drills — Anatomy and Physiology of Pregnancy

Authored from `sources/Ch13_Fact_Bank.md` (Lowdermilk, Cashion, Alden,
Olshansky & Perry, *Maternity & Women's Health Care*, 13th ed.).

Blueprint weight is **7 questions of 200 (3.5%), depth `standard`** — the top of
that band — topic *"Physical and Physiologic Changes in Pregnancy; Diagnosis of
Pregnancy."* That earns **45 questions across 10 topics**.

**Scope.** Nothing is scoped out. The blueprint topic and the reading guide
agree here and together cover the chapter end to end (*Adaptations to Pregnancy*
pp. 252–262 with Fig. 13.1 and Table 13.4, *Diagnosis of pregnancy*
pp. 262–264), so the fact bank curated the whole chapter and so does this file.

The organizing idea, which the chapter states in its own opening sentence: **almost
every change here is caused by either a hormone — mostly progesterone relaxing
smooth muscle, or estrogen increasing vascularity — or mechanical pressure from
the growing uterus.** When a stem asks "why," it is nearly always one of those
two, and the discipline this chapter rewards is answering with the one the book
names rather than with a general appeal to "the hormones of pregnancy."

Emphasis went where the fact bank put it: the four verified tables, and the
**normal-versus-pathologic boundaries** the chapter keeps drawing — physiologic
anemia against anemia, physiologic dyspnea against cardiac decompensation,
dependent edema against preeclampsia, glucosuria against gestational diabetes,
Braxton Hicks against preterm labor. Those boundaries are where a nursing exam
lives, because each one is a decision between reassuring and escalating.

Topics are one per body system, which is unusually literal for this course, but
it is what the chapter is: a system-by-system survey. The names still say what
to restudy, and a red topic points at one section of one chapter.

**One deliberate exclusion worth naming.** The **presumptive, probable, and
positive** classification of the signs of pregnancy is **not drilled here**, even
though the individual signs are. The fact bank is explicit that the three-category
framework belongs to **Chapter 14** and that it was not reconstructed from memory
into this chapter's bank. Inventing it here would have meant drilling content the
source did not supply. It arrives in Week 2.

**What else the band could not buy.** Cut, in this order: uterine weight and
volume figures and the hen's-egg/orange/grapefruit size landmarks; the breast
lactogenesis hormone sequence beyond the fact that lactation is inhibited until
progesterone falls; the vaginal pH shift and Lactobacillus detail; heart-rate
onset and percentage figures; liver function test specifics beyond alkaline
phosphatase; the sleep-by-trimester pattern, ocular changes, and syncope;
restless leg syndrome and leg cramps; total body water and sodium retention
figures; and most of Table 13.4's hormone-by-hormone effect column. Kept without
exception: all three named early signs, the trimester-specific anemia
thresholds, the respiratory volume changes, the appendix displacement, the
proteinuria threshold, the diuretic Safety Alert, the falls-risk chain, and
every hCG number, because each of those either decides an action or is the
difference between a normal finding and a complication.

Topic: named signs of early pregnancy

Q: At a first prenatal visit at 6 weeks, the examiner notes softening of the cervical tip. Which sign does this describe?
A) Goodell sign
B) Hegar sign
C) Chadwick sign
D) Ballottement
Answer: A
Rationale: "In a normal, unscarred cervix, softening of the cervical tip can be observed about the beginning of the 6th week. This probable sign of pregnancy, the Goodell sign, is due to increased vascularity, slight hypertrophy, and hyperplasia" (p. 253).
Trap: (B) is the reflex pick because both signs are softening findings at about the same gestational age. The discriminator is which structure: Goodell is the cervix, and Hegar is the lower uterine segment. Two of these three signs are about texture and one is about color, which is the fastest way to sort them.

Q: At the same visit, the examiner notes softening and compressibility of the lower uterine segment. Which sign does this describe, and what symptom does the resulting uterine position produce?
A) Hegar sign, which produces exaggerated uterine anteflexion so the fundus presses on the bladder, causing urinary frequency
B) Goodell sign, which produces cervical friability and postcoital spotting
C) Chadwick sign, which produces leukorrhea
D) Hegar sign, which produces painless irregular contractions
Answer: A
Rationale: "At approximately 6 weeks of gestation, softening and compressibility of the lower uterine segment (uterine isthmus) occurs (Hegar sign)," which "results in exaggerated uterine anteflexion during the first 3 months. In this position, the uterine fundus presses on the urinary bladder, causing the woman to experience urinary frequency" (p. 252).
Trap: (B) is the mirror error of the previous item, trading the two softening signs. This item also connects the sign to a symptom the patient reports, which is why first-trimester urinary frequency is a mechanical finding rather than an infection until dysuria appears.

Q: A patient at 7 weeks has a violet-blue discoloration of the vaginal mucosa and cervix. Which sign is this, and what causes it?
A) Chadwick sign, caused by increased vascularity
B) Goodell sign, caused by softening of the cervical tip
C) Hegar sign, caused by compressibility of the isthmus
D) Melasma, caused by increased melanotropin
Answer: A
Rationale: "Increased vascularity results in the violet-blue color of the vaginal mucosa and cervix, known as the Chadwick sign. This is evident at 6 to 8 weeks of pregnancy" (p. 253).
Trap: (B) and (C) are the two texture signs offered against the one color sign, which is how this trio usually appears on an exam. (D) is a real pigmentation change from a later chapter section, but melasma is on the face and appears after week 16, so both site and timing are wrong.

Q: During a bimanual examination at 17 weeks, the examiner taps gently upward on the cervix, and the fetus rises and then sinks back against the examining finger. What is this technique called, and who perceives it?
A) Ballottement, perceived by the examiner
B) Quickening, perceived by the woman
C) Ballottement, perceived by the woman
D) Braxton Hicks contractions, perceived by both
Answer: A
Rationale: Ballottement is the "passive movement of the unengaged fetus," a "technique of palpating a floating structure by bouncing it gently and feeling it rebound," generally possible between 16 and 18 weeks. The examiner "places a finger within the vagina and taps gently upward on the cervix, causing the fetus to rise. The fetus then sinks, and a gentle tap is felt on the finger" (p. 253).
Trap: (C) keeps the right term and gives it to the wrong person, which is the distinction that matters: ballottement is an examiner's finding and quickening is the woman's. Both occur in roughly the same window, so timing alone cannot separate them.

Q: A nulliparous patient at 17 weeks is worried because she has not yet felt fetal movement. Which response is accurate?
A) Quickening occurs at 18 weeks or later in a nulliparous woman, so this is expected
B) Quickening occurs at 14 to 16 weeks regardless of parity, so evaluation is needed
C) Quickening occurs at 14 to 16 weeks in a nulliparous woman and later in a multiparous woman
D) Absence of movement at 17 weeks indicates fetal demise until proven otherwise
Answer: A
Rationale: Quickening, "the first recognition of fetal movements," occurs at "14 to 16 weeks" in the multiparous woman and at "the 18th week or later" in the nulliparous woman. It is "commonly described as a flutter and is difficult to distinguish from peristalsis," and "the week in which quickening occurs provides a tentative clue in dating the duration of gestation" (p. 253).
Trap: (C) inverts the parity relationship, which is the error worth catching because it runs the same direction as lightening later in pregnancy — the experienced woman recognizes the finding earlier, and the first-time mother later. A nurse who has the relationship backwards will reassure the wrong patient and alarm the right one.

Topic: uterine changes and fundal height
Subtopic: growth and position

Q: A patient at 20 weeks asks what is making her uterus grow at this stage. Which explanation is accurate?
A) After the third month, uterine enlargement is primarily the result of mechanical pressure of the growing fetus
B) Uterine enlargement throughout pregnancy is driven entirely by estrogen and progesterone
C) Uterine enlargement after the third month results from continued hyperplasia of new muscle fibers
D) Uterine enlargement is caused by increasing amniotic fluid volume alone
Answer: A
Rationale: "High levels of estrogen and progesterone stimulate significant uterine growth in the first trimester" through increased vascularity, hyperplasia, hypertrophy, and development of the decidua. But "after the third month, uterine enlargement is primarily the result of mechanical pressure of the growing fetus" (p. 252).
Trap: (B) is the reflex pick because hormones explain so much else in this chapter, and it is wrong specifically because the driver changes partway through. The chapter's two explanatory categories, hormones and mechanical pressure, hand off to each other here, which is why the timing clause matters.

Q: A nurse palpating a pregnant abdomen notes that the uterus is rotated to the right. How should this be interpreted?
A) It is expected, probably because of the presence of the rectosigmoid colon on the left side
B) It is abnormal and suggests a uterine anomaly or an adnexal mass
C) It is expected, caused by the greater weight of the right-sided placenta
D) It is abnormal and suggests fetal malposition
Answer: A
Rationale: "As the uterus enlarges and rises in the abdomen, it rotates to the right, probably because of the presence of the rectosigmoid colon on the left side" (p. 252).
Trap: (B) treats a normal asymmetry as pathology. The same rightward displacement explains why the renal pelvis and ureter dilate more on the right, which is why right-sided pyelonephritis is the classic presentation in pregnancy — one anatomic fact with two consequences.

Subtopic: fundal height and lightening

Q: At which gestational age would the nurse expect to palpate the fundus at the level of the umbilicus?
A) 20 to 22 weeks
B) 12 to 14 weeks
C) 28 to 30 weeks
D) 36 to 38 weeks
Answer: A
Rationale: The fundus is palpated above the symphysis pubis between the 12th and 14th weeks and "rises gradually to the level of the umbilicus" at 20 to 22 weeks, nearly reaching the xiphoid process at term (p. 252).
Trap: (B) names the landmark from one row earlier in the same sequence, which is the reliable way this item is missed. The three landmarks are worth learning as a set with their weeks attached, because fundal height is used as a rough dating check at every visit.

Q: A nulliparous patient at 38 weeks reports that she is breathing more easily and that her abdomen looks lower. What does this indicate?
A) Lightening, which in a nullipara occurs approximately 2 weeks before the onset of labor
B) Lightening, which indicates that labor has begun
C) An abnormal decrease in fundal height requiring immediate evaluation
D) Braxton Hicks contractions pulling the fundus downward
Answer: A
Rationale: "Between weeks 38 and 40" the fundal height decreases "as the fetus begins to descend into the pelvis (lightening)." "In general, lightening occurs in the nullipara approximately 2 weeks before the onset of labor and in the multipara at the start of labor" (p. 252).
Trap: (B) applies the multipara's timing to a nullipara, which would send a woman to the hospital two weeks early. This is the same parity-dependent pattern as quickening: in a first pregnancy the landmark arrives with more warning, and in a later one it arrives closer to the event.

Subtopic: contractility and souffles

Q: A patient at 30 weeks reports irregular, painless tightening of her abdomen that stops when she walks around. Which finding would distinguish these from true labor contractions?
A) True labor contractions increase in intensity and duration and cause cervical dilation; Braxton Hicks contractions do not
B) True labor contractions are painless, whereas Braxton Hicks contractions are painful
C) True labor contractions cease with walking, whereas Braxton Hicks contractions intensify with walking
D) Only the presence of bloody show can distinguish them
Answer: A
Rationale: Braxton Hicks contractions are "irregular and painless" and "usually cease with walking or exercise." They "can be mistaken for true labor; however, they do not increase in intensity or duration or cause cervical dilation" (p. 253).
Trap: (C) reverses which contractions stop with activity. The chapter's stated discriminators are progression and cervical change, and cervical change is the one that settles it, because a patient's report of intensity is subjective and the cervix is not.

Q: A patient at 30 weeks reports regular abdominal tightening. She has had no cervical examination. What is the priority nursing action?
A) Evaluate for cervical change, because preterm labor contractions can be mistaken for Braxton Hicks contractions, leading to a delay in seeking treatment
B) Reassure her that these are Braxton Hicks contractions, which are normal after the fourth month
C) Instruct her to walk, since Braxton Hicks contractions cease with exercise
D) Schedule her for evaluation at her next routine prenatal visit
Answer: A
Rationale: The chapter states the danger in both directions: Braxton Hicks contractions can be mistaken for true labor, and "conversely, preterm labor contractions can be mistaken for Braxton Hicks contractions, which can lead to a delay in seeking treatment" (p. 253).
Trap: (B) is the plausible reassurance, and it is exactly the error the source names. At 30 weeks the cost of wrongly reassuring is preterm birth, so the reasoning has to run toward evaluation rather than toward the reassuring explanation, however common that explanation is.

Q: On auscultation of a pregnant abdomen the nurse hears a rushing sound synchronous with the maternal pulse. What is this, and what would a sound synchronous with the fetal heart rate be?
A) The uterine souffle, maternal blood flowing through the uterine arteries; a funic souffle is fetal blood coursing through the umbilical cord
B) The funic souffle, maternal blood in the uterine arteries; a uterine souffle is fetal blood in the cord
C) The uterine souffle, fetal blood in the umbilical cord; a funic souffle is maternal in origin
D) A systolic ejection murmur transmitted from the maternal heart
Answer: A
Rationale: The uterine souffle is "a rushing or blowing sound of maternal blood flowing through uterine arteries to the placenta that is synchronous with the maternal pulse," while the funic souffle "is synchronous with the fetal heart rate and is caused by fetal blood coursing through the umbilical cord" (p. 253).
Trap: (B) swaps the two names, which is the entire content of this item — the sounds are told apart by which pulse they match, and matching the wrong one reverses whose circulation is being heard.

Topic: cardiovascular adaptations and maternal positioning

Q: A patient at 24 weeks has a blood pressure lower than her prepregnancy baseline, although her cardiac output has increased substantially. Which explanation is accurate?
A) Systemic vascular resistance decreases by 30% to 50% because of the vasodilatory effects of progesterone, prostaglandins, and relaxin
B) Cardiac output does not actually rise in pregnancy; the measurement is unreliable
C) Blood pressure falls because blood volume decreases in the second trimester
D) Blood pressure falls because maternal heart rate decreases in pregnancy
Answer: A
Rationale: "Although CO increases significantly during pregnancy, maternal blood pressure remains the same or decreases slightly. This is due to reduced SVR caused primarily by the vasodilatory effects of progesterone, prostaglandins, and relaxin." Systemic vascular resistance "decreases by 30% to 50% and is lowest at 16 to 34 weeks" (p. 256).
Trap: (C) and (D) both invert a real pregnancy change: blood volume rises 40% to 45% and heart rate rises 10 to 20 beats per minute. The apparent paradox resolves only through resistance, and the diastolic nadir at 24 to 32 weeks is why a "normal" second-trimester reading can conceal developing hypertension.

Q: A patient's blood pressure readings have varied across three prenatal visits. Which factor should the nurse consider first?
A) Position, since brachial blood pressure is highest sitting, lowest in the lateral recumbent position, and intermediate supine
B) Time of day only, since circadian rhythm is the dominant influence
C) Cuff size only, since arm circumference changes little in pregnancy
D) Position is irrelevant to brachial readings in pregnancy
Answer: A
Rationale: "Brachial blood pressure is highest when the woman is sitting, lowest when she is lying in the lateral recumbent position, and intermediate when she is supine" (p. 256). Age, activity level, health problems, circadian rhythm, alcohol, smoking, anxiety, and pain also influence readings.
Trap: (D) is the assumption behind most inconsistent prenatal blood pressures. Because the three positions produce a predictable ordering, a reading taken in a different position from the last one can look like a trend when it is only a posture change.

Q: A patient at 30 weeks becomes lightheaded and faint several minutes after lying flat for an ultrasound. Which finding would the nurse expect, and what is the intervention?
A) A reflex bradycardia with cardiac output reduced by half; reposition her to the left lateral position
B) A reflex tachycardia with cardiac output increased; elevate her legs while she remains supine
C) A rise in blood pressure from aortic compression; place her in a sitting position
D) No physiologic change; her symptoms are most likely anxiety
Answer: A
Rationale: "After 4 to 5 minutes, a reflex bradycardia is noted, CO is reduced by half, and the woman feels faint. This condition is called supine hypotensive syndrome or vena cava syndrome." Cardiac output falls "as much as 25% to 30%" when a pregnant woman turns from left lateral recumbent to supine, and some women drop more than 30 mm Hg systolic (p. 256).
Trap: (B) substitutes tachycardia for bradycardia, which is the compensation a reader expects rather than the one the book describes. Getting this backwards matters because a bradycardic, faint pregnant patient who is lying flat needs repositioning, not a workup.

Q: A patient at 34 weeks reports ankle edema, leg varicosities, and hemorrhoids. Which explanation should the nurse give?
A) Compression of the iliac veins and inferior vena cava by the uterus raises venous pressure and reduces blood flow in the legs except in the lateral position
B) These findings indicate preeclampsia and require immediate evaluation
C) These findings result from increased systemic vascular resistance in the third trimester
D) These findings indicate venous thromboembolism until proven otherwise
Answer: A
Rationale: "Compression of the iliac veins and inferior vena cava by the uterus causes increased venous pressure and reduced blood flow in the legs except when the woman is in the lateral position," producing "dependent edema, varicose veins in the legs and vulva, and hemorrhoids" and contributing to "the increased risk for venous thromboembolism" (p. 256).
Trap: (B) escalates a mechanical, positional finding into a hypertensive disorder. Dependent edema in the legs is expected; what would change the picture is hypertension with proteinuria, which this stem does not supply.

Q: On auscultation of a patient at 28 weeks, the nurse hears splitting of the first and second heart sounds, a third heart sound, and a systolic ejection murmur at the left sternal border. How should these be interpreted?
A) All are expected findings in pregnancy and are transient, usually disappearing shortly after birth
B) A systolic ejection murmur is always pathologic and requires echocardiography
C) A third heart sound in pregnancy indicates fluid overload requiring diuresis
D) These findings indicate cardiac decompensation and require immediate referral
Answer: A
Rationale: "By the end of the first trimester, there is an audible splitting" of the first and second heart sounds. "The majority of pregnant women also have a third heart sound after midpregnancy resulting from rapid diastolic filling. Approximately 96% of women develop systolic ejection murmurs that are most audible over the left sternal border." "The auscultatory changes are transient and usually disappear shortly after birth" (p. 256).
Trap: (C) is the reflex from adult cardiac assessment, where a third heart sound suggests failure. In pregnancy it is present in the majority of women from rapid diastolic filling, and treating it as overload could lead to a diuretic — which this chapter separately warns causes severe hypovolemia and reduced placental perfusion.

Topic: hematologic changes and laboratory values

Q: A patient at 26 weeks has a hemoglobin of 10.8 g/dL. How should the nurse interpret this?
A) It is within normal limits for the second trimester, where anemia is defined as hemoglobin less than 10.5 g/dL
B) It indicates anemia, since the threshold is 11 g/dL in every trimester
C) It indicates anemia and requires transfusion
D) It cannot be interpreted without a serum ferritin level
Answer: A
Rationale: A pregnant woman is anemic with "hemoglobin less than 11 g/dL or hematocrit less than 33%" in the first or third trimester, and with "hemoglobin less than 10.5 g/dL or hematocrit less than 32%" in the second trimester (p. 257).
Trap: (B) applies the first- and third-trimester cutoff to a second-trimester patient, which labels a normal value as anemia. The threshold is lower in the second trimester precisely because that is when hemodilution is most pronounced — the physiology and the cutoff move together.

Q: A patient asks why her hemoglobin is lower than before pregnancy if she is being told her blood count is normal. Which explanation is accurate?
A) Plasma volume increases faster than red blood cell production, producing hemodilution called physiologic anemia; red cell mass itself rises 20% to 30%
B) Red blood cell production decreases in pregnancy, which is why the hemoglobin falls
C) The hemoglobin falls because iron is diverted entirely to the fetus
D) The finding is always pathologic and indicates iron deficiency
Answer: A
Rationale: "Because the plasma increase is greater than the increase in RBC production, there is a decrease in the normal hemoglobin and hematocrit values. This state of hemodilution is referred to as physiologic anemia of pregnancy," which is "more noticeable during the second trimester." Red cell mass increases "250 to 450 mL, or approximately 20% to 30% over prepregnancy values," and the percentage of increase "depends on the amount of iron available" (p. 257).
Trap: (B) is the intuitive misreading of a falling hemoglobin — that the marrow must be making fewer cells. Red cell mass genuinely rises; it is simply outpaced by plasma, which is why the finding is a dilution rather than a production problem.

Q: A pregnant patient at 30 weeks has a white blood cell count of 14,000 per cubic millimeter. She is afebrile with no localizing symptoms. How should the nurse interpret this?
A) It is within the expected range for pregnancy, where the white count ranges from 5000 to 15,000
B) It indicates infection and requires cultures and antibiotics
C) It indicates a hematologic malignancy and requires referral
D) It is below the expected pregnancy range and suggests immunosuppression
Answer: A
Rationale: Table 13.2 gives a pregnant white blood cell range of 5000 to 15,000 per cubic millimeter, against a nonpregnant 5000 to 10,000. The count "begins to increase as early as the 2nd month," primarily in granulocytes, nonclassic monocytes, and polymorphonuclear leukocytes, while "the lymphocyte count stays approximately the same" (p. 257).
Trap: (B) applies a nonpregnant reference range, which is the practical error this table exists to prevent. An elevated white count is not by itself evidence of infection in pregnancy, so the febrile and localizing findings, absent here, are what would carry the diagnosis.

Q: A nurse is reviewing expected laboratory changes in pregnancy. Select all the statements that are accurate.
A) Fibrinogen increases from a nonpregnant 200 to 400 mg/dL to a pregnant 400 to 650 mg/dL
B) Platelet count shows no significant change
C) Fasting blood glucose decreases relative to nonpregnant values
D) Hematocrit is expected to be above 33%
E) Platelet count decreases markedly, which is why bleeding risk rises
Answer: A, B, C, D
Rationale: Table 13.2 gives fibrinogen as 400 to 650 mg/dL in pregnancy against a nonpregnant 200 to 400; platelets as "no significant change"; fasting glucose as 60 to 105 mg/dL against a nonpregnant 70 to 110; and hematocrit as above 33% (p. 257).
Trap: (E) is the single wrong option, and it inverts the direction of pregnancy's coagulation change in both halves. Platelets do not fall, and pregnancy is a hypercoagulable rather than a bleeding-prone state, so the option is wrong about the value and about what it would mean.

Q: A patient asks why pregnancy increases her risk of blood clots. Which explanation is accurate?
A) Clotting factors VII, VIII, IX, and X and fibrinogen increase, inhibitors such as protein S decrease, and fibrinolytic activity is depressed, producing a five- to sixfold increased risk
B) Platelet count and function increase markedly, producing a hypercoagulable state
C) Clotting factors II and V increase while all other factors remain unchanged
D) Pregnancy is a hypocoagulable state, and clot risk rises only after birth
Answer: A
Rationale: "Pregnancy is considered a hypercoagulable state in which women are at a five- to sixfold increased risk for thromboembolic problems," through "increases in various clotting factors (i.e., factors VII, VIII, IX, and X and fibrinogen) and decreases in factors that inhibit coagulation (e.g., protein S)," while "fibrinolytic activity ... is depressed." This "provides a protective function to decrease the chance of bleeding but also makes the woman more vulnerable to thrombosis, especially after cesarean birth" (p. 257).
Trap: (C) names the wrong factors, which the fact bank flags as the checkable error in this list. (B) reaches for platelets, which the same table says do not significantly change — the hypercoagulability is a plasma-factor phenomenon, not a platelet one.

Topic: respiratory adaptations and acid-base balance

Q: A nurse is reviewing expected respiratory changes in pregnancy. Select all the statements that are accurate.
A) Respiratory rate is unchanged or only slightly increased
B) Tidal volume increases by approximately 40%
C) Minute ventilation increases by 30% to 50%
D) Expiratory reserve volume decreases by approximately 20%
E) Vital capacity decreases markedly as the diaphragm rises
Answer: A, B, C, D
Rationale: Table 13.3 gives respiratory rate as "unchanged or slightly increased," tidal volume increased 40%, vital capacity "unchanged," inspiratory capacity increased 6%, expiratory reserve volume decreased 20%, total lung capacity "unchanged to slightly decreased," minute ventilation increased 30% to 50%, and oxygen consumption increased 20% to 40% (p. 258).
Trap: (E) is the single wrong option and it is the one that intuition supplies: the diaphragm does rise as much as 4 cm, so vital capacity ought to fall. It does not, because the costal angle increases and the transverse thoracic diameter widens by about 2 cm, compensating — which is why total lung capacity is also nearly unchanged.

Q: A patient at 28 weeks reports feeling short of breath at rest, which began in the second trimester. She has no orthopnea, chest pain, or edema beyond mild ankle swelling. How should the nurse interpret this?
A) This is consistent with physiologic dyspnea of pregnancy, which occurs with mild exertion or at rest and begins in the first or second trimester
B) Dyspnea at rest is never physiologic and always indicates cardiac decompensation
C) Physiologic dyspnea begins only in the third trimester, so this requires evaluation
D) This indicates pulmonary embolism until proven otherwise
Answer: A
Rationale: "Pregnancy-related dyspnea is common, beginning in the first or second trimester. This physiologic dyspnea occurs with mild exertion or at rest." The chapter adds that it is "important to differentiate physiologic from pathologic dyspnea, such as may be caused by cardiac decompensation" (p. 257).
Trap: (B) is the reflex escalation, and it is wrong on the specific point that separates the two patterns — physiologic dyspnea does occur at rest, so "at rest" is not the discriminator. Onset in the first or second trimester without other cardiac findings is the reassuring pattern.

Q: An arterial blood gas on a healthy pregnant patient shows a decreased PaCO2, decreased bicarbonate, and a slightly increased pH. How should the nurse interpret this?
A) A compensated respiratory alkalosis, which is the expected acid-base state of pregnancy
B) A metabolic alkalosis caused by vomiting earlier in pregnancy
C) A respiratory acidosis caused by upward displacement of the diaphragm
D) A laboratory error, since pregnancy does not alter acid-base balance
Answer: A
Rationale: "Pregnancy is a state of chronic mild hyperventilation with reduced arterial carbon dioxide and increased oxygen over nonpregnant levels." "Progesterone may be responsible for increasing the sensitivity of the respiratory center receptors so that PaCO2 decreases, the base excess (bicarbonate) decreases, and pH increases slightly. These alterations in acid-base balance create a state of respiratory alkalosis" (p. 257).
Trap: (C) reasons from the mechanical change to the wrong direction: the diaphragm does rise, but ventilation increases rather than falls, so carbon dioxide is blown off rather than retained. Naming it metabolic, as (B) does, misses that the primary change is in carbon dioxide.

Q: What purpose does the maternal respiratory alkalosis of pregnancy serve for the fetus?
A) It facilitates transport of carbon dioxide from the fetus to the mother and release of oxygen from the mother to the fetus
B) It increases maternal oxygen storage for use during labor
C) It prevents fetal respiratory acidosis by raising fetal carbon dioxide
D) It has no fetal purpose and is an incidental consequence of progesterone
Answer: A
Rationale: The acid-base changes "also facilitate the transport of CO2 from the fetus to the mother and O2 release from the mother to the fetus" (p. 257). Maternal oxygen consumption rises steadily, reaching 40% above nonpregnant levels by term.
Trap: (D) is the pick for anyone who has learned the change without the reason. The gradient the alkalosis creates is what allows a placenta working by diffusion to move gases in both directions efficiently, which connects this chapter to the fetal oxygenation adaptations in Chapter 12.

Topic: gastrointestinal adaptations in pregnancy

Q: A patient in the third trimester reports worsening heartburn. Which mechanism should the nurse describe?
A) Increased progesterone decreases the tone and motility of smooth muscle, causing reflux, slower gastric emptying, and reverse peristalsis
B) Increased estrogen causes hypersecretion of hydrochloric acid
C) The enlarging uterus compresses the esophagus directly
D) Gastric emptying accelerates in pregnancy, forcing acid upward
Answer: A
Rationale: "Increased progesterone causes decreased tone and motility of smooth muscles," resulting in reflux, "slower emptying time of the stomach, and reverse peristalsis." The woman "may experience acid indigestion, or heartburn (pyrosis), beginning as early as the first trimester and intensifying through the third trimester" (p. 258).
Trap: (D) reverses the motility change, and (B) reaches for the wrong hormone — Table 13.4 in fact credits estrogen with *decreasing* secretion of hydrochloric acid and pepsin. The chapter's discipline is to answer "why" with the mechanism it names, and for gastrointestinal smooth muscle that mechanism is progesterone.

Q: A pregnant patient at 32 weeks has suspected appendicitis. Where would the nurse expect her pain to localize?
A) High and to the right, in the right upper quadrant, because the appendix is displaced upward and laterally away from the McBurney point
B) At the McBurney point in the right lower quadrant, as in a nonpregnant patient
C) In the left lower quadrant, because the uterus rotates to the right and displaces the appendix leftward
D) Diffusely across the lower abdomen with no localization
Answer: A
Rationale: The appendix is "displaced upward and laterally, high and to the right, away from the McBurney point," which makes appendicitis "difficult to diagnose in pregnancy" (p. 258).
Trap: (B) is the answer a nonpregnant assessment would give, and it is the reason appendicitis is missed in pregnancy. The displacement is mechanical and progressive, so the later the gestation, the further from the expected location the pain will be.

Q: A patient at 7 weeks reports nausea that is worst in the evening. She asks whether this means something is wrong, since it is called morning sickness. Which response is accurate?
A) Only about half of women have symptoms in the morning; for some it is worse in the evening and for others constant, and it typically peaks at 8 to 12 weeks and subsides by the end of the first trimester
B) Evening nausea is atypical and suggests a gastrointestinal disorder
C) Nausea beginning at 7 weeks is late and should be evaluated
D) Nausea that is not worst in the morning indicates hyperemesis gravidarum
Answer: A
Rationale: Up to 80% of pregnant women experience nausea with or without vomiting. "Although ... commonly referred to as 'morning sickness,' only about half of the women experience the symptoms in the morning. For some, it is worse in the evening, and others experience symptoms constantly." It "generally appears at 4 to 6 weeks of gestation, peaks by 8 to 12 weeks, and usually subsides by the end of the first trimester" (p. 258).
Trap: (D) reaches for the complication on the strength of an atypical timing that the source explicitly calls typical. The escalation criteria are different ones entirely, and confusing "not in the morning" with "severe" would send a normally progressing patient for an unnecessary workup.

Q: Which pattern of nausea and vomiting in pregnancy warrants further evaluation?
A) Vomiting that is severe, persists beyond the first trimester, or is accompanied by fever, pain, or weight loss
B) Any vomiting occurring more than once daily in the first trimester
C) Nausea that is worse in the evening than in the morning
D) Nausea accompanied by an increased appetite in the second trimester
Answer: A
Rationale: "Rarely does NVP have harmful effects on the embryo, fetus, or woman. When the vomiting is severe or persists beyond the first trimester or when it is accompanied by fever, pain, or weight loss, further evaluation is necessary, and medical intervention is often needed. The diagnosis of hyperemesis gravidarum is likely in such cases" (p. 258).
Trap: (D) describes a normal finding — "by the end of the second trimester, the appetite increases in response to increasing metabolic needs" — dressed up as a warning sign. The three escalation criteria are duration, severity, and the accompanying findings, and none of them is frequency alone.

Topic: urinary adaptations and urinalysis findings

Q: A pregnant patient develops pyelonephritis. Why is the right side affected more often, and what predisposes her to infection at all?
A) The renal pelvis and ureter dilate more on the right because the heavy uterus is displaced to the right by the sigmoid colon, and the resulting urinary stasis increases susceptibility
B) The right ureter is naturally shorter, and pregnancy has no effect on urinary stasis
C) The left side dilates more, and the right-sided presentation reflects referred pain
D) Susceptibility increases because glomerular filtration rate falls in pregnancy
Answer: A
Rationale: "In the latter part of pregnancy, the renal pelvis and ureter dilate more on the right side than on the left because the heavy uterus is displaced to the right by the sigmoid colon." The consequence is that "a larger volume of urine is held in the pelves and ureters, and urine flow rate is slowed, causing urinary stasis. ... Because of urinary stasis and potential stagnation, pregnant women are more susceptible to urinary tract infections" (p. 259).
Trap: (D) inverts the renal change — glomerular filtration rate increases by 50% during the first trimester and remains elevated. The susceptibility comes from stasis in dilated collecting structures, not from reduced filtration.

Q: A patient in the first trimester reports urinary frequency, and another in the third trimester reports the same. Neither has dysuria. Which explanation is accurate?
A) Frequency results initially from increased bladder sensitivity and later from compression of the bladder by the uterus
B) Both are caused by compression of the bladder by the enlarging uterus
C) Both indicate urinary tract infection and require culture
D) Frequency in the first trimester is always abnormal
Answer: A
Rationale: "Urinary frequency results initially from increased bladder sensitivity and later from compression of the bladder." Bladder irritability, nocturia, and urgency "(without dysuria)" are reported in early pregnancy and again during the third trimester (p. 259). In early pregnancy the anteflexion produced by the Hegar sign also places the fundus against the bladder.
Trap: (B) applies one mechanism to both windows, which misses that the uterus is not yet large enough to compress anything in the first trimester. The qualifier that carries the clinical weight is "without dysuria" — frequency with dysuria is an infection, and this patient population is already predisposed.

Q: A routine prenatal urinalysis at 28 weeks shows 2+ glucose. The patient is anxious about the visit. How should the nurse interpret this?
A) Glucosuria can occur in normal pregnancy because tubular reabsorption of glucose is impaired, and 2+ levels can be seen with increased anxiety, but gestational diabetes must still be considered
B) Glucosuria at any level is diagnostic of gestational diabetes
C) Glucosuria is never normal in pregnancy and indicates renal tubular disease
D) Glucosuria requires no further consideration, since it is universal in pregnancy
Answer: A
Rationale: "In pregnant women, tubular reabsorption of glucose is impaired, causing glucosuria to occur at varying times and to varying degrees. ... Although glucosuria can be found in normal pregnancies (2+ levels can be seen with increased anxiety states), the possibility of diabetes mellitus and gestational diabetes must be considered" (pp. 259–260).
Trap: (B) and (D) sit on opposite sides of the same error, treating the finding as either diagnostic or meaningless. The source holds both halves at once: it can be normal, and it still has to be considered, which is why glucosuria prompts screening rather than either a diagnosis or a dismissal.

Q: A patient at 34 weeks has swelling of both ankles that improves overnight. Her blood pressure is normal and her urine shows no protein. What should the nurse recommend?
A) Rest in a side-lying position, since dependent edema requires no treatment and lying down triggers the normal diuretic response
B) A mild diuretic to reduce the fluid accumulation
C) Strict sodium restriction to below normal dietary levels
D) Immediate evaluation for preeclampsia
Answer: A
Rationale: "The pooling of fluid in the legs in the latter part of pregnancy decreases renal blood flow and GFR. This pooling is sometimes referred to as physiologic or dependent edema and requires no treatment. The normal diuretic response to the water load is triggered when the woman lies down, preferably on her side" (p. 260). Renal function "is most efficient when the woman lies in the lateral recumbent position and least efficient when the woman assumes a supine position."
Trap: (B) is directly contradicted by the chapter's Safety Alert: the renal system "can be overstressed by excessive dietary sodium intake or restriction or by the use of diuretics. Severe hypovolemia and reduced placental perfusion are two consequences of using diuretics during pregnancy." (C) is the other half of the same alert, since restriction is named alongside excess.

Topic: integumentary and musculoskeletal changes

Q: A patient at 22 weeks has a blotchy brownish discoloration over her cheeks, nose, and forehead. What is this, and what should the nurse teach?
A) Melasma, which begins after the 16th week, is intensified by sun exposure, usually fades after birth, and often recurs with oral contraceptive use
B) Linea nigra, which is a midline change that fades after birth
C) Striae gravidarum, which affect 50% to 80% of women in the second half of pregnancy
D) Palmar erythema, which is related to increased estrogen levels
Answer: A
Rationale: Melasma, also called chloasma or the mask of pregnancy, is "a blotchy, brownish hyperpigmentation of the skin over the cheeks, nose, and forehead," occurring in about 90% of pregnant women "beginning after the 16th week and increasing gradually until term." "The sun intensifies this pigmentation," and it "usually fades after birth but often recurs with oral contraceptive use or subsequent pregnancies" (p. 260).
Trap: (B) and (C) are the two pigment and stretch changes that appear as melasma's usual distractors, and each is distinguished by site: linea nigra is the midline vertical line from symphysis to fundus, and striae are on abdomen, thighs, and breasts. Site alone separates all three.

Q: A patient asks whether the stretch marks appearing on her abdomen can be prevented or removed with a cream. Which response is accurate?
A) No topical therapy has been shown to affect the course of striae; after birth they usually fade but never disappear completely
B) Daily application of vitamin E oil prevents striae in most women
C) Striae resolve completely within 3 months postpartum
D) Striae indicate excessive weight gain and can be prevented by limiting it
Answer: A
Rationale: Striae gravidarum "appear in 50% to 80% of pregnant women during the second half of pregnancy" over the abdomen, thighs, and breasts, and "reflect separation within the underlying connective (collagen) tissue of the skin." "The tendency to develop striae may be familial," "no topical therapy has been shown to affect the course of striae," and after birth they "usually fade, although they never disappear completely" (p. 260).
Trap: (C) borrows the resolution timeline that belongs to vascular spiders, which "usually disappear within the first 3 months postpartum." Striae fade but persist, and promising a patient that they will resolve sets up a disappointment the source explicitly forecloses.

Q: A patient reports significant hair loss 3 months after giving birth. How should the nurse interpret this?
A) It is expected; the rate of scalp hair loss slows during pregnancy, and increased hair loss may be noted in the postpartum period
B) It is abnormal and suggests postpartum thyroiditis
C) It is expected, because hair loss accelerates during pregnancy and continues afterward
D) It indicates iron deficiency and requires supplementation
Answer: A
Rationale: "The rate of scalp hair loss slows during pregnancy; increased hair loss may be noted in the postpartum period" (p. 261).
Trap: (C) keeps the right conclusion and reverses the mechanism, which matters for what the nurse tells the patient. Hair is not falling out faster than normal after birth so much as catching up on shedding that was deferred during pregnancy, and knowing which way it runs is what makes the reassurance credible.

Q: A patient at 32 weeks reports low back pain and a waddling gait, and has nearly fallen twice. Which explanation and priority should the nurse give?
A) The forward shift in her center of gravity, increased lordosis, joint laxity, and widening of the symphysis pubis at 28 to 32 weeks increase her risk for falling, so safety teaching is the priority
B) These findings indicate a neurologic disorder and require imaging
C) She should stop all physical activity until after birth
D) The waddling gait indicates pelvic infection and requires evaluation
Answer: A
Rationale: Abdominal distention tilts the pelvis forward and decreases abdominal muscle tone, so "the woman's center of gravity shifts forward," an "increase in the normal lumbosacral curve (lordosis) develops," and a compensatory cervicodorsal curvature develops to maintain balance. "By 28 to 32 weeks of gestation, there is a marked widening of the symphysis pubis," and "a waddling gait is common." The Safety Alert states that "pregnant women are at increased risk for falling as a result of the shifting center of gravity, impaired balance, and joint laxity" (p. 261).
Trap: (C) is the overcautious response the fact bank warns against — the safety-teaching item is answered from the postural chain, not by restricting activity. Stopping all activity does not correct a center-of-gravity shift and introduces its own harms.

Topic: pregnancy hormones and endocrine adaptations

Q: A nursing student is asked which gland secretes oxytocin and which secretes prolactin. Which response is accurate?
A) Oxytocin comes from the posterior pituitary gland and prolactin from the anterior pituitary gland
B) Both come from the anterior pituitary gland
C) Oxytocin comes from the anterior pituitary and prolactin from the posterior pituitary
D) Both are placental hormones
Answer: A
Rationale: Table 13.4 gives serum prolactin's source as the "anterior pituitary gland," with the effect of preparing the breasts for lactation, and oxytocin's source as the "posterior pituitary gland," which "stimulates uterine contractions; stimulates milk ejection from breasts after birth" (p. 262).
Trap: (C) swaps the two lobes, which is the whole content of this item. The pair is worth holding because both hormones act on the same two organs, the uterus and the breast, so their effects do not distinguish their sources.

Q: A patient asks why her insulin requirements have risen since the first trimester. Which explanation is accurate?
A) The hormones of pregnancy act as insulin antagonists, and both progesterone and estrogen decrease the mother's ability to use insulin, while the pancreas increases insulin production to compensate
B) Insulin itself acts as the antagonist, which is why more is needed
C) Human placental lactogen is the only hormone responsible for insulin resistance
D) Insulin needs decrease after the first trimester as the placenta supplies fetal glucose
Answer: A
Rationale: "After the first trimester, insulin needs increase. The hormones of pregnancy act as insulin antagonists to ensure adequate glucose supply for the fetus," and the beta cells "undergo hypertrophy and hyperplasia to increase insulin production" (p. 263). Table 13.4 lists both progesterone and estrogen as decreasing "mother's ability to use insulin," human chorionic somatomammotropin as decreasing "maternal metabolism of glucose," and cortisol as increasing "peripheral resistance to insulin."
Trap: (B) inverts the table, which lists insulin as the hormone whose production rises to compensate rather than the antagonist. (C) is incomplete rather than false, and incompleteness is the point: gestational diabetes is a multi-hormone phenomenon, so naming one hormone as the sole cause misses how the resistance is built.

Q: A patient with untreated hypothyroidism is 8 weeks pregnant and asks why prompt treatment matters for the baby. Which explanation reflects this chapter?
A) Maternal thyroxine is important for development of the fetal central nervous system, and before 12 weeks the fetus cannot produce it and depends on maternal transfer
B) The fetus produces its own thyroxine from the fourth week, so maternal levels are unimportant
C) Maternal thyroid hormone has no role in fetal development at any stage
D) Fetal thyroxine production begins at 20 weeks, so the dependent window extends to midpregnancy
Answer: A
Rationale: "Maternal thyroxine is important for the development of the fetal central nervous system; before 12 weeks of gestation the fetus is unable to produce it and is dependent on maternal transfer" (pp. 262–263). Maternal thyroid hormones rise and basal metabolic rate increases up to 25% over the course of pregnancy.
Trap: (D) extends a real window to a wrong endpoint, which changes the urgency of treatment. Note also that Chapter 12 states maternal thyroxine "does not readily cross the placenta"; the two statements concern different things — the efficiency of transfer versus the fetus's own capacity to produce — so cite whichever chapter a stem is drawn from rather than treating them as a contradiction.

Topic: pregnancy testing and hcg interpretation

Q: A patient asks how early a pregnancy test can detect pregnancy. Which response is accurate?
A) Human chorionic gonadotropin can be detected in maternal serum or urine as soon as 7 to 8 days before the expected menses, and levels above 25 international units per liter are diagnostic
B) It cannot be detected until 2 weeks after the missed period
C) It can be detected at the moment of fertilization, before implantation
D) Detection requires a serum sample; urine tests cannot provide accurate results
Answer: A
Rationale: Human chorionic gonadotropin "is the earliest biological marker for pregnancy," with beta-hCG production beginning "as early as the day of implantation" and detection possible "in maternal serum or urine as soon as 7 to 8 days before the expected menses." Levels "greater than 25 IU/L are diagnostic for pregnancy," and "both serum and urine tests can provide accurate results" (pp. 263–264).
Trap: (C) moves the start of production one step too early. Production begins at implantation, not at fertilization, which is why there is a window after conception during which no test can be positive regardless of sensitivity.

Q: A patient at 6 weeks has serial hCG levels that are rising more slowly than expected. What should the nurse anticipate as possible explanations?
A) Impending miscarriage or ectopic pregnancy, since levels normally double approximately every 2 days for the first 4 weeks
B) Multiple gestation, since more than one fetus dilutes the rate of rise
C) Gestational trophoblastic disease, which characteristically produces low levels
D) A normal variant requiring no further evaluation
Answer: A
Rationale: Human chorionic gonadotropin levels "usually double approximately every 2 days for the first 4 weeks," peaking 60 to 70 days after implantation. "Abnormally slow increase or lower levels" suggest "impending miscarriage or ectopic pregnancy," while higher than normal levels suggest "abnormal gestation (e.g., fetus with Down syndrome, gestational trophoblastic disease) or multiple gestations" (p. 263).
Trap: (B) and (C) both name conditions from the high-hCG list and attach them to a low result. The two lists run in opposite directions, and a slow rise is the one pattern that carries an immediate safety implication, because ectopic pregnancy is on it.

Q: A patient reports that her home pregnancy test was negative but she still has not had a period. Which teaching is most appropriate?
A) Repeat the test in 1 week, since testing too early before a significant rise in hCG is a common error producing a false negative, and use a first-voided morning specimen
B) Accept the negative result as definitive and wait for her period
C) Repeat the test immediately using an afternoon specimen
D) The negative result indicates a hormone-producing tumor and requires immediate evaluation
Answer: A
Rationale: The teaching guidance directs the patient to "use a first-voided morning urine specimen" and, "if the test done at the time of your missed period is negative, repeat the test in 1 week if you still have not had a period," and to contact the provider "if the test result is positive or if the test result is negative and you still have not had a period." Testing "too early in pregnancy before a significant rise in hCG level" is named as "a common error in performing home pregnancy tests" (pp. 263–264).
Trap: (D) borrows an error source from the wrong column — hormone-producing tumors cause false positives, not false negatives. (B) treats a single negative as conclusive, which the teaching sheet specifically instructs against when the period has still not arrived.

Q: A patient taking an anticonvulsant has a positive urine pregnancy test, and a different patient taking promethazine has a negative one. Which error should the nurse consider in each case?
A) Anticonvulsants and tranquilizers can cause a false positive; diuretics and promethazine can cause a false negative
B) Anticonvulsants cause a false negative; promethazine causes a false positive
C) Neither medication class affects pregnancy test results
D) Both medication classes cause false positives
Answer: A
Rationale: False positive results can be caused by "medications such as anticonvulsants and tranquilizers" and by "hormone-producing tumors." False negative results can be caused by "diuretics and promethazine" and by "doing the test too early in pregnancy before a significant rise in hCG level." Improper specimen collection and laboratory errors can cause either (pp. 263–264).
Trap: (B) swaps the two drug lists, which is the only thing this item tests and is easy to do because both are short lists of unrelated drugs. The practical consequence differs by direction: a false negative delays prenatal care, while a false positive is usually resolved at the confirmatory visit.
