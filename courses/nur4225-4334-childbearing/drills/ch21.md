# Chapter 21 drills — Nursing Care of the Family During the Postpartum Period

Authored from `sources/Ch21_Fact_Bank.md` (Lowdermilk, Cashion, Alden, Olshansky & Perry, 13th ed., ch. 21, pp. 425–440).

Blueprint weight is **~7 questions of 200 (3.5%), depth `standard`** — topics
*Postpartum Nursing Care, Rhogam, fundus, bladder, risk factors, psychosocial
concerns*. The Week 1–4 midterm reading guide does not name this chapter, so the
midterm does not reach it; the blueprint weights it at seven, which is more than
several assigned chapters carry, so the final almost certainly does. Scope comes
from the blueprint alone, and it earns **45 drills across 10 topics**, the top of
the `standard` band.

The organizing insight the fact bank names is the spine of this set: **the
postpartum assessment is a bleeding assessment wearing other clothes.** Fundus,
bladder, and lochia are three readings of one question — is the uterus
contracted? A boggy or displaced fundus, a distended bladder, and heavy lochia
are the same problem at three points in a causal chain, which is why the book
names maintaining uterine tone and preventing bladder distention as "the two most
important interventions for preventing excessive bleeding."

Every distinction the chapter states in both directions is drilled from both
ends: a boggy fundus and a firm one that is still bleeding, a fundus that is high
and one that is deviated, bleeding as a volume and bleeding as a rate, perineal
pain on day 1 and on day 4, rubella and Tdap, and an Rh-negative mother with an
Rh-positive baby and one without.

Topic names are kept distinct from Chapter 20's, which covers the physiology
behind these same findings. Where the two chapters overlap — lochia stages, the
fundal timeline, the vital-sign ranges — this file drills the **assessment and
the action**, and Chapter 20 drills the **physiology**.

**What is left out.** Comfort measures in procedural detail, the rest and
ambulation material, bowel function beyond the Table 21.1 boundary, breastfeeding
and lactation suppression (Chapter 25 carries those), and the whole of **discharge
teaching and follow-up care (pp. 436–440)** — self-management education, sexual
activity and contraception after birth, routine checkups, home visits, telephone
follow-up, and community referral. That last block is the largest omission and is
where the exam would go if it reached past the blueprint's six topics.

Topic: postpartum assessment findings
Subtopic: reading the fundus

Q: On postpartum day 1 the nurse palpates a fundus that is boggy and higher than expected. What are the two possible causes?
A) Uterine atony or a distended bladder
B) Infection or dehydration
C) Retained placenta only
D) Normal involution
Answer: A
Rationale: Table 21.1 gives the normal finding as a fundus that is "firm, midline; first 24 h at level of umbilicus; involutes ≈1 cm (1 fingerbreadth)/day," and the potential complication as "soft, boggy, higher than expected level: uterine atony or distended bladder."
Trap: (C) names a cause of atony rather than the finding's differential. The value of holding both possibilities is that they call for different first actions — massage for one, voiding for the other.

Q: The fundus is firm but deviated to the right of midline. What does this indicate, and what should the nurse do first?
A) Uterine atony; massage the fundus
B) A distended bladder; assist the woman to void
C) Uterine rupture; notify the provider
D) Normal variation; document and continue
Answer: B
Rationale: Table 21.1 gives "lateral deviation: distended bladder." The chapter explains the mechanism: "a full bladder causes the uterus to be displaced above the umbilicus and well to one side of midline in the abdomen. It also prevents the uterus from contracting normally."
Trap: (A) treats every fundal abnormality as atony. A firm, deviated fundus is a bladder problem, and massaging it without emptying the bladder leaves the cause in place.

Subtopic: the rest of the assessment

Q: Which findings in Table 21.1 fall within normal limits for the postpartum woman? Select all that apply.
A) A pulse of 58 beats/min
B) A temperature of 37.8°C (100°F) at 8 hours after birth
C) Deep tendon reflexes of 2+
D) Lochia rubra, scant to moderate, with a few clots and a fleshy odor
E) Deep tendon reflexes of 3+
Answer: A, B, C, D
Rationale: Table 21.1 gives a normal pulse of 50 to 90 beats/min, a temperature range of 36.2°C to 38°C, deep tendon reflexes of 1+ to 2+, and lochia that is scant to moderate with few clots and a fleshy odor.
Trap: (E) is the potential complication line: "DTRs ≥3+: preeclampsia." Postpartum preeclampsia is caught twice in this table — through reflexes and through blood pressure.

Q: A woman reports excessive perineal discomfort. How does the timing change what the nurse suspects?
A) Excessive discomfort in the first 1 to 2 days suggests a hematoma; after day 3 it suggests infection
B) Discomfort at any time suggests infection
C) Discomfort after day 3 suggests a hematoma
D) Perineal discomfort is always expected and needs no differentiation
Answer: A
Rationale: Table 21.1 states it in one line: "excessive discomfort first 1–2 days: hematoma; after day 3: infection." Other perineal complications listed are pronounced edema and bruising, and redness, warmth, or drainage.
Trap: (C) reverses the timeline, and the two causes call for very different responses — one is a bleeding problem and one is an infection.

Q: Why does the chapter say Homan sign is no longer part of the routine postpartum assessment?
A) Because of concern about its limited sensitivity and specificity in diagnosing venous thromboembolism and the potential risk for dislodging a clot when the test is performed
B) Because venous thromboembolism does not occur postpartum
C) Because it has been replaced by routine ultrasound
D) Because it is painful
Answer: A
Rationale: The table's footnote states that Homan sign "was traditionally included in routine postpartum assessments; however, it is no longer common practice because of concern about its limited sensitivity and specificity in diagnosing venous thromboembolism and the potential risk for dislodging a clot when the test is performed."
Trap: (B) would remove the assessment for redness, tenderness, and pain that Table 21.1 still lists under legs — the concern is real; the maneuver is what was abandoned.

Topic: preventing excessive bleeding
Subtopic: who is at risk and why

Q: Which women are at risk for postpartum hemorrhage, and what is the most frequent cause of excessive bleeding after birth?
A) Only women with a history of hemorrhage; the most frequent cause is laceration
B) All women who have given birth; the most frequent cause is uterine atony
C) Only women who had a cesarean birth; the most frequent cause is surgical bleeding
D) Only multiparous women; the most frequent cause is retained placenta
Answer: B
Rationale: "All women who have given birth are at risk for excessive bleeding that can progress to postpartum hemorrhage," and "the most frequent cause of excessive bleeding after birth is uterine atony."
Trap: (A) narrows a universal risk to a subgroup, which is the reasoning that skips assessments on a woman with no risk factors.

Q: What does the chapter name as the two most important interventions for preventing excessive bleeding?
A) Maintaining good uterine tone and preventing bladder distention
B) Administering intravenous fluids and oxytocic medications
C) Bed rest and ice packs
D) Frequent vital signs and blood typing
Answer: A
Rationale: "The two most important interventions for preventing excessive bleeding are maintaining good uterine tone and preventing bladder distention." If atony occurs, "the relaxed uterus distends with blood and clots, blood vessels in the placental site are not clamped off, and excessive bleeding results."
Trap: (B) names the treatments used after atony has occurred — "additional interventions likely to be used are administration of intravenous fluids and oxytocic medications" — rather than the measures that prevent it.

Q: A woman has heavy bright red vaginal bleeding, and her fundus is firmly contracted at the umbilicus in the midline. What should the nurse suspect?
A) Uterine atony requiring more vigorous massage
B) Vaginal or vulvar hematoma or an unrepaired laceration of the vagina or cervix
C) Normal lochia rubra
D) A full bladder
Answer: B
Rationale: "Excessive blood loss after birth can also be caused by vaginal or vulvar hematomas or unrepaired lacerations of the vagina or cervix. These potential sources might be suspected if excessive vaginal bleeding occurs in the presence of a firmly contracted uterine fundus."
Trap: (A) applies the massage answer to a uterus that is already doing its job, and it is the reflex response because massage is the right answer so much of the time.

Subtopic: recognizing excessive loss

Q: Which findings does the Nursing Alert identify as indicating excessive blood loss requiring immediate assessment, intervention, and provider notification? Select all that apply.
A) A perineal pad saturated in 15 minutes or less
B) Pooling of blood under the buttocks
C) A pad with a 10-cm stain after 4 hours
D) A gush of dark blood when the woman first stands
E) One pad saturated over 8 hours
Answer: A, B
Rationale: The Nursing Alert names exactly two findings: "a perineal pad saturated in 15 minutes or less and pooling of blood under the buttocks are indications of excessive blood loss, requiring immediate assessment, intervention, and notification of the obstetric health care provider."
Trap: (D) is pooled lochia leaving the relaxed vagina, and (E) is a slow rate that the chapter explicitly contrasts with heavy bleeding. Rate, not stain size, is what makes bleeding excessive.

Q: A woman's perineal pad shows only a small amount of blood, but her pulse is rising. What does the Safety Alert direct the nurse to do?
A) Document the small amount and reassess in an hour
B) Always check for blood under the mother's buttocks as well as on the perineal pad, because blood can flow between the buttocks onto the linens and excessive bleeding can go undetected
C) Increase oral fluids
D) Recheck the pulse in the other arm
Answer: B
Rationale: The Safety Alert states: "The nurse always checks for blood under the mother's buttocks as well as on the perineal pad. Although the amount on the perineal pad can appear to be small, blood can flow between the buttocks onto the linens under the mother. When this happens, excessive bleeding can go undetected."
Trap: (A) trusts the pad, which is exactly the assumption the alert corrects — and a rising pulse is one of the sensitive early indicators of hypovolemia.

Topic: estimating and quantifying blood loss
Subtopic: how loss is measured

Q: What does the chapter recommend as the most accurate way to determine postpartum blood loss?
A) Visual estimation using the scant, light, moderate, and heavy scale
B) Quantification by weighing clots and items saturated with blood, where 1 mL equals 1 g
C) Counting the number of pads used
D) Serial hematocrit values
Answer: B
Rationale: "Quantification of blood loss by weighing clots and items saturated with blood (1 mL = 1 g) is recommended as the most accurate way to objectively determine blood loss."
Trap: (A) is the fallback method, and the chapter notes two sources of error in it: "different brands of perineal pads vary in their saturation volume and soaking appearance," and "a perineal pad with an enclosed cold pack lacks absorbency."

Q: Two women each have one saturated perineal pad. What additional information does the nurse need, and why?
A) How long it has been since the pad was changed, because any estimation of lochial flow is inaccurate and incomplete without considering the time factor
B) The brand of the pad only
C) Their hemoglobin values
D) Nothing further; one saturated pad is one saturated pad
Answer: A
Rationale: "Any estimation of lochial flow is inaccurate and incomplete without considering the time factor. A woman who saturates a perineal pad in 1 hour or less is bleeding much more heavily than a woman who saturates one perineal pad in 8 hours." So the nurse "asks the woman how long it has been since her perineal pad was changed."
Trap: (D) treats blood loss as a volume when the chapter treats it as a rate, which is the same reasoning behind the 15-minute alert threshold.

Q: Fig. 21.1 gives measurements for the perineal pad saturation scale. Which pairing is correct?
A) Scant is less than 2.5 cm; light is less than 10 cm; moderate is more than 10 cm; heavy is one pad saturated within 2 hours
B) Scant is less than 10 cm; heavy is one pad saturated within 8 hours
C) Moderate is less than 2.5 cm; heavy is more than 10 cm
D) The scale has no measurements attached
Answer: A
Rationale: Fig. 21.1's caption gives the measurements: "scant (<2.5 cm), light (<10 cm), moderate (>10 cm), or heavy (one pad saturated within 2 hours)."
Trap: (B) stretches the heavy threshold to 8 hours, which the chapter uses as its example of light bleeding.

Subtopic: what monitors hemorrhage

Q: Why is blood pressure not a reliable indicator of impending shock from early postpartum hemorrhage, and which indicators are better?
A) Because compensatory mechanisms prevent a significant drop until 30% to 40% of blood volume is lost; respirations, pulse, skin condition, urinary output, and level of consciousness are more sensitive
B) Because postpartum blood pressure equipment is inaccurate
C) Because blood pressure always rises in hemorrhage
D) Because blood pressure is affected by breastfeeding
Answer: A
Rationale: "Blood pressure is not a reliable indicator of impending shock from early postpartum hemorrhage because compensatory mechanisms prevent a significant drop in blood pressure until the woman has lost 30% to 40% of her blood volume. Respirations, pulse, skin condition, urinary output, and level of consciousness are more sensitive indicators of hypovolemic shock."
Trap: (C) inverts the physiology. Table 21.1 does list hypotension as a sign of hemorrhage — it is simply a late one, which is why the other five indicators are what the nurse watches.

Topic: fundal massage
Subtopic: performing it

Q: How is fundal massage performed, according to the chapter and Fig. 21.2?
A) One hand presses deeply into the fundus while the other is placed on the woman's back
B) The upper hand is cupped over the fundus while the lower hand dips in above the symphysis pubis and supports the uterus while it is massaged gently
C) Both hands press downward on the abdomen simultaneously
D) The fundus is massaged vigorously with a closed fist
Answer: B
Rationale: Fig. 21.2's caption describes the technique: "the upper hand is cupped over the fundus; the lower hand dips in above the symphysis pubis and supports the uterus while it is massaged gently." The intervention itself is "stimulation by gently massaging the fundus until firm."
Trap: (D) adds force the chapter does not call for, and the procedure is already described as "a very uncomfortable procedure" when done correctly.

Q: During fundal massage, vaginal bleeding increases and several clots are expelled. How should the nurse interpret this?
A) As hemorrhage; stop the massage immediately
B) As expected, since fundal massage can cause a temporary increase in bleeding as pooled blood leaves the uterus, and clots can also be expelled
C) As a laceration
D) As a sign that the massage technique is wrong
Answer: B
Rationale: "Fundal massage can cause a temporary increase in the amount of vaginal bleeding seen as pooled blood leaves the uterus. Clots can also be expelled. The uterus can remain boggy even after massage and clot expulsion."
Trap: (A) stops the intervention because it appears to be causing the problem it is treating, which is the trap this sentence exists to prevent.

Subtopic: making it tolerable and knowing what comes next

Q: What does the chapter recommend to make fundal massage more tolerable and to give the woman some control?
A) Performing it without explanation to avoid anxiety
B) Explaining its purpose and the danger of uterine atony, assisting with breathing and relaxation, and teaching the woman to massage her own fundus, which enables her to maintain some control and decreases her anxiety
C) Premedicating with an opioid before every assessment
D) Delegating the massage to a family member
Answer: B
Rationale: "Fundal massage can be a very uncomfortable procedure," and explaining the purpose and the danger of atony while assisting with breathing and relaxation makes the woman more cooperative. "Teaching the woman to massage her own fundus enables her to maintain some control and decreases her anxiety."
Trap: (A) withholds the explanation that makes cooperation possible, and the discomfort is what makes the explanation necessary rather than optional.

Q: The uterus remains boggy after massage and clot expulsion. What interventions are likely to be used next?
A) Administration of intravenous fluids and oxytocic medications
B) Ice packs to the fundus
C) Repositioning to Trendelenburg only
D) Immediate hysterectomy
Answer: A
Rationale: When massage alone is insufficient, "additional interventions likely to be used are administration of intravenous fluids and oxytocic medications" — drugs that stimulate contraction of the uterine smooth muscle.
Trap: (B) treats the uterus like an inflamed joint. Cold is used on the perineum for edema, not on the fundus, where contraction is what is needed.

Topic: preventing bladder distention
Subtopic: why it matters and who is at risk

Q: How does a distended bladder cause postpartum bleeding?
A) It increases blood pressure
B) It displaces the uterus above the umbilicus and well to one side of midline and prevents the uterus from contracting normally
C) It compresses the vena cava
D) It causes coagulopathy
Answer: B
Rationale: "Uterine atony and excessive bleeding after birth can be the result of bladder distention. A full bladder causes the uterus to be displaced above the umbilicus and well to one side of midline in the abdomen. It also prevents the uterus from contracting normally."
Trap: (C) borrows a mechanism from pregnancy positioning, and it would point the nurse toward repositioning rather than toward emptying the bladder.

Q: Which intrapartum factors put a woman at risk for postpartum urinary retention? Select all that apply.
A) Epidural anesthesia
B) Episiotomy or extensive vaginal or perineal lacerations
C) Vacuum- or forceps-assisted birth
D) Prolonged labor, or an indwelling catheter such as with cesarean birth
E) Breastfeeding
Answer: A, B, C, D
Rationale: The risk factors named are "epidural anesthesia, episiotomy, extensive vaginal or perineal lacerations, vacuum- or forceps-assisted birth, or prolonged labor," and women who had indwelling catheters "can experience some difficulty as they initially attempt to void after the catheter is removed."
Trap: (E) increases uterine tone rather than impairing voiding — breastfeeding releases oxytocin, which contracts the uterus.

Subtopic: helping her void

Q: A woman has not voided 4 hours after birth and her fundus is deviated. What is the first priority?
A) Insert a sterile catheter
B) Assist the woman to the bathroom, or onto a bedpan if she is unable to ambulate
C) Administer an analgesic
D) Increase her intravenous fluids
Answer: B
Rationale: "The first priority is to assist the woman to the bathroom or onto a bedpan if she is unable to ambulate."
Trap: (A) is the last step in the ladder, used "if these measures are unsuccessful." Catheterization also raises the risk of urinary tract infection in a woman already at increased risk.

Q: Which measures does the chapter describe for stimulating voiding before catheterization is considered? Select all that apply.
A) Having the woman listen to running water
B) Placing her hands in warm water
C) Pouring water from a squeeze bottle over her perineum
D) Assisting her into the shower or sitz bath and encouraging her to void, with relaxation techniques
E) Applying firm suprapubic pressure until she voids
Answer: A, B, C, D
Rationale: The chapter lists listening to running water, placing the hands in warm water, pouring water over the perineum, the shower or sitz bath with relaxation techniques, and administering ordered analgesics, "as some women fear voiding because of anticipated pain."
Trap: (E) invents a maneuver the chapter does not describe, and pressing on a distended bladder in a woman with a lax pelvic floor is not a benign substitute for helping her void.

Q: What voiding pattern should the nurse expect in the first days after birth?
A) Diuresis begins about 12 hours after birth, and the woman can void 3000 mL per day
B) Urine output is decreased for the first 48 hours
C) Output above 1500 mL per day indicates a problem
D) Voiding should occur only once per shift
Answer: A
Rationale: Table 21.1 gives the normal finding: "diuresis begins ≈12 h after birth; can void 3000 mL/day," with the potential complications being an overdistended bladder "possibly causing uterine atony, excessive lochia," and dysuria, frequency, urgency, or burning indicating infection.
Trap: (C) treats a normal and expected volume as pathologic, which would lead to fluid restriction in a woman whose body is unloading the fluid of pregnancy.

Topic: perineal care and infection prevention
Subtopic: teaching the woman

Q: Which perineal hygiene instructions does the chapter give? Select all that apply.
A) Wipe from front to back — urethra to anus — after voiding or defecating
B) Change the perineal pad from front to back each time she voids or defecates
C) Wash the hands thoroughly before and after changing the pad
D) Wash the perineum with mild soap and warm water at least once daily, cleansing from symphysis pubis to anal area
E) Wipe from back to front to protect the episiotomy
Answer: A, B, C, D
Rationale: The chapter teaches wiping "from front to back (urethra to anus)," changing the pad "from front to back each time she voids or defecates," and washing the hands thoroughly before and after. Box 21.1 adds daily washing with mild soap and warm water, cleansing "from symphysis pubis to anal area."
Trap: (E) reverses the direction and would carry anal flora to the urethra and the episiotomy — and "perineal lacerations and episiotomies can increase the risk for infection as a result of disruption of skin integrity."

Q: According to Box 21.1, how often should the perineal pad be changed, and what should the nurse do at each change?
A) With each void or defecation, or at least four times per day, assessing the amount and character of lochia with each pad change
B) Once per shift, without assessment
C) Only when saturated
D) Every hour regardless of saturation
Answer: A
Rationale: Box 21.1 directs changing the pad "with each void or defecation or at least four times per day" and assessing "amount and character of lochia with each pad change." It also directs applying the pad front to back, "protecting inner surface of pad from contamination," and wrapping the soiled pad before placing it in a covered waste container.
Trap: (C) waits for saturation, which both delays the hygiene measure and loses the assessment opportunity that each change provides.

Subtopic: environment and staff

Q: Which infection-prevention measures does the chapter describe for the postpartum unit? Select all that apply.
A) Changing bed linens, disposable pads, and draw sheets as needed and frequently
B) Having women wear slippers when walking about to prevent contamination of the linens when they return to bed
C) Conscientious hand hygiene by staff and practice of standard precautions
D) Staff with herpetic lesions, strep throat, conjunctivitis, upper respiratory infections, or diarrhea avoiding contact with mothers and infants until no longer contagious
E) Restricting all visitors regardless of health status
Answer: A, B, C, D
Rationale: The chapter names each of these measures, and adds that "visitors with signs of illness are not permitted to enter the postpartum unit."
Trap: (E) excludes healthy visitors, whose presence the chapter treats as part of family-centered care — the restriction is specifically on those with signs of illness.

Q: Which laboratory tests does the chapter describe as routine in the postpartum period?
A) Hemoglobin and hematocrit on the first postpartum day to assess blood loss, especially after cesarean birth, with a urine specimen especially if an indwelling catheter was used, and tests for rubella immunity and Rh status if unknown
B) Daily complete blood counts for all women
C) Coagulation studies for every woman
D) No routine laboratory testing
Answer: A
Rationale: "Hemoglobin and hematocrit values are often evaluated on the first postpartum day to assess blood loss during birth, especially after cesarean birth." A urine specimen may be sent "especially if an indwelling urinary catheter was inserted during the intrapartum period," and if rubella immunity and Rh status are unknown, "tests to determine her status and the possible need for treatment should be performed at this time."
Trap: (D) would leave the two findings that drive discharge interventions — rubella status and Rh status — unknown at the moment they are needed.

Topic: postpartum vaccination
Subtopic: rubella

Q: A nonimmune woman is to receive the rubella vaccine before discharge. What must she be taught?
A) To avoid becoming pregnant for 28 days after receiving the vaccine because of the potential teratogenic risk to the fetus
B) To avoid pregnancy for 3 months
C) To stop breastfeeding for 1 week
D) That no precautions are needed
Answer: A
Rationale: Rubella vaccine is given subcutaneously before hospital discharge to women who are serologically nonimmune, with a titer of 1:8 or less or an enzyme immunoassay level under 0.8. "Women are cautioned to avoid becoming pregnant for 28 days after receiving the rubella vaccine because of the potential teratogenic risk to the fetus." The Legal Tip makes this part of informed consent.
Trap: (C) withholds breastfeeding needlessly: "the live attenuated rubella virus is not communicable in breast milk; therefore breastfeeding mothers can be vaccinated."

Q: A nonimmune postpartum woman lives with a household member who is immunocompromised. What does this mean for rubella vaccination?
A) Nothing; the vaccine is given as usual
B) The vaccine should not be given, because the virus is shed in urine and other body fluids
C) The vaccine should be given at double the dose
D) The vaccine should be given only if she is not breastfeeding
Answer: B
Rationale: "Because the virus is shed in urine and other body fluids, the vaccine should not be given if the woman or other household members are immunocompromised." Expected side effects are fever, transient arthralgia, rash, and lymphadenopathy.
Trap: (D) reaches for breastfeeding as the contraindication when the chapter has already cleared it — the household exposure, not the milk, is the problem.

Subtopic: varicella and tdap

Q: A postpartum woman with no varicella immunity receives the vaccine before discharge. What follow-up is required?
A) None
B) A second dose at the postpartum follow-up visit, 4 to 8 weeks after the first dose
C) A second dose at 6 months
D) A titer at 1 year
Answer: B
Rationale: Varicella vaccine is "administered before discharge" to postpartum women with no immunity, and "a second dose is given at the postpartum follow-up visit (4 to 8 weeks after the first dose)." Side effects are fever, mild rash, and transient arthralgia.
Trap: (A) treats it like the rubella vaccine, which does not require a second dose — this is the detail that separates the two.

Q: A postpartum woman has not previously received Tdap, and her partner and her mother will be caring for the newborn. What does the chapter direct?
A) Give the woman Tdap before discharge or as early as possible postpartum, and have the other adults vaccinated at least 2 weeks before contact with the infant; she may continue to breastfeed
B) Give Tdap only after she stops breastfeeding
C) Vaccinate the household members on the day the baby comes home
D) Defer Tdap until the 6-week visit in all cases
Answer: A
Rationale: Tdap is given to postpartum women who have not previously received it "before discharge from the hospital or as early as possible in the postpartum period," other adults and children around the newborn should be vaccinated "at least 2 weeks before contact with the infant to allow time for immunity to be established," and "women who receive the vaccine can continue to breastfeed."
Trap: (C) gets the vaccine right and the timing wrong, leaving the household unprotected during the interval that matters.

Q: Which postpartum vaccines carry the 28-day pregnancy-avoidance instruction?
A) Rubella and varicella
B) Tdap only
C) All three
D) None of them
Answer: A
Rationale: The Legal Tip specifies informed consent for "rubella and varicella vaccination in the postpartum period," including "that they should not become pregnant for 28 days after being vaccinated." Tdap carries no such instruction.
Trap: (C) extends a live-virus precaution to an inactivated vaccine, which would delay a pregnancy unnecessarily.

Topic: postpartum rh immune globulin
Subtopic: who receives it and when

Q: Which four conditions must be verified before giving Rh immune globulin postpartum?
A) The mother is Rh-negative, has not been sensitized, her Coombs test is negative, and the baby is Rh-positive
B) The mother is Rh-positive and the baby is Rh-negative
C) The mother is Rh-negative and the baby's type is unknown
D) The mother is Rh-negative and has a positive Coombs test
Answer: A
Rationale: The Medication Guide directs: "Postpartum: Verify that the woman is Rh-negative and has not been sensitized, that Coombs test is negative, and that baby is Rh-positive." The injection "within 72 hours after birth prevents sensitization in the Rh-negative woman who has had a fetomaternal transfusion of Rh-positive fetal red blood cells."
Trap: (C) skips the newborn's type, which is what determines whether there is anything to protect against — an Rh-negative baby means no exposure.

Q: What is the postpartum dose and route of Rh immune globulin, and what is the microdose used for?
A) One vial, 300 mcg, intramuscularly in the deltoid or gluteal muscle; the 50-mcg microdose is used for actual or threatened termination of pregnancy up to and including 12 weeks
B) One vial, 50 mcg, intramuscularly; the 300-mcg dose is used before 12 weeks
C) 300 mcg intravenously only
D) The dose is calculated by maternal weight
Answer: A
Rationale: The guide gives "1 vial (300 mcg) IM route in deltoid or gluteal muscle if mother is Rh-negative and newborn is Rh-positive," with the microdose of "1 vial (50 mcg) IM route in deltoid muscle," used antepartum "for Rh-negative women with actual or threatened termination of pregnancy up to and including 12 weeks." Rhophylac can be given by the IM or IV route.
Trap: (B) swaps the two doses, which would leave a postpartum woman with one-sixth of the protection she needs.

Q: A large fetomaternal transfusion is suspected. What determines the dose of Rh immune globulin?
A) A Kleihauer-Betke test; if more than 30 mL of fetal blood is present in the maternal circulation, the dosage must be increased
B) The newborn's weight
C) The volume of the mother's blood loss
D) The dose is never increased
Answer: A
Rationale: "If a large fetomaternal transfusion is suspected, the dosage needed should be determined by performing a Kleihauer-Betke test.... If more than 30 mL of fetal blood is present in the maternal circulation, the dosage of Rh immune globulin must be increased." Additional doses may also be needed after exposure to more than 15 mL of Rh-positive fetal cells.
Trap: (D) fixes a dose that the guide explicitly scales, and this is the same test used after placental abruption to guide the same drug.

Subtopic: safe administration

Q: A woman is to receive both Rh immune globulin and rubella vaccine before discharge. What does the Safety Alert direct?
A) Give only the Rh immune globulin and defer the vaccine for 3 months
B) Give both, and test her in 3 months to see whether she has developed rubella immunity, since Rh immune globulin suppresses the immune response; if not immune, she will need another dose of the vaccine
C) Give only the vaccine
D) Give them 24 hours apart
Answer: B
Rationale: "Rh immune globulin suppresses the immune response. Therefore the woman who receives both Rh immune globulin and a live virus immunization such as rubella must be tested in 3 months to see if she has developed rubella immunity. If not, she will need another dose of the vaccine."
Trap: (A) withholds one of two interventions that are both given before discharge. The answer is the retest, not the omission.

Q: Which nursing considerations apply when administering Rh immune globulin? Select all that apply.
A) Administer within 72 hours after birth or other potential maternal exposure to fetal blood cells
B) Verify the correct dosage and confirm the lot number and the woman's identity before administering, with a second registered nurse's verification if agency policy requires it
C) Observe the client for at least 20 minutes after administration for an allergic response
D) Document the lot number and expiration date in the client record
E) Withhold the drug if the woman is breastfeeding
Answer: A, B, C, D
Rationale: The Medication Guide lists the 72-hour window, dosage and identity verification with a second-nurse check per policy, observation for at least 20 minutes for allergic response, and documentation of lot number and expiration date. It also notes that the medication is made from human plasma, "a consideration if the woman is a Jehovah's Witness," and that the risk of transmitting infectious agents cannot be eliminated completely.
Trap: (E) invents a lactation contraindication. Adverse effects listed are myalgia, lethargy, localized tenderness and stiffness at the site, mild transient fever, malaise, and headache.

Topic: postpartum psychosocial assessment
Subtopic: warning signs

Q: Which findings appear in the Signs of Potential Complications box for postpartum psychosocial concerns? Select all that apply.
A) Unable or unwilling to discuss the labor and birth experience
B) Refuses to interact with or care for the baby — for example, does not name the baby, does not want to hold or feed the baby
C) Expresses disappointment over the baby's sex, or sees the baby as messy or unattractive
D) Partner or other family members react negatively to the baby
E) Asks many questions about infant care
Answer: A, B, C, D
Rationale: The box lists these along with referring to oneself as ugly and useless, excessive preoccupation with body image, marked depression, lacking a support system, the baby reminding the mother of a disliked person, difficulty sleeping, and loss of appetite. It notes that cultural appropriateness of actions must be considered.
Trap: (E) is a sign of engagement rather than concern. The findings most often missed are the neutral-sounding ones — not naming the baby, and being unwilling to discuss the birth.

Q: Who may first notice the psychosocial warning signs, according to the chapter?
A) Only the nurse
B) The partner or other family members, as the box states explicitly
C) Only the primary care provider at the 6-week visit
D) Only the woman herself
Answer: B
Rationale: The box introduces its list with the note that these signs "may be noticed by the partner or other family members," and directs that they be reported to the health care provider or clinic.
Trap: (D) relies on self-report from a woman whose symptoms may include being unable to discuss her experience, which is why the family's observations are named.

Subtopic: screening and support

Q: How does the chapter describe screening for postpartum depression?
A) Routine screening is done in some facilities before discharge using a tool such as the Edinburgh Postnatal Depression Scale, and screening should also be done after discharge, with pediatric providers routinely screening at the 1-, 2-, and 4-month well-child visits
B) Screening is done only if the woman reports symptoms
C) Screening occurs only at the 6-week postpartum visit
D) Screening is not recommended
Answer: A
Rationale: "Routine screening for PPD is done in some facilities before discharge using a tool such as the Edinburgh Postnatal Depression Scale," and "screening for PPD should also be done after discharge." Pediatric care providers "routinely perform maternal screening for PPD during infant follow-up at 1-, 2-, and 4-month well-child visits." A history of preconception or prenatal depression increases the risk.
Trap: (B) makes screening contingent on disclosure from a population the chapter identifies as unlikely to raise concerns spontaneously.

Q: A woman's birth ended in an unplanned cesarean, very different from her birth plan. She keeps returning to the story of her labor. How should the nurse interpret this?
A) As rumination requiring psychiatric referral
B) As an expected need: many women need to review and reflect on their labor and birth experience, and when the experience differed from the birth plan, both partners may need to mourn the loss of their expectations before they can adjust to the reality
C) As a sign she is not bonding with the infant
D) As a complaint about care that should be redirected
Answer: B
Rationale: "Many women need to review and reflect on their labor and birth experience. Their partners may have similar needs. If their birth experience was different from their birth plan (e.g., induction, epidural anesthesia, cesarean birth), both partners may need to mourn the loss of their expectations before they can adjust to the reality of their actual birth experience."
Trap: (D) shuts down the conversation the chapter says she needs, and being *unable or unwilling* to discuss the birth is the warning sign — not being willing to.

Q: What does the chapter describe as evidence that parents are adapting well to their new roles?
A) A realistic perception and acceptance of the newborn's needs and limited abilities, immature social responses, and helplessness, with positive interactions such as taking pleasure in the infant and in providing care, responding appropriately to infant cues, and providing comfort
B) Absence of any expressed fatigue or frustration
C) Declining all offers of help
D) Rooming-in for the entire stay
Answer: A
Rationale: "Parents are adapting well to their new roles when they exhibit a realistic perception and acceptance of their newborn's needs and limited abilities, immature social responses, and helplessness," with positive interactions described as "taking pleasure in the infant and in providing care, responding appropriately to infant cues, and providing comfort."
Trap: (B) sets a standard no new parent meets, and the chapter warns that fatigue "often evident during the hospital stay" is "likely to worsen after discharge."

Topic: postpartum care model and hand-off
Subtopic: the model of care

Q: What is the couplet, or mother/baby, model of care?
A) Nurses educated in both mother and infant care who function as primary nurses for both, even if the infant is kept in the nursery — a variation of rooming-in
B) A model in which separate nurses care for the mother and the infant
C) A model requiring the infant to remain in the nursery
D) A model used only for high-risk dyads
Answer: A
Rationale: "Most birth facilities use the couplet or mother/baby model of care. Nurses in these settings have been educated in both mother and infant care and function as primary nurses for both the mother and the infant, even if the infant is kept in the nursery. This approach is a variation of rooming-in."
Trap: (B) describes the arrangement couplet care replaced, and the phrase "even if the infant is kept in the nursery" is what makes the model about the nurse's assignment rather than the baby's location.

Q: What does the chapter say about bedside reporting?
A) It has been shown to improve client safety and satisfaction, because clients feel more involved in their plan of care and nurses can both visualize and communicate with the client at the time of report
B) It compromises privacy and is discouraged
C) It is used only on high-risk units
D) It replaces documentation
Answer: A
Rationale: "Bedside reporting has been shown to improve client safety and satisfaction. Clients feel more involved in their plan of care, which increases their satisfaction," and "holding reports at the bedside has enabled nurses to both visualize and communicate with the client at the time of report, which improves client safety."
Trap: (D) confuses a communication practice with the record. The hand-off report itself covers an extensive list, from gravidity and parity through the assessment of initial parent-infant interaction.

Q: What are the first steps in providing client-centered care when the woman arrives on the postpartum unit?
A) Confirm her identity by checking her wristband, match the infant's identification number with the corresponding band on her wrist, and determine how she wishes to be addressed and her pronouns of choice, noting these in the record and plan of care
B) Complete the physical assessment before speaking with her
C) Review her chart and introduce the plan of care
D) Wait for the provider's orders
Answer: A
Rationale: "The first step in providing client-centered care is to confirm the woman's identity by checking her wristband. At the same time, the infant's identification number is matched with the corresponding band on the mother's wrist." And: "the nurse determines how the mother wishes to be addressed and her pronouns of choice and notes her preferences in the medical record and the nursing plan of care."
Trap: (B) starts with the assessment and skips the identification step that protects both patients, which is why the chapter names it first.
