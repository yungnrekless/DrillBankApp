# Chapter 16 drills — Labor and Birth Processes

Authored from `sources/Ch16_Fact_Bank.md` (Lowdermilk, Cashion, Alden, Olshansky & Perry, 13th ed., ch. 16, pp. 315–329).

Blueprint weight is **~10 questions of 200 (5.0%), depth `full`** — topics *Labor
Processes, powers, positioning, gravidity and parity*. Ten questions land on
about ten assigned pages, so those pages are drilled close to exhaustively.
That earns **70 drills across 16 topics** — inside the `full` band of 55 to 75,
but deliberately short of its top: Chapter 18 carries nearly double this weight
and is the chapter that should sit at the ceiling.

The organizing insight the fact bank names is the spine of this set: **almost
every measurement in this chapter exists to answer one question — will this head
fit through this pelvis?** The fetal diameters, the pelvic diameters, the four
pelvic types, flexion, asynclitism, and the cardinal movements are the same
problem from different angles, and the recurring answer is that **flexion is what
makes the head small enough**.

Topics follow the chapter's own sequence — passenger, passageway, powers,
position, then the process of labor — because the exam asks the five Ps by name
and a stem usually turns on which P a finding belongs to.

Every distinction the chapter states in both directions is drilled from both
ends: anterior and posterior fontanel, flexion and extension, biparietal and
suboccipitobregmatic, minus and plus station, nullipara and multipara,
longitudinal and transverse lie, diagonal and obstetric conjugate, midplane and
outlet, primary and secondary powers, latent and active phase, internal and
external rotation, synclitism and asynclitism, estrogen up and progesterone down.

**Two deliberate omissions.** *Gravidity and parity* appear in this chapter's
blueprint topic line but are never defined in Chapter 16 — Boxes 14.2 and 14.3
define gravida, para, and GTPAL, and they are drilled with Chapter 14. And the
chapter's closing sections on **fetal adaptation (pp. 327–328)** and **maternal
adaptation (pp. 328–329)** are outside the reading guide's assignment and outside
this bank; the fetal heart rate half of that material belongs to Chapter 18.

Topic names avoid the Chapter 19 set already banked for this course
(`stages of labor framework`, `expected first stage progress`, `second stage
phases and duration`, `third stage management`, `uterine contraction
assessment`). Where the same fact is reachable from both chapters, this file
drills it from the *mechanism* side and leaves the *nursing care* side to
Chapter 19. That overlap is intentional and there is no cross-chapter dedup.

Topic: the five ps of labor
Subtopic: what counts as a p

Q: A new graduate on the labor unit is asked to name the factors that affect the process of labor and birth, the ones the chapter groups as the five Ps. Which factors belong to that list? Select all that apply.
A) The passenger, meaning the fetus and the placenta
B) The passageway, meaning the birth canal
C) The powers, meaning the contractions
D) The place of birth and the type of provider
E) The position of the mother
F) The psychologic response of the woman
Answer: A, B, C, E, F
Rationale: The chapter's own mnemonic is explicit: "At least five factors affect the process of labor and birth. These are easily remembered as the five Ps: passengers (fetus and placenta), passageway (birth canal), powers (contractions), position of the mother, and psychologic response."
Trap: (D) is the one to reject, and it pulls because the book does name those factors — "place of birth, preparation, type of provider ... nursing care, and procedures" — but files them as a separate list of other influences, deliberately outside the five. A P-word in an option is not proof it is one of the five Ps.

Q: A student asks whether the placenta should really be counted as a passenger, since it is delivered after the baby. Which response reflects the chapter?
A) Yes, because it must pass through the birth canal, though it rarely interferes with a normal vaginal birth
B) No, because it is maternal tissue rather than fetal tissue
C) Yes, because it is the structure most likely to obstruct the descent of the fetus
D) No, because it is expelled in the third stage rather than the second
Answer: A
Rationale: "Because the placenta also must pass through the birth canal, it can be considered a passenger along with the fetus; however, the placenta rarely interferes with the process of labor in a normal vaginal birth. An exception is the case of placenta previa."
Trap: (C) overstates the one exception into the rule. Placenta previa is named as the exception precisely because obstruction by the placenta is otherwise rare.

Q: A laboring woman's fear of the hospital and her expectations about pain are clearly shaping how she is coping. Which of the five Ps does the nurse's assessment belong to, and where does this chapter address it?
A) Powers, addressed in this chapter under secondary powers
B) Psychologic response, which this chapter names but defers to Chapter 19
C) Position of the mother, addressed in this chapter under positioning
D) Preparation, which the chapter lists as a sixth P
Answer: B
Rationale: The chapter names psychologic response as the fifth P and then says plainly: "The first four factors are presented here.... The fifth factor is discussed in Chapter 19."
Trap: (D) invents a sixth P. Preparation is real and the book names it, but as one of the other factors influencing birth, not as a P.

Topic: fetal skull landmarks and molding
Subtopic: the two fontanels

Q: During a vaginal examination after rupture of membranes, the nurse palpates a diamond-shaped soft spot measuring roughly 3 cm by 2 cm at the junction of the sagittal, coronal, and frontal sutures. Which structure has been palpated, and what does finding it help establish?
A) The posterior fontanel, which helps establish fetal lie
B) The anterior fontanel, which helps establish presentation, position, and attitude
C) The lambdoidal suture, which helps establish the degree of molding
D) The anterior fontanel, which helps establish the station of the presenting part
Answer: B
Rationale: The anterior fontanel is "diamond shaped," approximately 3 cm by 2 cm, at the junction of the sagittal, coronal, and frontal sutures. The chapter gives the clinical reason for finding it: "During labor after rupture of membranes, palpation of fontanels and sutures during vaginal examination reveals fetal presentation, position, and attitude."
Trap: (D) is the tempting half-right answer because the same examination does establish station — but station is measured against the ischial spines, not read off a fontanel.

Q: On the same examination the nurse palpates a triangular soft spot at the junction of the sutures of the two parietal bones and the occipital bone. Which fontanel is this, and when does it close?
A) The anterior fontanel, closing by 18 months
B) The posterior fontanel, closing 6 to 8 weeks after birth
C) The posterior fontanel, closing by 18 months
D) The anterior fontanel, closing 6 to 8 weeks after birth
Answer: B
Rationale: The posterior fontanel is triangular, approximately 1 cm by 2 cm, at the junction of the sutures of the two parietal bones and the occipital bone, and closes 6 to 8 weeks after birth.
Trap: (C) is the mirror error of the previous item — right fontanel, wrong closure time. The cue that survives a blank moment is that the bigger one takes longer to close: anterior is 3 by 2 cm and closes at 18 months, posterior is 1 by 2 cm and closes at 6 to 8 weeks.

Subtopic: molding

Q: Parents of a newborn born after a long second stage are distressed that their baby's head looks elongated and asymmetric. Which explanation does the chapter support?
A) The skull bones are not firmly united, so they slide over one another during labor and most newborn heads regain their normal shape within 3 days
B) The bones have separated at the sutures under pressure and will reunite over the first 18 months
C) This represents fluid collected under the scalp and will resolve once the swelling is reabsorbed
D) The head has been compressed against an android pelvis and the shape change is usually permanent
Answer: A
Rationale: "Because the bones are not firmly united, slight overlapping or molding of the shape of the head occurs during labor. This capacity of the bones to slide over one another also permits adaptation to the various diameters of the maternal pelvis." The chapter adds that molding "can be extensive, but the heads of most newborns assume their normal shape within 3 days after birth."
Trap: (B) sounds like a reasonable restatement but reverses the mechanism — the bones overlap rather than separate, and it is that overlap that lets the head adapt to the pelvis.

Topic: fetal head diameters and attitude
Subtopic: attitude and the diameter it presents

Q: A term fetus is described as having the chin flexed on the chest, the thighs flexed on the abdomen, and the legs flexed at the knees. Which of the five Ps does this description belong to, and what is the finding called?
A) Position; it describes an occiput anterior presentation
B) Attitude; it describes general flexion, the normal attitude
C) Lie; it describes a longitudinal lie
D) Presentation; it describes a vertex presentation
Answer: B
Rationale: "Attitude is the relation of the fetal body parts to one another," and the chapter's description of normal attitude is exactly this: "Normally the back of the fetus is rounded so that the chin is flexed on the chest, the thighs are flexed on the abdomen, and the legs are flexed at the knees."
Trap: (C) pulls because a flexed fetus is usually also longitudinal, but lie compares the fetal spine to the maternal spine. Attitude compares the fetus to itself; nothing in this stem mentions the mother.

Q: A vertex-presenting fetus is in complete flexion at the onset of labor. Which head diameter is presenting to the pelvis, and why does it matter?
A) The suboccipitobregmatic, about 9.5 cm, the smallest and most critical diameter, which allows the head to pass easily through the true pelvis
B) The biparietal, about 9.25 cm, the smallest anteroposterior diameter of the fetal head
C) The occipitofrontal, which is the diameter that defines engagement
D) The occipitomental, which is the diameter presented when the head is fully flexed
Answer: A
Rationale: The suboccipitobregmatic diameter is "about 9.5 cm at term" and is "the smallest and the most critical" of the anteroposterior diameters. "When the head is in complete flexion, this diameter allows the fetal head to pass easily through the true pelvis."
Trap: (B) is the classic swap: the biparietal is real and is 9.25 cm, but it is the largest transverse diameter, not an anteroposterior one. The two numbers are close enough that the roles get traded rather than the values.

Q: A fetus presents with the head in marked extension, or deflection. Which diameter now presents to the inlet, and what does the chapter say about it?
A) The suboccipitobregmatic, which remains the presenting diameter regardless of attitude
B) The occipitofrontal, which is slightly larger but still permits entry
C) The occipitomental, which is too large to permit the head to enter the pelvis
D) The biparietal, which widens as the head extends
Answer: C
Rationale: Fig. 16.5C shows "marked extension (deflection), so that largest diameter, which is too large to permit the head to enter the pelvis, is presenting" — the occipitomental. The governing rule is stated directly: "As the head is more extended, the anteroposterior diameter widens, and the head may not be able to enter the true pelvis."
Trap: (B) is the mirror error of the next item and the reason this pair exists — moderate extension gives the occipitofrontal, marked extension gives the occipitomental. Both are larger than the flexed diameter, but only one is described as too large to enter at all.

Q: A fetus is described as being in a military attitude, with the head neither flexed nor markedly extended. Which diameter is entering the pelvis?
A) The suboccipitobregmatic, because a neutral head is functionally flexed
B) The occipitofrontal, because moderate extension presents that large diameter
C) The occipitomental, because any loss of flexion presents the largest diameter
D) The bitemporal, because the head is neither flexed nor extended
Answer: B
Rationale: Fig. 16.5B labels the military attitude "moderate extension (military attitude) so that large diameter enters" — the occipitofrontal.
Trap: (C) is the mirror error of the previous item, jumping straight to the worst case. The chapter grades the three attitudes rather than treating any loss of flexion as equivalent: complete flexion gives 9.5 cm, moderate extension gives the occipitofrontal, marked extension gives the occipitomental.

Q: An ultrasound report gives a biparietal diameter of 9.25 cm at term. Which statement about that measurement is accurate?
A) It is the largest transverse diameter and an important indicator of fetal head size
B) It is the smallest anteroposterior diameter and the one flexion produces
C) It is the diameter that widens as the fetal head extends
D) It is measured from the suboccipital region to the bregma
Answer: A
Rationale: The biparietal diameter is "about 9.25 cm at term" and is "the largest transverse diameter and an important indicator of fetal head size. In a well-flexed cephalic presentation, the biparietal diameter is the widest part of the head entering the pelvic inlet." It is also the diameter whose passage through the inlet defines engagement.
Trap: (B) and (D) both describe the suboccipitobregmatic, which is the other number students hold in this slot. Transverse versus anteroposterior is the distinction that keeps them apart: 9.25 cm side to side, 9.5 cm front to back with the head flexed.

Topic: fetal presentation and lie
Subtopic: what presents first

Q: A term fetus is in a cephalic presentation with the occiput as the presenting part. How is this presentation noted, and how common is it?
A) Noted as vertex; cephalic presentations account for approximately 97% to 98% of births
B) Noted as vertex; cephalic presentations account for approximately 80% of births
C) Noted as occiput anterior; cephalic presentations account for approximately 97% to 98% of births
D) Noted as military; cephalic presentations account for approximately 90% of births
Answer: A
Rationale: "Presentation refers to the part of the fetus that enters the pelvic inlet first and leads through the birth canal during labor at term." Cephalic presentation occurs in "approximately 97% to 98% of births," the presenting part is usually the occiput, and "when the presenting part is the occiput, the presentation is noted as vertex."
Trap: (C) confuses presentation with position. Occiput anterior is a position — a three-letter notation relating the occiput to a quadrant of the maternal pelvis — while vertex names what is presenting.

Q: A fetus is found on examination to be presenting buttocks first, and a second fetus on the unit is lying with a shoulder over the inlet. Which presenting parts correspond to these two presentations?
A) The sacrum for the breech and the scapula for the shoulder presentation
B) The scapula for the breech and the sacrum for the shoulder presentation
C) The mentum for the breech and the scapula for the shoulder presentation
D) The sacrum for the breech and the mentum for the shoulder presentation
Answer: A
Rationale: Breech presentation occurs in approximately 2% to 3% of births at term and the presenting part is the sacrum; shoulder presentation occurs in fewer than 1% of births and the presenting part is the scapula.
Trap: (C) and (D) both reach for the mentum, which is the chin — it is the reference point for a face presentation, not for a breech or a shoulder. The four reference points are occiput, sacrum, mentum, and scapula, and they are easy to redistribute among the wrong presentations.

Subtopic: lie and its one absolute

Q: An ultrasound at the onset of labor shows the fetal spine at a right angle to the mother's spine, and the fetus remains in that orientation. What does the chapter state about the outcome?
A) The lie is oblique and will usually convert during labor
B) The lie is transverse, and vaginal birth cannot occur if the fetus stays in it
C) The lie is transverse, and vaginal birth is possible if the shoulder engages
D) The lie is longitudinal with a shoulder presentation
Answer: B
Rationale: "Lie is the relation of the long axis (spine) of the fetus to the long axis (spine) of the mother." In a transverse lie the fetal long axis is at a right angle to the mother's, and the chapter states the consequence without qualification: "Vaginal birth cannot occur when the fetus stays in a transverse lie."
Trap: (A) confuses the two abnormal lies. An oblique lie is the one that "usually converts to a longitudinal or transverse lie during labor"; the transverse lie is the chapter's one absolute.

Q: A fetus lies with its long axis parallel to the mother's long axis. Which statement follows from that finding alone?
A) The presentation is necessarily vertex
B) The presentation is either cephalic or breech, depending on which fetal structure enters the pelvis first
C) The attitude is one of general flexion
D) The position is occiput anterior
Answer: B
Rationale: "Longitudinal lies are either cephalic or breech presentations, depending on the fetal structure that first enters the mother's pelvis." The chapter also names what settles the presenting part: "Factors that determine the presenting part include fetal lie, fetal attitude, and extension or flexion of the fetal head."
Trap: (A) is the mirror error of the previous item — knowing the lie narrows the presentation to two options but does not pick between them. A longitudinal lie is equally compatible with a breech.

Topic: fetal position notation
Subtopic: reading the three letters

Q: A cervical examination is documented as ROA. What does that notation state about the fetus?
A) The occiput is the presenting part and is in the right anterior quadrant of the mother's pelvis
B) The fetus's own right side is anterior, with the occiput presenting
C) The occiput is the presenting part and is in the right anterior quadrant of the fetal pelvis
D) The sacrum is the presenting part and is in the right anterior quadrant of the mother's pelvis
Answer: A
Rationale: "Position is the relationship of a reference point on the presenting part (occiput, sacrum, mentum [chin], or sinciput [deflexed vertex]) to the four quadrants of the mother's pelvis." The book's own worked example reads: "ROA means that the occiput is the presenting part and is located in the right anterior quadrant of the maternal pelvis."
Trap: (B) misassigns the reference frame, which is the single most common error with this notation. The first letter names the mother's right or left side, never the fetus's.

Q: A second examination is documented as LSP. What does that notation state?
A) The occiput is presenting in the left posterior quadrant, a vertex presentation
B) The sacrum is presenting in the left posterior quadrant, a breech presentation
C) The fetal spine lies in the left posterior quadrant
D) The scapula is presenting in the left posterior quadrant, a shoulder presentation
Answer: B
Rationale: The book's second worked example: "LSP means that the sacrum is the presenting part and is located in the left posterior quadrant." The sacrum is the reference point for a breech.
Trap: (C) is the trap this notation sets every time — reading the middle S as "spine" instead of "sacrum." The middle letter always names the presenting part, and the four values are O for occiput, S for sacrum, M for mentum, and Sc for scapula.

Q: The nurse is teaching a student to decode position notation. Which statements about the three letters are accurate? Select all that apply.
A) The first letter gives the right or left side of the mother's pelvis
B) The first letter gives the right or left side of the fetus
C) The middle letter gives the specific presenting part of the fetus
D) The final letter gives the anterior, posterior, or transverse portion of the maternal pelvis
E) The final letter gives the station of the presenting part
Answer: A, C, D
Rationale: The three-part notation gives, in order, "the location of the presenting part in the right or left side of the mother's pelvis," then "the specific presenting part of the fetus," then "the location of the presenting part in relation to the anterior, posterior, or transverse portion of the maternal pelvis."
Trap: (E) is the one that pulls hardest, because station is documented in the same breath as position on a cervical examination. Station is a separate measurement against the ischial spines and never appears inside the three letters.

Topic: station, engagement, and synclitism
Subtopic: reading station

Q: A cervical examination places the lowermost portion of the presenting part 1 cm above the ischial spines. How is this station documented, and what does it indicate?
A) Plus 1, indicating the presenting part has descended past the spines
B) Minus 1, indicating the presenting part has not yet reached the level of the spines
C) Zero, indicating engagement
D) Minus 1, indicating the presenting part is 1 cm below the spines
Answer: B
Rationale: "Station is the relationship of the presenting fetal part to an imaginary line drawn between the maternal ischial spines and is a measure of the degree of descent." When the lowermost portion of the presenting part is 1 cm above the spines, "it is noted as being minus 1."
Trap: (A) inverts the sign, which is the reversal this measurement invites. Minus is above the spines and less descended; plus is below and further along.

Q: Two hours later the presenting part is palpated 4 cm below the ischial spines. What does this station indicate?
A) Plus 4, and birth is imminent
B) Minus 4, and the fetus has not yet engaged
C) Plus 4, and the fetus is still above the midpelvis
D) Zero, because measurement below the spines resets the scale
Answer: A
Rationale: A presenting part below the spines is documented as plus, and the chapter names the threshold directly: "Birth is imminent when the presenting part is at plus 4 to plus 5 cm."
Trap: (B) is the mirror error of the previous item. An option that treats minus 2 as further along than plus 1, or reads a plus station as not yet descended, has the scale running backwards.

Q: A nurse documents station 0 on admission. Which statements are supported by that finding? Select all that apply.
A) The presenting part is at the level of the ischial spines
B) This station usually corresponds to engagement
C) Engagement means the largest transverse diameter of the presenting part, usually the biparietal, has passed through the pelvic inlet
D) The presenting part has passed the pelvic outlet
E) Birth is imminent
Answer: A, B, C
Rationale: "At the level of the spines, the station is referred to as 0 (zero)." Engagement "is the term used to indicate that the largest transverse diameter of the presenting part (usually the biparietal diameter) has passed through the maternal pelvic brim or inlet into the true pelvis," and it "usually corresponds to station 0."
Trap: (E) is the wrong-side option here, and it pulls because it is a true statement attached to the wrong number. Birth is imminent at plus 4 to plus 5, not at 0, which marks entry into the true pelvis rather than exit from it.

Q: Why does the chapter say station should be determined when labor begins?
A) So that the rate of descent of the fetus during labor can be assessed accurately
B) So that the estimated fetal weight can be calculated
C) Because station cannot be assessed once the membranes have ruptured
D) Because station determines the pelvic type
Answer: A
Rationale: "The station of the presenting part should be determined when labor begins so that the rate of descent of the fetus during labor can be assessed accurately." A single station means little; the change between two stations is the finding.
Trap: (C) reverses the relationship between station and membranes. Rupture of membranes makes palpation of fontanels and sutures possible, and descent is especially apparent after the membranes have ruptured.

Q: A nulliparous woman at 38 weeks is told at a prenatal visit that the baby has dropped, and a multiparous woman on the labor unit is found to have a freely movable head at 3 cm. Which statement about engagement fits both findings?
A) Both are abnormal and warrant evaluation for cephalopelvic disproportion
B) Engagement often occurs in the weeks just before labor in nulliparas and may occur before or during labor in multiparas
C) Engagement occurs only after labor is established, regardless of parity
D) Engagement occurs earlier in multiparas because the abdominal musculature is more relaxed
Answer: B
Rationale: Engagement "often occurs in the weeks just before labor begins in nulliparas and may occur before or during labor in multiparas." The cardinal-movements section gives the reason: firmer abdominal muscles in nulliparous pregnancies direct the presenting part into the pelvis, while in multiparous pregnancies the more relaxed musculature often leaves the head freely movable above the brim until labor is established.
Trap: (D) inverts the parity rule and is the mirror error to watch for, because relaxed abdominal muscles sound like they should let the head settle sooner. They do the opposite — they fail to direct it in.

Subtopic: synclitism and asynclitism

Q: A fetal head is noted to be deflected anteriorly in the pelvis rather than parallel to the anteroposterior plane. How should the nurse interpret this finding?
A) As asynclitism, which frequently occurs and can facilitate descent by positioning the head to accommodate the pelvic cavity
B) As asynclitism, which is always pathologic and predicts cephalopelvic disproportion
C) As synclitism, the normal position at engagement
D) As a transverse arrest requiring immediate cesarean birth
Answer: A
Rationale: "The head usually engages in the pelvis in a synclitic position (i.e., one that is parallel to the anteroposterior plane of the pelvis)." Asynclitism, in which "the head is deflected anteriorly or posteriorly in the pelvis," "frequently occurs, which can facilitate descent because the head is being positioned to accommodate to the pelvic cavity."
Trap: (B) treats a common and often helpful adaptation as a diagnosis, which is the reflex when an unfamiliar term appears in a stem.

Q: A labor is arrested despite a pelvis measured as normal in size, and the head is found to be extremely deflected. What does the chapter say about this situation?
A) Extreme asynclitism can cause cephalopelvic disproportion even in a normal-size pelvis, because the head is positioned so that it cannot descend
B) Cephalopelvic disproportion cannot occur when pelvic measurements are normal
C) The finding indicates an android pelvis regardless of the measurements
D) The finding is a normal variant and descent will resume without intervention
Answer: A
Rationale: "Extreme asynclitism can cause cephalopelvic disproportion, even in a normal-size pelvis, because the fetal head is positioned so that it cannot descend through the maternal pelvis."
Trap: (D) is the mirror error of the previous item — having learned that asynclitism is usually helpful, the reflex is to dismiss the extreme form too. The chapter draws the line at extreme, and (B) fails for the same reason: disproportion is about fit, not about measurements alone.


Topic: bony pelvis planes and obstetric measurements
Subtopic: the true pelvis and its three planes

Q: A student asks which part of the bony pelvis the fetus actually passes through. Which response is correct?
A) The false pelvis, the part above the brim, which forms the birth canal
B) The true pelvis, the part below the brim, divided into the inlet, the midpelvis, and the outlet
C) Both parts equally, since the brim is only an anatomic landmark
D) The true pelvis, which is divided into the inlet and the outlet only
Answer: B
Rationale: "The bony pelvis is separated by the brim, or inlet, into two parts: the false and the true pelves." And: "The false pelvis is the part above the brim and plays no part in childbearing. The true pelvis, the part involved in birth, is divided into three planes: the inlet, or brim; the midpelvis, or cavity; and the outlet."
Trap: (D) drops the midpelvis, which is the plane most stems turn on — it is where the ischial spines sit, where station is measured, and where internal rotation begins.

Q: A woman is found on examination to have a narrow subpubic angle. Why does this matter for labor?
A) It narrows the pelvic inlet, preventing engagement
B) The fetus must first pass beneath the pubic arch, and a narrow subpubic angle is less accommodating than a rounded wide arch
C) It shortens the anterior wall of the midpelvis
D) It makes the coccyx immobile in the latter part of pregnancy
Answer: B
Rationale: "Because the fetus must first pass beneath the pubic arch, a narrow subpubic angle is less accommodating than a rounded wide arch." The outlet is bounded by the pubic arch anteriorly, the ischial tuberosities laterally, and the tip of the coccyx posteriorly.
Trap: (D) contradicts the chapter, which notes that in the latter part of pregnancy "the coccyx is movable" — mobility at the back of the outlet is one of the accommodations that offsets a tight fit.

Q: A nurse is reviewing the relative size of the pelvic planes. Which statements are accurate? Select all that apply.
A) The midplane is normally the largest plane of the true pelvis
B) The outlet presents the smallest plane of the pelvic canal
C) The inlet is the narrowest point of the pelvic canal
D) The transverse, or interspinous, diameter of the midplane measures about 10.5 cm
E) The transverse, or intertuberous, diameter of the outlet measures 8 cm or more
Answer: A, B, D, E
Rationale: Table 16.1 states in its own descriptions that "the midplane of the pelvis normally is its largest plane and the one of greatest diameter," with an interspinous diameter of 10.5 cm, and that "the outlet presents the smallest plane of the pelvic canal," with an intertuberous diameter of 8 cm or more.
Trap: (C) is the option to reject, and it pulls because the inlet is where engagement is decided, so it feels like the bottleneck. The bottleneck by measurement is the outlet.

Subtopic: the conjugates

Q: During a pelvic examination the provider measures the diagonal conjugate at 12.5 cm. What does that measurement establish?
A) Whether the presenting part can enter the pelvic inlet
B) The anteroposterior diameter of the pelvic outlet
C) The interspinous diameter of the midplane
D) Whether a trial of labor is contraindicated
Answer: A
Rationale: Table 16.1 defines the diagonal conjugate as the "measurement that determines whether presenting part can enter the pelvic inlet," with a normal value of 12.5 to 13 cm. It is the conjugate obtained clinically, by hand.
Trap: (D) overreads the finding into a decision the chapter explicitly refuses to let it make: "pelvimetry results rarely contraindicate a trial of labor."

Q: A nursing student documents that the provider measured the obstetric conjugate at the bedside during a vaginal examination. Why should the nurse correct this entry?
A) The obstetric conjugate is obtained only by x-ray or estimated from the diagonal conjugate, at 1.5 to 2 cm less
B) The obstetric conjugate is measured only during the third trimester
C) The obstetric conjugate is another name for the true conjugate and measures at least 11 cm
D) The obstetric conjugate is measured from the ischial spines rather than the symphysis
Answer: A
Rationale: Table 16.1 defines the obstetric conjugate as the "measurement obtained only by x-ray or estimated from the diagonal conjugate," at "1.5 to 2 cm less than diagonal." Only the diagonal conjugate is measurable by hand.
Trap: (C) merges two separate rows of the table. The true conjugate, or conjugata vera, is the anteroposterior diameter of the inlet at 11 cm or more; the obstetric conjugate is the one derived by subtraction from the diagonal.

Topic: pelvic types and predicted labor outcomes
Subtopic: matching type to outcome

Q: A woman's pelvis is described as having a transversely rounded brim, straight side walls, blunt and widely separated ischial spines, a deep curved sacrum, and a wide subpubic arch. Which pelvic type is this, and what does Table 16.2 associate it with?
A) Gynecoid, associated with birth in the occiput anterior position
B) Anthropoid, associated with birth in the occiput posterior position
C) Platypelloid, associated with transverse arrest
D) Android, associated with an increased risk of cephalopelvic disproportion
Answer: A
Rationale: Table 16.2 gives the gynecoid pelvis a slightly ovoid or transversely rounded brim, straight side walls, blunt widely separated spines, a deep curved sacrum, and a wide subpubic arch, and names its effect on labor as the "classic female shape; associated with birth in OA position." It is the most common type at about 50%.
Trap: (D) picks the type whose features are the near-opposite of this stem. Android features are the ones that read as tight: heart-shaped brim, convergent side walls, prominent spines with a narrow interspinous diameter, and a narrow arch.

Q: A pelvis is described as heart shaped and angulated at the brim, deep, with convergent side walls, prominent spines and a narrow interspinous diameter, and a narrow subpubic arch. Which complication does Table 16.2 associate with this type?
A) Transverse arrest
B) An increased risk of cephalopelvic disproportion, in theory
C) Birth in the occiput posterior position
D) Precipitous birth
Answer: B
Rationale: These are the android features in Table 16.2, and the table's effect column reads: "In theory, has an increased risk of CPD." The android pelvis accounts for about 23% of pelves.
Trap: (C) belongs to the anthropoid pelvis, which also has a narrow subpubic arch and prominent spines — sharing two features with the android type is exactly why these two get swapped.

Q: A pelvis is oval with the brim wider anteroposteriorly, deep, with straight side walls, prominent spines, and a narrow subpubic arch. Which birth outcome does the chapter associate with it?
A) Birth in the occiput anterior position
B) Birth in the occiput posterior position
C) Transverse arrest
D) Shoulder dystocia
Answer: B
Rationale: Table 16.2 describes the anthropoid pelvis as oval and wider anteroposteriorly, and its effect column reads: "More often associated with birth in OP position." It accounts for about 24% of pelves.
Trap: (A) is the mirror error against the gynecoid row. A pelvis that is roomiest front to back tends to keep the occiput front to back as well, which lands it posteriorly rather than anteriorly.

Q: A pelvis is flattened anteroposteriorly and wide transversely, shallow, with blunt widely separated spines and a wide subpubic arch. Which complication does the chapter associate with this type?
A) An increased risk of cephalopelvic disproportion
B) Birth in the occiput posterior position
C) An increased likelihood of a transverse arrest
D) Failure of the head to engage before labor
Answer: C
Rationale: Table 16.2 describes the platypelloid, or flat, pelvis this way and gives its effect as: "In theory, increases the likelihood of a transverse arrest." It is the rarest type at about 3%.
Trap: (B) is the anthropoid outcome and is the mirror of this one along the same axis. A pelvis that is widest transversely holds the head transversely; a pelvis that is widest anteroposteriorly holds it in the anteroposterior plane.

Q: A nurse is reviewing pelvic types before a prenatal clinic. Which statements are accurate? Select all that apply.
A) Mixed types of pelves are more common than pure types
B) The gynecoid type is the most common, at about 50%
C) The android and anthropoid types both have a narrow subpubic arch
D) The platypelloid type is the most common after gynecoid
E) The anthropoid type accounts for about 24% of pelves
Answer: A, B, C, E
Rationale: The chapter names four basic types and states that "mixed types of pelves are more common than pure types." Table 16.2's frequencies are gynecoid 50%, android 23%, anthropoid 24%, and platypelloid 3%; the android and anthropoid rows both list a narrow subpubic arch.
Trap: (D) is the option to reject. Platypelloid is the rarest of the four at 3%, and it gets promoted in students' memory because it is the one with the most distinctive shape.

Answer: B
Rationale: Assessment of the pelvis is done during the first prenatal evaluation and "need not be repeated if the pelvis is of adequate size and suitable shape."

Q: A provider chooses to perform a more detailed pelvic examination in the third trimester. What does the chapter say about the accuracy of that timing?

Answer: B
Rationale: A third-trimester examination "may be more thorough and the results more accurate because there is relaxation and increased mobility of the pelvic joints and ligaments as a result of hormonal influences."

Topic: soft tissue passageway and the retraction ring
Subtopic: the two uterine segments

Q: After labor is established, the uterine body has a thick muscular upper segment and a thin-walled passive lower segment. What separates the two, and what does the arrangement accomplish?
A) A physiologic retraction ring separates them, and the contractions of the uterine body exert downward pressure on the fetus, pushing it against the cervix
B) A pathologic retraction ring separates them, and the lower segment thickens to expel the fetus
C) The internal os separates them, and the upper segment distends to accommodate the intrauterine contents
D) A physiologic retraction ring separates them, and the upper segment distends while the lower segment thickens
Answer: A
Rationale: "Uterine contractions cause the uterine body to have a thick and muscular upper segment and a thin-walled, passive, muscular lower segment. A physiologic retraction ring separates the two segments." The mechanics follow: "The lower uterine segment gradually distends to accommodate the intrauterine contents as the wall of the upper segment thickens.... The contractions of the uterine body thus exert downward pressure on the fetus, pushing it against the cervix."
Trap: (D) swaps which segment does what, which is the error that matters — the thin lower segment distends and the thick upper segment retracts, and reversing them makes the fetus travel the wrong way.

Q: A woman in second-stage labor with obstructed descent is found to have a ring that has become visible and palpable across the abdomen. What is the relationship of this finding to normal labor anatomy?
A) It is an entirely separate structure unrelated to the physiologic ring
B) It is a pathologic, or Bandl, ring that develops from the physiologic retraction ring under abnormal conditions
C) It is the normal physiologic ring, which becomes visible in every second-stage labor
D) It is the upper margin of the cervix as effacement completes
Answer: B
Rationale: Fig. 16.10C notes that a pathologic retraction, or Bandl, ring "that forms under abnormal conditions develops from the physiologic ring." Chapter 32 covers it with the rest of dystocia.
Trap: (C) treats a red flag as a routine finding. The physiologic ring is present in normal labor but is not a visible abdominal landmark; the pathologic ring signals obstructed labor.

Q: Which soft tissue structure does the chapter credit with helping the fetus rotate anteriorly as it passes through the birth canal?
A) The cervix
B) The pelvic floor muscles
C) The lower uterine segment
D) The introitus
Answer: B
Rationale: The soft tissues of the passageway "include the distensible lower uterine segment, the cervix, the pelvic floor muscles, the vagina, and the introitus," and of the pelvic floor the chapter says: "This structure helps the fetus rotate anteriorly as it passes through the birth canal." The cardinal-movements section adds that "both the levator ani muscles and the bony pelvis are important for achieving anterior rotation."
Trap: (C) is the structure doing the pushing rather than the turning. Distention of the lower segment transmits force downward; rotation is the pelvic floor's contribution.

Topic: cervical effacement and dilation
Subtopic: what each term measures

Q: A cervix normally 2 to 3 cm long and about 1 cm thick is examined in labor, and the examiner can palpate only a thin edge. What does this finding mean?
A) Effacement is complete, meaning the cervix has shortened and thinned
B) Dilation is complete, meaning the cervical opening has widened to about 10 cm
C) Effacement is 50%, since a palpable edge means effacement is partial
D) The cervix is ripe but effacement has not yet begun
Answer: A
Rationale: "Effacement of the cervix means the shortening and thinning of the cervix during the first stage of labor," and "only a thin edge of the cervix can be palpated by an examiner when effacement is complete." Degree of effacement is expressed in percentages from 0% to 100%.
Trap: (B) is the other end state and the one with a distinct physical finding: when dilation is complete the cervix "can no longer be palpated by an examiner" at all. Palpating a thin edge means effacement is done; palpating nothing means dilation is done.

Q: A woman's cervix is documented as fully dilated. Which statement is accurate?
A) The cervix measures approximately 10 cm and full dilation marks the end of the first stage of labor
B) The cervix measures approximately 10 cm and full dilation marks the end of the second stage of labor
C) The cervix measures approximately 5 to 6 cm, the point at which descent accelerates
D) Full dilation marks the beginning of the active phase of the first stage
Answer: A
Rationale: "The diameter of the cervix increases from less than 1 cm to full dilation (approximately 10 cm) to allow birth of a term fetus," and "full cervical dilation marks the end of the first stage of labor."
Trap: (C) borrows a real number from the cardinal-movements section — descent accelerates in the active phase once the cervix has dilated to 5 to 6 cm — and attaches it to the wrong endpoint.

Subtopic: how parity changes the pattern

Q: A nulliparous woman at term is 100% effaced and 1 cm dilated after several hours of regular contractions. How should the nurse interpret this pattern?
A) It is the expected pattern, since effacement generally progresses significantly in a first-time term pregnancy before more than slight dilation occurs
B) It suggests an arrest of labor and the provider should be notified
C) It suggests the contractions are Braxton Hicks rather than true labor
D) It is expected only if the membranes have ruptured
Answer: A
Rationale: "Effacement generally progresses significantly in first-time term pregnancy before more than slight dilation occurs." A primigravida who is completely effaced at 1 cm is following the chapter's own description, not stalling.
Trap: (B) is the reflex response to a number that has not moved, and it is what makes this pair worth drilling — the wrong reading converts a normal first labor into a diagnosis.

Q: A multiparous woman arrives at 4 cm dilated with 30% effacement. How should the nurse interpret this pattern?
A) As abnormal, because effacement should be complete before dilation begins
B) As expected, because in subsequent pregnancies effacement and dilation tend to progress together
C) As evidence of cervical scarring from a prior birth
D) As a sign that the cervix has not yet ripened
Answer: B
Rationale: "In subsequent pregnancies, effacement and dilation of the cervix tend to progress together." A multipara dilating ahead of her effacement is following the expected pattern for parity.
Trap: (A) is the mirror error of the previous item — applying the nulliparous sequence to a multipara. The rule to hold is that effacement leads in a first labor and the two move together in later ones.

Q: A woman with a history of cervical surgery is dilating slowly despite a good contraction pattern. Which factors does the chapter connect to cervical dilation? Select all that apply.
A) Scarring of the cervix from prior infection or surgery may slow dilation
B) Strong uterine contractions draw up the musculofibrous components of the cervix
C) Pressure exerted by the amniotic fluid while the membranes are intact can promote dilation
D) Force applied by the presenting part can promote dilation
E) Voluntary bearing-down efforts speed cervical dilation once labor is active
Answer: A, B, C, D
Rationale: Dilation occurs "by the drawing upward of the musculofibrous components of the cervix, caused by strong uterine contractions. Pressure exerted by the amniotic fluid while the membranes are intact or by the force applied by the presenting part can promote cervical dilation." The chapter separately notes that "scarring of the cervix as a result of prior infection or surgery may slow cervical dilation."
Trap: (E) is the one to leave unselected, and it is the most attractive wrong option in this chapter: the secondary powers "have no effect on cervical dilation." Pushing before full dilation accomplishes nothing the cervix needs.

Topic: primary versus secondary powers
Subtopic: which power does what

Q: A nurse is charting the forces of labor. Which description matches the primary powers?
A) Involuntary uterine contractions originating at pacemaker points in the upper uterine segment, responsible for effacement, dilation, and descent
B) Voluntary bearing-down efforts that augment the involuntary contractions after full dilation
C) Involuntary contractions originating in the lower uterine segment and moving upward in waves
D) The combined effect of the diaphragm and abdominal muscles on intraabdominal pressure
Answer: A
Rationale: "Involuntary uterine contractions, called the primary powers, signal the beginning of labor." They "originate at certain pacemaker points in the thickened muscle layers of the upper uterine segment," move downward over the uterus in waves separated by short rest periods, and "are responsible for the effacement and dilation of the cervix and descent of the fetus."
Trap: (C) reverses the direction of the wave and the segment it starts in, which matters because the whole mechanism depends on a thick upper segment retracting downward against a passive lower one.

Q: The same nurse charts the secondary powers. Which statement is accurate?
A) They are involuntary and become effective in the latent phase of the first stage
B) They are voluntary bearing-down efforts that have no effect on cervical dilation but are of considerable importance in expelling the infant after the cervix is fully dilated
C) They replace the primary powers once full dilation is reached
D) They increase the frequency and intensity of uterine contractions
Answer: B
Rationale: "Once the cervix has dilated, voluntary bearing-down efforts by the woman, called the secondary powers, augment the force of the involuntary contractions." And the dividing line: "The secondary powers have no effect on cervical dilation, but they are of considerable importance in the expulsion of the infant from the uterus and vagina after the cervix is fully dilated."
Trap: (C) is the mirror error of the previous item. The secondary powers augment the primary ones rather than replacing them — the woman pushes with a contraction, not instead of one.

Subtopic: describing a contraction

Q: A nurse times a woman's contractions from the beginning of one contraction to the beginning of the next. Which characteristic is being measured?
A) Duration
B) Frequency
C) Intensity
D) Resting tone
Answer: B
Rationale: The chapter defines the three descriptors precisely: frequency is "the time from the beginning of one contraction to the beginning of the next," duration is "length of contraction," and intensity is "strength of contraction at its peak."
Trap: (A) is the reflex pick when the timing runs across two contractions, and the manufactured error in the other direction is timing frequency from the end of one contraction to the beginning of the next — that interval is the rest period, not the frequency.

Subtopic: contractions independent of sensation

Q: Which statements about uterine contractions and maternal sensation does the chapter support? Select all that apply.
A) Women paralyzed by spinal cord lesions above the 12th thoracic vertebra have normal but painless uterine contractions
B) Epidural analgesia during labor does not decrease the frequency or intensity of contractions
C) Epidural analgesia reliably slows contractions, so oxytocin is routinely required afterward
D) Contractions originate independently of voluntary control, at pacemaker points in the uterus
Answer: A, B, D
Rationale: The chapter states that laboring women "paralyzed because of spinal cord lesions above the 12th thoracic vertebra have normal but painless uterine contractions," and that "use of epidural analgesia during labor does not decrease the frequency or intensity of contractions." Both facts follow from the primary powers being involuntary and uterine in origin.
Trap: (C) is the belief this passage exists to correct, and it is the one a stem will test by describing a woman whose labor seems to have stalled after an epidural. The chapter denies the premise.

Subtopic: the ferguson reflex

Q: A woman in the second stage reports a sudden overwhelming urge to bear down as the presenting part reaches the perineal floor. What mechanism does the chapter give for this urge?
A) Stretch receptors in the posterior vagina cause release of endogenous oxytocin, which triggers the urge to bear down
B) Direct pressure on the sacral nerve roots produces a reflex contraction of the abdominal muscles
C) Rising prostaglandin levels stimulate the levator ani muscles
D) The pressure of the presenting part on the bladder triggers a voiding reflex mistaken for the urge to push
Answer: A
Rationale: "When the presenting part of the fetus reaches the perineal floor, mechanical stretching of the cervix occurs. Stretch receptors in the posterior vagina cause release of endogenous oxytocin that triggers the maternal urge to bear down, or the Ferguson reflex."
Trap: (B) is the intuitive mechanical explanation, and the point of the item is that this reflex is hormonal — stretch leads to oxytocin release, not to a direct motor reflex arc.

Topic: positioning the laboring woman
Subtopic: why position is changed

Q: A woman in the first stage asks why her nurse keeps encouraging her to change positions. Which three benefits does the chapter name?
A) Reduced fatigue, increased comfort, and improved circulation
B) Faster cervical dilation, reduced bleeding, and improved fetal heart rate variability
C) Shorter second stage, reduced perineal trauma, and improved bonding
D) Increased contraction intensity, reduced pain, and faster descent
Answer: A
Rationale: "Position affects the woman's anatomic and physiologic adaptations to labor," and "frequent changes in position reduce fatigue, increase comfort, and improve circulation."

Q: A laboring woman asks which position she should be using. Which response reflects the chapter's guidance for the first stage?

B) She should be encouraged to find the positions that are most comfortable to her
C) She should remain upright until the second stage begins
D) She should alternate positions on a fixed schedule set by the nurse
Answer: B
Rationale: The chapter's conclusion from the benefits of position change is permissive rather than prescriptive: "A laboring woman should be encouraged to find positions that are most comfortable to her."
Trap: (D) is the reflex nursing answer — turn the patient on a schedule — and it converts the chapter's guidance into a protocol it does not state. The frequency of change matters; the specific position is the woman's choice.

Q: A nurse is preparing for a second-stage birth and is asked what determines the woman's position for pushing. Which answer reflects the chapter?

Topic: premonitory signs and the onset of labor
Subtopic: signs preceding labor

Q: A nulliparous woman at 38 weeks reports that she is suddenly breathing more easily but is urinating far more often. Which premonitory change explains both findings, and when does it typically occur?
A) Lightening, occurring about 2 weeks before term as the presenting part descends into the true pelvis
B) Engagement, occurring only after labor is established
C) Cervical ripening, occurring in the last days before labor
D) Bloody show, occurring as the cervix begins to dilate
Answer: A
Rationale: "In first-time pregnancies, the uterus sinks downward and forward about 2 weeks before term, when the presenting part of the fetus (usually the fetal head) descends into the true pelvis." Women then "feel less pressure below the ribcage and breathe more easily," but "more bladder pressure results from this shift. Consequently, a return of urinary frequency occurs."
Trap: (C) names a real premonitory sign that produces no symptoms the woman could report. The two symptoms in the stem are mechanical and point at a change in where the uterus sits.

Q: A multiparous woman at 39 weeks reports that she has felt no dropping at all. How should the nurse interpret this?
A) As an abnormal finding requiring evaluation for a contracted pelvis
B) As expected, because in a multiparous woman lightening may not occur until after uterine contractions are established and true labor is in progress
C) As evidence that the fetus is in a transverse lie
D) As expected only if this is her second pregnancy
Answer: B
Rationale: "In a multiparous woman, lightening may not occur until after uterine contractions are established and true labor is in progress." The same parity pattern governs engagement, and for the same reason — more relaxed abdominal musculature does not direct the presenting part into the pelvis before labor.
Trap: (A) is the mirror error of the previous item, applying the nulliparous two-week rule to a multipara and converting a normal finding into a workup.

Q: A woman at 39 weeks calls the office to report several changes over the past week. Which findings appear in Box 16.1 as signs preceding labor? Select all that apply.
A) Lightening and a return of urinary frequency
B) Backache and stronger Braxton Hicks contractions
C) A weight gain of 1 to 3 pounds
D) A surge of energy
E) Increased vaginal discharge and bloody show
F) Cervical ripening and possible rupture of membranes
Answer: A, B, D, E, F
Rationale: Box 16.1 lists lightening, return of urinary frequency, backache, stronger Braxton Hicks contractions, weight loss of 0.5 to 1.5 kg, a surge of energy, increased vaginal discharge and bloody show, cervical ripening, and possible rupture of membranes.
Trap: (C) reverses the direction of the weight change, and it is the option a Next-Generation case study actually uses. The premonitory change is a weight loss of 0.5 to 1.5 kg, about 1 to 3.5 pounds, "caused by water loss resulting from electrolyte shifts" driven by changing estrogen and progesterone levels.

Q: A woman reports strong, frequent, but irregular contractions that have not changed her cervix. What do these contractions represent, and what separates them from labor?
A) Braxton Hicks contractions; labor at or near term is defined by regular progression of contractions, progressive effacement and dilation, and progressive descent
B) Early first-stage labor; the irregularity is normal in the latent phase
C) Secondary powers beginning prematurely
D) Braxton Hicks contractions; the distinction is that true labor contractions are painful
Answer: A
Rationale: Braxton Hicks contractions are described as "strong, frequent, but irregular" and are listed among the signs preceding labor. Labor at or near term is defined by three findings: "(1) regular progression of uterine contractions, (2) progressive effacement and dilation of the cervix, and (3) progressive descent of the presenting part."
Trap: (D) rests on pain, which is the criterion students reach for and the chapter does not use — women with high spinal cord lesions have normal but painless contractions, and the three defining features say nothing about how labor feels.

Subtopic: what starts labor

Q: Which hormonal changes are associated with the onset of labor?
A) Increased estrogen, oxytocin, and prostaglandins, with decreasing progesterone
B) Increased progesterone, with decreasing estrogen and oxytocin
C) Increased progesterone and prostaglandins, with decreasing oxytocin
D) Decreased estrogen and prostaglandins, with increasing progesterone
Answer: A
Rationale: The chapter states the direction plainly: labor onset "is a result of increased concentrations of estrogen, oxytocin, and prostaglandins and decreasing progesterone levels."
Trap: (B) and (D) both have progesterone rising to start labor, which is backwards in a way worth naming — progesterone maintains the pregnancy, so its fall is what permits labor rather than what opposes it.

Topic: labor stage boundaries and phase patterns
Subtopic: where each stage begins and ends

Q: A nurse is orienting a student to the four stages of labor. Which pairing of stage and boundary is correct?
A) The first stage runs from the onset of regular uterine contractions to full dilation of the cervix
B) The first stage runs from the onset of regular contractions to the birth of the infant
C) The second stage runs from full dilation to the delivery of the placenta
D) The third stage runs from the birth of the infant until the woman is stable
Answer: A
Rationale: The first stage begins "from the onset of regular uterine contractions" and ends "to full dilation of the cervix." The second runs from full dilation to the birth of the infant, the third from the birth of the infant until the placenta is delivered, and the fourth from delivery of the placenta until the woman is stable.
Trap: (C) and (D) each shift one boundary by a single event, which is how these stages are usually missed — the endpoints are four discrete events, and sliding any one of them renames all the stages that follow.

Q: A woman delivered her infant 20 minutes ago and the placenta was delivered 8 minutes after the birth. Which stage of labor is she in now, and why does that stage exist?
A) The third stage, which exists to observe for retained placental fragments
B) The fourth stage, which exists because the woman begins to recover physically from birth and must be observed for complications such as abnormal bleeding
C) The fourth stage, which continues for 24 hours after birth
D) The third stage, which continues until the fundus is firm
Answer: B
Rationale: The fourth stage begins "with the delivery of the placenta" and lasts "until the woman's condition is considered stable in the immediate postpartum period, usually within 1 hour after giving birth." Its purpose: the woman "begins to recover physically from birth, so it is an important time to observe for complications, such as abnormal bleeding."
Trap: (C) is right about the stage and wrong about its length, and the hour is the number worth holding — stems that stretch the fourth stage to a full day are borrowing from postpartum nursing care rather than from this chapter's definition.

Q: How long after the birth of the infant does the placenta normally separate, according to the chapter?
A) With the third or fourth strong uterine contraction after the birth, and it can then be expelled with the next contraction
B) Within 30 minutes, after which it is considered retained
C) Immediately at the birth of the infant, with expulsion following on the first contraction
D) Only after the fundus has been massaged
Answer: A
Rationale: "The placenta normally separates from the uterine wall with the third or fourth strong uterine contraction after the infant has been born. After it has separated, the placenta can be expelled with the next uterine contraction."
Trap: (B) supplies a clock-based rule that this chapter does not give. The chapter counts contractions, not minutes, which is why a third stage that feels long can still be normal.

Subtopic: latent and active phases

Q: A nulliparous woman in the latent phase of the first stage has gone from 50% to 100% effaced over three hours, with station unchanged at minus 1. How should the nurse interpret this?
A) As arrest of descent, since station has not changed
B) As the expected latent-phase pattern, in which there is more progress in effacement and little increase in descent
C) As the expected active-phase pattern, in which descent accelerates
D) As evidence of cephalopelvic disproportion
Answer: B
Rationale: "During the latent phase, there is more progress in effacement of the cervix and little increase in descent. During the active phase, there is more rapid dilation of the cervix and increased rate of descent of the presenting part." This is also the pattern the chapter's own first-stage case study walks through.
Trap: (A) is the reflex reading of an unchanged number and the reason this contrast is drilled. Descent lagging effacement is what the latent phase is supposed to look like.

Q: A woman is fully dilated but reports no urge to push, and the fetus is descending with contractions. How should the nurse interpret this?
A) As the latent phase of the second stage, in which the fetus continues to descend passively and the urge to bear down is not strong, and some women do not experience it at all
B) As an indication to begin coached pushing immediately to prevent a prolonged second stage
C) As evidence that the woman is not yet fully dilated despite the examination
D) As the active pushing phase, since full dilation defines it
Answer: A
Rationale: During the second stage's latent phase, "the fetus continues to descend passively through the birth canal and rotate to an anterior position as a result of ongoing uterine contractions. The urge to bear down during this phase is not strong, and some women do not experience it at all." The active pushing phase begins when the presenting part presses on the stretch receptors of the pelvic floor and the woman has strong urges to bear down.
Trap: (D) is the mirror error and the more consequential one, because it turns a normal passive-descent phase into a reason to start pushing. Full dilation opens the second stage; it does not by itself mark the active pushing phase.

Topic: cardinal movements of labor
Subtopic: the sequence

Q: A nurse is describing the mechanism of labor to a student. In which order do the seven cardinal movements occur?
A) Descent, engagement, flexion, extension, internal rotation, external rotation, expulsion
B) Engagement, descent, flexion, internal rotation, extension, external rotation, expulsion
C) Engagement, flexion, descent, extension, internal rotation, restitution, expulsion
D) Descent, flexion, engagement, internal rotation, external rotation, extension, expulsion
Answer: B
Rationale: The chapter lists them in order: "engagement, descent, flexion, internal rotation, extension, external rotation (restitution), and birth by expulsion." It adds the caveat that "although these movements are discussed separately, in actuality a combination of movements occurs simultaneously. For example, engagement involves both descent and flexion."
Trap: (A) puts descent before engagement, which is the most common reordering because descent sounds like the more basic movement. Engagement is defined by the biparietal diameter passing the inlet, and the chapter lists it first.

Q: As the descending head meets resistance from the cervix, pelvic wall, or pelvic floor, it flexes so that the chin comes closer to the chest. What does this movement accomplish?
A) It permits the smaller suboccipitobregmatic diameter of 9.5 cm to present to the outlet rather than the larger diameters
B) It permits the biparietal diameter to pass the pelvic inlet, which defines engagement
C) It rotates the occiput from a transverse to an anterior position
D) It allows the head to emerge from under the symphysis pubis
Answer: A
Rationale: "As soon as the descending head meets resistance from the cervix, pelvic wall, or pelvic floor, it normally flexes so that the chin is brought into closer contact with the fetal chest. Flexion permits the smaller suboccipitobregmatic diameter (9.5 cm) rather than the larger diameters to present to the outlet."
Trap: (C) and (D) name the two movements that follow flexion — internal rotation and extension — and each is true of its own movement. Flexion changes which diameter presents; it does not turn or deliver the head.

Q: Which forces does the chapter name as producing descent of the presenting part? Select all that apply.
A) Pressure exerted by the amniotic fluid
B) Direct pressure exerted by the contracting fundus on the fetus
C) Force of the contraction of the maternal diaphragm and abdominal muscles in the second stage
D) Extension and straightening of the fetal body
E) Traction applied by the pelvic floor musculature
Answer: A, B, C, D
Rationale: Descent is "the progress of the presenting part through the pelvis," depending on four forces: pressure exerted by the amniotic fluid, direct pressure of the contracting fundus on the fetus, the force of the maternal diaphragm and abdominal muscles in the second stage, and extension and straightening of the fetal body. Descent accelerates in the active phase once the cervix has dilated to 5 to 6 cm and is especially apparent after the membranes have ruptured.
Trap: (E) is the option to leave unselected, and it pulls because the pelvic floor does have a named role — it helps the fetus rotate anteriorly. Rotation, not descent, is its contribution.

Subtopic: the two rotations

Q: Why must internal rotation occur, and where does it begin?
A) Because the inlet is widest transversely and the outlet widest anteroposteriorly; it begins at the level of the ischial spines
B) Because the inlet is widest anteroposteriorly and the outlet widest transversely; it begins at the pelvic inlet
C) Because the fetal shoulders must align with the anteroposterior diameter; it begins after the head is born
D) Because the levator ani muscles contract asymmetrically; it begins at the outlet
Answer: A
Rationale: "The maternal pelvic inlet is widest in the transverse diameter; therefore the fetal head passes the inlet into the true pelvis in the occipitotransverse position. The outlet is widest in the anteroposterior diameter; for the fetus to exit, the head must rotate." Rotation "begins at the level of the ischial spines but is not completed until the presenting part reaches the lower pelvis. As the occiput rotates anteriorly, the face rotates posteriorly."
Trap: (B) swaps the two widest diameters, which reverses the entire rationale for rotation. If the inlet were widest front to back, the head would need no turn at all.

Q: Immediately after the head is born, it turns briefly to the position it occupied when it was engaged in the inlet, and then turns further. What are these two movements, and what is delivered first as they complete?
A) Internal rotation and flexion, with the posterior shoulder delivered first
B) Restitution and external rotation, with the anterior shoulder delivered first from under the pubic arch
C) Restitution and extension, with the head delivered occiput last
D) External rotation and expulsion, with the posterior shoulder guided under the pubic arch
Answer: B
Rationale: "After the head is born, it rotates briefly to the position it occupied when it was engaged in the inlet. This movement is referred to as restitution," and "the 45-degree turn realigns the infant's head with the back and shoulders." Then "external rotation occurs as the shoulders engage and descend.... The anterior shoulder descends first. When it reaches the outlet, it rotates to the midline and is delivered from under the pubic arch. The posterior shoulder is guided over the perineum."
Trap: (D) reverses which shoulder goes where and is the mirror error of this pair. The anterior shoulder is the one that comes under the pubic arch; the posterior shoulder is guided over the perineum.

Q: During extension, in what order do the parts of the fetal head emerge, and what passes under the symphysis pubis first?
A) The chin, then the face, then the occiput, with the chin passing under the symphysis first
B) The occiput, then the face, then the chin, with the occiput passing under the lower border of the symphysis first
C) The occiput, then the chin, then the face, with the face passing under the symphysis first
D) The face and chin together, with the occiput delivered over the perineum first
Answer: B
Rationale: The head "emerges by extension: first the occiput, then the face, and finally the chin," and "the occiput passes under the lower border of the symphysis pubis first."
Trap: (A) inverts the sequence, which is the error that follows from picturing the head as flexed at the moment of birth. Flexion is what got the head through the pelvis; extension is what delivers it, so the occiput leads and the chin comes last.
