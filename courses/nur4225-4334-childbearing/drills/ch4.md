# Chapter 4 drills — Assessment and Health Promotion

Authored from `sources/Ch4_Fact_Bank.md` (Lowdermilk, Cashion, Alden, Olshansky
& Perry, *Maternity & Women's Health Care*, 13th ed.).

Blueprint weight is **6 questions of 200 (3.0%), depth `standard`**, topic
*"Menstruation and Ovarian Function."* That earns **45 questions across 10
topics** — the top of the `standard` band (30–45), and the count the four
already-banked chapters of this course use for a `standard` chapter.

**Scope follows the fact bank, which is already narrowed to the reading guide.**
Chapter 4 is a 32-page omnibus, and the Week 1 midterm reading guide assigns two
islands out of it: *Internal structures* pp. 48–49 and *Menstrual Cycle*
pp. 52–54 with Fig. 4.7. The fact bank curated only those and lists everything
else under its own "What this fact bank does not cover." Since the bank is the
authoring contract and inventing content is forbidden, drilling the whole
chapter was never actually on the table here — the unassigned material (sexual
response, barriers to care, substance use, eating disorders, human trafficking,
Pap technique, the well-woman exam) has no curated facts to draw on. That is a
deliberate scope limit, not an omission.

Sitting at the top of the band for a 6-item chapter is justified by density
rather than by weight: 6 exam questions are drawn from roughly 10 pages of
source, which is the highest sampling rate in Week 1, and the fact bank's trap
notes alone name 20 confusable distinctions.

Topics follow the chapter's own order — the internal structures first, then the
reproductive life span, then the menstrual cycle broken out one cycle at a time.
The cycle is deliberately **not** one topic. The chapter's signature distractor
is the cycle-name swap (offering endometrial phase names where an ovarian phase
is asked, or the reverse), so `endometrial cycle phases` and `ovarian cycle
phases and cycle length variation` are separate dashboard entries: if you invert
them, the red topic should say which cycle you invert.

Every invertible pair the fact bank flags is drilled from both ends — menarche
and puberty, menopause and climacteric, fundus and isthmus, proliferative and
secretory, follicular and luteal, ampulla and infundibulum, basal thermal sign
and mucus sign. A one-directional item lets you pass on a rule you hold
backwards, and this chapter is built out of such rules.

**What the band could not buy.** Cut, in this order: the three named functions
of the vagina and the five named functions of the uterus (list recall that
decides nothing at the bedside); the four pairs of uterine ligaments by name;
the numeric dimensions of the ovary and the 8-to-14 cm tube length; the "few
sensory nerve endings" note on the lower vagina; the prostaglandin naming
notation (letters, numbers, and Greek suffixes) and the cross-references to
dysmenorrhea in Ch 6 and preeclampsia in Ch 27, both of which are drilled where
they belong. Kept without exception: every confusable pair in the fact bank's
trap notes, the phase-alignment table, and the ovulation-timing rules, because
those are what the six blueprint questions will be built from.

**A citation note for whoever edits this next.** The fact bank's section 3
("The menstrual cycle — the numbers") still carries **PDF-era page numbers**
(pp. 168–172) that survived the 2026-09-02 re-citation to printed pages; they
fall outside the chapter's printed range of pp. 47–78. Facts taken from that
table are cited here by section rather than by page. Every other page cite below
is a printed page and is quoted from the re-cited bank.

Topic: vagina and cervix structure
Subtopic: vaginal wall and environment

Q: A patient recovering from a vaginal infection asks the nurse why the vagina does not become infected more often, given what it is exposed to. Which property of normal vaginal secretions should the nurse describe?
A) They are slightly acidic, at a pH of 4 to 5, which limits susceptibility to infection
B) They are slightly alkaline, at a pH of 8 to 9, which neutralizes invading organisms
C) They are neutral, at a pH of 7, which prevents irritation of the mucosa
D) They are strongly acidic, at a pH of 2 to 3, which sterilizes the vaginal canal
Answer: A
Rationale: Vaginal secretions are "slightly acidic (pH 4 to 5) so that vaginal susceptibility to infections is limited" (p. 48). The acidity is a defense, and it operates by limiting susceptibility rather than by sterilizing anything.
Trap: (B) inverts the source outright, and it is the intuitive inversion because students associate antiseptics with alkalinity. (D) overshoots a true direction into a false magnitude, which is the harder distractor: the pH is acidic, but 2 to 3 is gastric, not vaginal.

Q: A 58-year-old patient reports vaginal dryness and discomfort. On examination the nurse practitioner notes that the vaginal walls appear thin and smooth rather than folded. Which explanation fits this finding?
A) Estrogen deprivation has caused thinning of the walls and smoothing of the rugae
B) Repeated childbirth has permanently stretched the rugae beyond their elastic limit
C) The finding is abnormal at any age and requires biopsy of the vaginal wall
D) Progesterone withdrawal has caused the transverse mucosal folds to fuse
Answer: A
Rationale: During the reproductive years the vaginal mucosa is arranged in transverse folds called rugae. Estrogen deprivation "that occurs after birth, during lactation, and at menopause causes dryness and thinning of the vaginal walls and smoothing of the rugae" (p. 48).
Trap: (D) is the reflex pick for anyone who remembers that a hormone withdrawal is responsible but not which one — the source names estrogen deprivation specifically, and names three settings for it, only one of which is menopause.

Q: During a bimanual examination the examiner palpates the internal pelvic organs through the thin-walled pockets that surround the cervix. Which structures make this possible?
A) The fornices, anterior, posterior, and lateral pockets surrounding the cervix
B) The rugae, transverse mucosal folds along the length of the vaginal canal
C) The cul-de-sac of Douglas, a single recess anterior to the cervix
D) The transformation zone, where squamous and columnar epithelium meet
Answer: A
Rationale: "Anterior, posterior, and lateral pockets called fornices (singular: fornix) surround the cervix. The internal pelvic organs can be palpated through the thin walls of these fornices" (p. 48).
Trap: (C) names a real structure and gets its relationship to the cervix backwards — the cul-de-sac of Douglas is a deep pouch posterior to the cervix, formed by the posterior ligament, not an anterior one, and it is one recess rather than the set of pockets used for palpation.

Subtopic: cervical epithelium and the transformation zone

Q: A nursing student palpating a nonpregnant cervix during a supervised examination asks the preceptor what a normal cervix should feel like, and how it can possibly stretch during birth. Which response is accurate?
A) It feels firm, like the end of a nose, and it is mostly fibrous connective and elastic tissue, which is what allows it to stretch
B) It feels soft, like the lips, and it is mostly smooth muscle, which contracts to open during birth
C) It feels firm, like the end of a nose, because it is mostly smooth muscle in a figure-eight arrangement
D) It feels boggy and indistinct, and it is mostly glandular tissue that liquefies at term
Answer: A
Rationale: The cervix "feels firm (like the end of a nose) with a dimple in the center that marks the external os," and it is "made up of mostly fibrous connective tissue and elastic tissue, making it possible for the cervix to stretch during vaginal birth" (p. 49).
Trap: (C) pairs the correct texture with the wrong tissue, which is why it is the most attractive distractor — the figure-eight muscle arrangement is real but belongs to the middle myometrial layer of the uterine corpus, not to the cervix.

Q: A patient asks why the Papanicolaou test requires scraping cells from one specific spot just inside the cervical os rather than sampling the cervix broadly. Which rationale should the nurse give?
A) That spot is the squamocolumnar junction, the most common site for neoplastic changes
B) That spot is the only part of the cervix that can be reached with a spatula
C) That spot is where cervical mucus is produced, so it yields the most cells
D) That spot is the internal os, which marks the boundary of the uterine cavity
Answer: A
Rationale: Where squamous and columnar epithelium meet "is usually located just inside the cervical os. This junction is also called the transformation zone and is the most common site for neoplastic changes. Cells from this site are scraped for the Papanicolaou (Pap) test" (p. 49).
Trap: (B) is the reflex answer of convenience, and the fact bank flags it by name — a stem asking why the Pap samples that specific spot is answered by the neoplasia sentence, not by ease of access. (D) confuses the two openings: the internal os connects the uterine cavity to the endocervical canal, while the transformation zone sits at the external end.

Topic: uterine structure and wall layers
Subtopic: uterine divisions

Q: A nurse reviewing pelvic anatomy is asked to name the dome-shaped top of the uterus, the region where the uterine tubes enter. Which division does this describe?
A) The fundus
B) The isthmus
C) The corpus
D) The cervix
Answer: A
Rationale: The fundus is the "dome-shaped top of the uterus" and is the "site at which the uterine tubes (fallopian tubes) enter" the uterus (p. 48).
Trap: (C) is the near miss rather than a wrong region — the corpus is the upper triangular portion as a whole, and the fundus is its dome-shaped top. A stem that specifies where the tubes enter is asking for the more precise of the two.

Q: The same nurse is then asked which uterine division is the short constricted portion separating the corpus from the cervix, known in obstetrics as the lower uterine segment. Which division does this describe?
A) The isthmus
B) The fundus
C) The internal os
D) The ampulla
Answer: A
Rationale: "The isthmus, or lower uterine segment, is a short constricted portion that separates the corpus from the cervix" (p. 48).
Trap: (B) is the mirror error of the previous item, swapping the top of the uterus for the constriction at its base. (D) is the trap the fact bank warns about directly: isthmus is used twice in this chapter, once for the uterus and once for a section of the uterine tube, so read which organ the stem names before matching a name to a location.

Subtopic: myometrial layers

Q: Immediately after birth the myometrium contracts and bleeding from the placental site slows sharply, even though large vessels were torn open. Which myometrial layer accounts for this?
A) The middle layer, whose fibers run in all three directions in a figure-eight pattern encircling large blood vessels
B) The outer layer, whose longitudinal fibers are found mostly in the fundus
C) The inner layer, whose fibers encircle the internal cervical os
D) The endometrial basal layer, which regenerates the lining after separation
Answer: A
Rationale: The middle myometrial layer is made of "fibers from all three directions, which form a figure-eight pattern encircling large blood vessels," and "these fibers assist in ligat[ing]" those vessels (pp. 48–49). It is the living ligature that controls bleeding at the placental site.
Trap: (B) is the reflex pick because the outer longitudinal layer is the one associated with the fundus, and the fundus is where the nurse assesses tone after birth. Assessing there does not make it the layer that ligates — the outer layer assists in expelling the fetus.

Q: A nurse is explaining why menstrual blood does not normally flow backward into the uterine tubes, and why the cervix stays closed through pregnancy. Which myometrial layer performs both functions?
A) The inner layer, whose fibers surround where the tubes enter and encircle the internal cervical os
B) The middle layer, whose figure-eight fibers encircle the large blood vessels
C) The outer layer, whose longitudinal fibers are concentrated in the fundus
D) The peritoneal layer, which covers the uterus externally
Answer: A
Rationale: The inner myometrial fibers, arranged around where the tubes enter and around the internal cervical os, "help keep the cervix closed during pregnancy" and "prevent menstrual blood from flowing back into the uterine tubes during menstruation" (pp. 48–49).
Trap: (C) is the mirror error of the previous item's trap — the outer longitudinal layer expels the fetus, which is the opposite of keeping the cervix closed. All three layers are plausible only until you match each to its stated function.

Subtopic: endometrial layers

Q: A patient asks how the uterine lining can be shed every month and still be there the next month. Select all that are true of the endometrium during and after menstruation.
A) The endometrium is made up of three layers
B) The functional two-thirds, the compact and spongy layers, is what is shed
C) The basal layer is always retained
D) Regeneration of the lining begins near the end of the cycle
E) The entire endometrium is shed and is rebuilt from the myometrium
Answer: A, B, C, D
Rationale: The endometrium is a "highly vascular lining made up of three layers, the outer two of which are shed during menstruation" (p. 48). During the menstrual phase, shedding of "the functional two-thirds of the endometrium (the compact and spongy layers)" occurs, while "the basal layer is always retained, and regeneration begins near the end of the cycle" (p. 53).
Trap: (E) is the single wrong option and it is the one the fact bank flags — a distractor saying the entire endometrium sheds is wrong, and it is attractive because "shedding the lining" is how the process gets described in plain language. Nothing regenerates from the myometrium; the retained basal layer is the source.

Topic: uterine tubes and ovaries

Q: A patient with a history of tubal surgery asks where fertilization normally takes place. Which section of the uterine tube should the nurse name?
A) The ampulla, one of the middle portions of the tube
B) The infundibulum, the section closest to the ovary
C) The interstitial portion, the section closest to the uterus
D) The isthmus, the section immediately distal to the interstitial portion
Answer: A
Rationale: "The ovum is usually fertilized by the sperm in the ampulla portion of one of the tubes" (p. 49). The four sections run, uterus to ovary: interstitial, isthmus, ampulla, infundibulum.
Trap: (B) is the reflex pick because the infundibulum is the end that captures the ovum, so it feels like the place where sperm and ovum would first meet. Capture and fertilization happen at different points along the tube, and the order of the four sections is what this item is testing.

Q: The same patient asks what actually draws the released ovum into the tube in the first place. Which structure and section should the nurse name?
A) The fimbriae, the fringed ends of the infundibulum
B) The fimbriae, the fringed ends of the ampulla
C) The cilia of the interstitial portion, which sweep the ovum inward
D) The rhythmic contractions of the isthmus, which create suction at the ovary
Answer: A
Rationale: "The infundibulum has fimbriated (fringed) ends, which pull the ovum into the tube" (p. 49). Once inside, the ovum is moved "by rhythmic contractions of muscles of the tubes and by the current produced by the movement of the cilia that line the tubes."
Trap: (B) is the mirror error of the previous item, attaching the right structure to the wrong section. (C) and (D) name the two real transport mechanisms but assign them the job of capture, which belongs to the fimbriae alone.

Q: On pelvic examination of a healthy 24-year-old, the ovaries are described as nodular rather than smooth. How should the nurse interpret this?
A) It is an expected finding after menarche, caused by repeated rupture of follicles at ovulation
B) It is an abnormal finding that suggests polycystic change and warrants imaging
C) It is an expected finding before menarche and should resolve with regular cycles
D) It is an expected finding only after menopause, when the ovaries diminish in size
Answer: A
Rationale: "Before menarche, each ovary has a smooth surface; after menarche, they are nodular because of repeated ruptures of follicles at ovulation" (p. 49).
Trap: (B) is the reflex pick because "nodular" reads as pathology everywhere else in nursing assessment, and the fact bank flags exactly that: do not read nodular as pathology in this context. (C) inverts the marker — smooth is the pre-menarcheal state, not the post-menarcheal one.

Q: A nursing student is sorting reproductive hormones by the organ that secretes them. Select all the hormones that the ovaries themselves produce.
A) Estrogen
B) Progesterone
C) Androgen
D) Follicle-stimulating hormone
E) Gonadotropin-releasing hormone
Answer: A, B, C
Rationale: The ovaries have two functions, "ovulation and hormone production," and the hormones they produce are "estrogen, progesterone, and androgen" (p. 49). Follicle-stimulating hormone is an anterior pituitary product and gonadotropin-releasing hormone comes from the hypothalamus (p. 53).
Trap: (D) is the strongest wrong option because follicle-stimulating hormone is named for the ovarian structure it acts on, which invites the reader to file it as ovarian. The ovary is its target tissue, not its source. Androgen is the keyed option most often dropped, since it is easy to think of the ovary as producing only the two hormones named in every cycle diagram.

Topic: reproductive life span transitions
Subtopic: menarche and puberty

Q: A mother tells the school nurse that her daughter "reached puberty last month, when she got her first period." Which correction should the nurse offer?
A) The first menstrual period is menarche; puberty is the entire transitional stage between childhood and sexual maturity
B) The first menstrual period is puberty; menarche is the point at which ovulatory cycles begin
C) The two terms are interchangeable, and either is correct for a first period
D) The first menstrual period is the climacteric; puberty refers only to breast development
Answer: A
Rationale: "The term menarche denotes first menstruation," while puberty is "a broader term that denotes the entire transitional stage between childhood and sexual maturity" (p. 51).
Trap: (B) is the classic swap the fact bank names — a distractor that defines puberty as the first menstrual period. The two words are not a wide term and a narrow term for the same event; one is a single event inside the other.

Q: Which term names the entire transitional stage between childhood and sexual maturity, of which the first menstrual period is only one event?
A) Puberty
B) Menarche
C) Perimenopause
D) The follicular phase
Answer: A
Rationale: Puberty "denotes the entire transitional stage between childhood and sexual maturity"; menarche "denotes first menstruation" and is a single event within it (p. 51).
Trap: (B) is the mirror error of the previous item. Working the same distinction from the other end is the point: a learner who holds these two backwards will answer one of the pair correctly by luck and the other wrongly, and only the pair reveals it.

Q: A 12-year-old whose periods began four months ago tells the nurse that her cycles have been irregular and unpredictable, and asks whether that means she cannot get pregnant yet. Which response is accurate?
A) Early cycles are commonly irregular and anovulatory, but pregnancy can occur at any time after the onset of menses
B) Irregular cycles in the first year confirm that ovulation is not occurring, so pregnancy is not possible yet
C) Cycles are regular from menarche onward, so irregularity should be evaluated
D) Ovulatory cycles begin only after age 16, so contraception is unnecessary before then
Answer: A
Rationale: "Initially, menstrual periods are irregular and unpredictable and anovulatory (no ovum is released from the ovary)," and after "1 or more years" a hypothalamic-pituitary rhythm develops. The chapter states the teaching point outright: "All young adolescents of both sexes would benefit from knowing that pregnancy can occur at any time after the onset of menses" (p. 51).
Trap: (B) is the dangerous distractor, and it is dangerous precisely because its first half is true. Early cycles are commonly anovulatory; the source still says pregnancy can occur at any time after menses begin, so "commonly anovulatory" can never be converted into "cannot conceive."

Subtopic: perimenopause and menopause

Q: A 49-year-old reports that she has had no menstrual period for the past six months and asks whether she has gone through menopause. Which response is accurate?
A) Menopause can be dated with certainty only 1 year after menstruation ceases, so she is currently perimenopausal
B) Six months without a period confirms menopause, since the average age is 51.4 years
C) Menopause is confirmed by a single hormone level rather than by elapsed time
D) At 49 she is too young for menopause, since the age range begins at 51
Answer: A
Rationale: Menopause "refers only to the last menstrual period," and "in contrast to menarche, menopause can be dated with certainty only 1 year after menstruation ceases" (p. 54). Perimenopause is the roughly 4-year period preceding menopause, during which ovarian function declines.
Trap: (D) misreads 51.4 years as a lower bound when it is the average; the stated range is 35 to 60 years, so 49 is unremarkable. The deeper point is that menopause cannot be diagnosed prospectively at all — it is named in retrospect, which is what separates it from menarche.

Q: A patient asks what to call the whole transitional phase during which ovarian function and hormone production decline, spanning from the onset of premenopausal decline to the time when symptoms stop. Which term names it?
A) The climacteric
B) Menopause
C) Menarche
D) The luteal phase
Answer: A
Rationale: The climacteric is "a transitional phase during which ovarian function and hormone production decline. This phase spans the years from the onset of premenopausal ovarian decline to the postmenopausal time when symptoms stop" (p. 54), whereas menopause "refers only to the last menstrual period."
Trap: (B) is the mirror error of the previous item and completes the analogy the fact bank draws: menarche is to puberty as menopause is to climacteric. In both pairs, the everyday word names a single event and the technical word names the transition containing it.

Q: Three patients report irregular, unpredictable bleeding: a 13-year-old whose periods began eight months ago, a 28-year-old with previously regular cycles, and a 48-year-old whose cycles have been shortening. Which patient's finding most warrants evaluation rather than reassurance?
A) The 28-year-old, because anovulatory irregular bleeding is expected only at the two ends of the reproductive years
B) The 13-year-old, because bleeding should be regular within six months of menarche
C) The 48-year-old, because irregular bleeding is never expected before menopause is confirmed
D) All three, because irregular bleeding is abnormal at any age
Answer: A
Rationale: Irregular, unpredictable, anovulatory cycles are normal just after menarche, where periods are "irregular and unpredictable and anovulatory" (p. 51), and again in perimenopause, where "menstrual cycles may be anovulatory, resulting in irregular bleeding" (p. 54). The same finding in a 28-year-old falls outside both windows.
Trap: This is the fact bank's "anovulatory bleeding bookends the reproductive years" note turned into a discrimination. Age is the discriminating cue, not the bleeding pattern, which is identical in all three patients. (D) is the reflex pick for a reader who treats irregular bleeding as a finding rather than as a finding in a life stage.

Topic: menstrual cycle norms and flow characteristics

Q: A patient is starting a cycle diary and asks the nurse which day counts as day 1. Which instruction is correct?
A) The first day of bleeding is day 1 of the cycle
B) The day bleeding stops is day 1 of the cycle
C) The day of ovulation is day 1 of the cycle
D) The first day after bleeding stops is day 1 of the cycle
Answer: A
Rationale: The menstrual cycle overview defines day 1 as "the first day of bleeding," with an average cycle length of "28 days, but variations are normal" (pp. 51–53).
Trap: (C) is the reflex pick for a reader who has just learned that ovulation is the pivotal event of the cycle and assumes the numbering must start there. Ovulation is a midcycle event that is located by counting, so it cannot also be the origin of the count.

Q: A patient reports that her periods last 4 days. Where does this fall relative to the norms the chapter gives for flow duration and blood loss?
A) Within the normal range, since average duration is 5 days with a range of 3 to 6 days
B) Below the normal range, since duration under 5 days indicates scant flow
C) Within the normal range only if total blood loss also exceeds 80 mL
D) Above the normal range, since average duration is 3 days
Answer: A
Rationale: The chapter's menstrual cycle overview gives average flow duration as "5 days (range of 3 to 6 days)" and average blood loss as "50 mL (range of 20 to 80 mL)" (pp. 51–53).
Trap: (C) inverts the direction of the blood-loss range: 80 mL is the upper limit of normal, not a threshold a normal period must exceed. A distractor that turns a ceiling into a floor is the reliable way to catch a half-remembered number.

Q: A patient is alarmed that her menstrual blood does not seem to clot the way blood from a cut does, and asks whether something is wrong with her clotting. Which response is accurate?
A) Menstrual blood that does not appear to clot is reported in about 50% of women and is an expected finding
B) Absent clotting in menstrual blood indicates a coagulation disorder and requires testing
C) Menstrual blood never clots in any woman, because it is not true blood
D) Absent clotting suggests blood loss is above 80 mL and warrants evaluation
Answer: A
Rationale: The chapter's flow characteristics note that menstrual blood does not appear to clot in about 50% of women (pp. 51–53), which makes it a common normal variant rather than a sign of a clotting problem.
Trap: (C) overcorrects a true reassurance into a false absolute. Roughly half of women do report clots, so a patient who sees them is equally normal, and a nurse who says menstrual blood never clots will have to unsay it.

Q: Menstruation is described as beginning approximately 14 days after ovulation and as being controlled by a feedback system of three simultaneous cycles. Which three cycles are these?
A) Endometrial, hypothalamic-pituitary, and ovarian
B) Endometrial, cervical, and ovarian
C) Follicular, luteal, and ischemic
D) Proliferative, secretory, and menstrual
Answer: A
Rationale: Menstruation is "periodic uterine bleeding that begins approximately 14 days after ovulation. It is controlled by a feedback system of three cycles: endometrial, hypothalamic-pituitary, and ovarian" (p. 52).
Trap: (C) and (D) are the chapter's signature move in miniature — both list phases rather than cycles, and (C) makes it harder by mixing phases from two different cycles. A stem asking for cycles is not asking for phase names, and the three cycles are named for the tissues that run them.

Topic: endometrial cycle phases

Q: A student is asked to place the four phases of the endometrial cycle in the order the chapter states them, beginning with day 1. Which sequence is correct?
A) Menstrual, proliferative, secretory, ischemic
B) Proliferative, menstrual, secretory, ischemic
C) Menstrual, secretory, proliferative, ischemic
D) Follicular, proliferative, luteal, secretory
Answer: A
Rationale: The endometrial cycle has four phases in the stated order: "(1) the menstrual phase, (2) the proliferative phase, (3) the secretory phase, and (4) the ischemic phase" (p. 53).
Trap: (D) is the cycle-name swap the fact bank calls the chapter's signature distractor, interleaving ovarian phase names with endometrial ones. It is attractive because every name in it is real and in the right general order; none of the endometrial phases is called follicular or luteal.

Q: An endometrial biopsy shows rapid growth with the surface restored and the lining thickened eight- to tenfold, and growth appears to be leveling off. Which phase does this describe, and which hormone drives it?
A) The proliferative phase, driven by estrogen from the ovarian follicles
B) The secretory phase, driven by progesterone from the corpus luteum
C) The ischemic phase, driven by falling estrogen and progesterone
D) The menstrual phase, driven by withdrawal of both hormones
Answer: A
Rationale: The proliferative phase runs "from about the fifth day to the time of ovulation." The endometrial surface is restored in about 4 days, then thickens "8- to 10-fold, with a leveling off of growth at ovulation," and the phase "depends on estrogen stimulation derived from ovarian follicles" (p. 53).
Trap: (B) is the pick for anyone who reads "growth" as the whole story and forgets which hormone builds versus which hormone enriches. The fact bank's rule settles it: if the stem names progesterone or the period after ovulation, it cannot be the proliferative phase — and here the leveling off at ovulation says the phase is ending, not beginning.

Q: An endometrial specimen taken 8 days after ovulation is described as edematous and vascular, with the texture of heavy, soft velvet and luxuriant with blood and glandular secretions. Select all that correctly characterize this phase.
A) It is the secretory phase
B) It extends from the day of ovulation to about 3 days before the next menstrual period
C) Large amounts of progesterone are produced during it
D) It creates a suitable protective and nutritive bed for a fertilized ovum
E) It depends on estrogen stimulation derived from the ovarian follicles
Answer: A, B, C, D
Rationale: The secretory phase "extends from the day of ovulation to about 3 days before the next menstrual period." After ovulation "large amounts of progesterone are produced. An edematous, vascular, functional endometrium is now apparent," and at its end the endometrium reaches "the thickness of heavy, soft velvet ... luxuriant with blood and glandular secretions, creating a suitable protective and nutritive bed for a fertilized ovum" (p. 53).
Trap: (E) is the single wrong option and it belongs to the wrong side of the same pair — estrogen from the ovarian follicles drives the proliferative phase. It is the option most likely to be checked, because estrogen is present throughout the cycle and the sentence is quoted verbatim from the chapter, just about the other phase.

Q: Two days before an expected period, the spiral arteries go into spasm, blood supply to the functional endometrium is blocked, and necrosis develops. Which phase is this, and what triggers it?
A) The ischemic phase, triggered by the rapid decrease in progesterone and estrogen as the corpus luteum regresses
B) The menstrual phase, triggered by the surge of luteinizing hormone
C) The ischemic phase, triggered by rising progesterone from a maturing corpus luteum
D) The secretory phase, triggered by withdrawal of estrogen alone
Answer: A
Rationale: In the ischemic phase the corpus luteum regresses, and "with the rapid decrease in progesterone and estrogen levels, the spiral arteries go into spasm." The blood supply "is blocked and necrosis develops," the functional layer separates from the basal layer, and bleeding begins, "marking day 1 of the next cycle" (p. 53).
Trap: (C) keeps the right phase and reverses its trigger, which is the harder error to catch because naming the phase feels like the whole question. Ischemia here is a withdrawal event: both hormones fall, and it is the fall that causes the spasm.

Q: A patient asks what is actually happening in the uterus on the first two days of her period. Which description is accurate?
A) The functional two-thirds of the endometrium is shed after periodic vasoconstriction in the upper layers, while the basal layer is retained
B) The full thickness of the endometrium separates, and regeneration begins only after bleeding stops
C) The basal layer is shed first, and the compact and spongy layers follow
D) The endometrium is digested by glandular secretions released at the end of the secretory phase
Answer: A
Rationale: In the menstrual phase, "shedding of the functional two-thirds of the endometrium (the compact and spongy layers) is initiated by periodic vasoconstriction in the upper layers," and "the basal layer is always retained, and regeneration begins near the end of the cycle" (p. 53).
Trap: (C) inverts which layer goes and which stays, and it is the inversion that matters clinically: the retained basal layer is what makes the next cycle possible. (B) gets the timing of regeneration wrong as well, since regeneration begins near the end of the cycle rather than after bleeding has finished.

Topic: ovarian cycle phases and cycle length variation

Q: A patient's cycles consistently run 35 days rather than 28. Which phase accounts for the extra week?
A) The follicular phase, since almost all variation in ovarian cycle length comes from variation in its length
B) The luteal phase, since it lengthens to accommodate a longer cycle
C) Both phases lengthen proportionally, so each contributes about half
D) Neither phase; the extra days are added to the menstrual phase
Answer: A
Rationale: The follicular, or preovulatory, phase "varies in length from woman to woman. Almost all variations in ovarian cycle length are the result of variations in the length of the follicular phase" (p. 53). The luteal phase "usually requires 14 days (range of 13 to 15 days)."
Trap: (B) is the reflex pick and the fact bank says a distractor lengthening the luteal phase is always wrong. The luteal phase is the fixed one: a 35-day cycle is a 21-day follicular phase plus a 14-day luteal phase.

Q: A pathology report describes a secretory endometrium. Select all that are true of the ovarian phase occurring at the same time.
A) It is the luteal phase
B) It begins immediately after ovulation
C) It ends with the start of menstruation
D) The corpus luteum reaches its peak of functional activity 8 days into it
E) Its length accounts for almost all variation in ovarian cycle length
Answer: A, B, C, D
Rationale: The luteal, or postovulatory, phase "begins immediately after ovulation and ends with the start of menstruation," usually requiring 14 days with a range of 13 to 15. "The corpus luteum reaches its peak of functional activity 8 days after ovulation, secreting the steroids estrogen and progesterone" (p. 53). It runs concurrently with the secretory endometrium.
Trap: (E) is the single wrong option and it is the follicular phase's defining property attached to the luteal phase. The item is also the cycle-name swap in its usual form: asked which ovarian phase corresponds to a secretory endometrium, the answer is luteal, never "secretory," because secretory is not an ovarian phase name at all.

Q: A patient with a regular 35-day cycle asks when she ovulates. Which calculation should the nurse use?
A) About day 21, counting back 14 days from the expected next period
B) About day 17, counting forward 14 days plus the extra 3 days of cycle length
C) About day 14, since ovulation occurs on day 14 regardless of cycle length
D) About day 28, since ovulation occurs 7 days before the next period
Answer: A
Rationale: Menstruation "begins approximately 14 days after ovulation" (p. 52), and the luteal phase is the fixed 14-day interval, so ovulation is located by counting back from the next expected period rather than forward from the last one. In a 35-day cycle that places ovulation at about day 21.
Trap: (C) is the near-universal reflex, because day 14 is true only of the 28-day cycle used to illustrate the diagram. (B) is the subtler error: counting forward from day 1 is the wrong direction entirely, and it happens to give day 17 here, which is close enough to look defensible.

Q: Fourteen days after ovulation, with no fertilization, a patient's progesterone and estrogen levels fall and her period begins. What has happened to the structure that had been producing those hormones?
A) The corpus luteum has regressed, having peaked in function 8 days after ovulation
B) The corpus luteum has been shed with the functional endometrium
C) The graafian follicle has failed to rupture, trapping the ovum
D) The corpus luteum has become the corpus albicans immediately at ovulation
Answer: A
Rationale: "The corpus luteum reaches its peak of functional activity 8 days after ovulation, secreting the steroids estrogen and progesterone." If no implantation occurs, "the corpus luteum regresses and steroid levels decrease. Two weeks after ovulation, if fertilization and implantation do not occur, the functional layer of the uterine endometrium is shed through menstruation" (p. 53).
Trap: (B) confuses the two things that end at the same time — the corpus luteum is an ovarian structure and is not part of what the uterus sheds. (C) describes the prostaglandin-deficit picture from later in the chapter, in which the ovum stays trapped in the follicle, which means no corpus luteum forms at all.

Topic: hypothalamic pituitary ovarian feedback

Q: At the very end of a cycle, blood levels of estrogen and progesterone are low. According to the chapter, what does this low level trigger?
A) It stimulates the hypothalamus to secrete gonadotropin-releasing hormone, which stimulates anterior pituitary secretion of follicle-stimulating hormone
B) It suppresses the hypothalamus, allowing the anterior pituitary to release luteinizing hormone directly
C) It stimulates the anterior pituitary to secrete gonadotropin-releasing hormone, which acts on the ovary
D) It stimulates the ovary to secrete follicle-stimulating hormone, which matures the next follicle
Answer: A
Rationale: "Low blood levels of these ovarian hormones stimulate the hypothalamus to secrete gonadotropin-releasing hormone (GnRH). In turn, GnRH stimulates anterior pituitary secretion of FSH. FSH stimulates development of ovarian graafian follicles and their production of estrogen" (p. 53).
Trap: (B) is the reflex pick for a reader who assumes every falling hormone level must be inhibitory. This loop runs the other way: it is the fall in ovarian hormones that releases the hypothalamus to start the next cycle. (C) puts the right hormone in the wrong gland, which is the commonest structural error in this loop.

Q: A patient using a urine luteinizing hormone predictor kit gets her first positive result this morning and asks what it means about the timing of ovulation. Which response is accurate?
A) The surge precedes expulsion of the ovum by about 24 to 36 hours, so ovulation has not yet occurred
B) The surge occurs at the moment of ovulation, so the ovum is being released now
C) The surge follows ovulation by about 24 hours, so ovulation has already happened
D) The surge occurs about 5 days before ovulation, so intercourse can be deferred
Answer: A
Rationale: A marked surge of luteinizing hormone, together with a smaller peak of estrogen on day 12, precedes "the expulsion of the ovum from the graafian follicle by about 24 to 36 hours. LH peaks at about day 13 or 14 of a 28-day cycle" (p. 53).
Trap: (B) is the reflex answer and the fact bank flags it: a stem saying ovulation occurs at the moment of the luteinizing hormone peak is wrong by about a day. The surge and the release are close enough to feel simultaneous, which is exactly why the interval is asked.

Q: Follicle-stimulating hormone and luteinizing hormone are released by the anterior pituitary. What is their target tissue, and what does that tissue then do?
A) The ovary, which produces ova and secretes estrogen and progesterone
B) The endometrium, which thickens and then sheds
C) The hypothalamus, which then secretes gonadotropin-releasing hormone
D) The corpus luteum only, which forms after ovulation
Answer: A
Rationale: "The target tissue for these hormones is the ovary, which produces ova and secretes estrogen and progesterone" (p. 53).
Trap: (B) names the tissue where the visible result of the cycle appears, which makes it feel like the target. The endometrium responds to the ovarian steroids, not to the pituitary gonadotropins, so it sits one step further down the chain.

Q: A patient being evaluated for infertility asks how many eggs she "uses up" in a cycle in which she conceives no pregnancy. Which statement reflects the chapter's account of follicle selection?
A) From 1 to 30 follicles begin to mature in each ovary, and the preovulatory luteinizing hormone surge affects a selected follicle
B) Exactly one follicle begins to mature each cycle, and it is the one that ovulates
C) All follicles that begin to mature complete ovulation, which is why multiple births are common
D) More than one follicle is selected in about 1 in 10 menstrual cycles
Answer: A
Rationale: "Before ovulation, from 1 to 30 follicles begin to mature in each ovary under the influence of FSH and estrogen. The preovulatory surge of LH affects a selected follicle" (p. 53). More than one follicle is selected in about 1 in 100 menstrual cycles.
Trap: (D) keeps the right idea and moves the decimal, which is the version most likely to survive a quick read — the source says 1 in 100, not 1 in 10. (B) is the mental picture the standard cycle diagram creates, in which a single follicle is drawn maturing alone.

Topic: ovulation signs and fertility awareness

Q: A patient charting her basal body temperature reports that it rose two days ago and has stayed up. What does this tell her?
A) Ovulation has already occurred; the rise follows ovulation as progesterone levels increase
B) Ovulation is about to occur within 24 hours; the rise is the best predictor of the fertile window
C) Ovulation has been suppressed this cycle; a sustained rise indicates an anovulatory cycle
D) Ovulation occurs at the moment of the lowest temperature, which was three days ago
Answer: A
Rationale: "Before ovulation a woman's basal body temperature is often less than 37°C (98.6°F); after ovulation, with increasing progesterone levels, her basal body temperature rises" (p. 53). The temperature sign is therefore retrospective — it confirms that ovulation happened.
Trap: (B) is the reflex pick, because a rise reads as a warning that something is coming. Progesterone is what raises the temperature, and progesterone rises only after the corpus luteum forms, so the sign cannot arrive before the event it reports.

Q: A patient trying to conceive asks which sign tells her that ovulation is about to occur, so that she can time intercourse. Which sign should the nurse teach?
A) Cervical mucus that is thin and clear and stretches like egg white, a quality termed spinnbarkeit
B) A sustained rise in basal body temperature above her usual baseline
C) Cervical mucus that is thick and viscous, which indicates peak fertility
D) The cessation of all cervical mucus, which marks the fertile window
Answer: A
Rationale: "At the time of ovulation, cervical mucus is thin and clear. It looks, feels, and stretches like egg white," and this stretchable quality is spinnbarkeit (p. 53). "Preovulatory and postovulatory mucus is viscous (thick) so that sperm penetration is discouraged."
Trap: (B) is the mirror error of the previous item: the temperature sign confirms ovulation after the fact and is the wrong tool for timing intercourse, while the mucus sign is the prospective one. (C) inverts the source in the way the fact bank flags — thick mucus is the sperm-hostile state, not the fertile one.

Q: A 22-year-old reports one-sided lower abdominal pain that occurs around the middle of each cycle and lasts a few hours, sometimes with light spotting. She is afebrile, with a soft abdomen and no rebound tenderness. What is the nurse's priority action?
A) Reassure her that this is mittelschmerz, a normal ovulatory finding, and provide education
B) Arrange urgent evaluation for appendicitis, since the pain is one-sided and lower
C) Advise her that midcycle pain always indicates an ovarian cyst requiring imaging
D) Instruct her to stop tracking her cycles, since the pain makes the data unreliable
Answer: A
Rationale: Mittelschmerz is localized lower abdominal pain that "coincides with ovulation," and "some spotting may occur" (p. 53). With no fever, rebound tenderness, or other red flags, the default is reassurance and education.
Trap: (B) is the reflex escalation, and the fact bank draws the boundary explicitly: only a scenario adding fever, rebound tenderness, or a positive pregnancy test overrides the default of reassurance. This stem deliberately supplies the negatives, so escalating means treating a normal cyclic finding as an acute abdomen.

Q: A patient asks the nurse to help her separate normal midcycle events from findings that need evaluation. Select all the midcycle findings the chapter describes as expected.
A) Localized lower abdominal pain coinciding with ovulation
B) Thin, clear, stretchy cervical mucus
C) A small amount of visible bleeding, reported by about 10% of women
D) A basal body temperature that rises after ovulation
E) Thick, viscous cervical mucus at the time of ovulation
Answer: A, B, C, D
Rationale: Mittelschmerz coincides with ovulation and some spotting may occur; cervical mucus at ovulation is thin, clear, and stretchy; after ovulation estrogen levels decrease, and for "90% of women, only a small amount of withdrawal bleeding occurs, and it goes unnoticed," while "in 10% of women, there is sufficient bleeding for it to be visible," termed midcycle bleeding; and basal body temperature rises after ovulation with increasing progesterone (p. 53).
Trap: (E) is the single wrong option and it is the one drawn from the wrong side of the mucus rule — viscous mucus is the preovulatory and postovulatory state, so at the time of ovulation it is the abnormal description. (C) is the keyed option most often dropped, because visible midcycle bleeding is uncommon enough at 10% to read as pathology.

Topic: prostaglandins in reproduction

Q: A nurse reads that prostaglandins are involved in menstruation and wants to know where in the body they are produced. Which statement is accurate?
A) They are produced in most organs of the body including the uterus, and menstrual blood is a potent prostaglandin source
B) They are produced only by the ovaries, under the influence of luteinizing hormone
C) They are produced only by the placenta, which is why they matter in pregnancy
D) They are produced only by the endometrial basal layer and stored until menstruation
Answer: A
Rationale: Prostaglandins are "oxygenated fatty acids classified as hormones." They "are produced in most organs of the body including the uterus. Menstrual blood is a potent PG source," they are "metabolized quickly by most tissues," and they are biologically active in minute amounts (pp. 53–54).
Trap: (B) and (C) are both the fact bank's flagged error in different clothing — a distractor restricting prostaglandin production to the ovaries or the placenta is wrong. Restriction is the tempting move because the chapter discusses prostaglandins in a reproductive context, and it is easy to convert "matters here" into "made here."

Q: A patient is being evaluated because her prostaglandin levels fail to rise along with her luteinizing hormone surge. Which problem should the nurse anticipate?
A) Anovulation and infertility, because the ovum remains trapped within the graafian follicle
B) Heavy menstrual bleeding, because the endometrium cannot vasoconstrict
C) Preterm labor, because uterine contractility is unopposed
D) Absent menstruation, because the endometrium never proliferates
Answer: A
Rationale: Prostaglandins "may play a key role in ovulation. If PG levels do not rise along with the surge of LH, the ovum remains trapped within the graafian follicle" (pp. 53–54).
Trap: (B) is the reflex pick because the chapter's other prostaglandin role is menstrual — they cause regression of the corpus luteum and sloughing of the endometrium. The fact bank names the distinction directly: a prostaglandin deficit at the time of the surge presents as an ovulation and fertility problem, not a bleeding problem.

Q: A patient is receiving a prostaglandin preparation for cervical ripening before an induction. Which effects of prostaglandins on the uterus explain this use?
A) They increase myometrial response to oxytocic stimulation, enhance uterine contractions, and cause cervical dilation
B) They relax the myometrium and soften the cervix by blocking oxytocin receptors
C) They act only on the endometrium, and their effect on the cervix is indirect
D) They prevent cervical dilation until the myometrium is fully contracted
Answer: A
Rationale: Prostaglandins "increase myometrial response to oxytocic stimulation, enhance uterine contractions, and cause cervical dilation. They may be a factor in the initiation of labor, the maintenance of labor, or both" (pp. 53–54). More generally they affect "smooth muscle contractility and modulation of hormonal activity."
Trap: (B) is attractive because "ripening" sounds like softening and softening sounds like relaxation. Prostaglandins do the opposite on the muscle side: they potentiate the response to oxytocic stimulation rather than blocking it, which is also why they are not a neutral cervical agent.

Q: A patient asks whether anything in semen itself affects the uterus after intercourse. Which response reflects the chapter?
A) Prostaglandins from ejaculated semen increase the motility of uterine musculature, which may assist sperm transport toward the oviduct
B) Semen has no effect on the uterus; sperm move entirely under their own motility
C) Prostaglandins from semen relax the uterus so that sperm are not expelled
D) Prostaglandins from semen thicken cervical mucus to protect the sperm
Answer: A
Rationale: "The introduction of PGs into the vagina or the uterine cavity (from ejaculated semen) increases the motility of uterine musculature, which may assist the transport of sperm through the uterus and into the oviduct" (pp. 53–54).
Trap: (C) is the intuitive inversion, since a reader reasoning from first principles will assume the uterus must be quieted so that sperm are not swept out. The source says the opposite: increased uterine motility is what assists transport. (D) borrows the mucus rule from the ovulation section and attributes it to the wrong source and the wrong direction.
