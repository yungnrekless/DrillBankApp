# Chapter 19 drills — Nursing Care of the Family During Labor and Birth

Authored from `sources/Ch19_Fact_Bank.md` (Lowdermilk, Cashion, Alden, Olshansky
& Perry, *Maternity & Women's Health Care*, 13th ed.; chapter author Renee
Oakley Spain).

Blueprint weight is **11 questions**, topics *"Stages of Labor, SVE, false labor,
fetal position, newborn triage after delivery"* — depth `full`, which earns 55 to
75 drills across 10 to 15 topics. This file carries **73 across 16 topics**. It
is the course's second-heaviest chapter after Ch 18, and unusually the reading
guide's six islands and the blueprint's five topics overlap almost completely,
so nearly everything the fact bank curated is exam-relevant.

The topic count deliberately overshoots the band by one. Triage and rupture of
membranes were each first written as a single 7-question topic, which is the
per-topic ceiling, and each was carrying two genuinely different restudy units:
what the nurse is legally obliged to do at first contact versus how a presenting
woman is sorted and dispositioned, and how rupture is confirmed versus what is
done once it has happened. A dashboard entry that goes red should name one thing
to open, so both were split. The question count, which is the hard constraint,
stays inside the band.

Topics follow the nurse's actual path through a labor — triage, then membranes,
then the admission assessment broken into its four instruments (intervals,
Leopold, contractions, vaginal examination), then labs, then progress through
the four stages, then the newborn, the placenta, and the perineum — rather than
the chapter's section order, because a stem usually turns on which instrument
answers the question rather than where the chapter discussed it.

Every distinction the chapter states in both directions is drilled from both
ends: true and false labor, nitrazine alkaline and acidic, point of maximal
intensity above and below the umbilicus, Leopold's flexed and extended
prominence, early-phase and active-phase assessment intervals, palpation and
intrauterine pressure catheter, passive and active third-stage management, signs
of second-stage and third-stage onset, second-stage durations with and without
regional anesthesia, skin-to-skin and delayed cord clamping, and midline and
mediolateral episiotomy. Inverting those is the reflex error this chapter
produces.

**Left out deliberately, and why.** The fact bank scoped out supportive care
during labor, the physical nursing care table (hygiene, oral versus intravenous
intake, bowel elimination, ambulation), the cultural and psychosocial content
beyond Box 19.3, the mechanism of birth itself and emergency-birth guidelines,
and the fourth stage's detailed recovery management, which belongs to Chapters
21 and 22. These drills follow that scope. Also dropped for count: the birth
plan box, the recording-of-birth legal tip, partograms beyond a mention, and
placental disposal customs.

Topic: stages of labor framework
Subtopic: the four stages and their boundaries

Q: A nurse documents that a woman has entered the second stage of labor. Which finding justifies that entry?
A) The cervix is 6 cm dilated and contractions are 2 to 3 minutes apart
B) The cervix is fully dilated at 10 cm with complete effacement
C) The woman reports an irresistible urge to push
D) The fetal head is visible at the perineum
Answer: B
Rationale: The second stage "begins with full cervical dilation (10 cm) and complete effacement (100%) and ends with the baby's birth." The chapter is explicit that full dilation is "the only certain objective sign that the second stage of labor has begun."
Trap: (C) is the reflex pick because the urge to push is the most visible marker of the second stage, and Box 19.10 does list it among the signs that suggest onset. Those signs "can appear earlier in labor," and women labouring without regional anesthesia can feel an irresistible urge as early as 5 cm, most often because the presenting part has descended below the ischial spines.

Q: Which sequence correctly describes where the third and fourth stages begin and end?
A) Third stage begins with full dilation and ends with the birth; fourth stage begins with the birth
B) Third stage begins with the birth of the baby and ends when the placenta is expelled; fourth stage begins with expulsion of the placenta
C) Third stage begins when the placenta separates and ends when the fundus is firm; fourth stage begins one hour after birth
D) Third stage begins with the birth and ends one hour later; fourth stage covers the next 24 hours
Answer: B
Rationale: "The third stage of labor lasts from the birth of the baby until the placenta is expelled. It is generally by far the shortest stage of labor." The fourth stage "begins with the expulsion of the placenta and lasts until the woman is stable in the immediate postpartum period, usually within the first hour after birth."
Trap: (A) shifts every boundary one stage earlier, which is the most available error because the birth itself feels like the natural end of labor rather than a midpoint. The placenta, not the baby, ends the third stage.

Q: A woman's placenta was delivered at 0340. At 0450 the nurse charts that she remains in the fourth stage. Which statement about this period is accurate?
A) The fourth stage ends at exactly one hour after birth regardless of maternal status
B) The stage usually lasts within the first hour after birth, but the immediate postpartum recovery period usually includes at least the first 2 hours, based on maternal status
C) The fourth stage lasts 24 hours and ends with the first ambulation
D) The fourth stage has no defined endpoint
Answer: B
Rationale: The chapter gives two numbers that are easy to collapse into one. The stage itself lasts "usually within the first hour after birth," while "the immediate postpartum recovery period lasts longer. It usually includes at least the first 2 hours after birth, based on maternal status."
Trap: (A) treats the hour as a hard boundary when the chapter attaches the recovery period to maternal status rather than the clock. The two-number structure is the testable part.

Subtopic: the two phases of the first stage

Q: A nursing student describes a laboring woman as being "in transition at 9 cm." Which correction reflects the current edition?
A) The first stage now comprises two phases, latent and active, and the transition phase is no longer used
B) Transition now begins at 7 cm rather than 8 cm
C) Transition is now considered part of the second stage
D) The description is correct as stated
Answer: A
Rationale: "Traditionally the first stage of labor was considered to comprise three phases: the latent phase (0 through 3 cm of dilation), the active phase (4 to 7 cm of dilation), and the transition phase (8 to 10 cm of dilation). However, these definitions have changed based on research." The contemporary division is latent and active only, with active beginning at 6 cm and ending at complete dilation.
Trap: (B) and (D) both preserve a phase this edition retired, and the pull is strong because "transition" remains in common clinical speech and in older course materials. The chapter attributes the change to contemporary women being older and heavier, progressing more slowly in the active phase and experiencing longer labors than Friedman's half-century-old curves predicted.

Q: A multiparous woman is 5 cm dilated. Her nurse tells the oncoming shift she is in active labor because multiparas enter the active phase earlier. Which correction applies?
A) Multiparas enter the active phase at 4 cm; nulliparas enter at 6 cm
B) Active labor begins at 6 cm for both nulliparous and multiparous women; only after 6 cm do multiparas progress more rapidly
C) Multiparas enter active labor at 6 cm and nulliparas at 4 cm
D) Parity does not affect the rate of progress at any point
Answer: B
Rationale: "Active labor begins at 6 cm for both nulliparous and multiparous women." The parity effect appears only afterward: "During the early phase of the first stage of labor, nulliparous and multiparous women progress at similar rates. After reaching a cervical dilation of 6 cm, however, multiparous women progress more rapidly."
Trap: (A) inverts where the parity difference lives, and it is the intuitive inversion because multiparous labors really are faster overall, so students assume the speed advantage starts at the beginning. It starts at 6 cm. (D) discards the parity effect entirely.

Topic: true versus false labor
Subtopic: general

Q: A woman at 39 weeks calls to report contractions. Which description most strongly suggests true labor?
A) The contractions become more intense when she walks
B) The contractions stop when she changes position
C) The contractions are felt in the abdomen above her umbilicus
D) A warm shower relieves the contractions
Answer: A
Rationale: The Teaching for Self-Management box states that true labor contractions "become more intense with walking or position change," while false labor contractions "often stop with walking or position change." What activity does to the contraction is one of the two cleanest discriminators the box offers.
Trap: (B) and (D) are both false-labor findings, and (D) in particular pulls because relief from comfort measures reads as good news rather than as diagnostic information. True labor contractions "continue despite use of comfort measures."

Q: A second woman calls with contractions that stop when she walks the hallway and are felt in her abdomen above the umbilicus. Which pattern is she describing?
A) True labor, because the contractions are palpable and located anteriorly
B) False labor, because the contractions stop with walking and are felt above the umbilicus
C) True labor entering the latent phase
D) Cannot be determined without a contraction monitor
Answer: B
Rationale: False labor contractions "occur irregularly or become regular only temporarily," "often stop with walking or position change," "can be felt in the back or the abdomen above the umbilicus," and "can often be stopped through the use of comfort measures." True labor pain is "usually felt in the lower back, radiating to the lower portion of the abdomen."
Trap: (A) is the mirror error of the previous item, and the location clue is the one most often reversed: above the umbilicus is the false-labor location, while the lower back radiating forward is the true-labor one.

Q: A woman arrives with contractions she describes as strong and regular. Her cervix is unchanged from her examination three hours earlier. Which statement is the definitional basis for calling this false labor?
A) Her contractions are not painful enough to indicate labor
B) Her contractions are not causing cervical dilation
C) She is nulliparous and therefore not yet in labor
D) Her contractions are more than five minutes apart
Answer: B
Rationale: The arbiter is cervical change, not contraction quality. The chapter names the emotional cost of this exact conversation: a woman "may feel discouraged, angry, or confused on learning that the contractions that feel so strong and regular to her do not indicate true labor because they are not causing cervical dilation."
Trap: (A) is the reflex pick because contraction quality is what both the woman and the nurse can feel, and the teaching box itself is built on contraction characteristics. Those characteristics are screening clues; the cervix is the definition.

Q: When should teaching about the stages of labor and the signs of its onset take place?
A) At the first prenatal visit
B) During the third trimester of pregnancy
C) At the time of admission to the labor unit
D) During the second trimester, before childbirth classes begin
Answer: B
Rationale: "Teaching regarding the stages of labor and the signs indicating its onset should occur during the third trimester of pregnancy."
Trap: (C) is the reflex pick because that is when the information becomes immediately relevant, and it is also when it is least usable — a woman contracting in triage is not in a position to learn how to tell whether she is contracting.

Q: Which statement about when nulliparous and multiparous women typically present for admission is accurate?
A) Nulliparous women typically present in the active phase; multiparous women in the latent phase
B) Both groups typically present in the active phase
C) Nulliparous women may seek admission in the latent phase; multiparous women may not present until the active phase
D) Presentation timing is unrelated to parity
Answer: C
Rationale: "Many nulliparous women planning to give birth in a hospital or birth center may seek admission in the latent (early) phase because they have not experienced labor before and are unsure of the 'right' time to come in. Multiparous women may not present to the birth center or hospital until they are in the active phase of the first stage of labor."
Trap: (A) is the mirror error and it is available because experience reads as caution — students expect the woman who has done this before to arrive earlier. Experience does the opposite: she waits.

Topic: telephone triage and emtala obligations
Subtopic: general

Q: A woman calls the labor unit at 0200 describing contractions and asks the nurse whether she is in labor and should come in. Which response reflects the chapter's guidance?
A) Assess her contraction pattern by phone and advise her whether this is true labor
B) Advise her to call her obstetric health care provider or to come to the hospital if she feels the need to be checked, and document the call
C) Tell her to wait until contractions are five minutes apart for one hour before calling back
D) Instruct her to time contractions for two hours and call back with the results
Answer: B
Rationale: "Many hospitals, however, discourage the nurse from giving advice by telephone regarding what to do because of legal liability. Nurses are often instructed to tell women who call with questions to call their obstetric health care provider (HCP) or to come to the hospital if they feel the need to be checked." The nature of the conversation, "including any advice or instructions given and the person's response to the instructions, should be documented in the client's record."
Trap: (A) and (C) both diagnose or triage over the phone, which is exactly the liability the chapter names. (C) is especially tempting because the five-one-one rule is widely taught, and it substitutes a rule for the assessment the woman is entitled to.

Q: An uninsured woman presents to obstetric triage reporting contractions. The unit is full. Which action is required under the Emergency Medical Treatment and Active Labor Act?
A) She must be assessed, stabilized, and treated at the hospital where she presents, regardless of insurance status or ability to pay
B) She may be redirected to a public hospital if the unit has no beds
C) She must be assessed but may be transferred before stabilization if another facility accepts her
D) She may be discharged by the triage nurse if the cervix is unchanged
Answer: A
Rationale: The Legal Tip states that "pregnant women who present with contractions or who may be in labor are considered unstable and must be assessed, stabilized, and treated at the hospital where they present regardless of their insurance status or ability to pay." The act is "a federal regulation enacted to protect pregnant women during an emergency regardless of their insurance status or ability to pay for care."
Trap: (B) frames a capacity problem as a legitimate reason to redirect, which is the most plausible-sounding violation because it does not turn on insurance at all. (D) is the next question's error.

Q: A triage nurse assesses a woman whose cervix is closed and whose contractions are irregular. What limits the nurse's ability to discharge her?
A) Nothing; a closed cervix rules out labor
B) She is presumed to be in true labor until a qualified health care provider certifies that she is not
C) She may be discharged after a second examination two hours later
D) Only the charge nurse may authorize the discharge
Answer: B
Rationale: The Legal Tip states the presumption and its direction: "A pregnant woman presenting to an obstetric triage area is presumed to be in 'true' labor until a qualified HCP certifies that she is not." Named urgent problems that bring women to triage include labor, decreased fetal movement, rupture of membranes, and recent trauma.
Trap: (A) is the reflex pick because a closed cervix genuinely is strong evidence against labor, and the nurse's own assessment feels sufficient. The presumption runs toward labor, and lifting it requires a qualified provider's certification, not a nurse's finding.

Topic: labor triage acuity and disposition
Subtopic: the maternal fetal triage index

Q: Four women arrive in obstetric triage simultaneously. Using the Maternal Fetal Triage Index, which one is Priority 1, STAT?
A) A woman at 38 weeks with regular contractions and spontaneous rupture of membranes
B) A woman at 32 weeks reporting decreased fetal movement
C) A woman with fetal parts visible on the perineum and active bearing-down efforts
D) A woman at 39 weeks with a temperature of 100.6°F and no other complaint
Answer: C
Rationale: Priority 1 captures abnormal STAT vital signs, the need for immediate lifesaving intervention, or imminent birth, and the index lists "fetal parts visible on the perineum" and "active maternal bearing-down efforts" as imminent birth. Priority 1 also covers maternal cardiac compromise, severe respiratory distress, seizing, hemorrhaging, acute mental status change, signs of placental abruption or uterine rupture, and prolapsed cord.
Trap: (B) pulls because decreased fetal movement sounds like a fetal emergency; the index places it under Priority 2, urgent. (D) is a Priority 3 finding, since a temperature above 100.4°F sits in the prompt tier while 101.0°F or higher moves it to urgent.

Q: The Maternal Fetal Triage Index lists a maternal heart rate threshold at more than one priority level. Which pairing is correct?
A) Priority 1 at a heart rate above 120; Priority 2 at above 130
B) Priority 1 at a heart rate below 40 or above 130; Priority 2 at above 120 or below 50
C) Priority 1 at a heart rate below 50; Priority 2 at below 40
D) The heart rate appears only at Priority 2
Answer: B
Rationale: The STAT tier lists maternal heart rate below 40 or above 130, apnea, oxygen saturation below 93%, systolic pressure at or above 150 or diastolic at or above 110 or a pressure below 60 or palpable only, no fetal heart rate detected by doppler, and a fetal heart rate below 110 for more than 60 seconds. The urgent tier widens to a heart rate above 120 or below 50, temperature at or above 101.0°F, respiratory rate above 26 or below 12, and saturation below 95%.
Trap: (A) and (C) both narrow the STAT range inward, which is the intuitive error because the more urgent tier feels like it should trigger sooner. The STAT thresholds are the more extreme numbers at both ends; the urgent tier catches the values on the way there. The index's own disclaimer notes that its vital signs "are suggested values" that each clinical team adjusts for its population and region.

Subtopic: who is admitted and who goes home

Q: A low-risk nulliparous woman in latent labor lives 45 minutes away, has no car, and has a history of a 3-hour labor with her first birth. Which factor most supports admitting her rather than sending her home?
A) Her low-risk status
B) Her nulliparity
C) Her lack of transportation, distance, and history of rapid labor
D) Her request to stay
Answer: C
Rationale: The chapter sorts on practical logistics rather than on the cervix. Women who live near the facility with adequate support and transportation may be sent home; those who live "at a considerable distance," lack "adequate support and transportation," or have "a history of rapid labors" are admitted in latent labor.
Trap: (A) is the reflex pick because low risk sounds like a discharge criterion, and it is the criterion for the ideal of laboring at home. Low risk permits going home only when the logistics permit it too. Note that the stem's history is internally inconsistent with "nulliparous," which is why (B) cannot be the reasoning either.

Q: A hospital operates an early labor lounge. What does it accomplish?
A) It admits women in latent labor to a lower-acuity bed
B) It allows women in early labor to remain on site as outpatients until they are admitted or discharged, so clients are not admitted until active labor
C) It provides antepartum testing for high-risk women
D) It replaces obstetric triage for low-risk women
Answer: B
Rationale: The early labor lounge is "a room for women who are in early labor and wish to remain on site. Women using the early labor lounge remain there as outpatients until they are admitted or discharged." It gives providers "an alternative to discharging clients who are reluctant to leave," and "clients are therefore not admitted until they are in active labor." Labor triage units similarly "offer time without official hospital admission to evaluate for labor or other concerns."
Trap: (A) is the reflex pick because a woman occupying a room in the hospital reads as admitted. The whole design of the lounge turns on her outpatient status; that is what keeps the latent-phase admission from happening.

Topic: confirming rupture of membranes
Subtopic: general

Q: A nitrazine test on vaginal fluid turns the tape deep blue. How should the nurse interpret this?
A) Membranes are probably intact, because the fluid is acidic
B) Membranes are probably ruptured, because amniotic fluid is alkaline
C) The result is inconclusive without a fern test
D) The result indicates infection rather than rupture
Answer: B
Rationale: Nitrazine dye "differentiates amniotic fluid, which is slightly alkaline, from urine and purulent material [pus], which are acidic." Blue-green at pH 6.5, blue-gray at 7.0, and deep blue at 7.5 all read as membranes probably ruptured. Results are reported as positive or negative.
Trap: (A) inverts the pH direction, which is the single most invertible fact in this section. The anchor to hold is that amniotic fluid is the alkaline one, so the blue end of the tape is the positive result.

Q: A nitrazine test on a second woman's vaginal fluid reads olive-green. What does this indicate?
A) Membranes are probably ruptured
B) Membranes are probably intact, at a pH of about 6.0
C) The specimen was contaminated with blood
D) The test is positive and the fern test may be omitted
Answer: B
Rationale: Yellow at pH 5.0, olive-yellow at 5.5, and olive-green at 6.0 all identify "vaginal and most body fluids that are acidic," reading as membranes probably intact.
Trap: (A) is the mirror error of the previous item. Note also that false results are possible "because of presence of bloody show or semen or insufficient amount of amniotic fluid" — and two of those three push the result toward a false positive, so a blue reading in a woman with bloody show deserves confirmation.

Q: A fern test on vaginal fluid shows a frond-like crystalline pattern. A student says this proves the membranes are ruptured. Which caution does the chapter add?
A) Ferning also occurs in the cervical mucus test when estrogen levels are high
B) Ferning appears only if the specimen is examined while still wet
C) Ferning is unreliable after 37 weeks
D) Ferning indicates infection rather than amniotic fluid
Answer: A
Rationale: The procedure is to spread a drop of fluid on a clean glass slide, "allow the fluid to dry," and examine it under the microscope for "ferning (a frond-like crystalline pattern)." The chapter names the decoy: "Do not confuse this with the cervical mucus test, when high levels of estrogen also cause ferning."
Trap: (B) inverts the procedure — the specimen must dry before it will fern, so examining it wet produces a false negative. Absence of ferning is also not proof of intact membranes: it "alerts staff to possibility that amount of specimen was inadequate or that specimen was urine, vaginal discharge, or blood."

Topic: nursing care after rupture of membranes
Subtopic: general

Q: A laboring woman's membranes rupture spontaneously with a large gush of clear fluid. What is the nurse's first action?
A) Change the underpad and assess the odor and amount of fluid
B) Take the woman's temperature
C) Assess the fetal heart rate and pattern for several minutes
D) Notify the obstetric health care provider
Answer: C
Rationale: The SAFETY ALERT names both the action and the reason: "The umbilical cord may prolapse when the membranes rupture. The FHR and pattern should be assessed for several minutes immediately after ROM to determine fetal well-being, and the findings should be documented."
Trap: (A) and (B) are both real parts of the assessment and both are wrong as a first action, because neither would detect a prolapsed cord in the window that matters. Documentation after rupture must include the time of rupture, the fetal heart rate immediately before and after, and the color, estimated amount, and odor of the fluid.

Q: Which statement about spontaneous rupture of membranes at term is accurate?
A) Rupture of membranes at term always means labor has begun
B) Labor is initiated at term by spontaneous rupture in approximately 25% of women, and a lag rarely exceeding 24 hours may precede contractions
C) Labor is initiated by rupture in approximately 75% of women at term
D) Rupture during labor occurs most commonly in the latent phase
Answer: B
Rationale: "Labor is initiated at term by SROM in approximately 25% of pregnant women," and "a lag period, rarely exceeding 24 hours, may precede the onset of labor." Rupture during labor "most commonly occurs in the active phase of the first stage of labor."
Trap: (A) is the reflex equation of rupture with labor onset, and it fails on both halves — the fraction is a minority, and even when rupture does start things, contractions may lag by up to a day.

Q: A woman's membranes ruptured six hours ago. Which nursing measures does the chapter specify to identify infection?
A) Limit vaginal examinations and monitor maternal temperature and vaginal discharge at least every 2 hours
B) Obtain a vaginal culture every 4 hours
C) Perform a vaginal examination hourly to track progress
D) Restrict oral intake and begin prophylactic antibiotics
Answer: A
Rationale: "When membranes rupture, microorganisms from the vagina can then ascend into the amniotic sac, causing chorioamnionitis and placentitis to develop. For this reason, vaginal examinations must be limited, and maternal temperature and vaginal discharge must be monitored frequently (at least every 2 hours) to quickly identify signs of infection."
Trap: (C) is exactly the practice the chapter restricts, and it pulls because tracking progress feels like attentive nursing. The chapter also closes the reassuring loophole: "Even when membranes are intact, however, microorganisms can ascend and cause infection."

Q: A woman in uncomplicated active labor at 7 cm is progressing normally. The provider suggests amniotomy to speed things up. Which consideration applies?
A) Amniotomy reliably shortens labor and should be performed
B) Amniotomy is discouraged if there is no medical reason, because it can increase the sensation of pressure and pain and is not necessary for a normal birth
C) Amniotomy is contraindicated after 6 cm
D) Amniotomy eliminates the risk of cord prolapse
Answer: B
Rationale: Artificial rupture of membranes, or amniotomy, "is discouraged if there is no medical reason for it because it can increase the laboring woman's sensation of pressure and pain and is not necessary for a normal birth to occur." It is performed by the obstetric provider using a plastic AmniHook or a surgical clamp.
Trap: (D) inverts the risk in the most dangerous direction — rupture, spontaneous or artificial, is precisely when the cord may prolapse, which is why the fetal heart rate is assessed for several minutes afterward either way.

Topic: first stage assessment intervals
Subtopic: general

Q: A woman is in the early phase of the first stage of labor. According to Table 19.2, how often are her blood pressure, pulse, and respirations assessed?
A) Every 15 to 30 minutes
B) Every 30 to 60 minutes
C) Every hour
D) Every 5 to 15 minutes
Answer: B
Rationale: Table 19.2 sets maternal vital signs and presence of bloody show at every 30 to 60 minutes in the early phase, tightening to every 15 to 30 minutes in the active phase. Uterine activity and fetal heart rate and pattern are assessed every 15 to 30 minutes in both phases.
Trap: (A) is the active-phase interval and is the most available wrong answer because 15 to 30 minutes appears three times in the table. The vital-sign row is the one that changes between phases; uterine activity and fetal heart rate do not.

Q: The same table specifies an assessment whose interval tightens more sharply between the early and active phases than the vital signs do. Which is it?
A) Fetal heart rate and pattern
B) Uterine activity
C) Changes in maternal appearance, mood, affect, energy level, and involvement of the partner
D) Temperature
Answer: C
Rationale: The psychosocial row moves from every 30 minutes in the early phase to every 5 to 15 minutes in the active phase, a sharper tightening than the vital signs' shift from 30-to-60 to 15-to-30 minutes. Uterine activity and fetal heart rate stay at 15 to 30 minutes in both phases.
Trap: (A) and (B) pull because they are the assessments that feel most clinically urgent, and both intervals are in fact unchanged between the phases. The row that intensifies most is the one about how the woman is coping.

Q: A laboring woman's membranes ruptured 90 minutes ago. How often should her temperature now be assessed?
A) Every 4 hours, unchanged
B) Every 2 hours
C) Every hour
D) Every 30 minutes
Answer: B
Rationale: Table 19.2 states the rule as a conditional: temperature "every 4 h until membranes are ruptured, then every 2 h." Temperature is monitored to identify signs of infection or a fluid deficit such as dehydration from inadequate intake.
Trap: (A) is the reflex pick for anyone who memorized the interval without the condition attached to it. Rupture is the event that changes the interval, and it is the same reasoning that limits vaginal examinations after rupture.

Q: Select all situations in which the chapter requires the fetal heart rate to be assessed, beyond the routine interval in Table 19.2.
A) After rupture of membranes
B) After any change in the contraction pattern or maternal status
C) Before and after the woman receives medication
D) Before and after a procedure is performed
E) Only at the scheduled 15- to 30-minute interval
Answer: A, B, C, D
Rationale: The chapter lists three event-driven categories on top of the clock: "it is essential to assess the FHR after rupture of membranes (ROM), after any change in the contraction pattern or maternal status, and before and after the woman receives medication or a procedure is performed." On admission, uterine contractions and fetal heart rate are usually monitored electronically for at least a 20- to 30-minute baseline period, though birth centers monitor intermittently.
Trap: (E) is the one wrong option and it states the error the whole list exists to correct — treating the interval as a ceiling rather than a floor. A stem in which a nurse defers a fetal heart rate check because the last one was recent is applying the clock where an event governs.

Topic: leopold maneuvers and fetal position
Subtopic: general

Q: Before performing Leopold maneuvers, which preparation steps does the chapter specify?
A) Ask the woman to empty her bladder and place a rolled towel under one hip
B) Position the woman flat and supine with legs extended
C) Have the woman lie on her left side with knees drawn up
D) Perform the maneuvers during a contraction for better definition
Answer: A
Rationale: The preparation is hand hygiene, asking the woman to empty her bladder, positioning her supine with one pillow and knees slightly flexed, and placing "a small rolled towel under the woman's right or left hip to displace the uterus off major blood vessels (prevents supine hypotensive syndrome)."
Trap: (B) is the reflex pick because abdominal palpation seems to require a flat abdomen, and it recreates the aortocaval compression the rolled towel exists to prevent. The bladder step is easy to skip and is itself testable.

Q: The nurse palpates the uterine fundus and finds a part that is round, firm, and freely movable. Which conclusion follows?
A) The fetus is in a breech presentation with the buttocks in the fundus
B) The head is in the fundus, suggesting a breech presentation
C) The fetal back is anterior
D) The presenting part is engaged
Answer: B
Rationale: The first maneuver palpates the fundal contents, and the chapter gives the tactile discriminator: "The head feels round, firm, and freely movable; the breech feels less regular and softer." A head in the fundus means the breech is presenting. This maneuver identifies fetal lie, longitudinal or transverse, and presentation, cephalic or breech.
Trap: (A) makes the right diagnosis with the wrong anatomy, which is the reflex error — students correctly hear "breech presentation" but then place the buttocks at the top instead of the bottom. What is in the fundus is the opposite of what is presenting.

Q: On the fourth maneuver, the nurse finds the cephalic prominence on the same side as the fetal small parts. What does this indicate?
A) The head is extended and the face is presenting
B) The head is flexed and the vertex is presenting
C) The fetus is in a transverse lie
D) The presenting part is not yet engaged
Answer: B
Rationale: The fourth maneuver, performed facing the woman's feet, outlines the fetal head and palpates the cephalic prominence. Prominence "on the same side as the small parts means the head is flexed and the vertex presents."
Trap: (A) is the mirror error and it is the one most often held backwards, because there is no intuitive reason why the prominence should sit opposite the back when the head is flexed. Documentation after the maneuvers records presentation, position, and lie, and whether the presenting part is flexed or extended, engaged, or free-floating.

Q: On a different woman, the cephalic prominence is palpated on the same side as the fetal back. What does this indicate?
A) The head is flexed and the vertex is presenting
B) The head is extended and the face is presenting
C) The third maneuver was performed incorrectly
D) The fetus is in a breech presentation
Answer: B
Rationale: Prominence on the same side as the back means the head is extended and the face presents, which is the inverse of the flexed finding in the previous item.
Trap: (A) is the mirror error of the previous item. Hold the pairing by the side rather than by the word: small parts means flexed and vertex, back means extended and face.

Q: A nurse is locating the point of maximal intensity to place the ultrasound transducer. In a vertex presentation, where is the fetal heart rate usually heard loudest?
A) Above the maternal umbilicus in the midline
B) Below the maternal umbilicus, in either the right or left lower quadrant
C) At the level of the umbilicus on the side opposite the fetal back
D) At the fundus
Answer: B
Rationale: The point of maximal intensity "is the location on the maternal abdomen at which the FHR is heard the loudest. It is usually directly over the fetal back." In a vertex presentation the fetal heart rate "can usually be heard below the mother's umbilicus in either the right or the left lower quadrant." The point of maximal intensity "is where the nurse places the ultrasound transducer when an electronic fetal monitor is used."
Trap: (C) places the transducer opposite the back, which is exactly backwards — the sound transmits through the fetal back, which is why the point of maximal intensity sits over it.

Q: The fetal heart rate on a different woman is heard most clearly above the umbilicus. What does this suggest?
A) The transducer has been misplaced and should be moved lower
B) A breech presentation
C) A transverse lie
D) A maternal rather than fetal heart rate is being detected
Answer: B
Rationale: In a breech presentation the fetal heart rate "is most easily heard above the mother's umbilicus." The location of the point of maximal intensity follows the presentation rather than the equipment.
Trap: (A) is the mirror error of the previous item and it is the reflex response of a nurse who expects to find the heart rate low — it treats a diagnostic finding as an equipment problem, and moving the transducer would lose the signal rather than improve it.

Topic: uterine contraction assessment
Subtopic: the four descriptors

Q: A nurse charts that contractions are occurring every 4 minutes. Which contraction characteristic has been documented?
A) Duration, the time between the onset and the end of a contraction
B) Frequency, the time from the beginning of one contraction to the beginning of the next
C) Intensity, the strength of a contraction at its peak
D) Resting tone, the tension in the uterine muscle between contractions
Answer: B
Rationale: Frequency is "how often uterine contractions occur; the time that passes from the beginning of one contraction to the beginning of the next contraction." Duration is "the time that passes between the onset and the end of a contraction," intensity is "the strength of a contraction at its peak," and resting tone is "the tension in the uterine muscle between contractions."
Trap: (A) is the reflex confusion, because frequency and duration are both measured in the same units and both describe time. Frequency is measured beginning to beginning, which is why it includes the interval between contractions.

Q: A nurse palpates a laboring woman's fundus during a contraction and finds it firm and difficult to indent with the fingertips. How is this contraction documented?
A) Mild, comparable to pressing a finger to the tip of the nose
B) Moderate, comparable to pressing a finger to the chin
C) Strong, comparable to pressing a finger to the forehead
D) Hypertonic, requiring immediate notification
Answer: B
Rationale: The chapter attaches a tactile analogy to each level: mild is a "slightly tense fundus that is easy to indent," like the tip of the nose; moderate is a "firm fundus that is difficult to indent," like the chin; strong is a "rigid, boardlike fundus that is almost impossible to indent," like the forehead.
Trap: (C) is the near-miss because "difficult to indent" and "almost impossible to indent" differ by one adverb. The three-point facial scale exists precisely to make that gradation reproducible between nurses.

Subtopic: what each method can and cannot measure

Q: A nurse wants an accurate measurement of a laboring woman's uterine resting tone. Which method provides it?
A) Palpation of the fundus between contractions
B) The external electronic monitor tracing
C) An intrauterine pressure catheter
D) The woman's own report of how relaxed her uterus feels
Answer: C
Rationale: Internal electronic monitoring with an intrauterine pressure catheter is "the most accurate way of assessing the intensity of uterine contractions and the resting tone of the uterus." The woman's description, the nurse's palpation, and the external monitor all yield frequency and duration; the first two are "more subjective and less precise" on intensity, and the external monitor provides only "some" information by comparing admission contractions with later ones.
Trap: (B) is the reflex pick because the external tracing prints a numeric value that looks like a measurement. What it graphs is not calibrated pressure, which is the whole reason the internal catheter exists.

Q: A woman reports severe pain with her contractions, but the nurse palpates them as mild. How should the nurse proceed?
A) Reassure her that mild contractions cannot cause severe pain
B) Document the palpated intensity and treat the reported discomfort as valid
C) Document only the woman's report, since it is more accurate
D) Wait for the electronic monitor to confirm the intensity before responding
Answer: B
Rationale: The chapter draws the line precisely: "a woman's assessment of the strength of her contractions may be less accurate than that of the nurse or HCP, although the amount of discomfort reported is valid." Strength and discomfort are separate findings, and only one of them is being questioned.
Trap: (A) uses a true premise about strength estimation to dismiss a report the chapter explicitly protects, and it is the reflex response for a nurse whose palpation disagrees with the patient. Her pain is not an estimate of her contractions.

Subtopic: when a labor pattern is a problem

Q: A woman has been in the active phase for longer than the expected labor pattern predicts. Her fetal heart rate is reassuring and she is afebrile. Which interpretation is correct?
A) This is arrest of labor and requires immediate notification
B) Active labor can last longer than the expected patterns, and this is not a cause for concern unless the maternal-fetal unit shows signs of stress
C) Cesarean birth should be anticipated
D) Oxytocin augmentation is indicated by protocol
Answer: B
Rationale: The SAFETY ALERT states it directly: "Active labor can actually last longer than the expected labor patterns because each woman and each labor is different. This finding is not a cause for concern unless the maternal-fetal unit exhibits signs of stress (e.g., abnormal FHR patterns, maternal fever)."
Trap: (A) is the reflex pick because a labor exceeding a published curve reads as a deviation demanding action. The discriminator is the maternal-fetal status, not the clock. Contrast this with the separate alert on contraction characteristics that exceed or fall below acceptable ranges, which "must be promptly reported."

Q: Select all findings that appear in the chapter's Signs of Potential Complications for labor.
A) Contractions lasting 90 seconds or more
B) More than five contractions in a 10-minute period
C) Relaxation of the uterus between contractions lasting less than 30 seconds
D) Maternal temperature of 38°C, or 100.4°F, or higher
E) Bloody show that is pink and sticky
Answer: A, B, C, D
Rationale: The list includes intrauterine pressure at or above 80 mm Hg or resting tone at or above 20 mm Hg by internal monitoring, contractions lasting 90 seconds or more, more than five contractions in 10 minutes, uterine relaxation under 30 seconds, fetal bradycardia or tachycardia and abnormal variability or decelerations, irregular fetal heart rate or suspected arrhythmias, meconium-stained or bloody fluid, arrest of dilation, effacement, or descent, maternal temperature at or above 38°C, foul-smelling discharge, and persistent bright or dark red bleeding.
Trap: (E) is the one wrong option, and it describes normal bloody show: it "is distinguished from vaginal bleeding by the fact that it is pink and feels sticky because of its mucoid nature." What belongs on the list is persistent bright or dark red bleeding, which is the finding pink sticky show is contrasted against.

Topic: the vaginal examination in labor
Subtopic: general

Q: A nurse has performed a vaginal examination on a woman whose membranes ruptured two hours ago. An hour later the nurse plans another examination to track progress. Which principle applies?
A) Hourly examinations are the standard for tracking active-phase progress
B) The examination is performed only when indicated, because it may introduce microorganisms into the vagina if the membranes are ruptured
C) Examinations may be performed freely once membranes have ruptured
D) The examination should be repeated only after the next set of vital signs
Answer: B
Rationale: The vaginal examination "reveals whether the woman is in true labor" and lets the examiner determine whether the membranes have ruptured, but because it "is often stressful and uncomfortable for the woman and may introduce microorganisms into the vagina if the membranes are ruptured, it is performed only when indicated by the status of the woman and the fetus."
Trap: (A) is the reflex pick because frequent checks feel like careful labor management, and it is exactly the practice the chapter rations. Table 19.2 lists the vaginal examination as "as needed" in both phases rather than on any interval at all.

Q: Select all situations the chapter names as indications for a vaginal examination in labor.
A) On admission
B) Before administering medications such as analgesics or an oxytocin infusion
C) On maternal report of perineal pressure or the urge to bear down
D) When variable decelerations of the fetal heart rate are noted
E) Every two hours to document progress on the partogram
Answer: A, B, C, D
Rationale: The named indications are on admission, before administering medications, when significant change has occurred in uterine activity, on maternal request or perception of perineal pressure or the urge to bear down, when membranes rupture, and when variable decelerations are noted.
Trap: (E) is the one wrong option and it substitutes a routine interval for an indication, which is the error the entire indication list exists to prevent. Every item on the real list is an event or a decision point, not a clock.

Q: During a vaginal examination the examiner notes significant caput succedaneum. Which measurement does the chapter say this may compromise?
A) Cervical dilation
B) Cervical effacement
C) Determination of station
D) Assessment of cervical position
Answer: C
Rationale: Box 19.6 flags "molding of the head with development of caput succedaneum (may affect accuracy of determination of station)." The examination assesses cervical dilation, effacement, and position; presenting part, position, and station; status of membranes as intact, bulging, or ruptured; and characteristics of amniotic fluid if the membranes are ruptured.
Trap: (A) attaches the caveat to the wrong measurement, and it pulls because dilation is the number everyone cares most about. Caput is scalp swelling below the presenting bone, so it makes the presenting part feel lower than it is — a station problem, not a dilation one.

Q: Which action does the chapter specify immediately before the examiner inserts fingers for a vaginal examination?
A) Position the woman flat and supine to straighten the birth canal
B) Obtain the woman's permission to touch her, after positioning her to prevent supine hypotension and draping for privacy
C) Ask the woman to bear down to bring the presenting part into reach
D) Administer an analgesic to reduce discomfort
Answer: B
Rationale: The procedure specifies a sterile glove with antiseptic solution or soluble gel, positioning "the woman to prevent supine hypotension," draping "to ensure privacy," and then, "after obtaining the woman's permission to touch her," gently inserting the index and middle fingers. Afterward the examiner explains the findings to the woman and documents and reports them.
Trap: (A) recreates supine hypotension in a procedure whose own instructions name preventing it, and it is the reflex position because it is the easiest one for the examiner.

Topic: labor admission laboratory tests
Subtopic: general

Q: A woman's blood type and Rh status were documented at her first prenatal visit. On admission in labor, the hospital laboratory repeats them. Which explanation is correct?
A) The prenatal result has expired after 40 weeks
B) Even when the tests were performed during pregnancy, the hospital laboratory or blood bank must verify the results in-house
C) Rh status can change during pregnancy
D) The repeat is required only for women who are Rh negative
Answer: B
Rationale: The chapter specifies blood type, Rh status, and atypical antibody status on admission, and adds: "Even if these tests have already been performed during pregnancy, the laboratory or transfusion services department (blood bank) in the hospital must verify the results in-house."
Trap: (C) invents a biological reason for an institutional rule, and it is the kind of answer students construct when they cannot recall the stated one. The requirement is about the blood bank being able to act on its own verified result, not about the result changing.

Q: A woman arrives in labor with no documented human immunodeficiency virus status. Which approach does the chapter direct?
A) Screen with a rapid test unless she declines, that is, opts out of testing
B) Obtain written informed consent before offering the test
C) Defer testing until after birth and test the newborn
D) Testing is required and cannot be declined during labor
Answer: A
Rationale: "Any woman whose human immunodeficiency virus (HIV) status is undocumented at the time of labor should be screened with a rapid HIV test unless she declines (opts out of) testing."
Trap: (D) removes the woman's ability to decline, which is the one thing an opt-out model preserves. The distinction between opt-out and mandatory is what makes this testable.

Q: A woman requests an epidural. Which admission laboratory test do many hospitals require before it is initiated?
A) A clean-catch urinalysis
B) A rapid group B streptococcus screen
C) A complete blood count
D) A type and screen only
Answer: C
Rationale: The complete blood count yields hematocrit along with white blood cell count, red blood cell count, hemoglobin level, and platelet count, and "many hospitals require that a CBC be done before epidural anesthesia is initiated." Chapter 17 supplies the reason: a recent platelet count is reviewed before a spinal or epidural because thrombocytopenia could cause a hematoma.
Trap: (D) pulls because both tests are blood draws done on admission, and the type and screen exists for hemorrhage rather than for the block. The platelet count is the piece the anesthesia provider needs, and it comes with the complete blood count.

Q: A woman's group B streptococcus status is unknown on admission and rapid testing is not available at this facility. What does the chapter indicate?
A) Testing may be deferred until after birth
B) Prophylactic antibiotics may be administered
C) The newborn should be isolated after birth
D) A vaginal culture should be sent and results awaited before birth
Answer: B
Rationale: "If the woman's group B streptococcus status is not known, a rapid test may be done on admission ... If rapid testing is not available, prophylactic antibiotics may be administered."
Trap: (D) is the reflex pick because a culture is the definitive test, and it fails on timing — a standard culture takes longer than most labors, so waiting for it means treating no one. Treating empirically is the chapter's fallback.

Topic: expected first stage progress
Subtopic: general

Q: According to Table 19.1, which contraction pattern characterizes the early phase of the first stage?
A) 2 to 3 minutes apart, lasting 80 to 90 seconds, strong by palpation
B) 2 to 30 minutes apart and possibly irregular, lasting 30 to 40 seconds, mild to moderate by palpation
C) 5 minutes apart, lasting 60 seconds, moderate by palpation
D) 10 minutes apart, lasting 20 seconds, mild by palpation
Answer: B
Rationale: The early phase, 0 to 5 cm, shows contractions "2–30 min apart; may be irregular," lasting "30–40 s," and "mild to moderate by palpation." The active phase, 6 to 10 cm, shows contractions 2 to 3 minutes apart lasting 80 to 90 seconds and strong by palpation.
Trap: (A) is the active-phase row, and the wide 2-to-30-minute early range is what makes (C) and (D) look like reasonable middle grounds. The table gives a range broad enough to include irregularity, not a single representative pattern.

Q: A nulliparous woman is 6 cm dilated. According to Table 19.1, what station is expected at this point, and how does it differ for a multiparous woman?
A) Nulliparous 0 station; multiparous minus 1
B) Nulliparous minus 1; multiparous 0
C) Both at 0 station
D) Both at plus 1 station
Answer: A
Rationale: The table's station row gives nulliparous women 0 station by 6 cm and multiparous women minus 1 by 6 cm. The early-phase cell for station is blank in the book.
Trap: (B) is the mirror error, and it pulls because multiparous labor is faster overall, so students expect the multipara to be further descended. She is not: the multipara's speed advantage after 6 cm is in dilation, and her presenting part characteristically engages later.

Q: A woman in the active phase is described in the chart as calm, talkative, walking through contractions, and following directions readily. According to Table 19.1, this behavior is characteristic of which phase, and what caveat applies?
A) The active phase, which is characterized by alert, cooperative behavior
B) The early phase; women with epidural analgesia may not demonstrate the expected active-phase behaviors
C) The active phase, but only in multiparous women
D) The early phase, which rules out active labor regardless of cervical findings
Answer: B
Rationale: Table 19.1 assigns "able to walk or talk through most contractions" and "alert, follows directions readily" to the early phase. Active-phase behavior is "more serious, doubtful of pain control, more apprehensive," with attention "more inwardly directed" and some difficulty following directions. The table's own footnote adds that "women who have epidural analgesia for pain relief may not demonstrate some of these behaviors during the active phase."
Trap: (D) elevates behavior over the cervix, and it is the reflex error the epidural footnote exists to prevent. With a block in place the behavioral cues go quiet, and the vaginal examination becomes the evidence.

Q: Which change in bloody show would the nurse expect as a woman moves from the early phase into the active phase?
A) From pink-to-bloody and copious, to pale pink and scant
B) From brownish discharge or pale pink mucus in scant amount, to pink-to-bloody mucus in moderate to copious amount
C) From absent, to bright red bleeding
D) Show does not change between the phases
Answer: B
Rationale: The table describes early-phase show as "brownish discharge, mucous plug, or pale pink mucus" in "scant" amount, becoming "pink-to-bloody mucus" in "moderate to copious" amount in the active phase.
Trap: (C) escalates normal show into frank bleeding, which is the finding on the Signs of Potential Complications list rather than an expected progression. Show is pink and sticky because it is mucoid; persistent bright or dark red bleeding is a complication.

Topic: second stage phases and duration
Subtopic: general

Q: A nulliparous woman with an epidural has been in the second stage for 2 hours and 45 minutes. Which statement reflects the chapter's guidance?
A) This exceeds the limit and cesarean birth is indicated
B) The suggested threshold for considering operative vaginal birth in a nulliparous woman with regional anesthesia is 3 or more hours
C) The threshold for a nulliparous woman is 2 hours regardless of anesthesia
D) There is no published guidance on second-stage duration
Answer: B
Rationale: The professional societies suggest considering operative vaginal birth when the second stage exceeds 2 or more hours in a nulliparous woman without regional anesthesia and 3 or more hours with it; for multiparous women, 1 hour without and 2 hours with. Even then, "as long as some progress is being made, and the maternal and fetal status is reassuring, there is no need for rigid time limits or arbitrary rules."
Trap: (C) drops the anesthesia adjustment, which is the whole structure of the four numbers. The rule for reading them is that regional anesthesia adds one hour and multiparity subtracts one.

Q: A multiparous woman without regional anesthesia has been in the second stage for 70 minutes, making steady descent, with a reassuring fetal heart rate. What is the nurse's interpretation?
A) She has passed the 1-hour threshold, so operative vaginal birth should now be considered, though progress and reassuring status argue against rigid limits
B) She has passed the 2-hour threshold and requires immediate intervention
C) She remains within the 2-hour threshold that applies to all multiparous women
D) Duration limits do not apply to multiparous women
Answer: A
Rationale: The multiparous threshold without regional anesthesia is 1 hour, so 70 minutes has passed it, and passing it prompts "consideration of operative vaginal birth" rather than automatic intervention. The chapter also notes that the clock is hard to start, because the second stage begins at full dilation and "the precise moment that this occurs is not easily determined because it depends on when a vaginal examination is performed."
Trap: (C) is the mirror error of the previous item, applying the with-anesthesia number to a woman without a block. (B) treats a threshold as a mandate, which the chapter explicitly declines to do while progress and status are reassuring.

Q: A woman reaches full dilation but has no urge to push. Her fetus continues to descend passively and rotate anteriorly. Which phase describes this?
A) The active pushing phase, in which the Ferguson reflex is activated
B) The latent phase of the second stage, also called delayed pushing, laboring down, or passive descent
C) Prolonged second stage requiring intervention
D) The transition phase of the first stage
Answer: B
Rationale: The latent phase of the second stage "is a period of rest and relative calm" during which "the fetus continues to descend passively through the birth canal and rotate to an anterior position." The urge to bear down "is not strong, and some women do not experience it at all or only during the acme (peak) of a contraction."
Trap: (A) describes the phase that follows, in which "the woman has strong urges to bear down, as the Ferguson reflex is activated when the presenting part presses on the stretch receptors of the pelvic floor," releasing oxytocin from the posterior pituitary and provoking stronger expulsive contractions.

Q: A nulliparous woman with epidural analgesia reaches full dilation. The nurse plans to let her labor down for an hour before pushing. Which evidence applies?
A) Delayed pushing is the standard of care for all women with regional anesthesia
B) Nulliparous women with regional anesthesia should begin pushing at the start of the second stage, because delayed pushing has not been shown to improve the chance of vaginal birth and raises hemorrhage and infection risk
C) Delayed pushing shortens the total second stage
D) Delayed pushing reduces the rate of operative vaginal birth
Answer: B
Rationale: The chapter states the exception directly: "nulliparous women with regional anesthesia should begin pushing at the start of the second stage of labor. In these women, delayed pushing has not been shown to improve the chance of vaginal birth. It does, however, increase the risk of hemorrhage and maternal and neonatal infection."
Trap: (C) inverts the finding — delayed pushing produces "an increase in total length of the second stage but a decrease in active pushing time," so it trades one clock for another. (D) inverts it too: delaying by an hour or more is associated with a small increase in cesarean and operative vaginal births.

Q: Select all findings that Box 19.10 lists as signs suggesting the onset of the second stage of labor.
A) Uncontrolled shivering
B) An episode of vomiting
C) Urge to push or feeling the need to have a bowel movement
D) Apparent lengthening of the umbilical cord
E) Involuntary bearing-down efforts
Answer: A, B, C, E
Rationale: The box lists increased frequency and intensity of contractions, the urge to push or the feeling of needing a bowel movement, an episode of vomiting, increased bloody show, uncontrolled shivering, verbalizations of feeling out of control, and involuntary bearing-down efforts. Two qualifiers attach: the signs "can appear earlier in labor," and "women with an epidural block may not exhibit such signs."
Trap: (D) is the one wrong option and it belongs to the third stage, where lengthening of the cord signals placental separation. Both lists contain a bleeding change and a visible physical change, which is what makes them swappable.

Q: A woman without regional anesthesia is 5 cm dilated and reports an irresistible urge to bear down. Which explanation does the chapter give?
A) The cervix has dilated further than the last examination showed
B) This most often relates to the station of the presenting part below the level of the ischial spines
C) This indicates a precipitous labor requiring immediate preparation for birth
D) This is a sign of maternal exhaustion rather than descent
Answer: B
Rationale: Women labouring without regional anesthesia "can experience an irresistible urge to bear down before full cervical dilation. For some, this occurs as early as 5 cm dilation. This is most often related to the station of the presenting part below the level of the ischial spines."
Trap: (A) is a reasonable clinical hypothesis and the chapter supplies a different one, which is why it needs to be recalled rather than reasoned. Descent, not dilation, is what triggers the urge, which is the same mechanism behind the Ferguson reflex.

Topic: immediate newborn care after birth
Subtopic: general

Q: A newborn is delivered and the head, shoulders, and body emerge over 40 seconds. What does the chapter specify as the time of birth?
A) The moment the head is delivered
B) The moment the anterior shoulder is delivered
C) The precise time when the entire body is out of the mother
D) The moment of the first cry
Answer: C
Rationale: "The time of birth is the precise time when the entire body is out of the mother and must be recorded. In the case of multiple births, each birth is noted in the same way."
Trap: (A) is the reflex pick because the head emerging is the visually dramatic moment and is when the clock feels like it should start. The definition is the whole body, and it matters for multiples, where each birth time is documented separately.

Q: Immediately after an uncomplicated vaginal birth, what is the recommended sequence for a healthy term newborn?
A) Transfer to the radiant warmer for drying, assessment, and identification, then return to the mother
B) Place the baby prone on the mother's bare abdomen or chest and dry, place a cap on the head, remove the wet blankets, and cover both with fresh warm blankets
C) Suction the mouth and nose on the perineum, then place skin to skin
D) Complete the Apgar score at the warmer before initiating skin-to-skin contact
Answer: B
Rationale: Skin-to-skin contact is recommended "immediately after vaginal birth and as soon as possible following cesarean birth," and the sequence is exactly as listed. It should "be considered standard of care because this practice conveys many benefits and has no adverse effects," and "routine assessments and procedures can be completed with the newborn on the woman's abdomen or chest."
Trap: (C) includes a practice the chapter has retired: suctioning "the newborn's mouth and nose on the perineum (after the head is out but before the rest of the baby is born) is no longer recommended." (D) separates the baby for an assessment that is explicitly performed during skin-to-skin contact.

Q: What are the three major priorities in the immediate care of the newborn, and how is the third addressed?
A) Airway, respiratory effort, and preventing cold stress, by drying and covering the newborn on the mother, with a radiant warmer named as less optimal
B) Airway, cord clamping, and identification, using a radiant warmer as first choice
C) Apgar scoring, airway, and eye prophylaxis, with warming deferred
D) Respiratory effort, glucose stabilization, and bonding
Answer: A
Rationale: The priorities are "maintaining a patent airway, supporting respiratory effort, and preventing cold stress by drying and preferably covering the newborn with a warmed blanket while on the mother's abdomen or chest or, less optimally, placing the newborn under a radiant warmer."
Trap: (B) elevates the radiant warmer to first choice, and it is the reflex pick because a warmer is purpose-built equipment and the mother's chest is not. The chapter ranks the warmer second explicitly, which is what makes moving a stable newborn to it the wrong first action.

Q: A partner asks why the cord is not being clamped right away. Which explanation and benefit are accurate for a term newborn?
A) Clamping is delayed at least 30 to 60 seconds to allow physiologic transfer of blood, which increases hemoglobin at birth and iron stores in the first months of life
B) Clamping is delayed 5 minutes to allow the placenta to separate
C) Delayed clamping is done only for preterm newborns
D) Delayed clamping prevents neonatal jaundice
Answer: A
Rationale: "It is now recommended that the umbilical cord not be clamped until at least 30 to 60 seconds after birth to allow for physiologic transfer of blood to the newborn." In term infants it "increases hemoglobin levels at birth and increases iron stores in the first several months of life." Preterm benefits include improved transitional circulation, better red blood cell volume, decreased need for transfusion, and lower incidence of necrotizing enterocolitis and intraventricular hemorrhage.
Trap: (D) inverts the one named cost. "Because a small increase in jaundice requiring phototherapy has been noted in term infants after delayed cord clamping, agencies should have a plan in place to monitor for and treat neonatal jaundice."

Q: A student states that both skin-to-skin contact and delayed cord clamping have no adverse effects. Which correction applies?
A) Both carry documented adverse effects
B) Skin-to-skin contact is described as having no adverse effects; delayed cord clamping carries a small increase in jaundice requiring phototherapy
C) Delayed cord clamping is described as having no adverse effects; skin-to-skin contact risks hypothermia
D) Neither has been studied well enough to say
Answer: B
Rationale: The phrase "conveys many benefits and has no adverse effects" belongs to skin-to-skin contact, which is why the chapter recommends it as standard of care. Delayed cord clamping carries the phototherapy caveat. Skin-to-skin contact also raises blood glucose levels during the first 2 hours of life compared with newborns who did not receive it.
Trap: (C) is the mirror error, swapping which intervention carries the caveat. It pulls because leaving a wet newborn on the mother's chest sounds like the thermal risk, and the drying-cap-fresh-blanket sequence exists precisely to remove it.

Q: Which staffing does the professional organization recommend for each birth, and what is each nurse responsible for?
A) One nurse, who cares for both mother and newborn
B) At least two nurses, one responsible for the newborn and one assisting with delivery of the placenta and care of the mother; in multiple births, each baby has their own nurse
C) Two nurses, both assigned to the mother, with the provider assessing the newborn
D) One nurse plus a respiratory therapist at every birth
Answer: B
Rationale: "AWHONN (2022) recommends that at least two nurses be present for each birth. One nurse is responsible for care of the newborn, while the other helps the obstetric HCP with delivery of the placenta and care of the mother." The organization "also recommends that in cases of multiple births, each baby has their own nurse." If resuscitation is anticipated, a team able to perform it should be present.
Trap: (A) is the arrangement a busy unit defaults to, and it is what the recommendation exists to prevent — the third stage and the newborn transition happen simultaneously and each needs someone watching it.

Topic: third stage management
Subtopic: separation and its signs

Q: A student asks why a uterotonic is needed for the placenta to deliver. Which sentence from the chapter explains it?
A) The placenta separates when the cord is pulled with adequate traction
B) A placenta cannot detach itself from a flaccid, relaxed uterus, because the placental site is not reduced in size
C) The placenta separates when the cervix closes below it
D) Separation depends on maternal pushing effort alone
Answer: B
Rationale: The chapter's mechanism is that the placenta is anchored "much in the same way a postage stamp is attached to a sheet of postage stamps," and "after the birth of the fetus, strong uterine contractions and the sudden decrease in uterine size and volume cause the placental site to shrink," breaking the anchor villi. Hence: "A placenta cannot detach itself from a flaccid (relaxed) uterus because the placental site is not reduced in size."
Trap: (A) makes traction the mechanism rather than an adjunct, which is the setup for uterine inversion. Controlled cord traction is always paired with counterpressure "to support the uterus," and it presupposes a contracted uterus rather than substituting for one.

Q: Select all findings that suggest the onset of the third stage of labor.
A) A firmly contracting fundus
B) A change in the uterus from a discoid to a globular ovoid shape
C) A sudden gush of dark blood from the introitus
D) Apparent lengthening of the umbilical cord
E) An episode of vomiting with uncontrolled shivering
Answer: A, B, C, D
Rationale: Box 19.13 lists a firmly contracting fundus, the discoid-to-globular shape change as the placenta moves into the lower uterine segment, a sudden gush of dark blood from the introitus, apparent lengthening of the cord, and the finding of vaginal fullness or fetal membranes at the introitus.
Trap: (E) is the one wrong option and it belongs to the second stage. Both lists contain a bleeding change and a physical change, which is what makes them swappable; the third-stage list is about the uterus and the cord, while the second-stage list is about the woman's sensations and behavior.

Subtopic: passive versus active management

Q: A provider administers oxytocin immediately after the baby is born but before the placenta is expelled, then applies gentle continuous controlled cord traction with counterpressure. Which approach is this?
A) Expectant, or passive, management of the third stage
B) Active management of the third stage
C) Manual extraction of the placenta
D) Controlled cord traction without uterotonic support
Answer: B
Rationale: "When AMTSL is practiced, oxytocic medication (usually oxytocin) is administered immediately after the baby is born but before the placenta is expelled. Gentle continuous controlled umbilical cord traction and counterpressure are used to support the uterus until the placenta separates," and "immediately after the placenta is expelled, the uterine fundus is massaged."
Trap: (A) is the mirror of the next item, and the timing of the uterotonic is the entire difference between the two. Fundal massage happens in both approaches, always after expulsion, so massage is not the discriminator.

Q: A different provider watches for signs of separation, instructs the woman to push to expel the placenta, and then massages the fundus and gives oxytocin. Which approach is this, and what is the expected timing?
A) Active management; the placenta is expelled within 5 minutes
B) Expectant, or passive, management; the placenta is usually expelled within 15 minutes after the birth
C) Active management; the placenta is expelled within 15 minutes
D) Expectant management, which is the approach recommended by professional societies
Answer: B
Rationale: Passive management is "patiently watching for signs that the placenta has separated from the uterine wall spontaneously and monitoring for spontaneous expulsion"; the woman is instructed to push, "the placenta is usually expelled within 15 minutes after the birth of the baby," and "as soon as the placenta is expelled, the uterine fundus is massaged, and medication to contract the uterus (usually oxytocin [Pitocin]) is administered."
Trap: (D) is the mirror error on the recommendation. Active management is what the professional society recommends, and its benefits are "a shorter duration of third-stage labor, less risk for postpartum hemorrhage, and decreased risk for anemia" for both the woman and the newborn.

Q: According to the chapter's evidence-based practice box, which route of prophylactic oxytocin is more effective for preventing postpartum hemorrhage in the third stage?
A) Intramuscular, because absorption is more sustained
B) Intravenous, which can be given as a bolus after vaginal birth
C) The routes are equivalent
D) Intramuscular for vaginal birth and intravenous for cesarean
Answer: B
Rationale: "IV oxytocin is more effective than intramuscular injection for preventing postpartum hemorrhage in the third stage of labor and can be administered as an IV bolus after vaginal birth." The three most evidence-based interventions named are prophylactic oxytocin, delayed cord clamping, and controlled cord traction.
Trap: (A) constructs a pharmacologic rationale for the wrong answer, which is what students do when the fact itself is not recalled. Note the box's own trade-off on oxytocin: compared with no uterotonics it "may decrease blood loss and the requirement of additional uterotonics, but it may also prolong the third stage of labor."

Q: A nurse recovering a woman after birth notices increasing bleeding. Which immediate interventions does the evidence box direct all staff to be able to implement?
A) Emptying the bladder, massaging the uterus, and notifying the health care provider
B) Elevating the legs and administering oxygen
C) Starting a second intravenous line and drawing a type and cross
D) Placing the woman in Trendelenburg position and calling a rapid response
Answer: A
Rationale: Nurses "carefully assess the fundus and bleeding while recovering the immediate postpartum woman and are frequently the first to notice PPH," and all staff "should be able to identify excessive bleeding and implement the immediate interventions of emptying the bladder, massaging the uterus, and notifying the health care provider." Postpartum hemorrhage "is the major cause of maternal death worldwide."
Trap: (B) and (D) are the general shock interventions a student imports from a medical-surgical context, and neither addresses the cause. A full bladder displaces the uterus and prevents it contracting, which is why emptying it comes before anything else.

Topic: perineal trauma and episiotomy
Subtopic: general

Q: A laceration extends through the external anal sphincter and into the rectal mucosa. How is it classified, and why does the classification matter?
A) Third degree; it determines the suture material used
B) Fourth degree; third- and fourth-degree tears must be carefully repaired so the woman retains fecal continence
C) Second degree; it extends into the perineal body
D) Third degree; it involves the sphincter but spares the mucosa
Answer: B
Rationale: A first-degree laceration is "confined to the skin," a second "extends into the perineal body," a third "involves injury to the external anal sphincter muscle," and a fourth "extends completely through the anal sphincter and the rectal mucosa." Third- and fourth-degree lacerations "must be carefully repaired so that the woman will retain fecal continence."
Trap: (A) and (D) both stop at the sphincter, which is the discriminator between third and fourth degree. Once the rectal mucosa is involved the injury is fourth degree, and that boundary is the one the classification exists to mark.

Q: Which comparison of midline and mediolateral episiotomy is accurate?
A) Midline episiotomies are associated with a higher incidence of third- and fourth-degree lacerations; mediolateral episiotomies may be more painful
B) Mediolateral episiotomies are associated with a higher incidence of third- and fourth-degree lacerations; midline episiotomies may be more painful
C) Midline episiotomies carry both a higher laceration rate and more pain
D) The two are equivalent in every respect
Answer: A
Rationale: Midline episiotomies "are associated with a higher incidence of third- and fourth-degree lacerations," whereas mediolateral ones "may be more painful." The chapter leaves the comparison genuinely unresolved rather than declaring a winner.
Trap: (C) assigns both drawbacks to one type, which collapses a trade-off into a verdict, and it is the tidier-sounding answer for that reason. Each type has one disadvantage, and they are different ones.

Q: A woman asks whether an episiotomy will be performed routinely at her birth. Which response reflects current practice as the chapter describes it?
A) Routine episiotomy has no role in modern obstetric care and should be avoided whenever possible, though it may still be indicated in specific situations
B) Episiotomy is routine for all nulliparous women to prevent uncontrolled tearing
C) Episiotomy is indicated whenever a laceration appears likely
D) Episiotomy produces less blood loss and less postpartum pain than an intact perineum
Answer: A
Rationale: "Routine episiotomy has no role in modern obstetric care and should be avoided whenever possible. Indicated episiotomy, however, may still be performed in specific situations, such as the need to hasten birth when FHR abnormalities are present." Giving birth over an intact perineum yields "less blood loss, less risk for infection, and less postpartum pain."
Trap: (D) inverts which outcome favors which, and it survives on the intuition that a clean incision must heal better than a tear. The chapter attributes the better outcomes to the intact perineum. Prevention measures named include upright positioning, gentle open glottis pushing that coincides with the woman's natural urges, and massage with warm compresses during the second stage.
