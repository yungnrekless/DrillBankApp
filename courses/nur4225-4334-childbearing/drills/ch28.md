# Chapter 28 drills — Hemorrhagic Disorders

Authored from `sources/Ch28_Fact_Bank.md` (Lowdermilk, Cashion, Alden, Olshansky & Perry, 13th ed., ch. 28, pp. 600–617).

Blueprint weight is **~9 questions of 200 (4.5%), depth `full`** — topics *Early
Pregnancy Bleeding, molar pregnancy, ectopic, previa, abruption, DIC*. The Week 2
reading guide assigns about eleven of the chapter's eighteen pages, and those
eleven carry all nine questions.

The organizing insight the fact bank names is the spine of this set: **this
chapter is built out of paired look-alikes, and the exam lives on the pairs.**
Previa versus abruption is the explicit one and the book gives a whole table to
it, but complete versus partial mole, salpingectomy versus salpingostomy,
expectant versus active management, and clot formation versus bleeding in DIC are
the same move repeated. Each is drilled as a contrast, never as two separate
facts.

Every distinction the chapter states in both directions is drilled from both
ends: bright red painless bleeding and dark red painful bleeding, a soft uterus
and a boardlike one, visible blood loss and concealed blood loss, one sperm and
two, tube removed and tube preserved, ultrasound that rules a disorder in and
ultrasound that cannot rule one out, and cesarean as mandatory in previa versus
contraindicated in abruption with uncorrected coagulopathy.

**What is left out.** **Miscarriage and cervical insufficiency (pp. 600–605)** —
the part of "early pregnancy bleeding" the reading guide does not assign, and the
most likely place an exam would reach past the guide for this chapter. Also
omitted: cord insertion and placental variations including vasa previa, the two
Next-Generation case studies, and the Community Focus exercise.

Topic: bleeding in pregnancy overview
Subtopic: how bleeding is framed

Q: Which two hemorrhagic disorders does the chapter name as having the highest incidence of maternal mortality?
A) Miscarriage and cervical insufficiency
B) Ruptured ectopic pregnancy and placental abruption
C) Molar pregnancy and placenta previa
D) Placenta previa and disseminated intravascular coagulation
Answer: B
Rationale: "Ruptured ectopic pregnancy and abruptio placentae (placental abruption) have the highest incidence of maternal mortality." The chapter also states that "hemorrhagic disorders in pregnancy are medical emergencies."
Trap: (C) pairs the two disorders that bleed most visibly. Visible bleeding and mortality are not the same ranking — a ruptured ectopic and a severe abruption can both bleed internally.

Q: A woman at 22 weeks reports light vaginal bleeding and feels well otherwise. What does the chapter's key point direct?
A) Vaginal bleeding during pregnancy should always be regarded as a warning sign, and determining the cause is a priority
B) Light bleeding after 20 weeks is a normal variant requiring no workup
C) Bleeding is significant only when accompanied by pain
D) Bleeding is significant only in the first trimester
Answer: A
Rationale: The chapter's key point reads: "Vaginal bleeding during pregnancy should always be regarded as a warning sign, and determining the cause is a priority." Blood loss decreases oxygen-carrying capacity and puts the woman at risk for hypovolemia, anemia, infection, and preterm labor, and the fetus at risk for hypoxemia, hypoxia, anoxia, and preterm birth.
Trap: (C) is a reasonable-sounding filter that would miss placenta previa entirely, since previa bleeding is characteristically painless.

Q: Which disorders does the chapter list as the most common causes of bleeding in early pregnancy, and which as the major causes in late pregnancy?
A) Early: miscarriage, cervical insufficiency, ectopic pregnancy, and hydatidiform mole. Late: placenta previa and premature separation of the placenta
B) Early: placenta previa and abruption. Late: ectopic pregnancy and molar pregnancy
C) Early: DIC and abruption. Late: miscarriage and cervical insufficiency
D) Early and late causes are the same
Answer: A
Rationale: "The most common bleeding disorders of early pregnancy include miscarriage (spontaneous abortion), cervical insufficiency, ectopic pregnancy, and hydatidiform mole (molar pregnancy)," and "the major causes of bleeding in late pregnancy are placenta previa and premature separation of the placenta."
Trap: (D) collapses a split that the whole chapter is organized around, and gestational age is usually the first thing a stem tells you.

Topic: ectopic pregnancy presentation
Subtopic: where they implant

Q: Where are the vast majority of ectopic pregnancies located, and which part of that structure is most common?
A) The uterine tube, with approximately 70% in the ampulla
B) The ovary, with most on the surface
C) The abdominal cavity, with most near the omentum
D) The cervix, at the external os
Answer: A
Rationale: Ectopic pregnancies "are often called tubal pregnancies because the vast majority are located in the uterine (fallopian) tube," and "of all tubal ectopic pregnancies, approximately 70% are located in the ampulla, or largest portion of the tube." Ectopic pregnancies account for about 2% of all pregnancies in the United States and 3% of pregnancy-related maternal deaths.
Trap: (C) names a real but rare site. Fig. 28.3 gives the frequency order — ampulla, isthmus, interstitium, fimbria, tuboovarian ligament, ovary, abdominal cavity, cervix — and abdominal pregnancies are "thought to be the result of early tubal rupture, followed by reimplantation."

Q: Which factors does the chapter name as increasing the risk for ectopic pregnancy? Select all that apply.
A) Previous ectopic pregnancy
B) Abnormality of the fallopian tube and previous genital infections
C) Infertility and assisted reproductive technology such as in vitro fertilization
D) Smoking, and contraceptive methods such as previous tubal ligation or an intrauterine device
E) Grand multiparity with rapid successive births
Answer: A, B, C, D
Rationale: The listed risk factors are a previous ectopic pregnancy, tubal abnormality, previous genital infections, infertility, smoking, and assisted reproductive technology or certain contraceptive methods including previous tubal ligation and IUDs. The relationship runs both ways: tubal damage raises ectopic risk, and an ectopic pregnancy leaves the woman at risk for later fertility problems.
Trap: (E) sounds obstetric but is not on the list. Every real item on it is about the tube — its patency, its past infections, or an intervention performed on it.

Subtopic: the classic triad and rupture

Q: A woman presents with dull right lower quadrant pain, a period that was 10 days late and lighter than usual, and dark brown intermittent spotting. What should the nurse suspect?
A) Ectopic pregnancy, which classically presents with abdominal pain, delayed menses, and abnormal vaginal bleeding
B) Placenta previa
C) Molar pregnancy
D) Normal early pregnancy
Answer: A
Rationale: The three most classic symptoms are abdominal pain, delayed menses, and abnormal vaginal bleeding or spotting. "Abdominal pain occurs in almost every case. It usually begins as a dull, lower quadrant pain on one side," and most women report a period "delayed 1 to 2 weeks or lighter than usual, or an irregular period," with mild-to-moderate dark red or brown intermittent bleeding.
Trap: (D) is what this presentation is most often mistaken for, which is why the chapter sets a screening rule: "every woman with abdominal pain, vaginal spotting or bleeding, and a positive pregnancy test should undergo screening for ectopic pregnancy."

Q: A woman with a suspected ectopic pregnancy develops sharp shoulder pain along with lower abdominal pain. What does the shoulder pain indicate?
A) Referred pain from a musculoskeletal strain
B) Diaphragmatic irritation caused by blood in the peritoneal cavity, indicating rupture
C) A cardiac event
D) Normal round ligament pain
Answer: B
Rationale: "Referred shoulder pain results from diaphragmatic irritation caused by blood in the peritoneal cavity." It is a sign of rupture, appearing with generalized or deep lower quadrant acute abdominal pain.
Trap: (D) is the benign explanation a nurse might reach for in early pregnancy, and here it would delay recognition of an intraabdominal hemorrhage.

Q: A woman with a ruptured ectopic pregnancy is faint and dizzy but has only scant vaginal bleeding. How should the nurse interpret this?
A) Her symptoms are disproportionate and probably anxiety-related
B) Signs of shock relate to the amount of bleeding in the abdominal cavity and not necessarily to frank vaginal bleeding
C) Scant bleeding rules out significant hemorrhage
D) Shock cannot occur without visible bleeding
Answer: B
Rationale: The woman "may exhibit signs of shock such as faintness and dizziness related to the amount of bleeding in the abdominal cavity and not necessarily related to frank vaginal bleeding." Cullen sign — "an ecchymotic blueness around the umbilicus" — indicates hemoperitoneum, and signs of internal bleeding include vertigo, shoulder pain, hypotension, and tachycardia.
Trap: (C) uses visible blood as the measure of blood loss, which is the same error that makes a severe concealed abruption look stable later in this chapter.

Topic: ectopic pregnancy diagnosis
Subtopic: the discriminatory zone

Q: A woman with abdominal pain has a beta-hCG level of 2200 milli-international units/mL, and transvaginal ultrasound shows no intrauterine pregnancy. How should this be interpreted?
A) An ectopic pregnancy is very likely, because a normal intrauterine pregnancy should be visible above the discriminatory level
B) The pregnancy is too early to visualize and no action is needed
C) The findings confirm a molar pregnancy
D) The beta-hCG is too low to interpret
Answer: A
Rationale: "The term discriminatory zone addresses the concept that there is a beta-hCG level above which a normal intrauterine pregnancy should be visible on ultrasound," given as greater than 1500 to 2000 milli-international units/mL. Above 1500 with no intrauterine pregnancy seen, "an ectopic pregnancy is very likely." Serial levels may be redrawn every 48 hours to determine viability.
Trap: (B) is the correct reasoning below the discriminatory level and the wrong reasoning above it — which is the entire point of having a threshold.

Q: A serum progesterone level returns at 4 ng/mL in a woman being evaluated for possible ectopic pregnancy. What does this suggest, and what value would point the other way?
A) It suggests an ectopic or abnormal intrauterine pregnancy; a level greater than 25 ng/mL almost always rules out an ectopic pregnancy
B) It rules out ectopic pregnancy; a level greater than 25 ng/mL would confirm one
C) It is a normal value with no diagnostic meaning
D) It confirms a viable intrauterine pregnancy
Answer: A
Rationale: A progesterone level "less than 6 ng/mL suggests either an ectopic pregnancy or an abnormal intrauterine pregnancy," while a level "greater than 25 ng/mL almost always rules out the presence of an ectopic pregnancy."
Trap: (B) inverts the two thresholds. High progesterone is reassuring here, which runs against the instinct that a higher number means a worse result.

Subtopic: examination caution

Q: Why does the chapter direct that a vaginal examination be performed only once and with great caution in a suspected ectopic pregnancy?
A) Because approximately 20% of women with a tubal pregnancy have a palpable mass, and rupturing the mass during bimanual examination is possible
B) Because the examination is too painful to repeat
C) Because repeat examinations cause infection
D) Because the examination invalidates the beta-hCG result
Answer: A
Rationale: "A vaginal examination should be performed only once and with great caution. Approximately 20% of women with a tubal pregnancy have a palpable mass on examination. Rupturing the mass is possible during a bimanual examination; thus, a gentle touch is critical."
Trap: (B) is true as far as comfort goes and misses the reason, which is that the examination itself can precipitate the emergency being screened for.

Q: A woman with abdominal pain, vaginal spotting, and a positive pregnancy test is being evaluated. Which conditions appear in the chapter's differential for ectopic pregnancy? Select all that apply.
A) Miscarriage
B) Ruptured corpus luteum cyst and ovarian cysts
C) Appendicitis and salpingitis
D) Torsion of the ovary and urinary tract infection
E) Placenta previa
Answer: A, B, C, D
Rationale: The differential named is "miscarriage, ruptured corpus luteum cyst, appendicitis, salpingitis, ovarian cysts, torsion of the ovary, and urinary tract infection." The most important screening tools are quantitative beta-hCG levels and transvaginal ultrasound.
Trap: (E) is a late-pregnancy disorder and cannot be in an early-pregnancy differential — placenta previa bleeding is a second- and third-trimester event.

Topic: methotrexate therapy for ectopic pregnancy
Subtopic: who is eligible and what it does

Q: Which findings make a woman with an ectopic pregnancy a candidate for methotrexate therapy? Select all that apply.
A) She is hemodynamically stable with normal liver and kidney function
B) No fetal cardiac activity is noted on ultrasound
C) The tube size is less than 4 cm
D) The initial serum beta-hCG level is less than 5000 milli-international units/mL
E) The tube has already ruptured
Answer: A, B, C, D
Rationale: "The woman must be hemodynamically stable and have normal liver and kidney function to be eligible for methotrexate therapy," with best results when there is no fetal cardiac activity, the tube is under 4 cm, and the initial beta-hCG is under 5000. She must also be willing to comply with posttreatment restrictions and monitoring.
Trap: (E) describes the surgical situation. Methotrexate is a medical alternative to surgery for an unruptured, stable ectopic pregnancy; rupture removes the option.

Q: A woman treated with methotrexate asks for a stronger pain medication than acetaminophen. What does the Safety Alert direct, and why?
A) Any analgesic may be given as long as it is not an NSAID
B) She should refrain from taking any analgesic stronger than acetaminophen, because stronger analgesics can mask symptoms of tubal rupture
C) Opioids are preferred for this pain
D) No analgesia is permitted at all
Answer: B
Rationale: "Women receiving methotrexate to treat an ectopic pregnancy should refrain from taking any analgesic stronger than acetaminophen. Stronger analgesics can mask symptoms of tubal rupture." The paired teaching point runs the other way: she must report severe abdominal pain immediately as a sign of impending or actual rupture.
Trap: (C) is ordinary good pain management applied to the one situation where pain is the monitoring parameter. Treating the pain away removes the warning.

Subtopic: teaching after methotrexate

Q: Which instructions belong in the teaching for a woman receiving methotrexate for an ectopic pregnancy? Select all that apply.
A) Avoid foods and vitamins containing folic acid
B) Avoid sun exposure
C) Avoid sexual intercourse until the beta-hCG level is undetectable
D) Avoid gas-forming foods
E) Take a daily prenatal vitamin to support recovery
Answer: A, B, C, D
Rationale: Box 28.2's instructions include avoiding folic acid foods and vitamins, gas-forming foods, sun exposure, and intercourse until beta-hCG is undetectable, and keeping all follow-up appointments. Methotrexate "is an antimetabolite and folic acid antagonist that destroys rapidly dividing cells."
Trap: (E) would supply the folic acid the drug antagonizes, which is exactly what instruction A exists to prevent — and prenatal vitamins are the most natural thing in the world to keep taking.

Q: What should the nurse teach about methotrexate excretion after an intramuscular dose?
A) Urine contains drug metabolite at levels considered toxic for approximately 72 hours, highest during the first 8 hours; avoid getting urine on the toilet seat and double flush with the lid down; stools may contain residual drug for up to 7 days
B) The drug is fully excreted within 2 hours and no precautions are needed
C) Only stool precautions are needed, for 24 hours
D) Precautions are required for 30 days
Answer: A
Rationale: Box 28.2 teaches that "urine contains levels of drug metabolite that could be considered toxic for approximately 72 hours after receiving methotrexate. The levels are highest during the first 8 hours after treatment," with double flushing with the lid down, and that "her stools may contain residual drug for up to 7 days."
Trap: (B) treats an intramuscular dose as though it were gone once the injection is over. Methotrexate is a hazardous, high-alert drug, which is also why it is prepared in the pharmacy under a biologic safety cabinet and given with two pairs of gloves and an independent double check.

Q: What follow-up does the chapter specify after methotrexate treatment, and how long does resolution take?
A) A beta-hCG level on posttreatment days 4 and 7 and then weekly, until undetectable; complete resolution usually occurs within 7 weeks
B) A single beta-hCG level at 6 weeks
C) Monthly beta-hCG levels for 1 year
D) No follow-up is needed once pain resolves
Answer: A
Rationale: "It is common to check a beta-hCG level on posttreatment days 4 and 7 and then weekly thereafter to ensure that it continues to drop steadily until it becomes undetectable. Complete resolution of an ectopic pregnancy usually occurs within 7 weeks." The pregnancy itself "is absorbed by the body over the next 4 to 6 weeks."
Trap: (C) is the molar-pregnancy schedule, which does run about a year. The two hCG follow-ups are different lengths for different reasons: ectopic resolution versus surveillance for gestational trophoblastic neoplasia.

Topic: surgical management of ectopic pregnancy
Subtopic: choosing the procedure

Q: A woman with an unruptured tubal pregnancy who wants future children is scheduled for surgery. Which procedure fits, and what is done with the incision?
A) Salpingectomy, removing the entire tube, with the incision closed by primary intention
B) Salpingostomy, with an incision made over the pregnancy site and the products of conception removed, and the incision left to close by secondary intention because this results in less scarring
C) Salpingostomy, with the incision closed with fine sutures
D) Hysterectomy
Answer: B
Rationale: "If the tube has not ruptured and the woman desires future fertility," a salpingostomy is performed: "an incision is made over the pregnancy site in the tube, and the products of conception are gently and very carefully removed," and the incision is "not sutured but left to close by secondary intention instead, given that this method results in less scarring."
Trap: (C) is the surgically intuitive answer and the specific detail the chapter contradicts. Surgical choice depends on "the location and cause of the ectopic pregnancy, the extent of tissue involvement, and the woman's desires regarding future fertility."

Q: What perioperative nursing care does the chapter specify for a woman with a ruptured ectopic pregnancy?
A) Vital signs every 15 minutes or as needed according to the severity of bleeding, preoperative blood type and Rh status, complete blood count and quantitative beta-hCG, and Rh immune globulin postoperatively if appropriate
B) Vital signs every 4 hours and a type and screen only
C) No preoperative laboratory work is needed in an emergency
D) Rh immune globulin is given only if the woman is Rh positive
Answer: A
Rationale: "Before surgery, vital signs (pulse, respirations, and blood pressure [BP]) are assessed every 15 minutes or as needed, according to the severity of the bleeding and the woman's condition." Preoperative labs are blood type and Rh status, complete blood cell count, and serum quantitative beta-hCG, and the nurse "administers Rh immune globulin postoperatively if appropriate."
Trap: (D) reverses the Rh indication. Immune globulin protects an Rh-negative woman from sensitization; an Rh-positive woman has nothing to be sensitized to.

Q: What follow-up teaching applies to every woman after an ectopic pregnancy, regardless of treatment?
A) Use a contraceptive method for at least three menstrual cycles, and contact the provider as soon as she suspects she is pregnant because of the increased risk for recurrent ectopic pregnancy
B) Avoid pregnancy for 1 year
C) No contraception is needed since fertility is reduced
D) Report to the emergency department for any future positive pregnancy test
Answer: A
Rationale: "A contraceptive method should be used for at least three menstrual cycles to allow time for the woman's body to heal," and "every woman who has a history of ectopic pregnancy should be instructed to contact her health care provider as soon as she suspects that she might be pregnant because of the increased risk for recurrent ectopic pregnancy."
Trap: (C) assumes reduced fertility means no contraception is needed, when the chapter's point is the opposite — the next pregnancy needs early evaluation, not avoidance.

Topic: molar pregnancy types
Subtopic: complete versus partial

Q: An ultrasound shows a snowstorm pattern with no fetus, placenta, amniotic membranes, or fluid, and karyotyping shows 46XX. Which type of molar pregnancy is this, and how did it arise?
A) A complete mole, from fertilization of an egg whose nucleus has been lost or inactivated, with the sperm nucleus duplicating itself
B) A partial mole, from one ovum fertilized by two or more sperm
C) A complete mole, from one ovum fertilized by two sperm
D) A partial mole, from an empty egg
Answer: A
Rationale: "The complete mole results from fertilization of an egg in which the nucleus has been lost or inactivated," with the sperm nucleus duplicating itself to the diploid 46XX or 46XY, or an empty egg fertilized by two normal sperm. "The complete mole contains no fetus, placenta, amniotic membranes, or fluid," and the mole "resembles a bunch of white grapes."
Trap: (B) describes the partial mole. The cue that survives is to count the sperm and look for fetal parts: one duplicated sperm and no fetus is complete.

Q: Karyotyping of a molar pregnancy shows triploidy, 69XXY, and the specimen contains embryonic parts and an amniotic sac. Which type is this, and what accompanies it?
A) A complete mole, with a high risk for persistent gestational trophoblastic neoplasia
B) A partial mole, in which congenital anomalies, severe growth restriction, or both are usually present, with a lower risk for persistent GTN than a complete mole
C) A complete mole with an unusual karyotype
D) A normal twin pregnancy
Answer: B
Rationale: "In a partial mole, one apparently normal ovum is fertilized by two or more sperm," producing "triploidy (69XXY) or quadraploidy (92XXXY)." "Partial moles often have embryonic or fetal parts and an amniotic sac," with congenital anomalies and severe growth restriction usually present, and "the risk for persistent GTN is less than with a complete mole."
Trap: (A) is the mirror error of the previous item, and the karyotype settles it: a diploid 46 chromosome count is complete, triploid or quadraploid is partial.

Q: Why may a partial mole be missed clinically?
A) Because partial moles cause few of the symptoms of a complete mole and may be mistaken for an incomplete or missed miscarriage
B) Because partial moles do not produce hCG
C) Because partial moles never bleed
D) Because ultrasound cannot detect a partial mole
Answer: A
Rationale: "Partial moles cause few of these symptoms and may be mistaken for an incomplete or missed miscarriage." The complete mole produces the fuller picture — a uterus larger than dates, heavy bleeding, hyperemesis, and early preeclampsia.
Trap: (B) would remove the very marker the follow-up schedule is built on. Trophoblastic tissue secretes hCG in both types, which is what makes serial levels meaningful.

Q: What does the chapter say about the incidence and risk factors for hydatidiform mole?
A) It occurs in 1 in 1000 pregnancies in the United States, with higher risk after a prior molar pregnancy, at the extremes of reproductive age, and among Asian, Hispanic, and Native American women
B) It occurs in 1 in 100 pregnancies, with risk limited to women over 40
C) It occurs in 1 in 10,000 pregnancies, with no identified risk factors
D) It occurs only in first pregnancies
Answer: A
Rationale: "Hydatidiform mole occurs in 1 in 1000 pregnancies in the United States." At increased risk are "those who have had a prior molar pregnancy and those who are at the extremes of age for reproduction," and "Asian, Hispanic, and Native American women are more likely than women of other races/ethnicities to develop hydatidiform mole." The cause is unknown, possibly related to an ovular defect or a nutritional deficiency.
Trap: (B) keeps one true risk factor — advanced age — and drops the other end of the same factor. The risk sits at both extremes of reproductive age, not only the upper one.

Topic: molar pregnancy presentation and management
Subtopic: recognizing a mole

Q: A woman at 18 weeks has a blood pressure of 158/98 mm Hg with proteinuria, a uterus larger than expected for dates, and dark brown vaginal discharge. What should be suspected?
A) Early-onset preeclampsia requiring magnesium sulfate alone
B) Hydatidiform mole, because preeclampsia diagnosed before 24 weeks should prompt suspicion of a molar pregnancy
C) Placental abruption
D) A normal multifetal pregnancy
Answer: B
Rationale: "Preeclampsia occurs in approximately 70% of women with large, rapidly growing hydatidiform moles and occurs earlier than usual in the pregnancy. If preeclampsia is diagnosed before 24 weeks of gestation, hydatidiform mole should be suspected and ruled out."
Trap: (A) treats the hypertension as the diagnosis when the chapter treats it as a clue. Hyperthyroidism is the other serious complication, and "treatment of the hydatidiform mole restores thyroid function to normal."

Q: Which findings are characteristic of a complete hydatidiform mole? Select all that apply.
A) Vaginal bleeding that may be dark brown, resembling prune juice, or bright red
B) A uterus usually larger than indicated by menstrual dates, although some women have a smaller uterus
C) Anemia, excessive nausea and vomiting, and abdominal cramps from uterine distention
D) Passage of vesicles that are frequently avascular edematous villi
E) hCG levels that fall earlier than in a normal pregnancy
Answer: A, B, C, D
Rationale: Vaginal bleeding occurs in almost all cases, the discharge may be dark brown or bright red, the uterus is usually larger than dates, and anemia, hyperemesis, and cramping are relatively common. Women may also pass vesicles from the uterus.
Trap: (E) reverses the hormone pattern: "in a molar pregnancy, hCG levels are persistently high or rise beyond the time they would begin to decline in a normal pregnancy." That persistence is what the year of follow-up watches.

Q: What is the most accurate tool for diagnosing a hydatidiform mole, and what does it show?
A) Transvaginal ultrasound, showing a characteristic snowstorm pattern of multiple diffuse intrauterine masses
B) Serum progesterone
C) Abdominal radiography
D) Amniocentesis
Answer: A
Rationale: "Transvaginal ultrasound is the most accurate tool for diagnosing a hydatidiform mole," and "a characteristic pattern of multiple diffuse intrauterine masses, often called a snowstorm pattern, is seen in place of or along with an embryo or a fetus."
Trap: (D) would sample a cavity that, in a complete mole, has no amniotic fluid at all.

Subtopic: evacuation

Q: A molar pregnancy is diagnosed and evacuation is planned. Which method is used, and which is contraindicated?
A) Suction curettage is used; induction of labor with oxytocic agents or prostaglandin is not recommended because of the increased risk for embolization of trophoblastic tissue
B) Oxytocin induction is preferred; suction curettage is contraindicated
C) Hysterectomy is required in all cases
D) Expectant management only, since moles never abort spontaneously
Answer: A
Rationale: "Although most moles abort spontaneously, suction curettage offers a safe, rapid, and effective method of evacuating a hydatidiform mole if necessary," and "induction of labor with oxytocic agents or prostaglandin is not recommended because of the increased risk for embolization of trophoblastic tissue." Older women who desire sterilization may undergo hysterectomy instead.
Trap: (C) takes a discussed option and makes it universal. Hysterectomy is an alternative for a woman who wants sterilization, not the standard evacuation.

Topic: molar pregnancy follow-up
Subtopic: the surveillance year

Q: What beta-hCG follow-up schedule does the chapter specify after evacuation of a molar pregnancy?
A) Weekly measurements until the level is normal and remains normal for 3 consecutive weeks, then monthly for 6 to 12 months, with the follow-up period usually continuing for 1 year
B) A single level at 6 weeks
C) Weekly levels for 3 weeks only
D) Monthly levels for 3 months
Answer: A
Rationale: The schedule is "weekly measurements of the beta-hCG level until the level decreases to normal and remains normal for 3 consecutive weeks. Monthly measurements are then taken for 6 to 12 months. The follow-up assessment period usually continues for 1 year." Rising levels and an enlarging uterus during that time may indicate gestational trophoblastic neoplasia.
Trap: (C) stops at the first milestone. The three normal weeks end the weekly phase; they do not end surveillance.

Q: Why must pregnancy be avoided during the molar follow-up period, and which contraceptive does the chapter prefer?
A) Because rising hCG levels that are normal in pregnancy would obscure levels indicating GTN; combination oral contraceptives are preferred, and IUDs should not be used until beta-hCG levels are undetectable
B) Because pregnancy causes recurrence of the mole; an IUD is preferred immediately
C) Because contraception is needed only for 3 months; any method is acceptable
D) Because hormonal contraception is contraindicated after a mole
Answer: A
Rationale: "To avoid confusion in regard to rising levels of hCG that are normal in pregnancy but could indicate GTN, pregnancy should be avoided during the follow-up assessment period." And: "IUDs should not be used until beta-hCG levels are undetectable. Combination oral contraceptives are preferred because they are highly effective." Depo-Provera and a progestin implant are practical options for women who have difficulty with daily dosing.
Trap: (D) rules out the method the chapter prefers. The concern with the IUD is timing relative to hCG, not hormones.

Q: What residual risk remains after a molar pregnancy has been evacuated?
A) None, once the uterus is empty
B) Risk for recurrence or development of an invasive mole or choriocarcinoma, which is why surveillance continues
C) Risk limited to infertility
D) Risk only if the mole was partial
Answer: B
Rationale: "After the molar pregnancy has been evacuated, the woman is still at risk for recurrence or the development of invasive mole or choriocarcinoma." Molar pregnancy is one of the gestational trophoblastic neoplasias, which "have the potential for local invasion, distant metastasis, and death from disease."
Trap: (D) inverts the relative risk. The complete mole carries the higher risk for persistent GTN, and both types are followed.

Topic: comparing previa and abruption
Subtopic: the three discriminators

Q: According to the chapter's key point, which three findings differentiate placenta previa from placental abruption?
A) Type of bleeding, uterine tonicity, and presence or absence of pain
B) Gestational age, maternal age, and parity
C) Fetal position, amniotic fluid volume, and cervical dilation
D) Hemoglobin level, platelet count, and fibrinogen
Answer: A
Rationale: The chapter states that "placenta previa and placental abruption are differentiated by type of bleeding, uterine tonicity, and presence or absence of pain." Table 28.2 fills in the values: previa is bright red, painless, with a normal-tone uterus; abruption is dark red, painful, with an increased or hypertonic uterus.
Trap: (D) names laboratory findings that do separate the two — coagulopathy is "none" in previa and frequent in severe abruption — but they are confirmatory rather than the bedside discriminators the key point names.

Q: A woman at 34 weeks has sudden severe abdominal pain, a boardlike uterus, minimal dark red vaginal bleeding, and is becoming hypotensive and tachycardic. How should the nurse interpret the small amount of visible blood?
A) As evidence that blood loss is limited and the situation is stable
B) As consistent with a severe abruption, in which external bleeding may be absent to moderate while total blood loss exceeds 1500 mL because the bleeding is concealed
C) As evidence of placenta previa
D) As ruling out coagulopathy
Answer: B
Rationale: Table 28.2's grade 3 abruption column pairs external bleeding of "absent to moderate" with total blood loss of more than 1500 mL and shock that is "common, often sudden, profound." The chapter adds that in abruption, "shock may occur and is out of proportion to apparent blood loss."
Trap: (A) grades the emergency by what is visible, which is exactly what a concealed abruption defeats. An increasing fundal height is another sign of concealed bleeding.

Q: A woman with vaginal bleeding has a soft, nontender uterus with normal tone, a high presenting part, and a fetus in transverse lie, with a normal FHR tracing. Which disorder do these findings fit?
A) Placental abruption, grade 1
B) Placenta previa
C) Disseminated intravascular coagulation
D) Ruptured uterus
Answer: B
Rationale: Table 28.2 gives placenta previa a normal uterine tone, absent tenderness, a high presenting part that is not engaged, a fetal position "commonly transverse, breech, or oblique," a normally placed FHR pattern, and a placenta in the lower uterine segment. The abdominal findings described in the text match: "a soft, relaxed, nontender uterus with normal tone."
Trap: (A) is plausible because a mild abruption also has a normal-tone uterus and usually absent tenderness — but its bleeding is dark red and minimal, its placenta is in the upper segment, and the presenting part is variable to engaged rather than high.

Q: Which finding in the history would most raise suspicion for abruption rather than previa?
A) A previous cesarean birth
B) Chronic or gestational hypertension
C) Advanced maternal age
D) Carrying a male fetus
Answer: B
Rationale: "Maternal hypertension, whether chronic or pregnancy related, is the most consistently identified risk factor for abruption," and Table 28.2 lists hypertension as "commonly present" in grade 2 and grade 3 abruption while showing the usual distribution for previa.
Trap: (A), (C) and (D) are all previa risk factors — previous cesarean birth, advanced maternal age, and carrying a male fetus, the last "possibly because placental sizes are larger in pregnancies involving male fetuses."

Topic: placenta previa
Subtopic: types and presentation

Q: An ultrasound report describes a placenta whose edge lies 2 cm from the internal cervical os. How is this classified, and what term is used when the relationship has not been determined or the finding is in the second trimester?
A) Marginal placenta previa, with low-lying placenta used when the relationship is undetermined or the finding is in the second trimester
B) Complete placenta previa, with marginal used for an undetermined relationship
C) Low-lying placenta, with marginal used for an undetermined relationship
D) Vasa previa
Answer: A
Rationale: "In a marginal placenta previa the edge of the placenta is seen on transvaginal ultrasound to be 2.5 cm or closer to the internal cervical os." A previa is complete "if it totally covers the internal cervical os," and "when the exact relationship of the placenta to the internal cervical os has not been determined or in the case of apparent placenta previa in the second trimester, the term low-lying placenta is used."
Trap: (C) reverses the two terms. The 2.5 cm measurement is what makes a finding marginal; low-lying is the term used when there is no such measurement yet.

Q: A woman at 32 weeks has painless bright red vaginal bleeding. What does the chapter direct until proven otherwise, and what must not be done?
A) Assume placenta previa; no vaginal or rectal examinations are performed
B) Assume abruption; perform a vaginal examination to assess dilation
C) Assume labor; perform a sterile vaginal examination
D) Assume cervical insufficiency; place a cerclage
Answer: A
Rationale: "All women with painless vaginal bleeding after 20 weeks of gestation should be assumed to have a placenta previa until proven otherwise," and expectant management includes the absolute: "no vaginal or rectal examinations are performed, and the woman is told to avoid intercourse." Placenta previa is "typically characterized by painless, bright red vaginal bleeding during the second or third trimester."
Trap: (C) is the reflexive labor assessment and is the single most dangerous action in this stem, because a digital examination can perforate the placenta.

Q: A woman with placenta previa has lost a large amount of blood but her vital signs remain normal. How should the nurse interpret this?
A) Normal vital signs confirm that blood loss has been modest
B) A pregnant woman can lose up to 40% of her blood volume without showing signs of shock, so clinical presentation and decreasing urinary output may be better indicators of acute blood loss than vital signs alone
C) Vital signs are the most sensitive indicator of blood loss in pregnancy
D) Bleeding has stopped
Answer: B
Rationale: "Vital signs may be normal, even with heavy blood loss, because a pregnant woman can lose up to 40% of her blood volume without showing signs of shock. Clinical presentation and decreasing urinary output may be better indicators of acute blood loss than vital signs alone."
Trap: (A) and (C) both make vital signs the measure, which is the error this passage exists to prevent — and it is the mirror of the concealed-bleeding trap in abruption.

Subtopic: diagnosis and outcomes

Q: What imaging sequence does the chapter specify for suspected placenta previa?
A) Transabdominal ultrasound first, followed by transvaginal scan unless the transabdominal scan clearly shows the placenta is not in the lower uterine segment, because transvaginal scanning is better for accurately determining placental location
B) Transvaginal ultrasound only, because transabdominal scanning is contraindicated
C) Magnetic resonance imaging first
D) Digital examination followed by ultrasound
Answer: A
Rationale: "A transabdominal ultrasound examination should be performed initially followed by a transvaginal scan, unless the transabdominal scan clearly shows that the placenta is not located in the lower uterine segment. A transvaginal ultrasound scan is better than a transabdominal scan for accurately determining placental location."
Trap: (D) puts the contraindicated examination first. If the placenta is found to be normally implanted, a speculum examination may then be performed to rule out local causes of bleeding.

Q: Which complications does the chapter associate with placenta previa? Select all that apply.
A) Hemorrhage, which is the major maternal complication
B) Placenta accreta spectrum, an abnormally firm placental attachment that may require hysterectomy if bleeding cannot be controlled
C) Preterm birth as a major cause of perinatal morbidity and mortality
D) Blood transfusion reactions, anemia, venous thromboembolism, and infection
E) Maternal coagulopathy in most cases
Answer: A, B, C, D
Rationale: "The major maternal complication associated with placenta previa is hemorrhage," with placenta accreta spectrum as another serious complication, along with transfusion reactions, anemia, venous thromboembolism, infection, and the surgical and anesthetic risks of cesarean birth. Preterm birth is a major cause of perinatal morbidity and mortality.
Trap: (E) belongs to abruption. Table 28.2 lists coagulopathy as "none" for previa and as occasional to frequent DIC in grade 2 and grade 3 abruption.

Topic: previa care management
Subtopic: initial and expectant care

Q: A woman with placenta previa presents with acute bleeding at 31 weeks. Which initial measures does the chapter specify? Select all that apply.
A) Hospitalization in a labor and birth unit to assess maternal and fetal status
B) Large-bore intravenous access, 16- to 18-gauge, initiated immediately
C) Hemoglobin, hematocrit, platelet count, and coagulation studies, with a type and screen sample maintained at all times
D) Antenatal corticosteroids, since she is less than 34 weeks of gestation
E) A digital examination to assess whether labor has begun
Answer: A, B, C, D
Rationale: Women with acute bleeding are "initially hospitalized in a labor and birth unit," with large-bore IV access, the listed laboratory studies, a type and screen maintained in transfusion services, corticosteroids if under 34 weeks, and tocolytics if bleeding is associated with contractions.
Trap: (E) is the prohibited examination, and it remains prohibited no matter how urgent the situation feels.

Q: Which criteria must be met before a woman with placenta previa is managed at home? Select all that apply.
A) Her condition is stable with no vaginal bleeding for at least 24 hours before discharge
B) She is willing and able to comply with modified bed rest with bathroom privileges and pelvic rest
C) She lives within a short distance of the hospital and has constant access to transportation
D) She is able to keep all appointments for fetal testing, laboratory assessments, and prenatal care
E) She has completed a course of tocolytic therapy
Answer: A, B, C, D
Rationale: All four criteria are required together: stability with no bleeding for at least 24 hours, willingness and ability to comply with activity and pelvic rest, proximity to the hospital with constant transportation, immediate return if bleeding resumes, and the ability to keep all appointments.
Trap: (E) is not among the criteria. Tocolytics may be given when bleeding is associated with contractions, but completing a course is not what makes home care safe.

Q: A woman with confirmed placenta previa remains asymptomatic with normal fetal growth. When is birth planned, and by what route?
A) Cesarean birth between 36 and 37 weeks of gestation; cesarean birth is necessary for all women with ultrasound evidence of placenta previa
B) Vaginal birth at term if the previa is marginal
C) Cesarean birth at 39 weeks in every case
D) Induction of labor at 37 weeks
Answer: A
Rationale: "Cesarean birth should occur between 36 and 37 weeks of gestation if the woman remains asymptomatic, fetal growth is normal, and no other medical indications for birth are identified before that time," and "cesarean birth is necessary for all women who have ultrasound evidence of placenta previa and for most women with a low-lying placenta." If bleeding is excessive, the fetus is compromised, or the woman is laboring, immediate birth is indicated regardless of gestational age.
Trap: (C) applies the elective 39-week rule, which is overridden here — waiting to 39 weeks with a previa risks the massive bleeding the earlier date exists to avoid.

Q: After a cesarean birth for placenta previa, the fundus is firm at the umbilicus but bleeding continues. How should the nurse interpret this?
A) The bleeding cannot be uterine, since the fundus is firm
B) Postpartum hemorrhage may occur from a relaxed lower uterine segment even when the fundus is contracted firmly, because the lower segment lacks the interlacing muscle bundles that act as a living ligature
C) The bleeding indicates a cervical laceration only
D) A firm fundus rules out the need for further assessment
Answer: B
Rationale: "Blood loss may not stop with the birth of the infant. The large vascular channels in the lower uterine segment may continue to bleed because of the diminished muscle content of that segment. The natural mechanism to control bleeding characteristic of the upper part of the uterus ... is absent in the lower part of the uterus. Therefore postpartum hemorrhage may occur as a result of a relaxed lower uterine segment even if the fundus is contracted firmly."
Trap: (A) applies the standard postpartum assessment logic — firm fundus means uterine bleeding is controlled — to the one situation where the anatomy makes it false.

Topic: placental abruption
Subtopic: definition and risk

Q: How does the chapter define placental abruption, and when does it occur?
A) Detachment of part or all of a normally implanted placenta from the uterus, occurring in the decidua basalis after 20 weeks of gestation and before the birth of the infant
B) Implantation of the placenta over the cervical os
C) Detachment of an abnormally implanted placenta before 20 weeks
D) Separation of the placenta after the birth of the infant
Answer: A
Rationale: "Premature separation of the placenta, or abruptio placentae, is the detachment of part or all of a normally implanted placenta from the uterus," and "separation occurs in the area of the decidua basalis after 20 weeks of gestation and before the birth of the infant." The overall incidence is 1 in 100 births, and most cases occur between 26 and 37 weeks.
Trap: (B) defines previa. The word "normally implanted" is what separates the two definitions: abruption is a normally placed placenta that detaches, previa is an abnormally placed one that bleeds.

Q: Which risk factors does the chapter name for placental abruption? Select all that apply.
A) Maternal hypertension, chronic or pregnancy related
B) Cocaine or methamphetamine use, which causes vascular disruption in the placental bed
C) Penetrating or blunt abdominal trauma from motor vehicle accidents or intimate partner violence
D) Cigarette smoking, a previous abruption, uterine anomalies, and preterm prelabor rupture of membranes
E) Carrying a male fetus
Answer: A, B, C, D
Rationale: Maternal hypertension "is the most consistently identified risk factor for abruption." Stimulant use, trauma, smoking, prior abruption, uterine anomalies, and PPROM follow, and abruption is more likely in multiple gestations, "possibly related to rapid decompression of an overdistended uterus." Two previous abruptions carry a 25% recurrence risk.
Trap: (E) is a placenta previa risk factor. Sorting the two risk lists is often what a stem is really asking, since hypertension points one way and prior cesarean birth points the other.

Subtopic: recognizing and confirming it

Q: Which findings make up the classic presentation of placental abruption?
A) Vaginal bleeding, abdominal pain, uterine tenderness, and contractions, with mild-to-severe uterine hypertonicity and possibly a firm, boardlike abdomen
B) Painless bright red bleeding with a soft uterus
C) Shoulder pain and a palpable adnexal mass
D) Persistent vomiting and a uterus larger than dates
Answer: A
Rationale: "Classic symptoms of placental abruption include vaginal bleeding, abdominal pain, uterine tenderness, and contractions. Mild-to-severe uterine hypertonicity is present. Pain is mild to severe and localized over one region of the uterus or spread diffusely over the uterus resulting in a firm, boardlike abdomen."
Trap: (B) is the previa presentation and is the contrast this whole chapter is built on. (C) is a ruptured ectopic pregnancy, and (D) is a molar pregnancy.

Q: A woman with a severe abruption is taken to surgery, and the uterus appears purple or blue rather than its usual color, with loss of contractility. What is this called, and what causes it?
A) Couvelaire uterus, from extensive myometrial bleeding that damages the uterine muscle
B) Bandl ring, from obstructed labor
C) Uterine rupture
D) Normal intraoperative appearance
Answer: A
Rationale: "If blood accumulates between the separated placenta and the uterine wall, it may produce a Couvelaire uterus," in which "the uterus appears purple or blue rather than its usual bubble-gum pink color, and contractility is lost," following extensive myometrial bleeding that damages the muscle.
Trap: (B) is the pathologic retraction ring of obstructed labor from a different chapter, and it is a different kind of finding entirely — a shape, not a color change.

Q: An ultrasound in a woman with sudden severe localized uterine pain shows no retroplacental mass. How should this result be interpreted?
A) Abruption is ruled out
B) Negative findings do not rule out a life-threatening abruption; at least 50% of abruptions cannot be identified on ultrasound, and abruption is primarily a clinical diagnosis
C) The pain must be musculoskeletal
D) A repeat scan in 24 hours will be definitive
Answer: B
Rationale: "Although ultrasound can be used to rule out placenta previa, it cannot detect all cases of abruption. A retroplacental mass may be detected with ultrasound examination, but negative findings do not rule out a life-threatening abruption. In fact, at least 50% of abruptions cannot be identified on ultrasound." Placental abruption "is primarily a clinical diagnosis."
Trap: (A) is the reassurance a negative scan invites and the specific conclusion the chapter forbids. Ultrasound rules previa in and rules abruption neither in nor out.

Q: What does the Kleihauer-Betke test contribute in a woman with placental abruption?
A) It confirms the diagnosis of abruption
B) It detects fetal-to-maternal bleeding and is of no diagnostic value for abruption itself, but it guides Rh immune globulin therapy in Rh-negative women
C) It measures the degree of coagulopathy
D) It estimates the volume of concealed hemorrhage
Answer: B
Rationale: The Kleihauer-Betke test is ordered "to determine the presence of fetal-to-maternal bleeding (transplacental hemorrhage)," "although it is of no diagnostic value" for the abruption itself. Its real use is "to guide Rh immune globulin therapy in Rh-negative women who have had an abruption."
Trap: (C) is what the clotting studies do — fibrinogen, platelet count, partial thromboplastin time, and fibrin split products — and those are separate tests answering a separate question.

Topic: abruption management and birth
Subtopic: expectant versus active

Q: A woman at 30 weeks has a mild abruption, and both she and the fetus are stable. What does expectant management involve?
A) Close monitoring because the abruption may worsen at any time, serial growth assessment for growth restriction, nonstress tests or biophysical profiles, and corticosteroids to accelerate fetal lung maturity
B) Immediate cesarean birth
C) Discharge home with weekly visits
D) Tocolysis until 37 weeks in all cases
Answer: A
Rationale: Expectant management applies when the fetus is between 20 and 34 weeks and both the woman and the fetus are stable, and consists of close monitoring "because the abruption may worsen at any time," serial growth assessment, antepartum testing, and corticosteroids.
Trap: (C) misjudges how unstable the situation can become. The chapter's own caution is that the abruption may worsen at any time, which is why expectant management here is inpatient and intensive.

Q: A woman at 38 weeks has a moderate-to-severe abruption with a nonreassuring tracing. Which measures belong to active management? Select all that apply.
A) At least one large-bore intravenous line
B) Frequent vital signs watching for an increasing pulse rate and decreasing blood pressure
C) Serial hematocrit and hemoglobin levels and clotting studies
D) Continuous electronic fetal monitoring and an indwelling catheter, since urine output is an excellent indirect measure of maternal organ perfusion
E) Oral hydration and ambulation to promote labor progress
Answer: A, B, C, D
Rationale: Active management is used when the fetus is at or near term or the bleeding is moderate to severe and the woman or fetus is in jeopardy: at least one large-bore IV line, frequent vital signs, serial hematology and clotting studies, mandatory continuous EFM, an indwelling catheter because urine output is "an excellent indirect measure of maternal organ perfusion," and fluid and blood product replacement.
Trap: (E) belongs to a normal labor. A woman actively hemorrhaging is not being ambulated, and oral hydration does not replace the volume being lost.

Q: A woman with a severe abruption has uncorrected coagulopathy. What does the chapter say about the route of birth?
A) Cesarean birth is always required in abruption
B) Vaginal birth is usually preferable, and cesarean birth should not be attempted with severe and uncorrected coagulopathy because it can result in uncontrollable bleeding
C) Cesarean birth should be performed immediately regardless of coagulation status
D) Vaginal birth is contraindicated in all abruptions
Answer: B
Rationale: "Although vaginal birth is usually preferable, cesarean birth may become necessary," and "cesarean birth should not be attempted when the woman has severe and uncorrected coagulopathy because it can result in uncontrollable bleeding."
Trap: (A) and (C) apply the previa rule, where cesarean birth is necessary for every woman with ultrasound evidence of previa. The two disorders invert each other on this point, which is why it is worth holding as a pair.

Q: What determines the mother's prognosis after a placental abruption?
A) The extent of placental detachment, overall blood loss, degree of coagulopathy present, and the time that passes between placental detachment and birth
B) Maternal age alone
C) The gestational age alone
D) Whether the abruption was visible on ultrasound
Answer: A
Rationale: "The mother's prognosis depends on the extent of placental detachment, overall blood loss, degree of coagulopathy present, and the time that passes between placental detachment and birth." Large retroplacental hemorrhages of more than 60 mL carry a fetal mortality rate of 50% or greater, and retroplacental hematomas have a worse fetal prognosis than subchorionic hemorrhages.
Trap: (D) makes prognosis depend on a test the chapter says misses at least half of these cases.

Topic: disseminated intravascular coagulation
Subtopic: what dic is and what triggers it

Q: A nurse documents DIC as a patient's primary diagnosis. Why is this incorrect?
A) Because DIC is never a primary diagnosis; it results from some event that triggered coagulation
B) Because DIC is a laboratory artifact
C) Because DIC occurs only after birth
D) Because DIC is a synonym for postpartum hemorrhage
Answer: A
Rationale: "DIC is never a primary diagnosis; instead, it results from some event that triggered coagulation." It is "an acquired syndrome characterized by intravascular activation of coagulation that is widespread, rather than localized, and results in both excessive clot formation and hemorrhage," and is sometimes called consumptive coagulopathy or defibrination syndrome.
Trap: (D) collapses a cause and a consequence. Postpartum hemorrhage is one of the events that can trigger DIC, not another name for it.

Q: A woman with DIC is bleeding from her IV site, her gums, and her nose. What does the chapter say about the relationship between bleeding and clotting in DIC?
A) DIC is purely a bleeding disorder
B) Although bleeding is often the presenting sign of DIC, it is always preceded by clot formation, with widespread fibrin clots depleting clotting factors
C) Clotting occurs only after bleeding has been treated
D) The two processes are unrelated
Answer: B
Rationale: "Although bleeding is often the presenting sign of DIC, it is always preceded by clot formation." Tissue factor released from damaged tissues activates circulating clotting factors, resulting in "the widespread formation of fibrin clots within blood vessels, leading to depletion of clotting factors and excessive bleeding."
Trap: (A) is what the bedside picture suggests and is the reason the word consumptive matters — the bleeding exists because the clotting already consumed the factors.

Q: What does the chapter name as the most frequent obstetric trigger of DIC, and which other causes appear in Box 28.3? Select all that apply.
A) Placental abruption, the most frequent trigger through release of large amounts of tissue factor
B) Preeclampsia or eclampsia and HELLP syndrome
C) Amniotic fluid embolism and postpartum hemorrhage
D) Sepsis, acute fatty liver of pregnancy, and a retained intrauterine fetal demise
E) Gestational diabetes
Answer: A, B, C, D
Rationale: "In the obstetric population, DIC is most often triggered by the release of large amounts of tissue factor as a result of placental abruption." Box 28.3 lists placental abruption, preeclampsia or eclampsia and HELLP syndrome, amniotic fluid embolism, postpartum hemorrhage, sepsis, acute fatty liver of pregnancy, and retained intrauterine fetal demise.
Trap: (E) is not on the list, and every item that is on it involves either massive tissue injury or a source of tissue factor — placenta, amniotic fluid, or necrotic tissue.

Subtopic: findings and management

Q: Which laboratory results would the nurse expect in a woman with DIC? Select all that apply.
A) Decreased platelet count
B) Decreased fibrinogen
C) Prolonged prothrombin time and activated partial thromboplastin time
D) Increased fibrin degradation products and increased D-dimer
E) Increased platelet count with decreased D-dimer
Answer: A, B, C, D
Rationale: Box 28.4 gives the pattern: platelet count decreased, fibrinogen decreased, prothrombin time prolonged, activated partial thromboplastin time prolonged, fibrin degradation products increased, and D-dimer increased.
Trap: (E) is the manufactured inverse of the correct set, and it is attractive only if DIC is remembered as "a clotting problem" without the consumption that defines it.

Q: Which physical findings does Box 28.4 list in women with DIC?
A) Bleeding from at least three unrelated sites, spontaneous epistaxis, oozing from venipuncture sites, petechiae, ecchymosis, large subcutaneous hematomas, hypotension, and tachycardia, along with signs of thrombosis such as peripheral cyanosis and renal impairment
B) Bleeding from a single site only
C) Hypertension and bradycardia
D) Fever and rash
Answer: A
Rationale: Box 28.4 lists signs of thrombosis — peripheral cyanosis, renal impairment, drowsiness, confusion, coma, cardiorespiratory failure — alongside bleeding from at least three unrelated sites, spontaneous epistaxis, oozing from venipuncture sites, petechiae such as on the arm where a blood pressure cuff was placed, ecchymosis, large subcutaneous hematomas, hypotension, and tachycardia.
Trap: (B) misses what makes the picture diagnostic. Bleeding from one site is a local problem; bleeding from several unrelated sites at once is what says the coagulation system itself has failed.

Q: A woman develops DIC after a placental abruption. What is the first priority in medical management?
A) Administration of recombinant activated factor VII
B) Correction of the underlying cause, such as removal of the abrupted placenta or the dead fetus or treatment of infection or preeclampsia
C) Administration of heparin to stop the clotting cascade
D) Transfusion of platelets alone
Answer: B
Rationale: "Medical management in all cases of DIC involves correction of the underlying cause (e.g., removal of an abrupted placenta or the dead fetus or treatment of existing infection or preeclampsia/eclampsia)." Volume expansion, rapid replacement of blood products and clotting factors, optimizing oxygenation, achieving normal body temperature, and continued laboratory reassessment follow.
Trap: (A) and (D) are real components of treatment that cannot succeed while the trigger keeps releasing tissue factor. DIC "usually is 'cured' with the birth and as coagulation abnormalities resolve."

Q: Which nursing measures apply to a woman with DIC before birth? Select all that apply.
A) Monitoring urine output through an indwelling catheter, maintained at 30 mL/h or more, because renal failure is a consequence of DIC
B) Continuous electronic fetal monitoring
C) Maintaining the woman in a side-lying tilt to maximize blood flow to the uterus
D) Oxygen by nonrebreather face mask at 10 L/min per protocol or provider order
E) Encouraging ambulation to prevent venous stasis
Answer: A, B, C, D
Rationale: "Because renal failure is one consequence of DIC, urinary output is monitored closely by using an indwelling catheter. Urinary output must be maintained at 30 mL/h or more." And: "If DIC develops before birth, continuous EFM is necessary. The woman should be maintained in a side-lying tilt to maximize blood flow to the uterus," with oxygen at 10 L/min by nonrebreather mask per protocol.
Trap: (E) applies thromboprophylaxis logic to a woman who is actively hemorrhaging, and nursing care here includes "protecting the woman from injury" — the opposite of mobilizing her.
