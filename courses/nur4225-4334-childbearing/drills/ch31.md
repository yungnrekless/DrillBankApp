# Chapter 31 drills — Mental Health Disorders and Substance Abuse

Authored from `sources/Ch31_Fact_Bank.md` (Lowdermilk, Cashion, Alden, Olshansky & Perry, 13th ed., ch. 31, pp. 666–682).

Blueprint weight is **~6 questions of 200 (3.0%), depth `standard`** — topic
*Substance Use, PPD, PTSD, Screening Tools, Detox*. The Week 1–4 midterm reading
guide does not name this chapter, so the midterm does not reach it; the blueprint
weights it at six, so the final does. The blueprint gives one compound topic
naming five things, and each gets its own coverage here: **45 drills across 11
topics**.

The organizing insight the fact bank names is the spine of this set: **these
conditions are missed unless someone screens for them, and screening only works
if the follow-up is built in.** The book says it directly about depression — "the
effectiveness of screening is related to the follow-up for positive screening
results" — and repeats the structure for substance use, where the recommendation
is universal screening precisely because assumptions based on "age, race, or
socioeconomic status" fail.

Every distinction the chapter states in both directions is drilled from both
ends: postpartum blues and postpartum depression, depression and psychosis by
whether the woman acts on her thoughts, obsessive thoughts in obsessive-compulsive
disorder and in psychosis, methadone and buprenorphine, and agonist therapy
versus medically supervised withdrawal.

**What is left out.** Table 31.1 on antidepressants and Table 31.2 on mood
stabilizers and antipsychotics, the psychotropic-prescribing discussion, and
postpartum psychosis and bipolar disorder beyond the distinction that separates
them from depression. The blueprint names screening and detox rather than
psychotropic prescribing, and Chapter 30 already carries the overlapping
medication content.

Topic: postpartum blues versus depression
Subtopic: telling them apart

Q: A woman 4 days after birth has mood swings, tearfulness, difficulty sleeping, and loss of appetite, but is caring for her infant and herself without difficulty. How should the nurse interpret this?
A) As postpartum blues, a mild and transient depression occurring in 50% to 70% of women, in which functioning is not usually impaired and symptoms typically subside within a few days to 2 weeks; treatment is not needed
B) As postpartum depression requiring immediate medication
C) As postpartum psychosis
D) As a normal finding needing no follow-up at all
Answer: A
Rationale: Postpartum blues is "characterized by mood swings, feelings of sadness and anxiety, crying, difficulty sleeping, and loss of appetite; the woman's functioning is not usually impaired." It occurs "in 50% to 70% of women after birth," symptoms "typically subside within a few days to 2 weeks," and "treatment is not needed."
Trap: (D) is close but drops the surveillance, and the whole chapter turns on the follow-up: symptoms that persist past 2 weeks or that impair functioning are a different diagnosis.

Q: What distinguishes postpartum depression from postpartum blues?
A) Depression involves an intense and pervasive sadness with labile mood swings that can be severe, is more serious and persistent, lasts more than 2 weeks, and impairs functioning, affecting approximately 9% to 24% of women
B) Depression is milder and shorter
C) Depression occurs only after the sixth week
D) They are the same condition with different names
Answer: A
Rationale: Postpartum depression is "an intense and pervasive sadness with labile mood swings that can be severe," is "more serious and persistent," lasts "more than 2 weeks," and is "experienced by approximately 9% to 24% of women," with a recent meta-analysis reporting "a 12% incidence ... among healthy women who gave birth to healthy infants."
Trap: (D) collapses a distinction with different treatment implications — blues need nothing, depression needs support, psychotherapy, or medication by severity.

Subtopic: why it goes unreported

Q: Why does the chapter say a new mother's emotional distress often goes unaddressed by those around her?
A) Because birth is usually thought to be a happy event, so a new mother's emotional distress can puzzle and immobilize family and friends — when she most needs caring attention, they may either criticize or withdraw
B) Because families are indifferent
C) Because women rarely have symptoms
D) Because symptoms are always obvious and acted on
Answer: A
Rationale: "Because birth is usually thought to be a happy event, a new mother's emotional distress can puzzle and immobilize family and friends. When she most needs the caring attention of loved ones, they may either criticize or withdraw." The chapter states the stakes plainly: failure to assess, intervene, and refer "can result in tragic consequences such as suicide or infanticide."
Trap: (B) attributes to indifference what the chapter attributes to confusion, and the difference matters for how the nurse involves the family.

Q: What does the chapter say about partners and postpartum mood disorders?
A) Fathers or partners can experience postpartum blues or depression, although there is a lack of research evidence surrounding these topics
B) Only birth mothers are affected
C) Partners are affected more often than mothers
D) Partner symptoms are always secondary to the mother's
Answer: A
Rationale: "Fathers or partners can experience postpartum blues or depression, although there is a lack of research evidence surrounding these topics."
Trap: (B) excludes a population the chapter includes, and the conditions themselves "can interfere with attachment to the newborn and family integration" regardless of who has them.

Topic: screening tools for perinatal mood disorders
Subtopic: the edinburgh scale

Q: What is the most widely used screening tool in perinatal populations, and how is it structured and scored?
A) The Edinburgh Postnatal Depression Screen, comprising 10 statements about common symptoms of depression, with the woman choosing the response closest to how she has felt for the past week; the maximum score is 30, and women scoring 12 or higher may possibly have depression and need further assessment
B) The Patient Health Questionnaire–9, with a maximum score of 27 and a cutoff of 5
C) The Edinburgh scale, with 20 items and a cutoff of 20
D) A single question about mood
Answer: A
Rationale: "In perinatal populations the most widely used screening tool is the Edinburgh Postnatal Depression Screen (EPDS)." It "comprises 10 statements about the common symptoms of depression. The woman is asked to choose the response that is closest to describing how she has felt for the past week." And: "A maximum score on the EPDS is 30; women with scores of 12 or higher may possibly have depression and need further assessment."
Trap: (C) inflates both the item count and the cutoff, and the cutoff is what determines who gets further assessment.

Q: Which item on the Edinburgh scale requires special handling, and what else does the tool detect?
A) One item addresses suicidal thoughts, and responses to this item should be carefully examined, with positive responses warranting further assessment and prompt intervention; the tool is often used to screen for anxiety disorders because it includes three items specific to a woman's experience of anxiety
B) The sleep item, which must be scored twice
C) The appetite item, which is excluded from the total
D) No item requires special handling
Answer: A
Rationale: "One item on the tool addresses suicidal thoughts; responses to this item should be carefully examined. Positive responses warrant further assessment and prompt intervention." And: "The EPDS is often used to screen for anxiety disorders: it includes three items that are specific to a woman's experience of anxiety."
Trap: (D) treats the total score as the only output, when a positive response to one item can require action regardless of the total.

Subtopic: when and how screening works

Q: When should screening for perinatal depression occur? Select all that apply.
A) Before discharge from the birth setting, where postpartum nurses can screen for depression and anxiety disorders
B) At routine follow-up visits
C) By primary care providers during pediatric care visits for the infant
D) At the infant's 1-, 2-, and 4-month visits, per the AAP recommendation
E) Only once, at the 6-week postpartum visit
Answer: A, B, C, D
Rationale: Postpartum nurses can screen before discharge, "although this screening identifies some who are at risk, it is important that follow-up screening is also done" at routine follow-up visits and by primary care providers during pediatric visits. "The AAP recommends maternal depression screening at the infant's 1-, 2-, and 4-month visits."
Trap: (E) is a single point in a schedule the chapter deliberately spreads across months, because "the onset of PPD most often occurs within the first few months after birth."

Q: What does the chapter say determines whether screening is effective?
A) The effectiveness of screening is related to the follow-up for positive screening results; if results are positive or self-report shows signs of a psychiatric disorder, a formal screening is needed so that appropriate referrals can be made and treatment initiated promptly
B) The choice of tool determines effectiveness
C) The timing alone determines effectiveness
D) Screening is effective regardless of what follows
Answer: A
Rationale: "The effectiveness of screening is related to the follow-up for positive screening results. If screening results are positive or if the woman's self-report shows signs that she might be experiencing symptoms of a psychiatric disorder, a formal screening is needed so that appropriate referrals can be made and treatment can be initiated promptly."
Trap: (D) makes screening a completed task rather than a first step, which is the specific failure the sentence is written against.

Q: Which tools does the chapter name for anxiety and for posttraumatic stress disorder?
A) For anxiety: the Perinatal Anxiety Screening Scale, the Generalized Anxiety Disorder Scale–7, and the Patient Health Questionnaire–9; for PTSD: interviews, the Edinburgh scale, and a targeted tool such as the Primary Care PTSD Screen
B) Only the Edinburgh scale for both
C) No specific tools exist for either
D) Only clinical judgment is used
Answer: A
Rationale: The anxiety tools named are "the Perinatal Anxiety Screening Scale, the Generalized Anxiety Disorder Scale–7, and the Patient Health Questionnaire–9," and "screening for posttraumatic stress disorder (PTSD) is accomplished through interviews, use of the EPDS, and a targeted screening tool such as the Primary Care PTSD Screen." Screening is recommended by ACOG, AWHONN, the American Psychiatric Association, and the US Preventive Services Task Force.
Trap: (B) relies on a depression tool alone for conditions that have their own instruments, though the Edinburgh scale does contribute to both.

Topic: postpartum depression risk and etiology
Subtopic: what raises the risk

Q: What does the chapter identify as the most significant risk factor for postpartum depression?
A) History of psychiatric disorders before or during pregnancy, with family history of psychiatric disorders also possibly increasing the risk
B) Cesarean birth
C) Maternal age over 35
D) Breastfeeding difficulty
Answer: A
Rationale: "History of psychiatric disorders before or during pregnancy is the most significant risk factor for PPD; family history of psychiatric disorders may also increase the risk." The chapter adds: "While there is no single predictor of PPD, researchers have identified multiple risk factors."
Trap: (B) is on the risk list — along with hyperemesis gravidarum, preeclampsia, gestational hypertension, and preterm birth — but it is not the strongest, and the history is what should prompt anticipatory screening.

Q: A woman had postpartum depression after her first birth. What is her risk of recurrence?
A) Up to 70% of women with a history of postpartum depression will have a subsequent episode
B) About 10%
C) About 25%
D) Recurrence is no more likely than a first episode
Answer: A
Rationale: "Up to 70% of women with a history of PPD will have a subsequent episode."
Trap: (D) would remove the reason for identifying the history in prenatal care, and a 70% recurrence rate makes the next pregnancy a planned surveillance problem rather than a hopeful one.

Q: Which additional risk factors for postpartum depression does the chapter name? Select all that apply.
A) Physical, psychological, or sexual abuse; intimate partner violence; and lack of social support
B) Unmarried status, marital discord, divorce, low socioeconomic status, and financial hardship
C) Age under 24 or over 35, and unintended pregnancy
D) Personal history of severe premenstrual dysphoria, and complications of pregnancy and birth
E) Exclusive breastfeeding
Answer: A, B, C, D
Rationale: The chapter lists abuse and intimate partner violence, lack of social support, unmarried status, marital discord, divorce, low socioeconomic status, financial hardship, death of a loved one, natural disasters, mass conflict, age under 24 or over 35, unintended pregnancy, severe premenstrual dysphoria, and pregnancy and birth complications.
Trap: (E) is not among them, and the list's breadth is the point — it spans biology, relationships, and circumstance.

Subtopic: etiology

Q: What hormonal change does the chapter identify as an important etiologic factor in postpartum depression?
A) The change from the high levels of estrogen and progesterone at the end of pregnancy to the much lower levels of both after birth
B) Rising estrogen after birth
C) Rising progesterone after birth
D) Hormones play no role
Answer: A
Rationale: "The change from the high levels of estrogen and progesterone at the end of pregnancy to the much lower levels of both hormones that are present after birth is an important etiologic factor." Other contributors named are "changes in cortisol, oxytocin, melatonin, and thyroid hormone after birth; neuroinflammatory processes; and abnormal levels or activity of neurotransmitters (dopamine, norepinephrine, serotonin)," plus possible deficiencies of vitamin D, vitamin B12, folate, and omega-3 fatty acids.
Trap: (B) and (C) reverse the direction of the postpartum hormone shift, which Chapter 20 establishes as a steep fall reaching its lowest point about a week after birth.

Topic: recognizing postpartum depression
Subtopic: the symptoms

Q: Which symptoms are common in postpartum depression?
A) Fatigue, sleep disturbance, appetite disturbance, and irritability, with anxiety often prominent — either as a symptom of the depression or as a sign of a comorbid anxiety disorder
B) Euphoria and increased energy
C) Hallucinations in most cases
D) Isolated physical complaints with no mood change
Answer: A
Rationale: "Fatigue, sleep disturbance, appetite disturbance, and irritability are common. Anxiety is often a prominent symptom of PPD, or it can be a sign of a comorbid anxiety disorder." The chapter notes that "a majority of women have comorbidities such as generalized anxiety disorder."
Trap: (B) describes mania, and the chapter requires that "all women who present with depressive symptoms should be screened for bipolar disorder" precisely so that this is not missed.

Q: A woman with postpartum depression says she feels detached from her baby and does not feel love for him, and is ashamed to say so. How should the nurse understand this?
A) As a common symptom: feelings of detachment toward the newborn or not feeling love for the newborn are common symptoms of postpartum depression, and women feel guilt and shame for having these feelings and are not likely to verbalize or discuss them with anyone
B) As evidence that she should not care for the infant
C) As a rare finding indicating psychosis
D) As a sign she does not have depression
Answer: A
Rationale: "Feelings of detachment toward the newborn or not feeling love for the newborn are common symptoms of PPD. Women feel guilt and shame for having these feelings and are not likely to verbalize or discuss this with anyone. Many women feel especially guilty about having depressive feelings at a time when they believe they should be happy."
Trap: (B) responds to a disclosure with a consequence, which is exactly what makes women withhold it — and the chapter also notes that some women with depression "feel very emotionally attached and connected to their infants," and that connection "can prevent them from experiencing total despair."

Q: What two things does the chapter require be ruled out in a woman presenting with depressive symptoms after birth?
A) Bipolar disorder, since all women presenting with depressive symptoms should be screened for it, and medical causes such as thyroid disease
B) Anemia and infection
C) Substance use only
D) Nothing needs to be ruled out
Answer: A
Rationale: "All women who present with depressive symptoms should be screened for bipolar disorder," and "medical causes (e.g., thyroid disease) of depressive symptoms should be ruled out."
Trap: (D) would start an antidepressant in a woman whose symptoms might be thyroid disease or the depressed phase of bipolar disorder — two conditions for which that treatment is wrong.

Topic: ppd versus postpartum psychosis
Subtopic: thoughts of harm

Q: A woman with postpartum depression reports frightening obsessive thoughts about harming her infant. How does the chapter distinguish this from postpartum psychosis?
A) A woman with postpartum depression may have obsessive thoughts about harming the infant that frighten her, but she does not act on those thoughts and is vigilant about preventing any action that would bring harm to her infant; women experiencing postpartum psychosis are likely to act on their thoughts
B) Any thought of harming the infant indicates psychosis
C) Women with depression act on their thoughts and women with psychosis do not
D) The two cannot be distinguished clinically
Answer: A
Rationale: "A woman with PPD may have obsessive thoughts about harming the infant, and this can be very frightening to her. However, she does not act on those thoughts and is vigilant about preventing any action that would bring harm to her infant. In contrast, women experiencing postpartum psychosis are likely to act on their thoughts."
Trap: (B) is the response that punishes disclosure and drives the thoughts underground, and it is the reason the distinction is stated so explicitly.

Q: How is care for postpartum depression matched to severity?
A) Women with mild symptoms may need only support and counseling; psychotherapy may be needed for moderate to severe symptoms; and when nonpharmacologic treatment alone is not effective, medications may be used, often in combination with psychotherapy
B) All women require medication
C) Medication is used first in every case
D) Psychotherapy is reserved for women who refuse medication
Answer: A
Rationale: "Women with mild symptoms may need only support and counseling. Psychotherapy may be needed for moderate to severe symptoms, and in some cases, when nonpharmacological treatment alone is not effective, medications may be used, often in combination with psychotherapy."
Trap: (C) inverts the sequence, and the named psychotherapies — cognitive behavioral therapy and interpersonal psychotherapy — are increasingly delivered as internet-based programs and mobile applications, which widens access rather than narrowing it.

Topic: anxiety disorders and ptsd
Subtopic: the anxiety disorders

Q: A postpartum woman worries constantly and excessively about her infant's well-being, with muscle tension, restlessness, insomnia, and difficulty concentrating. Which disorder does this describe?
A) Generalized anxiety disorder, characterized by excessive and pervasive worrying, restlessness, inability to relax, difficulty concentrating, distress about making decisions, and worry out of proportion to the impact of the event
B) Panic disorder
C) Obsessive-compulsive disorder
D) Posttraumatic stress disorder
Answer: A
Rationale: Generalized anxiety disorder is "characterized by excessive and pervasive worrying, restlessness, inability to relax, difficulty concentrating, distress about making decisions, and obsession over things that are out of proportion to the impact of the event." The chapter adds: "The issues of concern to people with GAD are realistic, but the level of worry is much more intense than appropriate. Postpartum women may worry excessively about the well-being of their infants." Physical symptoms include muscle tension, irritability, restlessness, insomnia, fatigue, headache, nausea, diarrhea, and abdominal pain.
Trap: (B) is a different pattern entirely — episodic rather than continuous, with "the rapid onset of intense fear (typically peaking at 10 minutes)."

Q: A woman has episodes of sudden intense fear with palpitations, sweating, shortness of breath, dizziness, and a fear of losing control or dying, and has begun staying home. What has developed?
A) Panic disorder, with agoraphobia — fear of certain places and situations — which develops when panic disorder is untreated, so that women tend to remain at home
B) Generalized anxiety disorder
C) Postpartum psychosis
D) Obsessive-compulsive disorder
Answer: A
Rationale: Panic symptoms are "characterized by the rapid onset of intense fear (typically peaking at 10 minutes)," with "palpitations, sweating, shortness of breath, choking, nausea, abdominal discomfort, dizziness, unsteadiness, numbness or tingling, chills, or hot flashes," plus "a fear of losing control or fear of dying. There may or may not be an identifiable trigger." Untreated, women "may experience agoraphobia ..., and as a result, they tend to remain at home."
Trap: (C) escalates an anxiety disorder to a psychotic one, and the presence of an identifiable panic pattern with intact reality testing is what separates them.

Q: A woman with obsessive-compulsive disorder has intrusive thoughts about harming her infant. What does the chapter say about the risk?
A) Although the thoughts are very disturbing, there is no increased risk of actual harm being inflicted
B) The risk of harm is high and the infant should be removed
C) These thoughts indicate psychosis
D) These thoughts indicate a need for immediate hospitalization in every case
Answer: A
Rationale: In obsessive-compulsive disorder women may have "disturbing intrusive thoughts that are repetitive, unacceptable, and/or unwanted," themed around "contamination, causing harm, urges for symmetry and order, religious preoccupations, or offensive violent or sexual images," and "women may have obsessive thoughts about harming their infants; although the thoughts are very disturbing, there is no increased risk of actual harm being inflicted."
Trap: (B) is the reaction that costs a mother her infant over a symptom that carries no added risk, and it parallels the depression-versus-psychosis distinction exactly.

Subtopic: ptsd

Q: Which obstetric experiences does the chapter associate with a higher prevalence of posttraumatic stress disorder?
A) Preterm birth, preeclampsia with severe features, obstetric hemorrhage, emergency cesarean birth, stillbirth, or an infant in the neonatal intensive care unit
B) Only stillbirth
C) Only cesarean birth
D) No obstetric experiences are associated
Answer: A
Rationale: "PTSD has been found to be more prevalent in women who have experienced severe complications in pregnancy or during birth such as preterm birth, preeclampsia with severe features, obstetric hemorrhage, emergency cesarean birth, stillbirth, or an infant in the neonatal intensive care unit." It "can be triggered by traumatic birth experiences, especially in women who have a history of trauma (e.g., childhood abuse, intimate partner violence, rape), although new-onset PTSD can occur after a difficult birth."
Trap: (B) narrows the list to the most obviously traumatic event, and the chapter's point is that a survived emergency can be traumatic too — which is why Chapter 33 makes debriefing after hemorrhage part of nursing care.

Q: Which symptoms characterize posttraumatic stress disorder as the chapter describes it?
A) Reliving the traumatic event through involuntary memories, flashbacks, and recurrent nightmares, with persistent avoidance of stimuli associated with the event, plus difficulty sleeping, irritability, angry outbursts, difficulty concentrating, hypervigilance, and an exaggerated startle response
B) Euphoria and grandiosity
C) Compulsive rituals only
D) Panic attacks peaking at 10 minutes
Answer: A
Rationale: The chapter lists "reliving the traumatic event through involuntary memories, flashbacks, recurrent nightmares, and persistent avoidance of stimuli associated with the event," along with "difficulty sleeping, irritability, angry outbursts, difficulty concentrating, hypervigilance, and an exaggerated startle response." Depression "is a common comorbidity of PTSD."
Trap: (D) names panic disorder's signature, and the avoidance and re-experiencing pair is what makes the diagnosis PTSD.

Q: What is the first-line intervention for anxiety disorders in the perinatal period?
A) Psychotherapy, with common methods including trauma-focused cognitive behavioral therapy, exposure therapy, and eye movement desensitization and reprocessing; pharmacologic therapy may be used for women who prefer it or who face barriers to accessing psychotherapy, and the two are frequently combined
B) Benzodiazepines in all cases
C) Antidepressants alone
D) No treatment is recommended in the perinatal period
Answer: A
Rationale: "Psychotherapy is usually the first-line intervention for anxiety disorders; common methods include trauma-focused CBT, exposure therapy, and eye movement desensitization and reprocessing." For women who prefer medication and those with barriers to accessing psychotherapy, "pharmacologic therapy may be used. Psychotherapy and pharmacotherapy are frequently used in combination." SSRIs and SNRIs are often used, and benzodiazepines may treat short-term anxiety.
Trap: (B) starts with the class the chapter reserves for short-term use, and the nonpharmacologic strategies named — education, music therapy, breathing exercises, progressive muscle relaxation, guided imagery, and cognitive strategies — sit alongside all of it.

Topic: perinatal substance use disorder
Subtopic: recognition and screening

Q: Which warning signs in prenatal care may suggest a substance use disorder? Select all that apply.
A) Receiving no prenatal care, late entry into care, or sporadic care with multiple missed appointments
B) Keeping prenatal appointments but leaving without being seen
C) Noncompliance with recommended treatment
D) Evidence of poor nutrition, frequent encounters with law enforcement officials, or marital and family disputes
E) Attending every scheduled appointment
Answer: A, B, C, D
Rationale: The chapter names no prenatal care, late entry, sporadic care with missed appointments, leaving without being seen, noncompliance with recommended treatment, evidence of poor nutrition, frequent encounters with law enforcement, and marital and family disputes.
Trap: (E) would make attendance a rule-out, and the chapter's screening recommendation is universal for exactly that reason.

Q: Why does the chapter recommend universal screening for substance use in pregnancy?
A) Because substance use is prevalent in all populations, providers should never make assumptions based on age, race, or socioeconomic status, and universal screening is nonbiased and nonstigmatizing
B) Because it is legally required in all states
C) Because only high-risk women need screening
D) Because screening replaces the need for treatment referral
Answer: A
Rationale: "Universal screening for substance use in pregnant women is recommended. Because substance use is prevalent in all populations, providers should never make assumptions based on age, race, or socioeconomic status. Universal screening is nonbiased and nonstigmatizing."
Trap: (B) states a legal uniformity the chapter contradicts: "the legal implications of testing and the need for consent from the pregnant woman can vary among states," so providers "should be aware of local laws and legislative changes."

Subtopic: how substances reach the fetus

Q: How does the chapter describe the direct effects of drugs on the fetus by timing?
A) Early in gestation, drugs can cause pregnancy loss or significant structural abnormalities; during the fetal period, after major structural development is complete, drugs can affect the developing central nervous system; and throughout pregnancy, maternal drug use may result in fetal death
B) Drugs affect only the first trimester
C) Drugs affect only the third trimester
D) Timing does not affect the outcome
Answer: A
Rationale: "Early in gestation, drugs can cause pregnancy loss (miscarriage) or significant structural abnormalities in the fetus. During the fetal period, after major structural development is complete, drugs can affect the developing CNS. Throughout pregnancy, maternal drug use may result in fetal death."
Trap: (D) would make gestational age irrelevant, when the chapter uses it to explain why the same substance produces anomalies at one point and neurodevelopmental effects at another.

Q: What indirect effects on the fetus does the chapter name?
A) Altered delivery of nutrition to the fetus, from placental insufficiency or inadequate maternal diet, plus decreased access to and compliance with health care, increased exposure to violence, and increased risk for mental illness and infection
B) Only nutritional effects
C) There are no indirect effects
D) Only effects on the placenta
Answer: A
Rationale: The indirect effects are "altered delivery of nutrition to the fetus, either because of placental insufficiency or inadequate maternal diet," plus "decreased access to and/or compliance with health care, increased exposure to violence, and increased risk for mental illness and infection."
Trap: (C) would leave the nurse addressing only pharmacology, when several of the indirect effects are the ones nursing care can actually change.

Q: Which effects does the chapter attribute to specific substances? Select all that apply.
A) Tobacco: ectopic pregnancy, orofacial clefts, growth restriction, placenta previa, placental abruption, low birth weight, preterm prelabor rupture of membranes, and increased perinatal mortality
B) Alcohol: significantly increased risk for birth defects, with no amount at any time in pregnancy considered safe, plus increased risk for low birth weight and preterm birth
C) Cocaine: increased incidence of preterm labor, prelabor rupture of membranes, placental abruption, and preeclampsia, with growth restriction and low birth weight
D) Methamphetamines: low birth weight, microcephaly, decreased length, preterm birth, placental abruption, and congenital malformations of the brain and heart
E) Marijuana: an established safe threshold below which no effects occur
Answer: A, B, C, D
Rationale: The chapter lists each of these. Marijuana is "the most commonly used illicit drug in pregnancy," and "no safe threshold limits for marijuana use have been determined," with use associated with intrauterine growth restriction.
Trap: (E) inverts the marijuana finding, and the legal status is a separate matter — it is legal in several states while remaining "an illegal substance under federal law."

Q: A woman reports a small amount of alcohol use in early pregnancy before she knew she was pregnant. What counseling does the chapter support?
A) That low-level consumption of alcohol in early pregnancy is not an indication for pregnancy termination, but she should be advised to stop to minimize further risk
B) That termination should be considered
C) That continued light drinking is safe
D) That no counseling is needed
Answer: A
Rationale: The chapter states that "no amount of alcohol use at any time during pregnancy is considered safe," and also that low-level consumption in early pregnancy "is not an indication for pregnancy termination," though women "should be advised to stop to minimize further risk."
Trap: (C) is contradicted by the first half of that pair, and (B) by the second — the chapter holds both positions at once, and the counseling has to as well.

Topic: barriers and the nurse's stance
Subtopic: what keeps women from care

Q: Which barriers to substance use treatment does the chapter name? Select all that apply.
A) Social stigma, labeling, and guilt
B) Fear of losing custody of a child or children, or of criminal prosecution
C) Negative feedback from society and health care providers, who may condemn women for endangering the fetus and withhold support
D) Treatment programs that have not addressed the concurrent need for obstetric care and childcare for other children, plus long waiting lists and lack of health insurance
E) An excess of available treatment slots
Answer: A, B, C, D
Rationale: The chapter names stigma, labeling and guilt, fear of custody loss and prosecution, provider condemnation and withheld support, and system barriers including programs that do not address obstetric care or childcare, long waiting lists, and lack of insurance. Women with co-occurring disorders "face unique barriers because of the social stigma attached to both conditions and insufficient knowledge and training for health care providers."
Trap: (E) reverses the access problem, and the chapter's inclusion of provider behavior among the barriers is the item most easily overlooked.

Q: Which laboratory work does the chapter anticipate for a pregnant woman with a substance use disorder, and why may an early ultrasound be needed?
A) Screening for syphilis, hepatitis B and C, and HIV, a complete blood count, a skin test for tuberculosis, and testing for gonorrhea and chlamydia; an early ultrasound may be needed to determine gestational age because she may have had amenorrhea from her substance use or no idea when her last menstrual period occurred
B) Only a urine drug screen
C) Only a complete blood count
D) No additional laboratory work is needed
Answer: A
Rationale: Because these women "are at risk for a variety of infections and medical conditions," the workup includes screening for "syphilis, hepatitis B and C, and human immunodeficiency virus (HIV)," plus "a complete blood count and a skin test to screen for tuberculosis" and testing for gonorrhea and chlamydia. An early ultrasound may be needed "to determine gestational age because the woman may have had amenorrhea as a result of her substance use or have no idea when her last menstrual period occurred."
Trap: (B) reduces the workup to the test that carries the legal risk and misses the infections that carry the clinical one.

Q: Which principles does Box 31.3 give for caring for pregnant women with substance use disorder? Select all that apply.
A) Realize that the decision to become and remain sober can only be made by the individual with the disorder
B) Understand that nurses do not have the power to cure anyone — they serve as educators, supporters, and advocates
C) Treat women with substance use disorder with the same respect and consideration shown to other people, and become familiar with local treatment centers and which accept pregnant women
D) Remember that there are no hopeless cases, practice patience and persistence, and recognize it may take months or years to see the effects of your work
E) Withhold care until the woman agrees to enter treatment
Answer: A, B, C, D
Rationale: Box 31.3 gives each of these principles, and the chapter's framing sentence is that "nurses must understand that SUD is an illness and that women with SUD deserve to be treated with patience, kindness, consistency, and firmness when necessary."
Trap: (E) is the withholding of support the chapter names as a barrier, and it is the behavior Box 31.3 is written to replace.

Topic: smoking cessation and alcohol withdrawal
Subtopic: tobacco

Q: What does the chapter say about the timing and effectiveness of smoking cessation in pregnancy?
A) Women who quit smoking by the first trimester have infants whose growth is comparable to those born to nonsmokers, cessation programs during pregnancy are effective and should be offered to all pregnant smokers, and approximately 50% to 60% of women who quit during pregnancy tend to relapse within 1 year of giving birth
B) Quitting has no measurable benefit once pregnancy has begun
C) Quitting is beneficial only in the third trimester
D) Relapse after birth is rare
Answer: A
Rationale: "Women who quit smoking by the first trimester have infants whose growth is comparable to those born to nonsmokers," "smoking cessation programs during pregnancy are effective and should be offered to all pregnant smokers," and "approximately 50% to 60% of women who quit smoking during pregnancy tend to relapse within 1 year of giving birth."
Trap: (D) would end support at discharge, and the relapse figure is why cessation support has to continue into the postpartum year.

Q: What does the US Preventive Services Task Force recommend for pregnant women who use tobacco?
A) That they be advised to quit and provided with only behavioral interventions to assist with cessation, because evidence on pharmacotherapy in pregnancy is insufficient; nicotine replacement therapy should be used only after discussion about the known risks of continued smoking and the possible risks of the therapy
B) That all pregnant smokers receive nicotine replacement therapy
C) That no intervention be offered
D) That varenicline be first-line
Answer: A
Rationale: "The USPSTF recommends that pregnant women who use tobacco be advised to quit and provided with only behavioral interventions to assist with cessation," and "nicotine replacement therapy should be used in pregnant women only after discussion about the known risks of continued smoking and the possible risks associated with nicotine replacement therapy." On the cessation drugs, "neither medication has been associated with an increased risk for congenital anomalies, preterm birth, or low birth weight when taken during pregnancy," though data are limited. The quit line is 1-800-QUIT-NOW.
Trap: (B) reverses the recommendation, and the careful wording — a discussion weighing the risk of continued smoking against the risk of the therapy — is what makes nicotine replacement a shared decision rather than a default.

Subtopic: alcohol withdrawal

Q: A pregnant woman requires withdrawal from alcohol. What does the chapter specify about the setting, the timing of the greatest risk, and the drug class used?
A) Inpatient management; the peak time for withdrawal seizures in pregnant women is 24 hours after the last drink; and treatment consists of benzodiazepines such as chlordiazepoxide or diazepam
B) Outpatient management; seizures peak at 1 week; treatment is with antipsychotics
C) Inpatient management; seizures peak at 72 hours; treatment is with opioids
D) No specific management is described
Answer: A
Rationale: "Pregnant women requiring withdrawal from alcohol should be admitted for inpatient management." And: "In pregnant women, the peak time for withdrawal seizures is 24 hours after the last drink." Treatment "consists of the administration of benzodiazepines," with chlordiazepoxide (Librium) and diazepam (Valium) often used.
Trap: (B) sends a woman home during the period the chapter identifies as highest risk, and the 24-hour figure is what makes the admission non-negotiable.

Topic: opioid use disorder in pregnancy
Subtopic: agonist therapy

Q: What is the standard of care for pregnant women with an opioid use disorder?
A) Opioid agonist therapy, also called medication-assisted treatment, which includes methadone and buprenorphine; neither is associated with an increased risk for birth defects
B) Medically supervised withdrawal
C) Abstinence without medication
D) Inpatient detoxification in all cases
Answer: A
Rationale: "Since the 1970s, opioid agonist therapy (also referred to as medication-assisted treatment) has been considered the standard of care for pregnant women with an opioid use disorder. Opioid agonist therapy includes methadone and buprenorphine (Subutex or Suboxone)," and "neither buprenorphine nor methadone is associated with an increased risk for birth defects."
Trap: (C) is the recommendation for every other substance in this chapter and the one exception the text names: abstinence "should be recommended as the safest course of action unless the woman is using opioids."

Q: Why is buprenorphine increasingly favored over methadone in pregnancy? Select all that apply.
A) Women can be treated on an outpatient basis without the need for daily visits
B) Women are less likely to overdose on buprenorphine
C) Fewer adjustments in dosage are necessary as pregnancy progresses
D) Buprenorphine causes less severe neonatal abstinence syndrome than methadone
E) Buprenorphine eliminates neonatal abstinence syndrome entirely
Answer: A, B, C, D
Rationale: The chapter gives all four reasons: outpatient treatment without daily visits, lower overdose risk, fewer dosage adjustments across pregnancy, and less severe neonatal abstinence syndrome.
Trap: (E) overstates the last advantage — "anywhere from 40% to 80% of infants exposed to opioids, including methadone or buprenorphine, in utero require treatment for NAS."

Q: Why is medically supervised withdrawal from opioids not recommended during pregnancy?
A) Because agonist pharmacotherapy is preferred, since withdrawal is associated with high relapse rates and poorer outcomes, and because of a potential risk for fetal distress or fetal demise
B) Because withdrawal is always fatal to the fetus
C) Because withdrawal is illegal
D) Because withdrawal has been proven harmful in all studies
Answer: A
Rationale: "Opioid agonist pharmacotherapy is preferred over supervised medical withdrawal because withdrawal is associated with high relapse rates and poorer outcomes," and "medical withdrawal from opioids during pregnancy is currently not recommended, in part because of a potential risk for fetal distress or fetal demise."
Trap: (D) overstates the evidence in a way the chapter itself corrects: a study of more than 600 women found no fetal harm, but "because there is a high possibility of relapse associated with medical withdrawal from opioids during pregnancy, ... broad recommendations regarding this management strategy cannot be made at this time."

Q: A provider considers limiting a pregnant woman's methadone dose to reduce the severity of neonatal abstinence syndrome. What does the chapter say?
A) Neither the incidence nor the severity of neonatal abstinence syndrome correlates directly with the maternal methadone dose at birth, so limiting the methadone dose to minimize the risk for NAS is not warranted
B) Lower doses reliably produce milder withdrawal
C) The dose should be halved in the third trimester
D) Methadone should be stopped before birth
Answer: A
Rationale: "Neither the incidence nor the severity of NAS correlates directly with the maternal methadone dose at birth. Therefore limiting the methadone dose to minimize the risk for NAS is not warranted."
Trap: (B) is the intuition the sentence exists to correct, and acting on it would undertreat the mother without helping the infant.

Subtopic: stimulants

Q: What treatment does the chapter describe for cocaine and methamphetamine use in pregnancy?
A) Both call for immediate cessation and referral; effective psychosocial and behavioral treatments have been developed for pregnant substance users because pharmacotherapy is not available for cocaine, and for methamphetamine the most effective treatments are behavioral therapies such as cognitive behavioral therapy and contingency management, with no medications that counteract its specific effects
B) Both are treated with agonist therapy like opioids
C) Both are treated with benzodiazepines
D) No treatment is available for either
Answer: A
Rationale: Women using cocaine "should be advised to stop using immediately and be referred for substance abuse treatment. Effective psychosocial and behavioral treatments have been developed for pregnant substance abusers because pharmacotherapy is not available." Methamphetamine users "are urged to immediately stop all use," and "because methamphetamine users are extremely psychologically addicted, the rate of relapse is very high. The most effective treatments ... are behavioral therapies, such as CBT and contingency management interventions. There are currently no medications that counteract the specific effects of methamphetamine."
Trap: (B) extends the opioid model to stimulants, and the absence of an agonist is precisely why the behavioral treatments carry the whole load here.

Topic: intrapartum and postpartum care in substance use disorder
Subtopic: the clinical picture and the pain rule

Q: A laboring woman with an active substance use disorder is demanding and difficult to manage, with a low threshold for pain. How should the nurse respond to her reports of pain?
A) Recognize that even women who are actively abusing drugs experience pain during labor and after giving birth and may need both pharmacologic and nonpharmacologic interventions
B) Withhold analgesia because of the substance use
C) Provide only nonpharmacologic measures
D) Assume the reports are drug-seeking
Answer: A
Rationale: The chapter states it so it cannot be misread: "Even women who are actively abusing drugs experience pain during labor and after giving birth and may need both pharmacologic and nonpharmacologic interventions." It acknowledges that these women "are often particularly challenging during the intrapartum and postpartum periods because of manipulative and demanding behavior," with "poor control over their behavior and a low threshold for pain."
Trap: (D) converts a described clinical picture into a reason to disbelieve the patient, and the sentence in the chapter is written specifically against that reasoning.

Q: What approach does the chapter recommend to reduce staff conflict in caring for these women, and what else does it specify?
A) Developing a standardized plan of care so that clients have limited opportunities to play staff members against one another, promoting mother-infant attachment by identifying the woman's strengths and reinforcing positive maternal feelings and behaviors, and ensuring sufficient staffing for strict surveillance of visitors to prevent unsupervised drug use
B) Assigning a different nurse each shift
C) Limiting all visitors permanently
D) Avoiding discussion of infant care
Answer: A
Rationale: "Developing a standardized plan of care so that clients have limited opportunities to play staff members against one another is helpful." And: "Mother-infant attachment should be promoted by identifying the woman's strengths and reinforcing positive maternal feelings and behaviors," with staffing "sufficient to ensure strict surveillance of visitors and prevent unsupervised drug use."
Trap: (B) removes the consistency the standardized plan is meant to create, and (D) abandons the attachment work the chapter names as a goal.

Q: Where does intervention for perinatal substance use begin, and what does the chapter say about the timing of the opportunity?
A) With education about the specific effects on pregnancy, the fetus, and the newborn for each drug used, with consequences clearly communicated and abstinence recommended as the safest course unless the woman is using opioids; and women are often more receptive to making lifestyle changes during pregnancy than at any other time in their lives
B) With mandatory reporting to authorities
C) With immediate inpatient admission in all cases
D) With deferral of all discussion until after birth
Answer: A
Rationale: "Intervention ... begins with education about specific effects on pregnancy, the fetus, and the newborn for each drug used. Consequences of perinatal substance use should be clearly communicated, and abstinence should be recommended as the safest course of action unless the woman is using opioids." And: "Women are often more receptive to making lifestyle changes during pregnancy than at any other time in their lives." The care team includes an obstetric care provider, mental health provider, substance abuse counselor, nurse, and social worker.
Trap: (D) postpones the conversation past the window the chapter identifies as the most receptive one a woman may ever have.

Q: What does the chapter say about breastfeeding advice for a woman with a substance use disorder?
A) Advice must be individualized, because almost all abused substances appear in breast milk, some in greater amounts than others
B) Breastfeeding is contraindicated in all cases
C) Breastfeeding is always encouraged regardless of substance
D) No guidance is given
Answer: A
Rationale: "Advice regarding breastfeeding must be individualized. Almost all abused substances appear in breast milk, some in greater amounts than others."
Trap: (B) applies a blanket prohibition that Chapter 25's contraindication table does not support either — illicit drug use is an absolute contraindication there, but a woman in a methadone program with negative screens is explicitly excepted.
