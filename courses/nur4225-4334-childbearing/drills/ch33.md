# Chapter 33 drills — Postpartum Complications

Authored from `sources/Ch33_Fact_Bank.md` (Lowdermilk, Cashion, Alden, Olshansky & Perry, 13th ed., ch. 33, pp. 728–740).

Blueprint weight is **~6 questions of 200 (3.0%), depth `standard`** — topics
*Postpartum Hemorrhage, medications, risk factors, lacerations, shock*. The Week
1–4 midterm reading guide does not name this chapter, so the midterm does not
reach it; the blueprint weights it at six, so the final does. All five blueprint
topics fall in the chapter's first half, which earns **43 drills across 10
topics** and leaves the second half out.

The organizing insight the fact bank names is the spine of this set: **the first
question in any postpartum bleed is "is the uterus firm?", and the answer routes
everything after it.** Boggy means uterine atony — massage, empty the bladder,
oxytocin, then the other uterotonics. Firm and still bleeding means a laceration,
hematoma, retained fragment, or coagulopathy, and no amount of massage will fix
it. The book states the rule twice, and every management sequence in the chapter
follows from it.

Every distinction the chapter states in both directions is drilled from both
ends: early and late hemorrhage, a boggy fundus and a firm one, accreta and
percreta, vulvar and retroperitoneal hematomas, methylergonovine and carboprost
by contraindication, and estimated versus quantified blood loss.

**What is left out.** The chapter's second half — idiopathic thrombocytopenic
purpura, venous thromboembolic disorders, and postpartum infection including
Box 33.2, wound infections, and urinary tract infections — none of which the
blueprint names. Chapter 21's Table 21.1 carries the bedside findings that would
lead to those diagnoses.

Topic: defining postpartum hemorrhage
Subtopic: the current definition

Q: How does ACOG currently define postpartum hemorrhage, and what did it replace?
A) Cumulative blood loss of 1000 mL or more, or bleeding associated with signs and symptoms of hypovolemia within 24 hours of the birth process regardless of type of birth; it replaced the classic definition of 500 mL or more after vaginal birth or 1000 mL or more after cesarean birth
B) Blood loss of 500 mL after any birth
C) Any bleeding requiring transfusion
D) Blood loss of 2000 mL or more
Answer: A
Rationale: ACOG revised the definition "from the classic one (≥500 mL after vaginal birth or ≥1000 mL after cesarean birth) to (1) cumulative blood loss ≥1000 mL or (2) bleeding associated with signs/symptoms of hypovolemia within 24 hours of the birth process regardless of type of birth."
Trap: (B) is the old vaginal-birth threshold, and the change matters because the new definition applies one number to both routes and adds a clinical criterion that does not depend on measurement at all.

Q: Why did the definition change to emphasize quantitative measurement?
A) Because visual estimation can either overestimate or underestimate actual blood loss, so quantitative measurement is a more accurate way to identify hemorrhage
B) Because blood loss is now measured by hemoglobin alone
C) Because visual estimation always overestimates
D) Because the old definition was too sensitive
Answer: A
Rationale: "In practice settings, visual estimation can either overestimate or underestimate actual blood loss, so quantitative measurement is a more accurate way to identify hemorrhage." The chapter adds that health care professionals "are highly inaccurate in estimating blood loss in terms of volume," and that inaccurate estimation "has been shown to delay appropriate response to hemorrhage."
Trap: (C) keeps one direction of error. Most often estimates come in low, but the chapter names both directions, which is why measurement replaces judgment.

Subtopic: early versus late

Q: A woman develops heavy bleeding 10 days after birth. How is this classified, and what are the usual causes?
A) Early PPH, usually from uterine atony
B) Late or secondary PPH, occurring more than 24 hours and up to 12 weeks after birth, usually from subinvolution of the uterus, infection, retained placental fragments, or coagulopathy
C) Normal lochia
D) Late PPH, which occurs only after 6 weeks
Answer: B
Rationale: "Late or secondary PPH occurs more than 24 hours and up to 12 weeks after the birth," and its usual causes are subinvolution of the uterus, infection, retained placental fragments, or coagulopathy. Early, acute, or primary PPH "occurs within 24 hours of the birth."
Trap: (A) applies the early differential to a late bleed. Atony is the leading cause within the first 24 hours, but a woman bleeding on day 10 is being evaluated for something left behind or an infection.

Q: Why does the chapter emphasize discharge teaching about the signs of hemorrhage?
A) Because today's shortened stays increase the potential for acute episodes of PPH to occur outside the hospital or birth center, and PPH can occur with little warning and often is not recognized until the mother has profound signs or symptoms
B) Because hemorrhage never occurs in the hospital
C) Because families are legally responsible for monitoring
D) Because late PPH is more common than early PPH
Answer: A
Rationale: "Today's health care environment encourages shortened stays after birth, which increases the potential for acute episodes of PPH to occur outside the traditional hospital or birth center setting. Education of the woman and her family on the signs and symptoms of hemorrhage is critical to include in the nurse's discharge teaching." And: "It can occur with little warning and often is not recognized until the mother has profound signs or symptoms."
Trap: (D) inverts the frequency — atony in the first hours is the leading cause — and the teaching exists because discharge now happens before that risk window has fully closed.

Topic: risk factors and causes of pph
Subtopic: box 33.1

Q: Which factors does Box 33.1 list under uterine atony as risk factors for postpartum hemorrhage? Select all that apply.
A) An overdistended uterus, a large fetus, multiple fetuses, or hydramnios
B) Anesthesia and analgesia
C) High parity and obesity
D) Prolonged labor, oxytocin-induced labor, and chorioamnionitis
E) Forceps-assisted birth
Answer: A, B, C, D
Rationale: Box 33.1's atony list includes an overdistended uterus, large fetus, multiple fetuses, hydramnios, distention with clots, anesthesia and analgesia, previous history of uterine atony, high parity, obesity, prolonged or oxytocin-induced labor, and chorioamnionitis.
Trap: (E) is in the trauma column, along with vacuum-assisted birth, cesarean birth, unrepaired lacerations, ruptured uterus, uterine inversion, and the placenta accreta spectrum. The box sorts risks by mechanism, and the mechanism is what determines the treatment.

Q: Which factors appear in Box 33.1's "other" category?
A) Coagulation disorders, placental abruption, placenta previa, manual removal of a retained placenta, magnesium sulfate administration, uterine subinvolution, hypertensive disorders, intrauterine fetal demise, and failure to progress during the second stage
B) Only coagulation disorders
C) Only obesity and high parity
D) Only operative birth
Answer: A
Rationale: Box 33.1's third group lists all of these.
Trap: (B) narrows a long list whose value is its breadth — magnesium sulfate, for instance, is given for a different problem entirely and relaxes smooth muscle as a side effect.

Subtopic: atony and its timing

Q: When is the greatest risk for early postpartum hemorrhage, and why does uterine contraction control bleeding?
A) During the first hour after birth; the body of the uterus is a basket-weave of interlacing smooth muscle bundles through which many large maternal blood vessels pass, and bleeding is controlled when those fibers are well contracted
B) During the first 24 hours; bleeding is controlled by platelet plugs
C) On the second postpartum day; bleeding is controlled by vasoconstriction alone
D) After discharge; bleeding is controlled by the endometrium regenerating
Answer: A
Rationale: "The greatest risk for early PPH is during the first hour after birth." And: "The body of the uterus is like a basket-weave of strong, interlacing smooth muscle bundles through which many large maternal blood vessels pass. After the expulsion or removal of the placenta, bleeding is controlled when these smooth muscle fibers in the uterus are well contracted."
Trap: (B) names the ordinary hemostatic mechanism, which is not what controls postpartum bleeding — the muscle compressing the vessels is.

Q: Bleeding occurs after the placenta has been expelled. What is the most likely source, and how does this differ from bleeding earlier in the third stage?
A) Most often uterine atony, though it may also be a sign of uterine prolapse; bleeding between the birth of the newborn and separation of the placenta more often comes from lacerations of the birth canal or cervix, and bleeding between separation and expulsion from incomplete separation, undue manipulation of the fundus, or excessive cord traction
B) Always a laceration, at every point in the third stage
C) Always retained fragments
D) The source cannot be determined by timing
Answer: A
Rationale: The chapter sorts the etiology by stage: from birth of the newborn to separation of the placenta, "lacerations of the birth canal or the cervix"; from separation to expulsion, "incomplete placental separation, undue manipulation of the fundus, or excessive traction on the umbilical cord"; and after expulsion, "most often the result of uterine atony but may also be a sign of uterine prolapse." Uterine atony "is the leading cause of early PPH."
Trap: (D) discards a differential the chapter builds explicitly around timing, and the timing is often the only clue available at the moment.

Topic: retained placenta and placenta accreta
Subtopic: retained products of conception

Q: The placenta has not been expelled 35 minutes after birth, despite an upright posture, gentle cord traction, and uterine massage. What is this called, and what follows?
A) Retained placenta; the initial management is manual separation and removal by the midwife or physician, after which the woman has an increased risk for postpartum hemorrhage and infection
B) Normal third stage; continue to wait
C) Uterine inversion
D) Placenta accreta by definition
Answer: A
Rationale: "When the placenta has not been expelled within 30 minutes after birth despite an upright posture of the woman, gentle traction on the umbilical cord, and uterine massage, it is described as a retained placenta." Initial management "is manual separation and removal by the midwife or physician," and "after removal of a retained placenta, the woman has an increased risk for PPH and infection."
Trap: (D) jumps to the adherent-placenta diagnosis, which is a specific abnormality of implantation rather than a description of elapsed time.

Q: A woman has excessive bleeding and a boggy uterus after birth, and retained placental fragments are suspected. What may be required?
A) Manual exploration to remove the fragments, and uterine curettage if necessary
B) Observation only
C) Immediate hysterectomy
D) Bed rest and iron supplementation
Answer: A
Rationale: With retained fragments, "the client may have excessive bleeding and the uterus feels boggy (soft) due to uterine atony. The midwife or physician may attempt manual exploration to remove the fragments; uterine curettage may be necessary" — the removal of uterine contents using a curette or vacuum suction.
Trap: (C) skips the entire escalation ladder. Hysterectomy is reserved for uncontrolled bleeding after other measures fail.

Subtopic: the accreta spectrum

Q: How do placenta accreta, increta, and percreta differ?
A) Accreta is slight penetration of the myometrium, increta is deep penetration of the myometrium, and percreta is perforation of the myometrium and uterine serosa, possibly involving adjacent organs
B) Accreta is the deepest and percreta the most superficial
C) They describe the location of the placenta rather than its depth
D) They are three names for the same finding
Answer: A
Rationale: The chapter defines each: "Placenta accreta—slight penetration of myometrium"; "placenta increta—deep penetration of myometrium"; "placenta percreta—perforation of myometrium and uterine serosa, possibly involving adjacent organs."
Trap: (B) reverses the spectrum, and the depth determines the outcome: "hysterectomy may be indicated for all three types of placental adherence if bleeding is uncontrolled and is almost always required for placenta percreta and placenta increta."

Q: A woman is diagnosed antenatally with placenta accreta spectrum. What does the chapter say about attempting manual removal of the placenta at birth?
A) Manual removal should be attempted first to avoid surgery
B) Bleeding may not occur unless manual removal is attempted, and with more extensive forms bleeding becomes profuse when removal is attempted; less blood is lost if the diagnosis is made antenatally and no attempt is made to manually remove the placenta
C) Manual removal is always successful when the diagnosis is known
D) The diagnosis cannot be made before birth
Answer: B
Rationale: "Bleeding may not occur unless manual removal of the placenta is attempted. With more extensive forms ..., bleeding becomes profuse when removal of the placenta is attempted. Less blood is lost if the diagnosis is made antenatally and no attempt is made to manually remove the placenta." Diagnosis is possible before birth with ultrasound and MRI, and "cesarean birth is recommended when the diagnosis is made prenatally."
Trap: (A) applies the retained-placenta response to an adherent one, and it is the action that converts a controlled situation into a profuse hemorrhage. The rising incidence is "thought to be associated with the rise in cesarean birth rates."

Topic: lacerations and hematomas
Subtopic: recognizing a laceration

Q: A woman has continued bleeding despite a firm, contracted uterine fundus. What should be suspected, and what may the bleeding look like?
A) Uterine atony; the bleeding is always profuse
B) Hemorrhage related to lacerations; the bleeding can be a slow trickle, an oozing, or frank hemorrhage
C) Normal lochia rubra
D) Coagulopathy only
Answer: B
Rationale: "Hemorrhage related to lacerations should be suspected if bleeding continues despite a firm, contracted uterine fundus. This bleeding can be a slow trickle, an oozing, or frank hemorrhage."
Trap: (A) is contradicted by the firm fundus in the stem, and the description of the bleeding matters — a slow ooze from a laceration can add up to a hemorrhage while looking unremarkable at any single moment.

Q: Which factors does the chapter associate with lacerations of the birth canal? Select all that apply.
A) Operative birth
B) Precipitous or rapid birth
C) Congenital abnormalities of the maternal soft tissue and a contracted pelvis
D) Increased fetal size, abnormal presentation and position, and previous scarring from infection, injury, or surgery
E) Prolonged second stage with no intervention
Answer: A, B, C, D
Rationale: The chapter names operative birth, precipitous or rapid birth, congenital abnormalities of maternal soft tissue, and contracted pelvis, along with increased size and abnormal presentation and position of the fetus, the relative size of the presenting part and the birth canal, previous scarring, and vulvar, perineal, and vaginal varicosities.
Trap: (E) sounds plausible and is not among the listed factors. Prolonged labor appears in Box 33.1 under atony rather than trauma.

Q: A woman has a fourth-degree perineal laceration. Which order should the nurse question?
A) A stool softener
B) A rectal suppository for pain
C) Increased dietary fiber and fluids
D) Chemical ice packs to the perineum for the first 24 hours
Answer: B
Rationale: The Nursing Alert states: "To avoid injury to healing tissues, a woman with third- or fourth-degree lacerations is not given rectal suppositories or enemas." Care after repair includes position changes, analgesia, warm or cold applications, and chemical ice packs to the perineum for the first 24 hours following vaginal birth, with increased fiber and fluids and stool softeners to reduce constipation.
Trap: (A) and (C) are both part of the plan, and the reassurance that goes with them is worth knowing: "clients should be reassured that a bowel movement will not disrupt the integrity of the sutures."

Subtopic: hematomas

Q: A woman reports persistent, worsening perineal pain and a feeling of pressure in the vagina 2 hours after birth. What should the nurse suspect?
A) A hematoma, since pain is the most common symptom and the woman may report persistent or worsening perineal or rectal pain or a feeling of pressure in the vagina
B) Normal afterpains
C) Uterine atony
D) Endometritis
Answer: A
Rationale: Women with hematomas report "persistent or worsening perineal or rectal pain or a feeling of pressure in the vagina." Vulvar hematomas are "the most common type and often occur in association with operative vaginal birth, an episiotomy, or primigravidity. Pain is the most common symptom, and most vulvar hematomas are visible." Hematomas "are generally surgically evacuated."
Trap: (B) is the diagnosis that lets a bleeding collection go unrecognized, and Chapter 21's rule applies here too: excessive discomfort in the first 1 to 2 days suggests a hematoma.

Q: Which type of hematoma is least common but life threatening, and how might it present?
A) Vulvar hematoma, presenting with visible swelling
B) Retroperitoneal hematoma, caused by laceration of one of the vessels attached to the hypogastric artery — usually associated with rupture of a cesarean scar during labor — which can cause minimal pain, with the initial symptoms being signs of shock
C) Vaginal hematoma, presenting with visible bleeding
D) Cervical hematoma, presenting with fever
Answer: B
Rationale: Retroperitoneal hematomas "are the least common but are life threatening. They are caused by laceration of one of the vessels attached to the hypogastric artery, usually associated with rupture of a cesarean scar during labor." And: "a retroperitoneal hematoma can cause minimal pain, and the initial symptoms may be signs of shock."
Trap: (A) is the common and visible type, which is precisely why the rare one is dangerous — the usual cue, pain, may be absent, and shock arrives first.

Topic: uterine inversion and subinvolution
Subtopic: inversion

Q: Immediately after the placenta is delivered, the woman has sudden hemorrhage, shock, and pain, and the uterus is not palpable abdominally. What has occurred?
A) Uterine atony
B) Uterine inversion, in which the fundus collapses into the uterine cavity, turning inside out
C) Placental abruption
D) Amniotic fluid embolism
Answer: B
Rationale: "Uterine inversion occurs when the fundus collapses into the uterine cavity (turning inside out) after birth." And: "The primary presenting signs of uterine inversion are sudden and include hemorrhage, shock, and pain. The uterus is not palpable abdominally." It is rare — 1 in 2000 to 1 in 20,000 vaginal births — but potentially life threatening.
Trap: (A) shares the hemorrhage but not the absent fundus. An abdominally unpalpable uterus in a woman who has just given birth is the finding that separates them.

Q: What are the two named causes of uterine inversion?
A) Excessive umbilical cord traction when the placenta is attached high in the uterus, and fundal pressure when the uterus is not well contracted
B) Prolonged labor and macrosomia
C) Preeclampsia and magnesium sulfate
D) Chorioamnionitis and prolonged rupture of membranes
Answer: A
Rationale: The chapter names two causes, both iatrogenic: "excessive umbilical cord traction when the placenta is attached high in the uterus (such as at the fundus) and fundal pressure when the uterus is not well contracted."
Trap: (B) names risk factors for atony. What makes the inversion causes worth holding is that both are things a provider does, which makes the complication partly preventable.

Q: What is the management sequence for uterine inversion?
A) Fluid resuscitation, replacement of the uterus within the pelvic cavity, and correction of associated clinical conditions; tocolytics or halogenated anesthetics may be given to relax the uterus before replacement, and oxytocic agents are administered after the uterus is repositioned
B) Oxytocin first, then replacement of the uterus
C) Immediate hysterectomy
D) Aggressive fundal massage
Answer: A
Rationale: Management is "fluid resuscitation, replacement of the uterus within the pelvic cavity, and correction of associated clinical conditions. Tocolytics or halogenated anesthetics may be given to relax the uterus before attempting replacement. Oxytocic agents are administered after the uterus is repositioned," with broad-spectrum antibiotics initiated.
Trap: (B) reverses the drug order, which matters: a contracted uterus cannot be replaced, so relaxation comes first and contraction second. And afterward "care must be taken to avoid subsequent aggressive fundal massage."

Subtopic: subinvolution

Q: A woman 3 weeks postpartum has prolonged lochial discharge, irregular bleeding, and a larger-than-normal boggy uterus on pelvic examination. What is this, and how is it treated?
A) Subinvolution, usually caused by retained placental fragments and pelvic infection, treated with ergonovine or methylergonovine 0.2 mg every 3 to 4 hours for 24 to 48 hours, with dilation and curettage to remove fragments and antibiotics if infection is the cause
B) Normal involution
C) Uterine inversion
D) Endometriosis
Answer: A
Rationale: Subinvolution is "delayed return of the enlarged uterus to nonpregnant size and function," caused by "retained placental fragments and pelvic infection," and it presents with "prolonged lochial discharge, irregular or excessive bleeding, and sometimes hemorrhage," with "a larger-than-normal uterus that can be boggy." Treatment is methylergonovine or ergonovine, dilation and curettage, and antibiotics if infection is the cause.
Trap: (B) is what a woman still bleeding at 3 weeks may be told if her lochia is read as simply prolonged — the boggy, enlarged uterus is what makes it a diagnosis.

Topic: quantifying blood loss
Subtopic: how loss is measured

Q: When does quantification of blood loss begin for a vaginal birth, and how is it done?
A) Immediately after birth of the neonate, before delivery of the placenta, using a calibrated under-buttocks drape and weighing all blood-soaked items
B) After the placenta is delivered, by visual estimation
C) At the end of the recovery period, by counting pads
D) Only if hemorrhage is suspected
Answer: A
Rationale: For vaginal birth, quantification begins "immediately after birth of the neonate, before delivery of the placenta," using "a calibrated under-buttocks drape and weighing all blood-soaked items." For cesarean birth it begins "when the membranes are ruptured or after birth of the neonate," by "measuring fluids in suction canisters (subtracting irrigation fluid) and weighing all blood-soaked materials and clots."
Trap: (D) makes measurement conditional on suspicion, when AWHONN and ACOG recommend quantification "for all births, whether vaginal or cesarean" — precisely because suspicion arrives late.

Q: Blood loss appears excessive. According to the chapter's decision rule, what is the first step?
A) Start a second IV line
B) Evaluate the contractility of the uterus: if it is hypotonic or boggy, management is directed toward increasing contractility and minimizing blood loss; if it is firmly contracted and bleeding continues, the source must still be identified and treated
C) Administer methylergonovine
D) Notify the provider and wait
Answer: B
Rationale: "Whenever blood loss appears to be excessive, the first step is to evaluate the contractility of the uterus. If the uterus is hypotonic or boggy, management is directed toward increasing contractility and minimizing blood loss. If the uterus is firmly contracted and bleeding continues, the source of bleeding still must be identified and treated."
Trap: (C) reaches for a drug before the assessment that determines whether a drug is the answer at all — and methylergonovine would do nothing for a laceration.

Q: The uterus is firmly contracted and bleeding continues. What does the search for the source include?
A) Visual or manual inspection of the perineum, vagina, uterus, cervix, or rectum, as well as laboratory studies such as hemoglobin, hematocrit, coagulation studies, and platelet count
B) Repeat fundal massage only
C) Observation for 1 hour
D) Immediate transfusion without investigation
Answer: A
Rationale: The search includes "visual or manual inspection of the perineum, vagina, uterus, cervix, or rectum as well as laboratory studies (e.g., hemoglobin, hematocrit, coagulation studies, platelet count)."
Trap: (B) repeats the intervention that has already been shown not to apply, which is the most common way time is lost in a laceration bleed.

Topic: first-line management of atony
Subtopic: the sequence

Q: A woman has excessive postpartum bleeding with a boggy uterus. What is the initial intervention, and what follows?
A) Firm massage of the uterine fundus, expression of any clots, elimination of bladder distention, and a continuous IV infusion of 10 to 40 units of oxytocin in 1000 mL of lactated Ringer's or normal saline; if the uterus does not contract in response to oxytocin, other uterotonic medications are administered
B) Immediate administration of carboprost
C) Bimanual compression before any other measure
D) Transfusion of packed red blood cells first
Answer: A
Rationale: "The initial intervention in management of excessive postpartum bleeding due to uterine atony is firm massage of the uterine fundus," followed by "expression of any clots in the uterus," "elimination of bladder distention," and "continuous IV infusion of 10 to 40 units of oxytocin added to 1000 mL of lactated Ringer's solution or normal saline solution." Other uterotonics follow if the uterus does not respond.
Trap: (C) is a later step: bimanual compression means "inserting a fist into the vagina and pressing the knuckles against the anterior side of the uterus and then placing the other hand on the abdomen and massaging the uterus," used when the first measures have not worked.

Q: Which supportive measures accompany the uterotonic drugs in managing postpartum hemorrhage? Select all that apply.
A) Rapid administration of crystalloid solutions or blood products, or both, to restore intravascular volume
B) Oxygen by nonrebreather face mask
C) An indwelling urinary catheter to monitor urine output as a measure of renal perfusion, which reflects perfusion of other vital organs
D) Laboratory studies including a complete blood count with platelets, fibrinogen, fibrin split products, prothrombin time, and partial thromboplastin time, with blood type and antibody screen
E) Oral fluids to restore volume
Answer: A, B, C, D
Rationale: The chapter names each of these supportive measures alongside the drug therapy.
Trap: (E) substitutes an oral route for a hemorrhage requiring rapid intravascular replacement, and the woman may be heading for surgery.

Q: When first-line and drug measures fail, what is the escalating surgical order?
A) Uterine tamponade with packing or an intrauterine tamponade balloon, bilateral uterine artery ligation, ligation of uteroovarian arteries and infundibulopelvic vessels, and selective arterial embolization, with hysterectomy if these measures are ineffective
B) Hysterectomy first
C) Only arterial embolization
D) Only uterine packing
Answer: A
Rationale: Surgical management escalates through "uterine tamponade (uterine packing or an intrauterine tamponade balloon), bilateral uterine artery ligation, ligation of uteroovarian arteries and infundibulopelvic vessels, and selective arterial embolization." The chapter also names the Jada System, which "provides vacuum-induced hemorrhage control," and uterine compression suturing such as B-Lynch or Hayman vertical sutures. "If these treatment measures are ineffective, hysterectomy will likely be needed."
Trap: (B) goes to the definitive procedure before the uterus-sparing ones, and the ladder exists because most of these women want future pregnancies.

Q: What does the chapter say the nurse should do after a postpartum hemorrhage has been managed?
A) Avoid discussing the event to prevent distress
B) Help the woman and her family understand what occurred and the interventions that were needed, since taking time to debrief the events may help reduce birth trauma and negative memories associated with the birthing process
C) Refer all debriefing to the provider
D) Document only
Answer: B
Rationale: "It is important that the nurse help the woman and her family understand what occurred and the interventions that were needed. Taking time to debrief the events with the client may help to reduce birth trauma and negative memories associated with the birthing process." When excessive blood loss is recognized, "the obstetric hemorrhage team is activated to implement the PPH protocol."
Trap: (A) protects the nurse rather than the patient, and the chapter treats the debrief as part of preventing a lasting psychological injury.

Topic: uterotonic medications
Subtopic: contraindications

Q: A woman with postpartum hemorrhage has a blood pressure of 156/96 mm Hg. Which uterotonic should the nurse question?
A) Oxytocin
B) Methylergonovine, which is contraindicated in hypertension, preeclampsia, and cardiovascular disease — the nurse checks blood pressure before giving it and does not give it if the pressure is above 140/90 mm Hg
C) Misoprostol
D) Tranexamic acid
Answer: B
Rationale: The Medication Alert states that "the use of ergonovine or methylergonovine is contraindicated in the presence of hypertension or cardiovascular disease," and the medication guide directs: "Check blood pressure before giving, and do not give if >140/90 mm Hg." Its adverse effects include hypertension, hypotension, nausea, vomiting, and headache.
Trap: (C) is what remains available for exactly this woman: misoprostol's contraindication column reads "consider use only for clients with hypertension or asthma."

Q: A woman with a history of asthma has postpartum hemorrhage unresponsive to oxytocin. Which uterotonic should the nurse question?
A) Methylergonovine
B) Carboprost tromethamine, since prostaglandin F2alpha should not be given to clients with a history of asthma because it can cause bronchoconstriction
C) Misoprostol
D) Oxytocin
Answer: B
Rationale: The Medication Alert states: "Prostaglandin F2α should not be given to clients with a history of asthma, as it can cause bronchoconstriction." Carboprost is dosed at "0.25 mg (250 mcg) IM every 15–90 min up to 8 doses," with relative contraindications for hypertension and active cardiac, pulmonary, or hepatic disease.
Trap: (A) is contraindicated for a different reason — hypertension and cardiovascular disease — and this pair is worth holding together, because the two second-line drugs are ruled out by two different histories.

Q: Why does the chapter describe misoprostol's contraindication line as the mirror image of the other two agents'?
A) Because misoprostol has no contraindications
B) Because its entry reads "consider use only for clients with hypertension or asthma" — the two conditions that rule out methylergonovine and carboprost — so when those drugs are unavailable, misoprostol is what remains
C) Because misoprostol is contraindicated in both hypertension and asthma
D) Because misoprostol is not a uterotonic
Answer: B
Rationale: Misoprostol's contraindication entry reads "consider use only for clients with hypertension or asthma," the mirror image of methylergonovine's hypertension contraindication and carboprost's asthma contraindication. It is dosed at 600 to 1000 mcg once, and "an advantage of misoprostol is that it can be given rectally, sublingually, or orally."
Trap: (C) inverts the entry, which would leave a woman with hypertension and asthma with no second-line option at all.

Subtopic: doses and other agents

Q: What is the oxytocin regimen given in the Medication Guide for postpartum hemorrhage?
A) 10 to 80 units in 500 to 1000 mL of crystalloid solution at 125 to 200 milliunits/min intravenously, or 10 to 20 units intramuscularly
B) 1 to 2 units intravenous push
C) 40 units orally
D) 10 units subcutaneously every hour
Answer: A
Rationale: The guide gives "10–80 units in 500–1000 mL crystalloid solution ... at 125–200 milliunits/min IV; or 10–20 units IM," with adverse effects of nausea, vomiting, and water intoxication, and rare hypersensitivity as the only contraindication.
Trap: (B) is a bolus route and dose the guide does not use, and oxytocin given as a rapid push carries hemodynamic risks the infusion avoids.

Q: When is tranexamic acid used in postpartum hemorrhage, and what is its main contraindication?
A) It is an antifibrinolytic that may be used if initial medical therapy is not effective, given as 1 g by slow IV injection or diluted and administered over 10 minutes; it is contraindicated with a history or risk of thromboembolic disease
B) It is the first-line agent for all postpartum hemorrhage
C) It is contraindicated in asthma
D) It replaces blood product transfusion
Answer: A
Rationale: Tranexamic acid, "an antifibrinolytic agent, may be used in some settings if initial medical therapy is not effective." The guide gives "1 g slow IV injection or diluted in 50- or 100-mL IV fluid; administer over 10 min," with contraindications of "history or risk of thromboembolic disease" and caution in renal impairment, and directs the nurse to "monitor hemodynamics and assess for thromboembolic events."
Trap: (C) borrows carboprost's contraindication. Each uterotonic in this guide has its own, and mixing them is how a contraindicated drug gets given.

Topic: hemorrhagic shock
Subtopic: recognizing it

Q: Which findings appear in the Emergency box for hemorrhagic shock? Select all that apply.
A) Rapid, shallow respirations
B) A rapid, weak, irregular pulse
C) Cool, pale, clammy skin with decreasing urinary output
D) Decreasing blood pressure, identified as a late sign
E) Bounding pulse with warm, flushed skin
Answer: A, B, C, D
Rationale: The Emergency box lists rapid shallow respirations; a rapid, weak, irregular pulse; decreasing blood pressure marked as a late sign; cool, pale, clammy skin; decreasing urinary output; lethargy progressing to coma; anxiety progressing to coma; and decreased central venous pressure. Interventions are to call for assistance and equipment, start an IV infusion per standing orders, ensure a patent airway and give oxygen, and continue to monitor status.
Trap: (E) describes a warm, well-perfused patient — the opposite of the peripheral vasoconstriction that diverts blood to the brain and heart in shock.

Q: A postpartum woman's vital signs are still normal despite obvious bleeding. What does the chapter say about this?
A) Normal vital signs confirm she is stable
B) The classic signs of shock — tachycardia and hypotension — may not appear until the client has lost 30% to 40% of blood volume, and by the time vital signs are abnormal the client may be in an advanced stage of shock
C) Vital signs change before any blood loss occurs
D) Blood pressure is the earliest indicator
Answer: B
Rationale: "Hemorrhagic shock can occur rapidly, but the classic signs of shock (tachycardia, hypotension) may not appear until the postpartum client has lost 30% to 40% of blood volume. By the time vital signs are abnormal, the client may be in an advanced stage of shock."
Trap: (A) and (D) both rest on the monitor that fails last. Waiting for the blood pressure to fall is the specific error this passage exists to prevent.

Q: What early neurologic symptoms may a woman report as blood flow decreases, and how do they progress?
A) She may report seeing stars or feeling dizzy or nauseated, can become restless and orthopneic, and as cerebral hypoxia increases may become confused and react slowly or not at all to stimuli; some describe a headache
B) She becomes immediately unconscious with no warning symptoms
C) Neurologic symptoms appear only after cardiac arrest
D) She reports numbness in the extremities first
Answer: A
Rationale: "In early stages of decreased blood flow, the client may report 'seeing stars' or feeling dizzy or nauseated. She can become restless and orthopneic. As cerebral hypoxia increases, the woman can become confused and react slowly or not at all to stimuli. Some women describe symptoms of a headache."
Trap: (B) discards a progression that gives the nurse warning, and restlessness in a bleeding patient is one of the earliest signs there is.

Subtopic: managing it

Q: What are the two goals of managing hemorrhagic shock, and what is the fluid resuscitation rule?
A) Restoring circulating blood volume and eliminating the cause of the hemorrhage; crystalloid solution is given rapidly at 3 mL infused for every 1 mL of estimated blood loss
B) Restoring blood pressure alone; fluid is given at 1 mL per 1 mL of loss
C) Preventing infection and maintaining comfort; fluid is restricted
D) Transfusing immediately without crystalloid
Answer: A
Rationale: The goals are "restoring circulating blood volume and eliminating the cause of the hemorrhage (e.g., lacerations, uterine atony, or inversion)," restated as restoring "oxygen delivery to the tissues" and maintaining cardiac output. The rule: "a rapid IV infusion of crystalloid solution is given at a rate of 3 mL infused for every 1 mL of estimated blood loss (e.g., 3000 mL infused for 1000 mL of blood loss)." Two large-bore IV lines facilitate resuscitation.
Trap: (B) under-replaces by a factor of three, and the chapter also warns the other way: "fluid resuscitation must be monitored carefully because fluid overload can occur," most often with colloid therapy.

Q: What does the chapter identify as the most objective and least invasive assessment of adequate organ perfusion and oxygenation?
A) Blood pressure
B) A urine output of at least 30 mL/h and preferably 50 mL/h or more
C) Pulse oximetry
D) Level of consciousness
Answer: B
Rationale: "The most objective and least invasive assessment of adequate organ perfusion and oxygenation is a urine output of at least 30 mL/h and preferably ≥50 mL/h."
Trap: (C) is used but is explicitly qualified: pulse oximetry measurements "are not always accurate in a client with hypovolemia or decreased perfusion." Oxygen is given "preferably by a nonrebreather face mask, at 10 to 12 L/min."

Topic: coagulopathies and standards of care
Subtopic: when to suspect a coagulopathy

Q: Postpartum bleeding is continuous and no source can be identified. What should be suspected?
A) An inherited or acquired coagulopathy
B) Normal lochia
C) Uterine atony only
D) A laceration that will resolve on its own
Answer: A
Rationale: "When postpartum bleeding is continuous and there is no identifiable source, an inherited or acquired coagulopathy should be suspected." Women with idiopathic or immune thrombocytopenic purpura or von Willebrand disease "should be considered at risk for PPH," and acute causes include placental abruption, amniotic fluid embolism, preeclampsia with severe features, and HELLP syndrome.
Trap: (C) is the leading cause of early hemorrhage and has already been excluded by the absence of an identifiable source — including, by definition, a boggy uterus.

Q: Which laboratory findings would the nurse expect with a coagulopathy after postpartum hemorrhage? Select all that apply.
A) Increased prothrombin time and increased partial thromboplastin time
B) Decreased platelets and decreased fibrinogen
C) Increased fibrin degradation products
D) Prolonged bleeding time
E) Increased fibrinogen with decreased fibrin degradation products
Answer: A, B, C, D
Rationale: The abnormal values named are "increased prothrombin time, increased partial thromboplastin time, decreased platelets, decreased fibrinogen level, increased fibrin degradation products, and prolonged bleeding time." Treatment is "rapid replacement of blood products and clotting factors," plus tranexamic acid, recombinant activated factor VII, fibrinogen concentrate, or prothrombin complex concentrate.
Trap: (E) reverses the two consumption markers. A coagulopathy after severe hemorrhage arises "when there is depletion of clotting factors," so the factors go down and their breakdown products go up.

Q: During resuscitation, the nurse notes oozing at the intravenous insertion site and petechiae on the arm where the blood pressure cuff was applied. What should this suggest?
A) Normal bruising from the cuff
B) Disseminated intravascular coagulation, since oozing at sites of incisions or injections and petechiae or ecchymoses in areas not associated with surgery or trauma are signs to watch for
C) An allergic reaction to the IV fluid
D) A transfusion reaction only
Answer: B
Rationale: The chapter directs the nurse to watch for DIC through "oozing at the sites of incisions or injections" and "the presence of petechiae or ecchymoses in areas not associated with surgery or trauma."
Trap: (A) explains the petechiae away using the cuff, which is the example the chapter itself uses to show where they appear — the cuff makes them visible rather than causing them.

Subtopic: standards and preparedness

Q: What does the Legal Tip on bleeding emergencies establish?
A) That the standard of care for obstetric emergencies such as postpartum hemorrhage or hypovolemic shock is that provision should be made for nurses to implement nursing actions independently, with policies, procedures, standing orders or protocols, and clinical guidelines established by each facility and agreed on by the providers involved
B) That nurses must wait for provider orders in all emergencies
C) That only physicians may activate the hemorrhage protocol
D) That facilities are not required to have written protocols
Answer: A
Rationale: "The standard of care for obstetric emergency situations such as postpartum hemorrhage or hypovolemic shock is that provision should be made for nurses to implement nursing actions independently. Policies, procedures, standing orders or protocols, and clinical guidelines should be established by each health care facility in which births occur and should be agreed on by health care providers involved in the care of obstetric clients."
Trap: (B) is the assumption the tip is written against, and it is the one that costs minutes in a hemorrhage — which is why the standing orders exist in advance.

Q: What preparedness measures does the chapter say birth facilities must have, and what happens to women in hemorrhagic shock?
A) Standardized management protocols and regularly conducted emergency drills; women in hemorrhagic shock are likely to be transferred to a critical care unit
B) Protocols only, with no drills
C) Drills only, with no protocols
D) No specific preparedness is described
Answer: A
Rationale: "Birth facilities must develop standardized management protocols and regularly conduct emergency drills to be adequately prepared for PPH," and women in hemorrhagic shock "are likely to be transferred to a critical care unit."
Trap: (B) and (C) each keep half of a two-part requirement, and the drills are what make the protocol usable when the bleeding actually starts.
