# Chapter 18 drills — Fetal Assessment During Labor

Authored from `sources/Ch18_Fact_Bank.md` (Lowdermilk, Cashion, Alden, Olshansky & Perry, 13th ed., ch. 18, pp. 356–374).

Blueprint weight is **~18 questions of 200 (9.0%), depth `full`** — the course's
dominant chapter, nearly double the next heaviest, with 15 of those 18 falling on
the midterm. Topics: *Fetal Monitoring, decelerations, accelerations,
variability, baseline, categories, cord blood gas, legal responsibilities*.

That earns **the top of the `full` band, 55 to 75**, and this file sits there on
purpose: the reading guide's four rows plus the blueprint's eight topics between
them reach nearly the whole chapter, so almost nothing here is out of scope.

The organizing insight the fact bank names is the spine of this set: **every FHR
definition in this chapter is built from three measurements — how fast, how much,
and how long — and the exam lives on the numbers that separate one label from its
neighbor.** Abrupt versus gradual is **30 seconds**. An acceleration and a
variable deceleration share the **15 beats/min, 15 seconds, under 2 minutes**
template and differ only in direction. A prolonged deceleration and a baseline
change differ only at **10 minutes**. Those thresholds are drilled repeatedly and
from both sides, because a single grid answers most of the chapter.

Every distinction the chapter states in both directions is drilled from both
ends: early and late decelerations, variable and prolonged, bradycardia and a
prolonged deceleration, tachycardia and bradycardia, external and internal
monitoring, artery and vein cord gases, respiratory and metabolic acidemia,
auscultation intervals and electronic-monitoring intervals, absent through marked
variability, and terbutaline as a cause of tachycardia versus terbutaline as the
tocolytic for tachysystole.

**What is left out.** The chapter's two Next-Generation case studies restate
material drilled here and their answers live on Evolve rather than in the text;
the Evidence-Based Practice box's search strategy and cost discussion; and the
product descriptions of remote tracing access. **Pattern recognition from an
actual strip cannot be drilled from a fact bank** — Figs. 18.5 through 18.18 are
tracings whose spatial content is the teaching point, and only their captions
extract. These questions drill the definitions, the thresholds, and the
interventions; work from the textbook's own figures for visual practice.

Topic: basis for fetal monitoring and the compromise cascade
Subtopic: how fetal oxygen supply falls

Q: A nurse is reviewing the mechanisms by which fetal oxygen supply can decrease during labor. Which mechanisms does the chapter name? Select all that apply.
A) Reduction of blood flow through the maternal vessels from hypertension, hypotension, or hypovolemia
B) Reduction of the oxygen content in maternal blood from hemorrhage or severe anemia
C) Alterations in fetal circulation from cord compression, placental separation, or head compression
D) Reduction in blood flow to the intervillous space from uterine hypertonus or deterioration of the placental vasculature
E) Reduction in fetal hemoglobin production during the second stage of labor
Answer: A, B, C, D
Rationale: The chapter lists exactly four mechanisms by which fetal oxygen supply decreases, and they are the causal spine of every deceleration type: reduced maternal blood flow, reduced maternal oxygen content, altered fetal circulation, and reduced intervillous blood flow.
Trap: (E) is invented physiology that sounds plausible because labor is a stress. Every real mechanism in the list is about delivery of oxygen to the fetus, not about the fetus making less hemoglobin.

Subtopic: the compromise cascade

Q: An abnormal FHR pattern persists uncorrected. In what order does the chapter describe fetal compromise progressing?
A) Hypoxemia, then hypoxia, then metabolic acidosis, then acidemia
B) Hypoxia, then hypoxemia, then acidemia, then metabolic acidosis
C) Metabolic acidosis, then hypoxemia, then hypoxia, then acidemia
D) Acidemia, then hypoxemia, then hypoxia, then metabolic acidosis
Answer: A
Rationale: "Abnormal FHR patterns are associated with fetal hypoxemia, a deficiency of oxygen in the blood. If uncorrected, hypoxemia can deteriorate to severe fetal hypoxia, an inadequate supply of oxygen at the cellular level that can cause metabolic acidosis. Metabolic acidosis, in turn, can lead to acidemia or increased hydrogen ion content (decreased pH) in the blood."
Trap: (B) swaps the first two terms, which is the common inversion. Hypoxemia is a blood finding and comes first; hypoxia is the cellular consequence.

Q: A newborn's record documents metabolic acidemia at birth. What does the chapter say this may indicate?
A) A transient finding of no clinical consequence
B) Clinically significant interruption of fetal oxygenation, leading to cellular dysfunction, tissue dysfunction, or death
C) Deficiency of oxygen in the blood without cellular effect
D) An inadequate supply of oxygen at the cellular level only
Answer: B
Rationale: "Metabolic acidemia may indicate clinically significant interruption of fetal oxygenation, leading to cellular dysfunction, tissue dysfunction, or death." It is the end of the cascade, not a step within it.
Trap: (C) and (D) restate hypoxemia and hypoxia — the two earlier steps — which is the error that follows from holding the four terms as synonyms rather than as a sequence.

Subtopic: what monitoring can and cannot do

Q: A nurse is asked why electronic monitoring remains nearly universal despite its known limits. Which statements does the chapter support? Select all that apply.
A) EFM is used with more than 80% of all laboring women in the United States
B) Research has not shown that intrapartum FHR monitoring leads to a significant decrease in neonatal neurologic morbidity
C) EFM has high sensitivity, so moderate variability with accelerations nearly always means a well-oxygenated fetus
D) EFM has low specificity, with many false-positive results
E) Continuous EFM has been associated with a reduction in cesarean births
Answer: A, B, C, D
Rationale: The chapter reports that EFM is used in more than 80% of United States labors, that research has not shown a significant decrease in neonatal neurologic morbidity, and from the Evidence-Based Practice box that EFM has high sensitivity and low specificity. It also attributes continued reliance on EFM to "staffing patterns, staffing mix, and increased defensive practices in a litigious environment."
Trap: (E) reverses the finding that matters most in this box: "continuous EFM has been associated with an increase in cesarean births and instrumented vaginal births." The documented benefit is reduced neonatal seizures, with no clear difference in cerebral palsy or infant mortality.

Topic: intermittent auscultation technique and terminology
Subtopic: performing the procedure

Q: A nurse is preparing to auscultate the FHR with a Doppler device. According to Box 18.2, what is the first step?
A) Count the maternal radial pulse while listening to the FHR
B) Perform Leopold maneuvers to identify fetal presentation and position
C) Palpate the abdomen for the presence or absence of uterine activity
D) Count the FHR for 30 to 60 seconds after a contraction
Answer: B
Rationale: Box 18.2 begins with Leopold maneuvers: "Perform Leopold maneuvers by palpating the maternal abdomen to identify fetal presentation and position." Locating the fetal back is what makes the listening device land on the area of maximal intensity.
Trap: (A) is a real step in the same box and is the one most stems reach for, because differentiating the maternal from the fetal rate feels like the safety step. It is step 3, after the device has been placed.

Subtopic: what auscultation can and cannot document

Q: A nurse auscultating the FHR documents "moderate variability with occasional variable decelerations." Why is this documentation inappropriate?
A) Because those terms are visual descriptions of patterns produced on a monitor tracing and cannot be derived from auscultation
B) Because variability can only be documented with an internal spiral electrode
C) Because auscultation findings must be documented as a single number without qualifiers
D) Because those terms apply only to the second stage of labor
Answer: A
Rationale: The Nursing Alert states that when the FHR is auscultated it is "inappropriate to use the descriptive terms associated with EFM (e.g., moderate variability, variable deceleration) because most of the terms are visual descriptions of the patterns produced on the monitor tracing."
Trap: (C) overcorrects. Auscultation supports a baseline number or range, a rhythm description, and abrupt or gradual changes — it is not reduced to a bare number.

Q: Which findings can the nurse legitimately document from intermittent auscultation? Select all that apply.
A) A baseline number or range
B) A regular or irregular rhythm
C) Abrupt or gradual increases or decreases before, during, and after contractions
D) Bradycardia or tachycardia, which are numerically defined
E) Minimal baseline variability
Answer: A, B, C, D
Rationale: The Nursing Alert permits a "baseline number or range and a regular or irregular rhythm," the presence of "abrupt or gradual increases or decreases in FHR," and the numerically defined terms bradycardia and tachycardia. The chapter separately lists what the nurse can assess by IA: "the counted FHR, the rhythm, the presence of increases and decreases in the FHR, and whether these changes are abrupt or gradual."
Trap: (E) is the one to leave unselected, and it is attractive because minimal variability has a number attached to it — 5 beats/min or less. The number does not rescue it: variability is an amplitude range judged by eye on a tracing, which auscultation does not produce.

Q: A nurse is using intermittent auscultation as the primary method of fetal assessment when the unit census rises and another laboring woman is assigned to them. What does the chapter direct the nurse to do?
A) Continue auscultation at half the usual frequency until staffing improves
B) Inform the physician or nurse-midwife that continuous EFM will be used until staffing can be arranged to meet the standards
C) Delegate auscultation to unlicensed assistive personnel
D) Document the staffing change and continue the current plan
Answer: B
Rationale: "When used as the primary method of fetal assessment, auscultation requires a one-to-one nurse-to-client staffing ratio. If acuity and census change so that auscultation standards are no longer met, the nurse must inform the physician or nurse-midwife that continuous EFM will be used until staffing can be arranged to meet the standards."
Trap: (A) is the informal workaround and is exactly what the standard forbids — the frequencies are the standard, so stretching them means auscultation is no longer being performed to standard.

Topic: monitoring frequency standards
Subtopic: auscultation intervals

Q: A low-risk woman not receiving oxytocin is in the active phase of the first stage at 7 cm. How often does AWHONN suggest auscultating the FHR?
A) Every 5 to 15 minutes
B) Every 15 to 30 minutes
C) Every 30 minutes
D) Frequency at the discretion of the provider, since evidence is insufficient
Answer: B
Rationale: Box 18.3 gives every 15 to 30 minutes for the first stage active phase at 6 cm or more, and the same interval for the latent phase at 4 to 5 cm.
Trap: (D) is the correct answer for a different row — the latent phase below 4 cm, where the box says there is "insufficient evidence to make a recommendation." The dilation number in the stem is what selects the row.

Q: The same low-risk woman reaches the second stage and begins actively pushing. How often should the FHR now be auscultated?
A) Every 15 to 30 minutes
B) Every 15 minutes
C) Every 5 to 15 minutes
D) Every 30 minutes
Answer: C
Rationale: Box 18.3 gives every 15 minutes during passive fetal descent and every 5 to 15 minutes during active pushing.
Trap: (B) is the interval for the other half of the second stage and is the mirror error here. The second stage splits at the start of active pushing, and the interval tightens when it does.

Q: A nurse plans to apply the AWHONN auscultation frequencies to a woman whose labor is being augmented with oxytocin. What does the chapter say?
A) The frequencies apply, since they are based on stage and phase rather than on medications
B) The frequencies apply only to low-risk women who are not receiving oxytocin
C) The frequencies should be doubled when oxytocin is infusing
D) The frequencies apply once the oxytocin has reached a stable rate
Answer: B
Rationale: The footnote to Box 18.3 is part of the standard: "These suggested frequencies apply only to low-risk women who are not receiving oxytocin."
Trap: (C) invents a workaround where the box simply stops applying, and it is attractive because doubling the frequency sounds more cautious than abandoning the table.

Subtopic: electronic monitoring intervals

Q: A woman with no risk factors is being monitored electronically in the active phase of the first stage, and a second woman with risk factors is in the second stage. How often should each tracing be evaluated?
A) Every 30 minutes for the low-risk woman in the first stage, and at least every 5 minutes for the woman with risk factors in the second stage
B) Every 15 minutes for the low-risk woman in the first stage, and at least every 15 minutes for the woman with risk factors in the second stage
C) Every 30 minutes for both
D) Every 5 minutes for the low-risk woman in the first stage, and every 30 minutes for the woman with risk factors in the second stage
Answer: A
Rationale: With EFM, low-risk women are evaluated every 30 minutes in the active phase of the first stage and at least every 15 minutes in the second stage; when risk factors are present, the intervals are at least every 15 minutes and at least every 5 minutes.
Trap: (B) uses real numbers from the same grid in the wrong cells, which is how these four values are usually missed. Risk doubles the frequency at each stage, and the second stage is always tighter than the first.

Topic: external versus internal monitoring modes
Subtopic: what each mode can measure

Q: A tocotransducer is in place on a laboring woman's abdomen. Which uterine activity data can it provide?
A) Frequency and duration only
B) Frequency, duration, and intensity
C) Frequency, duration, intensity, and resting tone in millimeters of mercury
D) Intensity and resting tone only
Answer: A
Rationale: Table 18.2 states the tocotransducer "monitors frequency and duration of contractions by means of a pressure-sensing device applied to the maternal abdomen," and the text adds that it "can measure and record the frequency and approximate duration of UCs, but not their intensity."
Trap: (C) describes the IUPC, which is the only device that reports intensity and resting tone in millimeters of mercury. Intensity is the single word that separates the two modes.

Q: An intrauterine pressure catheter is placed. Which data does it add that the external mode could not provide?
A) The frequency of contractions
B) The duration of contractions
C) The intensity of contractions and the uterine resting tone in millimeters of mercury
D) The fetal heart rate baseline
Answer: C
Rationale: "The IUPC can objectively measure the frequency, duration, and intensity of UCs and uterine resting tone," converting pressure at the catheter tip into millimeters of mercury.
Trap: (A) and (B) are the mirror error of the previous item — both modes give frequency and duration, so neither is what the internal catheter adds.

Subtopic: prerequisites and signal quality

Q: A provider wants to place a fetal spiral electrode. Which conditions must be met?
A) Membranes ruptured, cervix dilated at least 2 to 3 cm, and the presenting part low enough to allow placement
B) Membranes intact and cervix dilated at least 4 cm
C) Membranes ruptured and the woman in the second stage of labor
D) Cervix fully dilated, regardless of membrane status
Answer: A
Rationale: Internal monitoring requires all three conditions together: "the membranes must be ruptured, the cervix sufficiently dilated (at least 2 to 3 cm), and the presenting part ... low enough" to allow placement. The spiral electrode "penetrates into the fetal presenting part by 1.5 mm."
Trap: (B) is impossible as written — an option applying a spiral electrode through intact membranes describes something that cannot be done, and it is the distractor to recognize on sight.

Q: External monitoring on a woman with obesity is producing weak and intermittent signals. Which statements about this problem does the chapter support? Select all that apply.
A) Maternal obesity, anterior placental attachment, and fetal position or movement can cause weak, absent, or false signals
B) Internal monitoring is more accurate because it is not interrupted by fetal or maternal movement or affected by maternal size
C) Integrated abdominal monitoring eliminates much of the signal loss caused by maternal or fetal movement or maternal obesity
D) Internal and external modes may be combined without difficulty
E) Integrated abdominal monitoring provides intensity in millimeters of mercury, making an IUPC unnecessary
Answer: A, B, C, D
Rationale: The chapter names obesity, anterior placental attachment, and fetal position or movement as causes of poor external signals; credits internal monitoring with accuracy because it is unaffected by movement or maternal size; describes integrated abdominal monitoring as eliminating much of that signal loss; and states that internal and external modes "may be combined ... without difficulty."
Trap: (E) is the limitation of the wireless system stated backwards: "it does not provide actual intensity measurement in millimeters of mercury as an IUPC does," and intensity and resting tone are still determined by palpation.

Q: A nurse is setting up external monitoring. Which placement and setup details match Box 18.8 and the chapter?
A) The ultrasound transducer over the fundus with gel, and the tocotransducer below the umbilicus without gel
B) The ultrasound transducer over the point of maximal FHR intensity, usually below the umbilicus, with conductive gel, and the tocotransducer over the fundus without gel or paste
C) Both transducers over the fundus, both with conductive gel
D) The ultrasound transducer below the umbilicus without gel, and the tocotransducer over the fundus with gel
Answer: B
Rationale: The ultrasound transducer goes over the point of maximal FHR intensity, "usually below the umbilicus," with conductive gel; the tocotransducer is placed "over the fundus above the umbilicus" and, per Box 18.8, "applied without gel or paste."
Trap: (A) swaps the two placements, which is the error that produces an unreadable tracing at the bedside. Gel goes with sound waves, never with the pressure-sensing tocotransducer.

Topic: uterine activity and montevideo units
Subtopic: normal uterine activity in labor

Q: A nurse reviews a laboring woman's contraction pattern against Table 18.1. Which findings fall within normal uterine activity during labor? Select all that apply.
A) Contraction frequency of 2 to 5 per 10 minutes
B) Contraction duration of 45 to 80 seconds, not generally exceeding 90 seconds
C) Average resting tone of 10 mm Hg
D) Relaxation time of 60 seconds or more in the first stage
E) Contraction frequency of 7 per 10 minutes in the second stage
Answer: A, B, C, D
Rationale: Table 18.1 gives frequency of 2 to 5 per 10 minutes overall with higher frequencies up to 5 in the second stage, duration of 45 to 80 seconds not generally exceeding 90, average resting tone of 10 mm Hg, and relaxation time of 60 seconds or more in the first stage and 45 seconds or more in the second.
Trap: (E) exceeds the table's own ceiling. Five contractions in 10 minutes is the upper end of normal, and the second stage reaches that ceiling rather than passing it.

Q: A contraction is palpated as moderate. What internal pressure would the chapter expect it to peak at, and what would a contraction palpated as mild peak at?
A) Moderate at 50 mm Hg or greater; mild at less than 50 mm Hg
B) Moderate at less than 50 mm Hg; mild at 50 mm Hg or greater
C) Moderate at 80 mm Hg or greater; mild at 40 mm Hg
D) Moderate at 10 mm Hg; mild at 5 mm Hg
Answer: A
Rationale: Table 18.1 states that contractions palpated as mild "would likely peak at less than 50 mm Hg if measured internally, whereas contractions palpated as moderate or strong would likely peak at 50 mm Hg or greater."
Trap: (D) borrows the resting tone value of 10 mm Hg and uses it as a contraction peak, which is a magnitude error worth catching: resting tone is what the uterus reads between contractions, not during one.

Subtopic: montevideo units

Q: How are Montevideo units calculated, and what monitoring mode do they require?
A) By adding the peak pressures of all contractions in a 10-minute window, with either external or internal monitoring
B) By subtracting the baseline uterine pressure from the peak pressure of each contraction in a 10-minute window and adding those values, using internal monitoring only
C) By multiplying contraction frequency by average duration, using external monitoring
D) By averaging the resting tone over 10 minutes, using internal monitoring only
Answer: B
Rationale: "Montevideo units (MVUs) are calculated by subtracting the baseline uterine pressure from the peak contraction pressure for each contraction that occurs in a 10-minute window and then adding together the pressures generated by each contraction." Table 18.1 adds that "MVUs are used only with internal monitoring of contractions."
Trap: (A) omits the subtraction of resting tone and, more importantly, allows external monitoring. A stem calculating MVUs from a tocotransducer tracing is describing something that cannot be done.

Q: A woman's contractions are generating 110 MVUs. How should the nurse interpret this value?
A) It is below the range associated with initiating spontaneous labor
B) It is in the range generally sufficient to initiate spontaneous labor, which is 80 to 120 MVUs
C) It exceeds the usual maximum for the first stage of labor
D) It is the expected value for the second stage of labor
Answer: B
Rationale: "Contraction intensities of 40 mm Hg or more and MVUs of 80–120 are generally sufficient to initiate spontaneous labor." First-stage activity usually ranges from 100 to 250 MVUs and "rarely exceeds 250 MVUs," while second-stage values may reach 300 to 400.
Trap: (C) would require a value above 250, and the gap between 120 and 250 is where most of active labor actually sits — a value can be adequate to start labor and still be low for established labor.

Topic: baseline fetal heart rate
Subtopic: deriving the baseline

Q: A nurse is determining the baseline FHR from a 10-minute segment. What does the definition exclude?
A) Periodic or episodic changes, periods of marked variability, and segments differing by more than 25 beats/min
B) Accelerations only
C) Decelerations only
D) All periods of moderate variability
Answer: A
Rationale: The baseline is "the average rate during a 10-minute segment that excludes periodic or episodic changes, periods of marked variability, and segments of the baseline that differ by more than 25 beats/min."
Trap: (D) would exclude the very thing that makes a baseline normal. Moderate variability is a characteristic of the baseline itself, not an excursion from it.

Q: A 10-minute tracing segment contains only 90 seconds of interpretable data. What does the chapter say about determining a baseline?
A) A baseline can be determined from any amount of interpretable data
B) There must be at least 2 minutes of interpretable baseline data in the 10-minute segment to determine the baseline
C) There must be at least 5 minutes of interpretable data
D) The baseline is determined from the preceding segment instead
Answer: B
Rationale: "There must be at least 2 minutes of interpretable baseline data in a 10-minute segment of tracing to determine the baseline FHR."
Trap: (D) supplies a workaround the chapter does not offer, and it is tempting because it sounds like continuity of care. The rule is a data-sufficiency threshold, not a substitution rule.

Q: Over a 10-minute segment the FHR varies between 130 and 140 beats/min. How is the baseline recorded?
A) 130 beats/min, taking the lowest rate
B) 135 beats/min, rounding the approximate mean to the closest 5 beats/min interval
C) 140 beats/min, taking the highest rate
D) A range of 130 to 140 beats/min
Answer: B
Rationale: "After 10 minutes of tracing is observed, the approximate mean rate is rounded to the closest 5 beats/min interval." The chapter's own example uses these numbers and records the baseline as 135 beats/min.
Trap: (D) is how an auscultated FHR may be documented — as a number or range — but a baseline derived from a tracing is a single rounded value.

Subtopic: autonomic control and normal range

Q: A fetus has a baseline of 140 beats/min. What is the normal range, and what autonomic change would raise the rate?
A) The normal range is 110 to 160 beats/min, and an increase in sympathetic response accelerates the FHR
B) The normal range is 120 to 160 beats/min, and an increase in parasympathetic response accelerates the FHR
C) The normal range is 110 to 160 beats/min, and an increase in parasympathetic response accelerates the FHR
D) The normal range is 100 to 150 beats/min, and an increase in sympathetic response slows the FHR
Answer: A
Rationale: "The normal FHR range is 110 to 160 beats/min." And: "An increase in sympathetic response results in acceleration of the FHR, whereas an increase in parasympathetic response produces a slowing of the FHR."
Trap: (C) keeps the right range and inverts the autonomic rule, which matters downstream — decelerations are described as "caused by the dominance of a parasympathetic response," so holding this backwards breaks the mechanism of every deceleration.

Topic: fetal heart rate variability
Subtopic: the four categories

Q: A tracing shows baseline fluctuations with an amplitude range detectable to the unaided eye but no greater than 5 beats/min. How is this variability classified?
A) Absent
B) Minimal
C) Moderate
D) Marked
Answer: B
Rationale: Minimal variability "has an amplitude range that is detectable to the unaided eye but is 5 beats/min or less." Absent variability "is defined as an amplitude range of the FHR fluctuations that is not detectable to the unaided eye."
Trap: (A) is the mirror error and turns on one word: absent means not detectable at all, while minimal means detectable but small. A stem that says the fluctuations can be seen has already excluded absent.

Q: A tracing shows moderate variability. What does the chapter say this predicts?
A) It reliably predicts a normal fetal acid-base balance, meaning the absence of fetal metabolic acidemia
B) It predicts imminent fetal compromise unless accelerations are also present
C) Its significance is not known and it likely represents a normal variant
D) It predicts a normal outcome only when accompanied by early decelerations
Answer: A
Rationale: "Moderate variability is considered normal. Its presence reliably predicts a normal fetal acid-base balance (absence of fetal metabolic acidemia). Moderate variability indicates that FHR regulation is not affected significantly by interruption of fetal oxygenation."
Trap: (C) is the chapter's statement about marked variability, not moderate. Mapping the significance line to the wrong category is the error this pair exists to catch.

Q: A tracing shows marked variability. How should the nurse interpret it?
A) As an emergency requiring immediate delivery
B) As a finding whose significance is not known and that in many cases likely represents a normal variant
C) As diagnostic of fetal metabolic acidemia
D) As equivalent to absent variability in its implications
Answer: B
Rationale: "The significance of marked variability is not known. In many cases, it likely represents a normal variant." Marked variability does place a tracing in category II, because category I requires moderate variability, but the chapter will not commit to more than that.
Trap: (A) is the exam's favorite move with this category — treating an unfamiliar extreme as an emergency. The book declines to.

Subtopic: interpreting reduced variability

Q: A fetus at 26 weeks shows minimal variability 20 minutes after the woman received an opioid. Which causes of absent or minimal variability does the chapter name besides interruption of fetal oxygenation? Select all that apply.
A) Fetal sleep cycles
B) Fetal tachycardia
C) Extreme prematurity
D) Medications that cause central nervous system depression
E) Maternal fever alone
Answer: A, B, C, D
Rationale: The chapter lists as other possible causes "fetal sleep cycles, fetal tachycardia, extreme prematurity, medications that cause CNS depression, congenital anomalies, and preexisting neurologic injury," and notes that absent or minimal variability "can result from interruption of fetal oxygenation" but is classified "either abnormal or indeterminate" depending on the rest of the tracing.
Trap: (E) is listed in Table 18.3 as a cause of tachycardia, not of reduced variability. The two lists sit two pages apart and get merged.

Q: How is variability measured, and what does it include?
A) It is measured from the peak to the trough of a single cycle and does not include accelerations or decelerations
B) It is measured from the baseline to the peak of an acceleration
C) It is measured as the difference between the highest and lowest rates over 10 minutes, including decelerations
D) It is reported as long-term and short-term components measured separately
Answer: A
Rationale: "Variability is quantified in beats per minute and is measured from the peak to the trough of a single cycle," and "it is a characteristic of the baseline FHR and does not include accelerations or decelerations of the FHR."
Trap: (D) describes the obsolete framework. "The NICHD definitions do not distinguish between long- and short-term variability ... because in actual practice they are visually determined as a unit."

Subtopic: sinusoidal pattern

Q: A tracing shows a regular, smooth, undulating wavelike pattern that has persisted for 25 minutes. What does this represent, and what does it classically indicate?
A) Marked variability, indicating a normal variant
B) A sinusoidal pattern, which classically occurs with severe fetal anemia
C) Moderate variability, indicating normal acid-base balance
D) A prolonged deceleration, indicating cord compression
Answer: B
Rationale: "A sinusoidal pattern is a regular, smooth, undulating wavelike pattern that persists for at least 20 minutes," and "this uncommon pattern classically occurs with severe fetal anemia." Variants are described with chorioamnionitis, fetal sepsis, and opioid administration. It is also one of only two direct routes into category III.
Trap: (A) is the reflex pick because both patterns look busy on a strip. The chapter settles it in one line: the sinusoidal pattern "is not included in the definition of FHR variability."

Topic: tachycardia and bradycardia
Subtopic: the definitions

Q: A fetal baseline is 168 beats/min and has been for the past 12 minutes. How is this documented?
A) Tachycardia, defined as a baseline greater than 160 beats/min for 10 minutes or longer
B) A prolonged acceleration, since it exceeds 2 minutes
C) Marked variability
D) Tachycardia, but only if it persists beyond 30 minutes
Answer: A
Rationale: "Tachycardia is a baseline FHR greater than 160 beats/min for 10 minutes or longer," and Table 18.3 repeats the threshold as "FHR >160 beats/min lasting >10 min."
Trap: (B) misapplies the acceleration rules, and the chapter closes that door directly: "acceleration of the FHR for more than 10 minutes is considered a change in baseline rate." Past 10 minutes it is no longer an event, it is the baseline.

Q: A fetal baseline has been 104 beats/min for the past 14 minutes. How does this differ from a prolonged deceleration, and why does the distinction matter?
A) It is bradycardia, a baseline below 110 for 10 minutes or longer; a prolonged deceleration lasts more than 2 but less than 10 minutes, and the causes and management of the two are very different
B) It is a prolonged deceleration, since any drop below baseline lasting over 10 minutes is prolonged
C) There is no meaningful difference; the terms are interchangeable
D) It is bradycardia only if variability is also absent
Answer: A
Rationale: "Bradycardia is a baseline FHR less than 110 beats/min for 10 minutes or longer." The chapter flags the pair explicitly: true bradycardia "must be distinguished from a prolonged deceleration because the causes and management of these two conditions are very different."
Trap: (B) is the mirror error, and the 10-minute line is what settles it in both directions: below 10 minutes it is a prolonged deceleration, at or beyond 10 minutes it is a baseline change.

Subtopic: causes and clinical weight

Q: Which findings does Table 18.3 list as possible causes of fetal tachycardia? Select all that apply.
A) Maternal fever
B) Parasympatholytic drugs such as atropine and hydroxyzine
C) Beta-sympathomimetic drugs such as terbutaline
D) Fetal anemia
E) Maternal hypothermia
Answer: A, B, C, D
Rationale: Table 18.3 lists interruption of fetal oxygenation, fetal cardiac arrhythmias, maternal fever, infection including chorioamnionitis, parasympatholytic drugs such as atropine and hydroxyzine, beta-sympathomimetic drugs such as terbutaline, maternal hyperthyroidism, fetal anemia, and drugs such as caffeine, cocaine, and methamphetamines.
Trap: (E) belongs to the bradycardia column, along with maternal hypoglycemia, heart block, viral infections, fetal heart failure, and structural defects. Maternal temperature appears in both columns in opposite directions, which is what makes it worth sorting.

Q: A stem describes isolated tachycardia with moderate variability and no decelerations. What does the chapter say about its predictive value?
A) Tachycardia alone poorly predicts fetal hypoxemia or acidemia, and becomes meaningful when accompanied by absent or minimal variability, recurrent decelerations, or both
B) Tachycardia alone is diagnostic of fetal metabolic acidemia
C) Tachycardia alone requires immediate cesarean birth
D) Tachycardia is never clinically significant
Answer: A
Rationale: "Tachycardia alone poorly predicts fetal hypoxemia or acidemia. It can indicate fetal hypoxemia or acidemia when accompanied by absent or minimal baseline variability, recurrent decelerations, or both." Elsewhere the chapter notes it can be an early sign of hypoxemia "especially when associated with late decelerations and minimal or absent variability."
Trap: (D) overcorrects in the other direction. Neither extreme is the chapter's position: the accompanying variability is what gives a rate finding its weight.

Topic: accelerations and fetal stimulation
Subtopic: defining an acceleration

Q: A term fetus shows an abrupt increase in FHR peaking 18 beats/min above the baseline, lasting 20 seconds, returning to baseline 40 seconds after it began. How is this documented and what does it indicate?
A) An acceleration, indicating that significant interruption of fetal oxygenation is not occurring
B) A variable deceleration, since the onset was abrupt
C) Marked variability
D) A change in baseline rate
Answer: A
Rationale: An acceleration is "a visually apparent, abrupt (onset to peak <30 seconds) increase in FHR above the baseline rate," with a peak "at least 15 beats/min above the baseline," lasting "15 seconds or more," with return to baseline in less than 2 minutes. "Accelerations indicate that significant interruption of fetal oxygenation is not occurring. Their presence is highly predictive of a normal fetal acid-base balance."
Trap: (B) shares every number in this stem except direction. Accelerations and variable decelerations use the same 15 beats/min, 15 seconds, under 2 minutes template; the acceleration goes up to a peak and the variable goes down to a nadir.

Q: A fetus at 29 weeks shows an abrupt increase of 12 beats/min above baseline lasting 12 seconds. How is this documented?
A) It does not meet the criteria for an acceleration at any gestational age
B) It meets the acceleration criteria, because before 32 weeks the definition is a peak of 10 beats/min or more lasting at least 10 seconds
C) It meets the criteria only if it is repeated within 2 minutes
D) It is documented as minimal variability
Answer: B
Rationale: "Before 32 weeks of gestation, the definition of acceleration is a peak of 10 beats/min or more above the baseline and a duration of at least 10 seconds." The 15 by 15 criteria apply at and after 32 weeks.
Trap: (A) applies the term criteria to a preterm fetus, which is the error the gestational-age exception exists to catch. The preterm exception applies to accelerations only, not to decelerations.

Subtopic: eliciting a response

Q: A tracing shows no spontaneous accelerations. The nurse prepares for fetal scalp or vibroacoustic stimulation. What response is sought, and what does it mean?
A) An acceleration of at least 15 beats/min for at least 15 seconds, indicating the absence of metabolic acidemia
B) A deceleration of at least 15 beats/min, indicating intact vagal tone
C) Any change in the baseline within 3 seconds
D) An acceleration of at least 10 beats/min for at least 10 seconds at any gestational age
Answer: A
Rationale: The desired result is "acceleration in the FHR of at least 15 beats/min for at least 15 seconds," and "an FHR acceleration indicates the absence of metabolic acidemia." An acceleration in response to digital or vibroacoustic stimulation "is highly predictive of a normal scalp blood pH."
Trap: (D) imports the preterm acceleration criteria into the stimulation test, where the chapter states the 15 by 15 response without a gestational-age exception.

Q: A fetus is in the middle of a variable deceleration and the nurse considers scalp stimulation to assess well-being. What does the chapter direct?
A) Stimulate during the deceleration, since that is when the response is most informative
B) Do not stimulate; fetal stimulation should be performed when the FHR is at baseline, and neither scalp nor vibroacoustic stimulation should be instituted if decelerations or bradycardia are present
C) Stimulate only if the deceleration exceeds 2 minutes
D) Stimulate and, if there is no acceleration, conclude that fetal compromise is present
Answer: B
Rationale: The chapter gives two absolute timing rules: "fetal stimulation should be performed at times when the FHR is at baseline," and "neither fetal scalp nor vibroacoustic stimulation should be instituted if FHR decelerations or bradycardia are present."
Trap: (D) also misstates a non-response: "fetal compromise is not necessarily indicated if the fetus does not respond to stimulation with an acceleration; however, further evaluation of fetal well-being is needed."

Topic: early and late decelerations
Subtopic: telling them apart on the tracing

Q: A deceleration begins gradually, with the onset to the lowest point taking about 40 seconds, and its onset, nadir, and recovery correspond to the beginning, peak, and end of the contraction. How is this documented?
A) An early deceleration, a mirror image of the contraction
B) A late deceleration, because the onset is gradual
C) A variable deceleration, because it is associated with a contraction
D) A prolonged deceleration
Answer: A
Rationale: An early deceleration is "a visually apparent, gradual (onset to lowest point ≥30 seconds) decrease in and return to baseline FHR associated with UCs," in which the onset, nadir, and recovery correspond to the beginning, peak, and end of the contraction — the mirror image of the contraction.
Trap: (B) is right that gradual onset narrows the answer to early or late and then fails to use the timing. Both are gradual; only the position of the nadir separates them.

Q: A second deceleration is also gradual, but it begins after the contraction has started, its lowest point occurs after the peak of the contraction, and it does not return to baseline until after the contraction ends. How is this documented, and what causes it?
A) An early deceleration, caused by head compression
B) A late deceleration, caused by a reflex fetal response to transient hypoxemia during a contraction that reduces delivery of oxygenated blood to the intervillous space
C) A variable deceleration, caused by cord compression
D) A prolonged deceleration, caused by uterine tachysystole
Answer: B
Rationale: A late deceleration "begins after the contraction has started, and the lowest point ... occurs after the peak," and "usually does not return to baseline until after the contraction ends." It reflects the fetal response to transient or chronic uteroplacental insufficiency.
Trap: (A) is the mirror error of the previous item, and the cue that separates them is positional rather than causal: "mirror image" is early, "shifted right" is late. Reasoning from cause cannot be done from a tracing alone.

Subtopic: mechanism and significance

Q: A nurse explains why early decelerations are identified at all, given that they require no intervention. Which explanation matches the chapter?
A) So they can be distinguished from late or variable decelerations, which can be abnormal and for which interventions are appropriate
B) Because they predict cephalopelvic disproportion
C) Because they are an early sign of uteroplacental insufficiency
D) Because they must be reported to the provider within 30 minutes
Answer: A
Rationale: "The value of identifying them is so that they can be distinguished from late or variable decelerations, which can be abnormal and for which interventions are appropriate." Box 18.5 states that early decelerations "have no known relationship to fetal hypoxemia" and are "considered clinically benign," with no nursing interventions required.
Trap: (C) attributes the late deceleration's mechanism to the early one. Early decelerations are a fetal autonomic response to changes in intracranial pressure and cerebral blood flow from head compression.

Q: During which part of labor does the chapter say early decelerations usually occur?
A) During the first stage when the cervix is dilated 4 to 7 cm, and sometimes during the second stage when the woman is pushing
B) Only during the latent phase before 4 cm
C) Only during the second stage
D) Throughout labor with no characteristic timing
Answer: A
Rationale: Early decelerations "usually occur during the first stage of labor when the cervix is dilated 4 to 7 cm. However, they are sometimes seen during the second stage when the woman is pushing."
Trap: (C) is a plausible guess because pushing compresses the head, and the chapter does allow that — but as the secondary setting, not the primary one.

Q: Which conditions does Box 18.6 list as causes of late decelerations? Select all that apply.
A) Uterine tachysystole
B) Maternal supine hypotension
C) Epidural or spinal anesthesia
D) Placental abruption and hypertensive disorders
E) A knot in the umbilical cord
Answer: A, B, C, D
Rationale: Box 18.6 lists uterine tachysystole, maternal supine hypotension, epidural or spinal anesthesia, placenta previa, placental abruption, hypertensive disorders, postterm gestation, intrauterine growth restriction, diabetes mellitus, and chorioamnionitis — all mechanisms that disrupt oxygen transfer to the intervillous space.
Trap: (E) is from Box 18.7, the variable deceleration list, along with a short cord, a nuchal cord, prolapse, and maternal position with the cord between the fetus and the pelvis. Cord causes produce variables; placental causes produce lates.

Q: Late decelerations are occurring along with absent baseline variability, an abnormal baseline rate, and no accelerations. What does this combination indicate?
A) That the fetus is tolerating labor, since late decelerations are benign
B) That fetal metabolic acidemia may be present
C) That the pattern is category I and may be followed routinely
D) That cord compression is the likely cause
Answer: B
Rationale: Box 18.6 states that when late decelerations occur "along with absent or minimal baseline variability, abnormal baseline rate, and no accelerations," they "can indicate fetal metabolic acidemia." If interruption of fetal oxygenation results in metabolic acidemia, late decelerations may also arise from direct hypoxic myocardial depression during a contraction.
Trap: (A) applies the early deceleration's significance to a late one. The two are the same shape on a strip, and only the timing distinguishes a benign finding from this one.

Topic: variable and prolonged decelerations
Subtopic: recognizing a variable

Q: A deceleration drops abruptly, reaching its lowest point 20 seconds after onset, falling 25 beats/min below the baseline, lasting 30 seconds, and it occurs between contractions. How is this documented?
A) A variable deceleration, defined by a visually abrupt decrease with onset to lowest point under 30 seconds
B) An early deceleration, because it is brief
C) A late deceleration, because it occurs away from the peak of a contraction
D) A prolonged deceleration, because it falls more than 15 beats/min
Answer: A
Rationale: "Variable deceleration of the FHR is defined as a visually abrupt (onset to lowest point <30 seconds) decrease in FHR below the baseline," at least 15 beats/min below baseline, lasting at least 15 seconds and returning to baseline in less than 2 minutes. Variables "can occur with or without UCs."
Trap: (D) uses the right depth criterion and the wrong duration. A prolonged deceleration must last more than 2 minutes; 30 seconds is nowhere near it.

Q: What shape do variable decelerations take, and how does that differ from early and late decelerations?
A) Variables have a U, V, or W shape with rapid descent and ascent, while early and late decelerations closely approximate the shape of the corresponding contraction
B) Variables mirror the contraction while early and late decelerations are irregular
C) All three have the same shape and differ only in timing
D) Variables are always shallower than early decelerations
Answer: A
Rationale: "Variable decelerations have a U, V, or W shape," characterized by a rapid descent and ascent, unlike early and late decelerations, "which closely approximate the shape of the corresponding UC."
Trap: (B) inverts the two descriptions. Mirroring the contraction is what makes a deceleration early or late; a sharp-edged shape is what makes it variable.

Q: A variable deceleration is preceded and followed by brief accelerations. What are these called, and what do they represent?
A) Overshoots, indicating fetal metabolic acidemia
B) Shoulders, an appropriate compensatory response to compression of the umbilical vein
C) Sinusoidal waves, indicating fetal anemia
D) Marked variability, of unknown significance
Answer: B
Rationale: "Some variable decelerations are preceded and followed by brief accelerations of the FHR known as shoulders, which is an appropriate compensatory response to compression of the umbilical vein."
Trap: (A) names the other term from Box 18.1's category II list, where "overshoots" and "shoulders" appear together among variable decelerations "with other characteristics." Shoulders come before and after and are compensatory; the terms are adjacent and are easily traded.

Q: Variable decelerations are recurring with a rising baseline, minimal variability, absent accelerations, and a slow return to baseline. How common are variables overall, and what does this particular combination indicate?
A) They occur in approximately 50% of all labors and usually have little significance, but this combination can indicate fetal metabolic acidemia
B) They occur in fewer than 10% of labors and any occurrence indicates acidemia
C) They occur in approximately 50% of labors and are benign regardless of accompanying findings
D) They occur only with cord prolapse
Answer: A
Rationale: Box 18.7 states that "variable decelerations occur in approximately 50% of all labors and usually have little clinical significance," but that with "a rising baseline, absent or minimal baseline variability, absent accelerations, and a slow return to baseline," they "can indicate fetal metabolic acidemia." Recurrent variables "indicate a repetitive disruption in the oxygen supply."
Trap: (C) keeps the reassuring half of the box and discards the qualifier, which is the error that lets a deteriorating tracing be read as ordinary.

Subtopic: prolonged decelerations

Q: The FHR drops 20 beats/min below the baseline and stays there for 6 minutes. How is this documented, and what does the chapter direct the nurse to do?
A) A prolonged deceleration; immediately notify the obstetric health care provider and initiate appropriate treatment
B) Bradycardia; continue routine monitoring
C) A variable deceleration; reposition and continue to observe
D) A baseline change; recalculate the baseline over the next 10 minutes
Answer: A
Rationale: "A prolonged deceleration is a visually apparent decrease (may be either gradual or abrupt) in FHR of at least 15 beats/min below the baseline and lasting more than 2 minutes but less than 10 minutes." The Nursing Alert directs nurses to "immediately notify the obstetric health care provider and initiate appropriate treatment of abnormal patterns" when a prolonged deceleration is seen.
Trap: (D) is what the same tracing would become at 10 minutes: "a deceleration lasting more than 10 minutes is considered a baseline change." The clock is what relabels it, and until then it is an event requiring action.

Q: Which causes of prolonged decelerations does the chapter name?
A) Head compression and fetal sleep cycles
B) Uterine tachysystole, acute maternal hypotension, maternal respiratory or cardiac arrest, placental abruption, uterine rupture, and cord prolapse
C) Maternal fever and chorioamnionitis
D) Fetal anemia and chorioamnionitis
Answer: B
Rationale: The causes of prolonged decelerations "can occur anywhere along the oxygen pathway," and the chapter lists uterine tachysystole, acute maternal hypotension, maternal respiratory or cardiac arrest, placental abruption, uterine rupture, and cord prolapse. The mechanism is that the causes of late or variable decelerations "last for an extended period."
Trap: (A) names benign findings and is the wrong altitude entirely — this list is the emergency list, and every item on it is an acute catastrophe or a sustained interruption.

Topic: deceleration nursing priorities and intrauterine resuscitation
Subtopic: what comes first

Q: Recurrent late decelerations appear while oxytocin is infusing. According to Box 18.6, what is the nurse's first action?
A) Assist the woman to a lateral position
B) Discontinue the oxytocin
C) Increase the rate of the maintenance IV solution
D) Notify the obstetric health care provider
Answer: B
Rationale: Box 18.6 lists the interventions in priority order "beginning with the first listed," and the first is "discontinue oxytocin if infusing." Repositioning, correcting hypotension by elevating the legs, increasing the maintenance IV rate, palpating for tachysystole, and notifying the provider follow in that order.
Trap: (A) is second on the same list and is the answer most often given first, because repositioning feels like the immediate bedside action. With oxytocin running, stopping it is the first action for both late and variable decelerations.

Q: Recurrent variable decelerations appear and no oxytocin is infusing. According to Box 18.7, what is the nurse's next action after the oxytocin step is not applicable?
A) Change the maternal position, side to side or knee chest
B) Elevate the woman's legs to correct hypotension
C) Assist with amnioinfusion
D) Prepare for immediate cesarean birth
Answer: A
Rationale: Box 18.7's order is discontinue oxytocin if infusing, change maternal position (side to side, knee chest), notify the obstetric health care provider, assist with vaginal or speculum examination to assess for cord prolapse, assist with amnioinfusion if ordered, and assist with birth if the pattern cannot be corrected.
Trap: (B) belongs to the late deceleration list. Elevating the legs treats hypotension and therefore placental perfusion; variables are a cord problem, and moving the woman is what relieves cord compression.

Q: A nurse places a woman with late decelerations in the knee-chest position. Why is this the wrong intervention for this pattern?
A) Knee chest appears only in the variable deceleration box, because it relieves cord compression
B) Knee chest is contraindicated in any laboring woman
C) Knee chest worsens maternal hypotension in all positions
D) Knee chest is reserved for the second stage of labor
Answer: A
Rationale: The knee-chest position appears in Box 18.7 for variable decelerations, where the problem is cord compression. Late decelerations are treated by stopping oxytocin, lateral positioning, correcting hypotension, and increasing IV fluids — measures aimed at placental perfusion.
Trap: (B) overstates a positioning choice into a contraindication. Knee chest is a real intervention wrongly applied, which is what makes it an attractive distractor rather than an obviously wrong one.

Subtopic: intrauterine resuscitation

Q: Which interventions does Box 18.9 list as the basic measures for an abnormal FHR pattern? Select all that apply.
A) Assist the woman to a side-lying lateral position
B) Increase maternal blood volume by increasing the rate of the primary IV infusion
C) Administer oxygen at 10 L by nonrebreather mask
D) For maternal hypotension, change to a lateral or Trendelenburg position
E) For uterine tachysystole, reduce or discontinue any uterine stimulant in use
Answer: A, B, D, E
Rationale: Box 18.9's basic interventions are lateral positioning and increasing the primary IV rate, which "improve uterine and intervillous space blood flow and increase maternal cardiac output." The box then adds measures for maternal hypotension, for uterine tachysystole, and for an abnormal pattern during the second stage.
Trap: (C) is the one to leave unselected, and it reflects long-standing practice rather than current guidance. Oxygen does not appear anywhere in Box 18.9.

Q: A student asks why oxygen is not given routinely for intrauterine resuscitation. Which response reflects the chapter?
A) Two large studies found that intrapartum oxygen supplementation does not improve umbilical artery pH, does not decrease neonatal acidemia or NICU admissions, and does not resolve category II tracings, so routine use is not recommended in individuals with normal oxygen saturation
B) Oxygen is withheld only because it delays other interventions
C) Oxygen is contraindicated because it causes maternal hyperoxia and fetal vasoconstriction
D) Oxygen remains the first-line intervention and the chapter endorses it
Answer: A
Rationale: The chapter states that two large studies demonstrated intrapartum oxygen supplementation "does not improve umbilical artery pH or decrease the incidence of neonatal acidemia or neonatal intensive care unit admissions. It also does not resolve category II FHR tracings. Therefore routine use of oxygen supplementation in individuals with normal oxygen saturation for fetal intrauterine resuscitation is not recommended."
Trap: (C) invents a mechanism the chapter does not give. The recommendation rests on outcome evidence — it did not help — not on a proposed harm.

Q: Uterine tachysystole is contributing to an abnormal FHR pattern and the oxytocin has already been stopped. Which additional measure does Box 18.9 name?
A) Administer a uterine relaxant such as terbutaline
B) Administer ephedrine or phenylephrine
C) Begin amnioinfusion
D) Apply fundal pressure between contractions
Answer: A
Rationale: For uterine tachysystole, Box 18.9 directs the nurse to "reduce or discontinue dose of any uterine stimulants in use (e.g., oxytocin [Pitocin])" and to "administer uterine relaxant (tocolytic) (e.g., terbutaline [Brethine])."
Trap: (B) is the hypotension entry from the same box. The chapter also cautions that "some of the items listed are not independent nursing interventions" — any medication requires a unit protocol or an order.

Topic: three-tier fhr categories
Subtopic: what each category requires

Q: A tracing shows a baseline of 140 beats/min, moderate variability, no late or variable decelerations, occasional early decelerations, and no accelerations. How is it categorized, and what does that mean?
A) Category I, normal and strongly predictive of normal fetal acid-base status, which may be followed routinely
B) Category II, because accelerations are absent
C) Category II, because early decelerations are present
D) Category III, because accelerations are absent
Answer: A
Rationale: Box 18.1's category I requires all of a baseline of 110 to 160, moderate variability, absent late or variable decelerations, and permits early decelerations and accelerations to be "either present or absent." Category I tracings "are normal and strongly predictive of normal fetal acid-base status at the time of observation" and "do not require any specific action."
Trap: (B) and (C) both treat a permitted finding as disqualifying. Only the first three criteria are requirements; accelerations and early decelerations are explicitly optional in either direction.

Q: A tracing shows absent baseline variability with no decelerations of any kind. How is it categorized?
A) Category I
B) Category II
C) Category III, because variability is absent
D) Category III, because absent variability always requires immediate intervention
Answer: B
Rationale: Box 18.1 places "absent baseline variability not accompanied by recurrent decelerations" in category II. Category III requires absent variability plus recurrent late decelerations, recurrent variable decelerations, or bradycardia — or a sinusoidal pattern.
Trap: (C) is the single most common category error. Absent variability by itself is not category III; it takes absent variability plus one of three specific findings.

Q: Which findings place a tracing in category III? Select all that apply.
A) A sinusoidal pattern
B) Absent baseline variability with recurrent late decelerations
C) Absent baseline variability with recurrent variable decelerations
D) Absent baseline variability with bradycardia
E) Recurrent late decelerations with moderate baseline variability
Answer: A, B, C, D
Rationale: Category III has only two routes: a sinusoidal pattern, or absent baseline variability accompanied by recurrent late decelerations, recurrent variable decelerations, or bradycardia. Category III tracings "are abnormal. Immediate evaluation and prompt intervention are required."
Trap: (E) is category II, and it is the hardest one to leave unselected because recurrent late decelerations sound like the worst finding on the list. Moderate variability keeps the tracing out of category III, because it "reliably predicts a normal fetal acid-base balance."

Q: How is category II defined, and what does it require of the nurse?
A) It is defined by its own list of severity criteria and requires immediate intervention
B) It includes all tracings not categorized as category I or category III, is indeterminate, and requires continued observation and evaluation
C) It is a normal category requiring no specific action
D) It applies only to preterm fetuses
Answer: B
Rationale: "Category II FHR tracings include all FHR tracings not categorized as category I or category III," they are "indeterminate," and they "require continued observation and evaluation." It is the residue of the other two categories rather than a severity level of its own.
Trap: (A) treats category II as a defined severity band. It is defined by exclusion, which is why so many real tracings land in it.

Q: A category III tracing is identified. What does the chapter direct?
A) Continued observation and evaluation
B) Immediate evaluation and prompt intervention
C) Routine follow-up, since category III describes a preterm variant
D) Repeat assessment in 30 minutes before acting
Answer: B
Rationale: "Category III FHR tracings are abnormal. Immediate evaluation and prompt intervention are required when these patterns are identified."
Trap: (A) is the correct response to category II, and mapping it onto category III is the error with the highest cost in this chapter.

Topic: cord blood acid-base determination
Subtopic: which vessel means what

Q: Cord blood is obtained from both the umbilical artery and the umbilical vein. What does each sample reflect?
A) Arterial values reflect fetal condition, and venous values indicate placental function
B) Arterial values indicate placental function, and venous values reflect fetal condition
C) Both reflect fetal condition, and the two are averaged
D) Both indicate placental function, and the artery is used only for confirmation
Answer: A
Rationale: The interpretation rule is the reason two samples are drawn: "Umbilical arterial values reflect fetal condition, whereas umbilical vein values indicate placental function."
Trap: (B) is the mirror error and the reason a "normal cord gas" stem that does not name the vessel has not given enough to answer.

Q: Which vessel carries the better-oxygenated blood in fetal circulation, and how do its values compare?
A) The artery, with a higher pH, lower PCO2, and higher PO2 than the vein
B) The vein, with a higher pH of 7.3 to 7.4, lower PCO2 of 35 to 45, and higher PO2 of 25 to 35 than the artery
C) The vein, with a lower pH and higher PCO2 than the artery
D) They are equivalent, since both sample the same circulation
Answer: B
Rationale: Table 18.4 gives arterial values of pH 7.2 to 7.3, PCO2 45 to 55, and PO2 15 to 25, against venous values of pH 7.3 to 7.4, PCO2 35 to 45, and PO2 25 to 35. The umbilical vein is the better-oxygenated vessel in fetal circulation.
Trap: (A) applies adult anatomy, where arteries carry oxygenated blood, to the fetal circulation, where the umbilical vein is the oxygenated vessel. This is the reversal the table exists to correct.

Subtopic: when to draw and how to type

Q: A newborn has an Apgar score of 4 at 5 minutes. What does the chapter say about cord blood gases?
A) They add nothing once an Apgar score has been assigned
B) ACOG and AAP suggest obtaining umbilical artery cord blood values when a newborn has an Apgar score of 5 or less at 5 minutes of age
C) They should be obtained only if the pH is known to be below 7.0
D) They should be obtained for every birth regardless of Apgar score
Answer: B
Rationale: Cord blood is "a useful adjunct to the Apgar score," especially after an abnormal or confusing FHR tracing or neonatal depression at birth, and the professional organizations "suggest obtaining umbilical artery cord blood values when a newborn has an Apgar score of 5 or less at 5 minutes of age."
Trap: (C) inverts the order of operations — the gas is what determines the pH, so it cannot be conditioned on a pH already being known.

Q: A cord gas shows pH 7.15, an elevated PCO2, and a base deficit of 6 mmol/L. Which type of acidemia does Table 18.5 identify?
A) Respiratory
B) Metabolic
C) Mixed
D) None, because the pH is above 7.0
Answer: A
Rationale: Table 18.5 sorts acidemia on two independent axes: all three types have a pH below 7.20, an elevated PCO2 marks the respiratory component, and a base deficit of 12 mmol/L or more marks the metabolic component. An elevated PCO2 with a base deficit under 12 is respiratory.
Trap: (D) treats the significant-acidemia threshold as the definition of acidemia. A pH below 7.20 defines acidemia; pH of 7.0 or less with a base deficit of 12 mmol/L or more is the stricter threshold for potentially significant metabolic acidemia.

Q: A second cord gas shows pH 7.10, a normal PCO2, and a base deficit of 14 mmol/L. Which type of acidemia is this, and what does the pH alone tell the nurse?
A) Metabolic acidemia; the pH alone cannot type the acidemia because all three types have a pH below 7.20
B) Respiratory acidemia; the pH alone identifies the type
C) Mixed acidemia; the pH alone identifies the type
D) No acidemia is present
Answer: A
Rationale: A normal PCO2 with a base deficit of 12 mmol/L or more is metabolic acidemia. Because respiratory, metabolic, and mixed acidemia all carry a pH below 7.20, the pH establishes that acidemia exists but never which kind.
Trap: (C) is the mirror error of the previous item — mixed requires both an elevated PCO2 and a base deficit of 12 or more, and this gas has only one of the two abnormal.

Topic: amnioinfusion and tocolysis
Subtopic: what amnioinfusion treats

Q: A woman with oligohydramnios has recurrent variable decelerations. What is the stated purpose of amnioinfusion?
A) To relieve intermittent umbilical cord compression that results in variable decelerations and transient fetal hypoxemia, by restoring amniotic fluid volume
B) To dilute meconium-stained amniotic fluid
C) To improve placental perfusion in late decelerations
D) To increase uterine resting tone and strengthen contractions
Answer: A
Rationale: Amnioinfusion is the infusion of room-temperature isotonic fluid into the uterine cavity when amniotic fluid volume is low, done "to relieve intermittent umbilical cord compression that results in variable decelerations and transient fetal hypoxemia." Without the buffer of amniotic fluid, the cord is easily compressed during contractions or fetal movement.
Trap: (B) and (C) are both historically taught indications the chapter now rules out in one sentence: "amnioinfusion has no known effect on late decelerations and is no longer recommended to dilute meconium-stained amniotic fluid."

Q: During an amnioinfusion, which monitoring rules does the chapter give? Select all that apply.
A) The uterine resting tone should not exceed 40 mm Hg during the procedure
B) The amount of vaginal fluid return must be estimated and documented
C) The volume returned should be approximately the same as the amount infused
D) The maximum amount of fluid typically infused is 1000 mL
E) The recorded resting tone during amnioinfusion is normally lower than usual
Answer: A, B, C, D
Rationale: The chapter sets the resting tone ceiling at 40 mm Hg, requires that fluid return be estimated and documented to prevent overdistention, states the returned volume should approximate the infused volume, and gives 1000 mL as the typical maximum. A bolus is usually given over 20 to 30 minutes, then slowed to a maintenance rate.
Trap: (E) reverses the caveat: "the recorded uterine resting tone during amnioinfusion appears higher than normal because of resistance to outflow and turbulence at the end of the catheter." Knowing the reading runs high is what keeps a nurse from overreacting to it — or from ignoring the 40 mm Hg limit.

Subtopic: tocolysis in labor

Q: A woman has excessive uterine activity that has not responded to position change, IV hydration, and reduction of the oxytocin infusion. What does the chapter describe next, and with which drug?
A) Tocolysis, relaxation of the uterus through medications that inhibit contractions, commonly with terbutaline
B) Amnioinfusion with warmed normal saline
C) Oxygen by nonrebreather mask
D) Immediate cesarean birth without further intervention
Answer: A
Rationale: "Tocolysis (relaxation of the uterus) can be achieved through the administration of medications that inhibit UCs," and "tocolysis improves blood flow through the placenta by inhibiting UCs." It is used when position change, IV hydration, and reducing or stopping oxytocin have not worked, and "a commonly used tocolytic in these situations is terbutaline (Brethine)." If the patterns improve the woman may continue labor; if not, immediate cesarean birth may be needed.
Trap: (D) skips a step the chapter explicitly places first, and terbutaline is in fact "frequently administered after a decision for cesarean birth has been made while preparations for surgery are underway" — the drug and the surgery are not alternatives.

Q: Terbutaline appears in this chapter both as a cause of an FHR finding and as a treatment. Which statement captures both roles?
A) It is listed in Table 18.3 as a beta-sympathomimetic cause of fetal tachycardia and is also the commonly used tocolytic for excessive uterine activity
B) It is listed as a cause of fetal bradycardia and is also used to augment labor
C) It causes late decelerations and is used to treat cord compression
D) It is contraindicated in labor for any purpose
Answer: A
Rationale: Table 18.3 lists "beta-sympathomimetic drugs e.g., terbutaline [Brethine]" among the causes of fetal tachycardia, and the chapter names terbutaline as the commonly used tocolytic for tachysystole and excessive contractions. Both statements are true at once.
Trap: (B) misassigns the direction of the drug's effect. Whether a stem wants terbutaline as a cause or as a treatment depends on whether the drug is already running or is being considered.

Topic: monitoring standards and documentation
Subtopic: the nurse's legal responsibilities

Q: A nurse reads a tracing as category III, and the obstetric provider disagrees and declines to come to the bedside. According to the Legal Tip, what is the nurse responsible for doing?
A) Documenting the disagreement and repeating the assessment in 15 minutes
B) Initiating the institutional chain of command
C) Proceeding with the provider's interpretation, since interpretation is a medical decision
D) Obtaining a second nurse's interpretation and charting both
Answer: B
Rationale: The Legal Tip makes nurses "legally responsible for correctly interpreting FHR patterns, initiating appropriate nursing interventions based on those patterns, and documenting the outcomes of those interventions," for "timely notification of the obstetric health care provider," and for "initiating the institutional chain of command should differences in opinion arise among health care professionals."
Trap: (A) and (D) are both charting responses to a clinical disagreement, and they are the reflex answers because documentation is genuinely one of the four responsibilities. When opinions differ, the named action is the chain of command.

Q: Which components must be evaluated as the five essential components of the FHR tracing, and what follows if one is abnormal?
A) Baseline rate, baseline variability, accelerations, decelerations, and changes or trends over time; corrective measures must be taken immediately
B) Baseline rate, variability, uterine activity, maternal pulse, and maternal position; the provider is notified within an hour
C) Baseline rate, variability, and decelerations only; the assessment is repeated in 30 minutes
D) Baseline rate, accelerations, decelerations, and category; the tracing is filed
Answer: A
Rationale: The five essential components are "baseline rate, baseline variability, accelerations, decelerations, and changes or trends over time," and "whenever one of these five essential components is assessed as abnormal, corrective measures must be taken immediately."
Trap: (B) imports uterine activity into the list. Uterine activity is required in documentation, but the five essential components of the FHR tracing are all FHR findings.

Subtopic: documentation requirements

Q: Which components must appear in all FHR and uterine activity documentation? Select all that apply.
A) Baseline FHR and baseline variability
B) Presence of accelerations
C) Presence of periodic or episodic decelerations
D) Frequency, duration, intensity, and resting tone of uterine activity
E) The FHR category assigned by the provider
Answer: A, B, C, D
Rationale: The six required components are baseline FHR, baseline variability, presence of accelerations, presence of periodic or episodic decelerations, uterine activity including frequency, duration, intensity, and resting tone, and changes or trends over time.
Trap: (E) adds an item the chapter does not require, and it is attractive because category assignment is central to interpretation. The required list is findings, not conclusions.

Q: How soon should FHR and uterine activity documentation be recorded, and what terminology is required?
A) Within 30 to 60 minutes of the assessment, procedure, or occurrence, using standardized FHR and uterine activity terminology
B) At the end of the shift, using whatever terminology the unit prefers
C) Within 24 hours, using standardized terminology
D) Immediately and only in the electronic record, since handwritten charting is prohibited
Answer: A
Rationale: "Documentation should be recorded near the time of the assessment, procedure, or occurrence (i.e., within 30 to 60 minutes) according to agency policy," and "standardized FHR and UA terminology must be used in all documentation related to EFM. All documentation must be accurate. Legibility is also crucial, particularly if charting is handwritten."
Trap: (D) overstates the legibility rule into a prohibition. The chapter anticipates handwritten charting and requires that it be legible.
