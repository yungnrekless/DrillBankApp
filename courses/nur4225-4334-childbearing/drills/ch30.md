# Chapter 30 drills — Medical-Surgical Disorders

Authored from `sources/Ch30_Fact_Bank.md` (Lowdermilk, Cashion, Alden, Olshansky & Perry, 13th ed., ch. 30, pp. 639–664).

Blueprint weight is **~9 questions of 200 (4.5%), depth `full`** — topics
*Medical-Surgical Conditions in Pregnancy, sickle cell, cardiac disease, seizure
medications, surgery, CPR, cholecystitis*. The chapter is 26 pages; the Week 2
reading guide assigns about twelve of them, so those twelve earn **denser**
treatment, not broader.

The organizing insight the fact bank names is the spine of this set: **this is a
chapter about priorities, and in every one of its emergencies the priority is the
mother.** Resuscitate the woman first and the fetus benefits; assess the fetus
first and both are worse off. The same shape recurs in the cardiac sections
(protect the heart's workload and the pregnancy follows), in the seizure sections
(control seizures, then minimize the drug), and in surgery (the surgical
indication wins; pregnancy changes the technique, not the decision).

Every distinction the chapter states in both directions is drilled from both
ends: NYHA class and Box 30.1 risk group, what the woman reports and what the
nurse observes, sickle cell trait and sickle cell anemia, iron given and iron
withheld, cholelithiasis and cholecystitis, the three rungs of the urinary tract
infection ladder, abdominal thrusts and chest thrusts, and what changes in CPR
during pregnancy versus what does not.

**What is left out.** The chapter's catalogue of individual cardiac lesions
(pp. 640–643) is outside the assigned pages and is represented here only through
its two classification systems, which are what a question can hang on without
naming a lesion. Also omitted: pulmonary disorders including asthma,
integumentary disorders, the trauma sections proper (pp. 658–660), and the
secondary survey with perimortem cesarean birth (pp. 662–664). If the exam
reaches past the guide for this chapter, the lesion catalogue and trauma are
where it would go.

Topic: cardiac disease classification in pregnancy
Subtopic: class versus risk group

Q: A woman with mitral stenosis is described as symptomatic with marked limitation of activity. Which New York Heart Association class does this describe, and what does the classification measure?
A) Class II; it measures the mortality rate associated with her lesion
B) Class III; it measures the degree of disability — what she can physically do
C) Class III; it measures the mortality rate associated with her lesion
D) Class IV; it measures disability
Answer: B
Rationale: The NYHA classification is by symptoms and activity limitation: class I asymptomatic without limitation, class II symptomatic with slight limitation, class III symptomatic with marked limitation, and class IV symptomatic with inability to carry on any physical activity without discomfort.
Trap: (D) is the class for a woman who cannot carry on any activity without discomfort — marked limitation is not the same as no activity at all. And the chapter notes that "the degree of disability experienced by the woman ... is often more important in the treatment and prognosis ... than the diagnosis."

Q: A woman has Marfan syndrome with aortic involvement. Which Box 30.1 risk group does this fall into, and what does the group number indicate?
A) Group I, indicating a mortality rate under 1%
B) Group II, indicating a mortality rate of 5% to 15%
C) Group III, indicating a mortality rate of 25% to 50%
D) Group III, indicating marked limitation of activity
Answer: C
Rationale: Box 30.1 sorts lesions by maternal mortality rate: group I under 1%, group II 5% to 15%, and group III 25% to 50%. Group III contains pulmonary hypertension, complicated coarctation of the aorta, endocarditis, and Marfan syndrome with aortic involvement.
Trap: (D) reads the group as though it were an NYHA class. Group numbers describe how likely the lesion is to kill her; class numbers describe what she can do. Marfan syndrome with a normal aorta sits in group II, so the same named syndrome appears in two groups depending on severity.

Q: Which statements about the NYHA classification in pregnancy are accurate? Select all that apply.
A) The functional classification is determined at 3 months and again at 7 or 8 months of gestation
B) Women may progress from class I or II to class III or IV during pregnancy as cardiac output increases
C) Women with cyanotic congenital heart disease do not fit into the NYHA classification
D) About 1% of pregnancies are complicated by serious heart disease
E) Once assigned, a woman's class does not change during pregnancy
Answer: A, B, C, D
Rationale: The class is determined at 3 months and again at 7 or 8 months, women "may progress from class I or II to III or IV during the pregnancy as cardiac output increases and more stress is placed on the heart," and women with cyanotic congenital heart disease do not fit the classification "because their exercise-induced symptoms have causes unrelated to heart failure." About 1% of pregnancies involve serious heart disease.
Trap: (E) contradicts the reason for the two assessment points. The risk group is fixed because the lesion is fixed; the class moves because the physiology does.

Q: Why does the chapter identify fever as a particular danger for a woman with cardiac disease?
A) Fever is the major cause of cardiac decompensation during pregnancy
B) Fever causes hypotension and reduced preload
C) Fever is unrelated to cardiac workload
D) Fever indicates endocarditis in every case
Answer: A
Rationale: "Fever is the major cause of cardiac decompensation during pregnancy." Infections are treated promptly "because they can complicate the condition by accelerating the heart rate and by direct spread of organisms (e.g., streptococci) to the heart structures," and the woman is told to notify her provider at the first sign of an upper respiratory infection, especially if fever is present.
Trap: (D) names a real complication and makes it the only explanation, which would delay treatment of the far more common causes. Perinatal mortality of up to 50% is anticipated with persistent cardiac decompensation.

Topic: recognizing cardiac decompensation
Subtopic: what the nurse observes

Q: Which findings belong to the objective signs column of the Signs of Potential Complications box for cardiac decompensation? Select all that apply.
A) Irregular, weak, rapid pulse of 100 beats/min or greater
B) Crackles at the base of the lungs after two inspirations and exhalations that do not clear after coughing
C) Rapid respirations of 25 breaths/min or greater
D) Cyanosis of the lips and nailbeds
E) A feeling of smothering
Answer: A, B, C, D
Rationale: The objective column lists an irregular, weak, rapid pulse of 100 or greater, progressive generalized edema, crackles at the lung bases that do not clear with coughing, orthopnea and increasing dyspnea, respirations of 25 or greater, a moist frequent cough, and cyanosis of the lips and nailbeds.
Trap: (E) is in the woman's subjective column, along with increasing fatigue or difficulty breathing with usual activities, frequent cough, palpitations, and generalized edema she notices as rings that no longer fit. The box splits the two deliberately, and a SATA item scores against one column.

Q: A woman at 26 weeks with cardiac disease has a pulse of 104 beats/min, a respiratory rate of 26 breaths/min, and pale, cool, clammy skin. How should the nurse interpret these findings?
A) As expected pregnancy changes at the peak of cardiac output
B) As concerning per the Nursing Alert thresholds, with pale, cool, clammy skin possibly indicating cardiac shock
C) As signs of infection only
D) As within normal limits since the pulse is under 120
Answer: B
Rationale: The Nursing Alert states that "a pulse rate of 100 beats/min or greater or a respiratory rate of 25 breaths/min or greater is a concern," and that "pale, cool, clammy skin may indicate cardiac shock." The nurse checks respiratory status frequently for dyspnea, coughing, or crackles at the lung bases.
Trap: (A) uses a true fact — cardiac output peaks between 20 and 26 weeks, or 25 and 30 weeks by the management page — to normalize findings the chapter sets explicit thresholds for.

Subtopic: teaching the woman

Q: Which instructions belong in teaching for a pregnant woman at risk for cardiac decompensation? Select all that apply.
A) Immediately report generalized edema, distention of neck veins, dyspnea, a frequent moist cough, or palpitations
B) Immediately report pain, redness, tenderness, or swelling in the extremities, or chest pain
C) Avoid constipation and straining with bowel movements by taking in adequate fluids and fiber, using a stool softener if helpful
D) Weigh yourself daily at the same time each day
E) Increase physical activity progressively to build cardiac reserve
Answer: A, B, C, D
Rationale: The Teaching for Self-Management box lists reporting signs of decompensation and of venous thromboembolism, avoiding the Valsalva maneuver by preventing constipation, daily weights at the same time, keeping all prenatal appointments, and limiting activity as instructed.
Trap: (E) inverts the activity instruction. The Valsalva maneuver is "forced expiration against a closed airway, which when released, causes blood to rush to the heart and overload the cardiac system" — which is why straining is what the bowel teaching is really about.

Q: When are the signs and symptoms of cardiac decompensation taught?
A) At the first prenatal visit and reviewed at each subsequent visit
B) Only at the 36-week visit
C) Only after the first episode of decompensation
D) At discharge after birth
Answer: A
Rationale: "Signs and symptoms of cardiac decompensation are taught at the first prenatal visit and reviewed at each subsequent visit."
Trap: (C) waits for the event the teaching exists to catch early. Decompensation is the inability of the heart to maintain sufficient cardiac output, and the woman is the one who notices the earliest symptoms.

Topic: antepartum cardiac management and drugs
Subtopic: reducing the workload

Q: What is the governing principle of antepartum therapy for a pregnant woman with heart disease, and which coexisting conditions are treated because of it?
A) Minimizing stress on the heart, which intensifies as cardiac output increases; emotional stress, hypertension, anemia, hyperthyroidism, and obesity are treated because each adds workload
B) Maximizing cardiac output to perfuse the placenta
C) Restricting all activity until birth
D) Preventing weight gain below 15 pounds
Answer: A
Rationale: "Therapy for the pregnant woman with heart disease is focused on minimizing stress on the heart, which intensifies as cardiac output increases," and "the workload of the cardiovascular system is reduced by appropriate treatment of any coexisting emotional stress, hypertension, anemia, hyperthyroidism, or obesity."
Trap: (B) has the physiology backwards for this population. Cardiac output rises 30% to 45% in normal pregnancy, and that rise is precisely the stress being managed.

Q: A woman with cardiac disease is prescribed warfarin. What nutritional teaching does the chapter specify, and which anticoagulant does Table 30.1 list as having no reported fetal side effects?
A) Avoid foods high in vitamin K such as raw dark green leafy vegetables, which counteract warfarin; heparin's side-effect column reads "None reported"
B) Increase vitamin K intake to prevent hemorrhage; heparin crosses the placenta
C) Avoid vitamin C; warfarin has no reported fetal effects
D) No dietary teaching is needed; both drugs are equivalent in pregnancy
Answer: A
Rationale: A woman taking warfarin needs teaching "to avoid foods high in vitamin K, such as raw, dark green leafy vegetables, which counteract the effects of warfarin," plus a folic acid supplement. Table 30.1 lists warfarin as crossing the placenta with fetal hemorrhage, embryopathy, and CNS abnormalities, while heparin's potential side effects are "None reported."
Trap: (B) reverses the vitamin K relationship. Vitamin K is the antidote to warfarin's effect, so loading up on it defeats the anticoagulation the woman needs.

Q: Which drug classes in Table 30.1 carry fetal structural harm in their side-effect column?
A) Digoxin and lidocaine
B) Warfarin and ACE inhibitors
C) Heparin and procainamide
D) Verapamil and quinidine
Answer: B
Rationale: Warfarin "crosses placenta; fetal hemorrhage, embryopathy, CNS abnormalities," and ACE inhibitors carry "oligohydramnios, IUGR, prematurity, neonatal hypotension, renal failure, anemia, death, skull ossification defect, limb contractures, patent ductus arteriosus." Digoxin has "no evidence for unfavorable side effects on the fetus," and heparin has none reported.
Trap: (D) names two drugs with real but lesser cautions — one uncertain fetal death with verapamil, and transient neonatal thrombocytopenia with high-dose quinidine — which is different from a list of structural anomalies.

Subtopic: cardiac surgery in pregnancy

Q: A woman requires cardiac surgery during pregnancy. What does the chapter say about the risks and timing?
A) Maternal mortality risk does not increase, but fetal mortality risk is 10% to 15%, especially with cardiopulmonary bypass; surgery should be postponed to the third trimester if possible, and hypothermia avoided
B) Both maternal and fetal mortality rise sharply, and surgery is deferred until after birth in all cases
C) Surgery is safest in the first trimester
D) Hypothermia is used deliberately to protect the fetus
Answer: A
Rationale: "The maternal mortality risk does not increase, but there is a fetal mortality risk of 10% to 15% if heart surgery is performed, especially if cardiopulmonary bypass is used." Surgery should be postponed to the third trimester if possible, "when the risk to the fetus is considerably decreased," and "hypothermia should also be avoided because it appears to be especially dangerous for the fetus." Ideally, correction happens before pregnancy.
Trap: (D) inverts a stated danger into a technique. Cooling is protective in some cardiac surgery and is specifically avoided here because of the fetus.

Topic: intrapartum care with cardiac disease
Subtopic: positioning and pushing

Q: A woman with cardiac disease is in the second stage of labor. Which measures support cardiac function? Select all that apply.
A) Keeping her head and shoulders elevated with body parts resting on pillows
B) Encouraging a side-lying position and avoiding the supine position, using a wedge under one hip if supine is necessary
C) Having her flex her knees with feet flat on the bed rather than using stirrups
D) Encouraging open-glottis pushing and avoiding the Valsalva maneuver
E) Placing her in lithotomy with stirrups to shorten the second stage
Answer: A, B, C, D
Rationale: Nursing care during labor "focuses on promoting cardiac function," with the head and shoulders elevated, a side-lying position, the supine position avoided, knees flexed with feet flat because "the use of stirrups is contraindicated" from popliteal vein compression, and open-glottis pushing because Valsalva "reduces diastolic ventricular filling and obstructs left ventricular outflow."
Trap: (E) takes a real goal — shortening the second stage — and attaches it to a contraindicated intervention. The chapter shortens the second stage with episiotomy and vacuum extraction or outlet forceps.

Q: Why is the ideal labor for a woman with heart disease described as short and pain-free, and what anesthesia is encouraged?
A) Because pain and prolonged effort increase cardiac workload; epidural anesthesia is encouraged, with care to avoid hypotension
B) Because anesthesia is contraindicated, so labor must be brief
C) Because general anesthesia is preferred for all cardiac patients
D) Because pain relief increases cardiac output
Answer: A
Rationale: "Physiologically the ideal labor for a woman with heart disease is one that is short and pain-free," and "use of epidural anesthesia is encouraged, although care must be taken to avoid hypotension, a common side effect of regional anesthesia." Mask oxygen is also important.
Trap: (C) reaches for general anesthesia, which the chapter does not favor here; the concern with regional anesthesia is a manageable side effect, not a contraindication.

Subtopic: medications and route of birth

Q: A woman with known cardiac disease develops preterm contractions, and terbutaline is ordered. Why should the nurse question this?
A) Beta-adrenergic agents such as terbutaline cause tachycardia, irregular pulse, myocardial ischemia, and pulmonary edema, and are contraindicated for women with known cardiac disease
B) Terbutaline is ineffective in cardiac disease
C) Terbutaline may only be given intravenously
D) Terbutaline is contraindicated only after 34 weeks
Answer: A
Rationale: "Beta-adrenergic agents such as terbutaline (Brethine) are associated with various side effects including tachycardia, irregular pulse, myocardial ischemia, and pulmonary edema. These medications are contraindicated for women with known cardiac disease."
Trap: (D) converts an absolute contraindication into a timing rule, which is how a plausible order gets past a nurse who half-remembers it.

Q: Immediately after the birth, which uterotonic does the chapter direct and which is avoided for a woman with cardiac disease?
A) Oxytocin is usually given to prevent hemorrhage; ergot products such as methylergonovine should not be used because they increase blood pressure
B) All uterotonics are avoided in cardiac disease
C) Methylergonovine is preferred because it acts faster
D) Only misoprostol may be used
Answer: A
Rationale: "Oxytocin is usually given immediately after birth to prevent hemorrhage. Ergot products (e.g., methylergonovine [Methergine]) should not be used because they increase blood pressure."
Trap: (B) is the overgeneralization the pair exists to prevent. Hemorrhage is itself a threat to a compromised heart, so withholding all uterotonics would create the problem it tries to avoid.

Q: What route of birth does the chapter prefer for women with cardiac disease, and why is cesarean birth not routinely recommended?
A) Spontaneous or induced labor with vaginal birth is preferred; cesarean birth carries risks of dramatic fluid shifts, sustained hemodynamic changes, and increased blood loss
B) Cesarean birth is preferred because it avoids the work of labor
C) Cesarean birth is required for any NYHA class above I
D) Vaginal birth is preferred only for class I women
Answer: A
Rationale: "Spontaneous or induced (with a favorable cervix) labor followed by vaginal birth is preferred for women with cardiac disease," and "cesarean birth is not routinely recommended for women who have cardiovascular disease because of the risks of dramatic fluid shifts, sustained hemodynamic changes, and increased blood loss."
Trap: (B) is the intuitive conclusion — surgery seems to spare the heart the work — and the chapter states the opposite because of what the surgery itself does to volume and blood loss.

Topic: postpartum cardiac care and contraception
Subtopic: the danger window

Q: When is the most hemodynamically dangerous period for a woman with compromised heart function, and why?
A) During the active phase of labor, from the work of contractions
B) The immediate postbirth period and especially the first 24 to 48 hours, because cardiac output increases rapidly as extravascular fluid is remobilized into the vascular compartment and intraabdominal pressure drops
C) At 20 to 26 weeks, when cardiac output peaks
D) At the 6-week postpartum visit
Answer: B
Rationale: The Safety Alert states that "the immediate postbirth period is hazardous for a woman whose heart function is compromised. Cardiac output increases rapidly as extravascular fluid is remobilized into the vascular compartment. At the moment of birth, intraabdominal pressure is reduced dramatically; pressure on veins is removed, the splanchnic vessels engorge, and blood flow to the heart is increased." The first 24 to 48 hours after birth are the most hemodynamically difficult.
Trap: (A) names the period that feels most strenuous. The volume shift at birth, not the muscular work before it, is what the heart has to absorb.

Q: A woman with cardiac disease had no evidence of compromise during pregnancy or labor. What should the nurse teach her about the first 2 weeks postpartum?
A) That the risk has passed once she is discharged
B) That during the first 2 postpartum weeks extravascular fluid is mobilized, diuresis begins, and vascular resistance increases, and women with no prior evidence of compromise may decompensate during this time
C) That cardiac output returns to normal within 24 hours
D) That decompensation occurs only in women who had symptoms antepartum
Answer: B
Rationale: "During the first 2 postpartum weeks, extravascular fluid is mobilized, diuresis begins, and vascular resistance increases, as the woman returns to a nonpregnant state," and women "who have demonstrated little or no evidence of cardiac compromise during the antepartum or intrapartum period may do so postpartum." Maternal cardiac output usually returns to normal by 2 weeks postpartum.
Trap: (D) uses a reassuring history to exclude a risk the chapter specifically attaches to women without one.

Subtopic: breastfeeding and contraception

Q: A woman with cardiac disease taking furosemide wants to breastfeed. What does the chapter say?
A) Breastfeeding is contraindicated with any cardiac medication
B) Breastfeeding is not contraindicated and most cardiac medications are compatible with it, but diuretics such as furosemide and hydrochlorothiazide may decrease the milk supply and can cause neonatal diuresis leading to dehydration
C) Diuretics have no effect on lactation
D) She must switch to formula because diuretics are unsafe for infants
Answer: B
Rationale: "Breastfeeding is not contraindicated," and "most medications used to manage cardiac disorders are compatible with breastfeeding." The exception is that diuretics "may decrease the milk supply," and because they "can cause neonatal diuresis that can lead to dehydration," the infant is monitored for voiding patterns and signs of dehydration.
Trap: (D) escalates a monitoring requirement into a prohibition. If a woman cannot feed at all, the chapter still keeps the baby at the bedside so she can bond "with a low expenditure of energy."

Q: Which contraceptive method does the chapter recommend as the nonpermanent option for women with high-risk cardiovascular conditions, and what is the framing principle?
A) The IUD, because it provides highly effective and long-lasting protection; in general the complications associated with pregnancy are greater than the risks associated with any form of contraception
B) Combined estrogen-progestin oral contraceptives, because they are best studied
C) Barrier methods only, since hormones are contraindicated
D) No contraception, since fertility is reduced by cardiac disease
Answer: A
Rationale: IUDs are "the nonpermanent method recommended for women with high-risk cardiovascular conditions because they provide highly effective and long-lasting protection," and the chapter frames the decision: "in general, the complications associated with pregnancy are usually greater than the risks associated with any form of contraception."
Trap: (B) names the method women "at particular risk for thromboembolism should avoid." Progestin-only pills may be used, parenteral progestins are safe and effective but cause irregular bleeding that is problematic on anticoagulants, and progestin implants are limited by a history of ischemic heart disease or stroke.

Q: A woman with congenital heart disease asks about her children's risk. What does the chapter say?
A) There is no heritable component
B) Both women and men with congenital heart disease are at increased risk of having affected children, the risk is greater for affected mothers than fathers, and children appear to inherit the risk for a defect in general rather than for a specific defect
C) Children inherit the identical defect
D) Only fathers transmit the risk
Answer: B
Rationale: "Both women and men with congenital heart disease are at increased risk for having children who also have congenital heart disease. The risk for affected mothers is greater than for affected fathers." Children "appear to inherit the risk for a defect in general rather than for a specific defect," which is why preconception and genetic counseling are considered essential.
Trap: (C) is the intuitive reading of heritability and would give false precision in counseling — the risk transmitted is general.

Topic: anemia in pregnancy
Subtopic: defining and treating iron deficiency

Q: At what hemoglobin values is anemia diagnosed in pregnancy, and why does anemia matter for cardiac function?
A) Less than 11 g/dL in the first and third trimesters and less than 10.5 g/dL in the second; anemia reduces oxygen-carrying capacity, so the heart compensates by increasing cardiac output, increasing its workload
B) Less than 12 g/dL throughout pregnancy; anemia has no cardiac implications
C) Less than 10 g/dL throughout pregnancy; the heart compensates by slowing
D) Less than 11 g/dL only in the second trimester
Answer: A
Rationale: "Anemia in pregnancy is defined as hemoglobin less than 11 g/dL in the first and third trimesters and less than 10.5 g/dL in the second trimester." It "results in a reduction of the oxygen-carrying capacity of the blood; the heart tries to compensate by increasing the cardiac output," which "increases the workload of the heart and stresses ventricular function."
Trap: (B) applies the nonpregnant threshold and misses the physiologic dilution of pregnancy — the mean hematocrit at term is 33.8%, against 37% to 47% in nonpregnant women.

Q: A woman's serum ferritin is 9 mcg/L with a low hemoglobin. What does this indicate, and what is the usual treatment?
A) Iron deficiency anemia, treated with one 325-mg tablet of ferrous sulfate twice each day
B) Folate deficiency, treated with 600 mcg of folic acid
C) Sickle cell anemia, treated with transfusion
D) A normal value requiring no treatment
Answer: A
Rationale: "The serum ferritin level reflects iron reserves," and a level "less than 12 mcg/L along with a low hemoglobin value" is diagnostic of iron deficiency. "Most women with iron deficiency anemia can absorb as much iron as they need by taking one 325-mg tablet of ferrous sulfate twice each day." Iron deficiency anemia accounts for approximately 75% of anemia in pregnancy.
Trap: (C) is the disorder where iron is specifically withheld, which makes it worth keeping straight from the one where iron is the treatment.

Subtopic: folate and megaloblastic anemia

Q: How much folic acid does the chapter recommend for women capable of becoming pregnant, and how much during pregnancy?
A) 400 mcg for women planning or capable of pregnancy, and 50% more — 600 mcg per day — during pregnancy
B) 600 mcg before pregnancy and 400 mcg during pregnancy
C) 1 mg before and during pregnancy
D) 4 mg for all women
Answer: A
Rationale: Women "planning a pregnancy or capable of becoming pregnant" are advised to take 400 mcg (0.4 mg), and pregnant women "50% more, or 600 mcg (0.6 mg)/day."
Trap: (C) and (D) are doses from other conditions in this same chapter: at least 1 mg daily in sickle cell anemia, and 2 to 4 mg in inflammatory bowel disease. Each disorder carries its own number.

Q: Which statements about megaloblastic anemia in pregnancy are accurate? Select all that apply.
A) Folate deficiency is its most common cause, but vitamin B12 deficiency must also be considered
B) It rarely occurs before the third trimester of pregnancy
C) Signs include pallor, fatigue, lethargy, glossitis, and skin roughness
D) Women with hemoglobinopathies, on anticonvulsants, with multifetal gestation, or with frequent pregnancies are at particular risk
E) It is diagnosed by a serum ferritin below 12 mcg/L
Answer: A, B, C, D
Rationale: "Folate deficiency is the most common cause of megaloblastic anemia during pregnancy, but a vitamin B12 deficiency must also be considered." It "rarely occurs before the third trimester," its signs include glossitis and skin roughness specifically, and the at-risk groups are as listed. B12 deficiency is seen more often after bariatric surgery, with Crohn disease, and with metformin use.
Trap: (E) is the iron-deficiency test. Ferritin measures iron stores and says nothing about folate.

Topic: sickle cell hemoglobinopathy
Subtopic: trait versus disease

Q: A woman has sickle cell trait. How does this differ from sickle cell anemia, and what does it mean for her pregnancy?
A) Trait is the SA hemoglobin pattern with a normal RBC life span and is usually asymptomatic; women with trait usually do well in pregnancy but remain at increased risk for preeclampsia, intrauterine fetal death, preterm birth, low birth weight, postpartum endometritis, and UTIs
B) Trait and anemia are the same condition with different names
C) Trait carries no increased pregnancy risk of any kind
D) Trait means an RBC life span of 5 to 10 days
Answer: A
Rationale: "Sickle cell trait (SA hemoglobin pattern) is sickling of the RBCs but with a normal RBC life span," and "most people with sickle cell trait are asymptomatic." Women with trait "usually do well in pregnancy" but carry the listed increased risks. Approximately 1 in 12 Black adults in the United States has sickle cell trait.
Trap: (C) is the overcorrection this pair exists to catch — "usually do well" is not the same as "no added risk," and the list of added risks is specific.

Q: A woman with sickle cell anemia asks about her children. What does the chapter state?
A) Her children have a 50% chance of being unaffected
B) All children born to a woman with sickle cell anemia will be affected in some way by the disease, and partner testing determines the risk of children with disease rather than trait
C) Her children will have trait only if the father has trait
D) The disease is not heritable
Answer: B
Rationale: "All children born to a woman with sickle cell anemia will be affected in some way by the disease." The partner is tested to determine whether children would have disease rather than trait. Sickle cell anemia is "a recessive, hereditary, familial hemolytic anemia," occurring in 1 in 708 Black people in the United States, with an RBC life span of only 5 to 10 days against the normal 120 days.
Trap: (A) applies standard recessive-inheritance arithmetic to a woman who is homozygous, which changes the math entirely.

Subtopic: managing sickle cell disease in pregnancy

Q: A woman with sickle cell anemia is admitted in a painful crisis. What triggers crises, and how are they managed?
A) Dehydration, hypoxia, or acidosis; crises are managed with analgesia, oxygen, and hydration
B) Excess iron intake; crises are managed with chelation
C) Hypertension; crises are managed with antihypertensives
D) Cold exposure only; crises are managed with warming
Answer: A
Rationale: "Crises are usually triggered by dehydration, hypoxia, or acidosis," and attacks are "attributed to vascular occlusion when RBCs assume a characteristic sickled shape." The chapter directs: "If crises occur, they are managed with analgesia, oxygen, and hydration." The frequency of painful crises "appears to be increased during pregnancy."
Trap: (B) confuses a treatment complication with a trigger. Iron overload is a real danger for these women, but it comes from unnecessary supplementation, not from the crisis mechanism.

Q: A woman with sickle cell anemia is admitted for prenatal care and the standard prenatal vitamin with iron is ordered. Why should the nurse question this?
A) Women with sickle cell anemia are not iron deficient, so routine iron supplementation, even in prenatal vitamins, should be avoided because these women can develop iron overload
B) Prenatal vitamins interfere with folic acid absorption
C) Iron triggers sickling directly
D) Prenatal vitamins are contraindicated in all hemoglobinopathies
Answer: A
Rationale: The Safety Alert states: "Women with sickle cell anemia are not iron deficient. Therefore routine iron supplementation, even in prenatal vitamins, should be avoided because these women can develop iron overload."
Trap: (D) overgeneralizes to thalassemia, where "iron therapy should be prescribed only for women who are iron deficient" but folic acid is recommended for all — a conditional rule rather than a blanket one.

Q: What supplementation and surveillance does the chapter specify for a pregnant woman with sickle cell anemia? Select all that apply.
A) Folic acid supplementation of at least 1 mg per day beginning as soon as pregnancy is diagnosed
B) Monitoring for the development of urinary tract infection or preeclampsia
C) Serial ultrasound examinations throughout pregnancy to monitor fetal growth
D) Antepartum fetal testing such as a nonstress test during the third trimester
E) Prophylactic transfusion, which has been shown to improve perinatal outcome
Answer: A, B, C, D
Rationale: "Folic acid supplementation of at least 1 mg (1000 mcg)/day should begin as soon as pregnancy is diagnosed," with monitoring for UTI and preeclampsia, serial growth ultrasounds, and third-trimester antepartum testing. Infections are treated aggressively with antibiotics.
Trap: (E) overstates the evidence in a specific way: "prophylactic transfusions ... have not been shown to improve perinatal outcome," although they "appear to significantly decrease the incidence of painful crises."

Q: How should a woman with sickle cell disease be cared for during labor?
A) Encouraged to labor in a side-lying position, possibly with supplemental oxygen, with adequate hydration maintained while preventing fluid overload, regional anesthesia recommended, and vaginal birth preferred with cesarean birth only for obstetric indications
B) Positioned supine with fluid restriction
C) Delivered by scheduled cesarean birth at 37 weeks
D) Given prophylactic transfusion in labor in every case
Answer: A
Rationale: Women are "encouraged to labor in a side-lying position," may require supplemental oxygen, and "adequate hydration should be maintained while preventing fluid overload." Regional anesthesia "is recommended because it provides excellent pain relief," and "vaginal birth is preferred. Cesarean birth should be performed only for obstetric indications." If no complications occur, pregnancy can continue until term.
Trap: (B) reverses both the position and the fluid plan, and dehydration is one of the three named crisis triggers.

Subtopic: thalassemia

Q: How do the two forms of beta-thalassemia differ in pregnancy?
A) The heterozygous minor form generally causes no maternal or infant complications if stable, while the homozygous major form usually causes severe anemia and congestive heart failure, with hepatosplenomegaly, bone deformities, and common infertility
B) The minor form is homozygous and more severe
C) Both forms are managed with routine iron supplementation
D) Neither form affects pregnancy
Answer: A
Rationale: Beta-thalassemia minor is "the heterozygous form" and women "generally do not experience associated maternal or infant complications if their condition is stable." The homozygous major form brings "hepatosplenomegaly and bone deformities caused by massive marrow tissue expansion," early death from infection or cardiovascular complications, common infertility, and in pregnancy "severe anemia and congestive heart failure."
Trap: (C) applies routine iron to a disorder where "iron therapy should be prescribed only for women who are iron deficient," the same caution that makes the sickle cell alert matter.

Topic: epilepsy and antiepileptic medications
Subtopic: choosing and dosing the drug

Q: A woman with epilepsy taking valproic acid asks about pregnancy. What does the Safety Alert state?
A) Valproic acid is the preferred drug in pregnancy
B) Valproic acid use during pregnancy is associated with major congenital malformations and adverse cognitive outcomes including lower IQ and an increased risk for autism and autism spectrum disorder, and it should not be taken by pregnant women
C) Valproic acid is safe if the dose is reduced
D) Valproic acid affects only the first trimester
Answer: B
Rationale: The Safety Alert states that valproic acid use "is associated with major congenital malformations as well as adverse cognitive outcomes including lower IQ and an increased risk for autism and autism spectrum disorder," and the chapter adds: "Valproate should not be taken by pregnant women due to known teratogenic effects."
Trap: (C) treats a teratogen as dose-manageable. The chapter does say anomalies are "often related to the dose of medication taken, rather than to epilepsy itself" — but for valproate the position is not to use it.

Q: Which antiepileptic medications does the chapter identify as the safest and most commonly prescribed for women of reproductive age?
A) Lamotrigine and levetiracetam
B) Valproic acid and phenytoin
C) Carbamazepine and valproate
D) Phenobarbital and primidone
Answer: A
Rationale: Lamotrigine and levetiracetam "are now the most commonly prescribed antiepileptic medications for women of reproductive age. Both appear to have relatively low major malformation rates associated with their use," and the Evidence-Based Practice box states that they "are the safest medications for pregnancy." Carbamazepine is "also a reasonable choice ... although its use is declining."
Trap: (C) pairs a reasonable choice with the contraindicated one, which is how a half-remembered list produces a dangerous answer.

Q: What prescribing principle does the chapter give for anticonvulsants in pregnancy, and what happens to the dose?
A) Only one anticonvulsant, at the lowest dose that keeps her seizure-free; blood levels are checked through pregnancy, the dose often needs to be increased as pregnancy progresses, and it needs to be reduced within the first 3 weeks postpartum if it was raised
B) Combination therapy at low doses is preferred to reduce any single drug's exposure
C) The dose is fixed at the prepregnancy level throughout
D) The drug is stopped in the first trimester and resumed at 14 weeks
Answer: A
Rationale: "If possible, only one anticonvulsant medication — at the lowest dose that is effective at keeping the woman seizure-free — should be prescribed during pregnancy." Because increased plasma volume affects drug metabolism and distribution, levels are checked throughout, "often the dosage will need to be increased as pregnancy progresses," and after birth doses raised during pregnancy "need to be reduced within the first 3 weeks postpartum."
Trap: (D) stops the drug to protect the fetus, which contradicts the chapter's whole position: achieving seizure control before conception is "extremely important," and women with frequent seizures before conception are likely to continue that pattern.

Q: Which folic acid recommendation does the Evidence-Based Practice box give for women of childbearing age who have epilepsy?
A) 0.4 mg (400 mcg) daily to optimize cognitive outcomes in their newborns
B) 4 to 5 mg daily for all women with epilepsy regardless of pregnancy plans
C) No supplementation, since anticonvulsants interfere with folate metabolism
D) 2 to 4 mg daily
Answer: A
Rationale: The Evidence-Based Practice box recommends that "all women of childbearing age who have epilepsy should take 0.4 mg (400 mcg) of folic acid each day to optimize cognitive outcomes in their newborns." The chapter elsewhere gives 0.4 to 1 mg daily for any woman of childbearing age on an anticonvulsant, and notes that some clinicians increase it to 4 to 5 mg when she is trying to conceive or is pregnant.
Trap: (D) is the inflammatory bowel disease dose from later in the same chapter. Three different folic acid numbers appear on the epilepsy page alone, and a fourth and fifth appear elsewhere in the chapter.

Subtopic: after the birth

Q: Which newborn safety measures does the chapter recommend for a mother with epilepsy? Select all that apply.
A) Having a partner assist with night feedings so she can get a prolonged period, typically 6 to 8 hours, of uninterrupted sleep
B) Bathing the baby only when another adult is present
C) Changing diapers on a pad placed on the floor rather than on a changing table
D) Using a stroller to transport the baby rather than an infant carrier strapped to the mother
E) Avoiding breastfeeding because anticonvulsants are unsafe in breast milk
Answer: A, B, C, D
Rationale: The chapter lists all four measures, including avoiding stairs whenever possible, and notes that "the sleep deprivation that can be associated with breastfeeding a newborn may put the woman at risk for seizures."
Trap: (E) contradicts the chapter's statement that "all the major anticonvulsant medications are found in breast milk in varying amounts, but few data suggest neonatal harm from exposure through breast milk," and the EVP box's note that antiepileptic drugs "tend to be safe for breastfeeding." The real risk is the sleep loss, and the measures address that.

Topic: other neurologic and autoimmune disorders
Subtopic: multiple sclerosis and bell palsy

Q: A woman with multiple sclerosis asks what to expect during and after pregnancy. What does the chapter say?
A) Remissions during pregnancy are common, and if an exacerbation occurs it is more likely during the postpartum period; pregnancy likely does not have an adverse long-term effect on the disease
B) Exacerbations are common during pregnancy and rare afterward
C) Pregnancy accelerates the long-term course of the disease
D) Cesarean birth is indicated for all women with MS
Answer: A
Rationale: "Remissions during pregnancy are common. If an exacerbation occurs, it is more likely to occur during the postpartum period," and pregnancy "likely does not have an adverse long-term effect on the course of the disease." No specific changes in routine obstetric care are recommended, epidural anesthesia does not worsen MS, and "cesarean birth is usually not indicated for women with MS."
Trap: (B) reverses the timing, which is the one thing a woman planning her support needs to get right. Women with bladder dysfunction are also screened routinely for UTIs.

Q: A woman at 30 weeks develops sudden unilateral facial weakness with maximal weakness within 48 hours, pain around the ear, and difficulty closing one eye. What is this, and what treatment applies?
A) Bell palsy; steroid therapy is the only medical treatment shown to influence the outcome, and to be effective it must begin within the first 3 to 5 days after the paralysis develops
B) A stroke requiring immediate thrombolysis
C) Preeclampsia with cerebral involvement
D) Multiple sclerosis relapse treated with disease-modifying therapy
Answer: A
Rationale: "Bell palsy is an acute idiopathic facial paralysis," presenting as "the sudden development of a unilateral facial weakness, with maximal weakness within 48 hours after onset," often with pain around the ear, difficulty closing the eye, hyperacusis, and loss of taste. "Most women recover without treatment. Steroid therapy is the only medical treatment that has been shown to influence the outcome," beginning within 3 to 5 days.
Trap: (C) is worth ruling in rather than out: women who develop Bell palsy during pregnancy "have an increased risk for gestational hypertension or preeclampsia," so the finding prompts evaluation even though it is not itself preeclampsia. Recovery is also worse in pregnancy — about half within a year, against 90% otherwise.

Subtopic: lupus and myasthenia gravis

Q: A woman with systemic lupus erythematosus asks when she should try to conceive, and what medications apply. Which response reflects the chapter?
A) Wait until she has been in remission for at least 6 months, since disease activity at the beginning of pregnancy predicts exacerbations; hydroxychloroquine should be continued if she becomes pregnant on it, and low-dose aspirin beginning early in pregnancy is recommended to decrease preeclampsia risk
B) Conceive as soon as possible, since pregnancy induces remission
C) Stop all medications before conceiving
D) Long-term NSAID therapy should be started in pregnancy
Answer: A
Rationale: "Disease activity at the beginning of pregnancy is an important predictor of exacerbations during pregnancy. Therefore women are advised to wait until they have been in remission for at least 6 months before attempting conception." A woman who becomes pregnant on hydroxychloroquine "should continue the medication because it may decrease the risk of disease flares," and "low-dose aspirin therapy beginning early in pregnancy is recommended ... because this medication may decrease their risk for developing preeclampsia."
Trap: (D) reverses an NSAID caution: they are used only occasionally and not long term "because of related oligohydramnios or ductus arteriosus closure." Preterm birth is the most common adverse pregnancy outcome in women with SLE.

Q: A woman with myasthenia gravis develops preeclampsia with severe features, and magnesium sulfate is ordered. What should the nurse do?
A) Administer it as ordered, since magnesium is the drug of choice for seizure prophylaxis
B) Question the order, because magnesium sulfate is contraindicated in women with myasthenia gravis — it inhibits the release of acetylcholine and can trigger myasthenic crisis
C) Administer it at half the usual dose
D) Administer it only after a negative acetylcholine receptor antibody test
Answer: B
Rationale: The Safety Alert states: "Magnesium sulfate is contraindicated in women with myasthenia gravis because it inhibits the release of acetylcholine and can trigger myasthenic crisis." Myasthenia gravis is "an autoimmune motor (muscle) end-plate disorder that involves acetylcholine use."
Trap: (A) is correct for nearly every other woman with preeclampsia, which is what makes this pairing worth holding as an absolute. Because myasthenia gravis does not affect smooth muscle, most women tolerate labor well, though vacuum or forceps assistance may be required and regional analgesia is preferred.

Q: A newborn of a mother with myasthenia gravis has a weak cry, difficulty suckling, a weak Moro reflex, and feeble limb movements at 24 hours of age. What is this, and what is the course?
A) Neonatal myasthenia, affecting approximately 10% to 15% of these newborns, from transfer of maternal antibodies against acetylcholine receptors, appearing within the first 72 hours, treatable with anticholinesterase medications and usually resolving by 6 weeks
B) Permanent congenital myasthenia gravis
C) Neonatal abstinence syndrome
D) Birth asphyxia
Answer: A
Rationale: "Approximately 10% to 15% of neonates born to women with MG develop neonatal myasthenia," which "results from the transfer of maternal antibodies against acetylcholine receptors across the placenta." Symptoms "usually appear within the first 72 hours after birth" and the disorder "can be treated with anticholinesterase medications and usually resolves by 6 weeks after birth."
Trap: (B) treats a transient antibody-mediated condition as an inherited one, which would change everything a family is told about their baby's future.

Topic: gastrointestinal and hepatic disorders in pregnancy
Subtopic: gallbladder disease

Q: A woman at 24 weeks has epigastric and right upper quadrant pain radiating to her back after a high-fat meal, with nausea, vomiting, and fever, and the pain is severe and prolonged. Which condition does this describe?
A) Cholelithiasis alone, since gallstones cause pain after fatty meals
B) Cholecystitis, inflammation of the gallbladder usually caused when a gallstone obstructs a cystic duct, in which the pain is more severe and prolonged and nausea, vomiting, and fever may be present
C) Appendicitis
D) Preeclampsia
Answer: B
Rationale: Cholelithiasis is "the presence of gallstones in the gallbladder," with biliary colic as the usual first symptom, occurring spontaneously or after a high-fat meal. Cholecystitis "is usually caused when a gallstone obstructs a cystic duct," and its pain "is usually more severe and prolonged," with nausea, vomiting, and fever.
Trap: (A) stops at the stones. The distinction drives management: cholelithiasis is often managed conservatively with surgery postponed until postpartum, while "women with recurrent biliary colic or acute cholecystitis generally require immediate cholecystectomy."

Q: A cholecystectomy is planned during pregnancy. What does the chapter say about timing and technique?
A) The second trimester has traditionally been considered safest, but the surgery is increasingly performed at any time during pregnancy, and laparoscopic cholecystectomy is the preferred procedure
B) Surgery is never performed during pregnancy
C) Only open cholecystectomy is safe in pregnancy
D) Surgery is performed only in the first trimester
Answer: A
Rationale: "Although the second trimester has traditionally been considered the safest time for this surgery, it is increasingly performed at any time during pregnancy because of improved surgical techniques and outcomes. While both laparoscopic and open cholecystectomy can safely be performed in all trimesters, laparoscopic cholecystectomy is the preferred procedure."
Trap: (C) is the older teaching, and cholecystitis is "the third most common indication for nonobstetric surgical intervention in pregnancy."

Q: Which dietary instructions belong in nutritional counseling for a pregnant woman with cholelithiasis or cholecystitis? Select all that apply.
A) Assess your diet for foods that cause discomfort and gas, and omit foods that trigger episodes
B) Limit dietary fat intake to 40 to 50 g/day
C) Limit protein to 10% to 12% of total calories, with most calories from carbohydrates
D) Prepare food without adding fats or oils as much as possible, and avoid fried foods
E) Increase dietary fat to slow gallbladder emptying
Answer: A, B, C, D
Rationale: The Teaching for Self-Management box lists identifying and omitting trigger foods, limiting fat to 40 to 50 g/day, limiting protein to 10% to 12% of calories with most calories from carbohydrates, preparing food without added fats or oils, and avoiding fried foods.
Trap: (E) inverts the central instruction. Fat is what stimulates gallbladder contraction, which is why the pain classically follows a high-fat meal.

Subtopic: inflammatory bowel disease and cholestasis

Q: A woman with Crohn disease becomes pregnant and asks whether to stop her medications. What does the chapter direct?
A) Stop all medications during the first trimester
B) Continue them, because discontinuing prepregnancy medications can cause a relapse or disease flare, and the drugs used appear to be safe during pregnancy; folic acid 2 to 4 mg daily and calcium supplementation are recommended
C) Switch to over-the-counter remedies
D) Stop only the corticosteroids
Answer: B
Rationale: "Discontinuing her prepregnancy medications can cause the woman to have a relapse or disease flare," and the drugs named — 5-aminosalicylates, prednisone, 6-mercaptopurine or azathioprine, occasionally biologics, and metronidazole or ciprofloxacin — "appear to be safe for use during pregnancy." A daily dose of 2 to 4 mg of folic acid "counteracts the antifolate actions of some medications," and calcium supplementation is necessary "because osteoporosis is a significant complication in up to one-third of these women."
Trap: (A) is the reflex response to any medication in pregnancy, and here it creates the flare that itself raises the risk of preterm birth, low birth weight, and small-for-gestational-age infants.

Q: A woman at 32 weeks reports intense generalized itching, worst at night and involving her palms and soles, with no rash. What should the nurse suspect, and what is the treatment?
A) Intrahepatic cholestasis of pregnancy, with no skin lesions present, elevated serum bile acids and liver function tests, treated with ursodeoxycholic acid
B) Polymorphic eruption of pregnancy, treated with topical steroids
C) A drug allergy
D) Scabies
Answer: A
Rationale: Intrahepatic cholestasis "is characterized by generalized pruritus that usually begins in the third trimester," with "no skin lesions present," itching that "commonly affects the palms and soles" and is "usually worse at night," elevated serum bile acids and liver function tests, and treatment with ursodeoxycholic acid. Birth at approximately 37 weeks should be considered, and it recurs in 40% to 90% of later pregnancies.
Trap: (B) names a condition with a rash, and the absence of skin lesions is the discriminator. Fetal complications include asphyxial events, meconium staining, stillbirth, and preterm birth, which is why the timing of birth matters.

Topic: urinary tract infections in pregnancy
Subtopic: the three rungs

Q: A routine first-visit urine culture in an asymptomatic woman grows 120,000 organisms/mL. How is this classified and managed, and what happens if it is untreated?
A) Asymptomatic bacteriuria; it is treated with antibiotics, because if untreated about one-third of pregnant women will develop acute pyelonephritis
B) Contamination; the specimen is repeated and no treatment is given
C) Cystitis; it is treated with a 3-day course
D) It requires no treatment because she has no symptoms
Answer: A
Rationale: "A clean-voided urine specimen containing more than 100,000 organisms/mL is diagnostic" of asymptomatic bacteriuria, and "all women should be screened for asymptomatic bacteriuria at their first prenatal visit." Treatment regimens may be single-dose or 3, 7, or 10 days. "If asymptomatic bacteriuria is not treated, about one-third of pregnant women will develop acute pyelonephritis," and it is also associated with preterm birth and low birth weight.
Trap: (D) is the reasoning the screening program exists to override — the absence of symptoms is what defines the diagnosis, not what excludes it.

Q: A pregnant woman reports dysuria, urgency, frequency, and suprapubic pain, with microscopic hematuria and no fever or flank pain. Which condition is this, and how is it treated?
A) Cystitis, in which symptoms are confined to the bladder rather than becoming systemic, often treated with a 3-day course of antibiotics that is usually 90% effective
B) Pyelonephritis, treated with IV antibiotics
C) Asymptomatic bacteriuria
D) Vaginitis
Answer: A
Rationale: "Cystitis (bladder infection) is characterized by dysuria, urgency, and frequency, along with lower abdominal or suprapubic pain," with white blood cells and bacteria in the urine and possible microscopic or gross hematuria, and "symptoms are confined to the bladder rather than becoming systemic." It is "often treated with a 3-day course of antibiotic therapy, which is usually 90% effective."
Trap: (B) is the next rung up and is distinguished by systemic findings — fever, chills, flank pain, and costovertebral angle tenderness — none of which this stem gives.

Q: A woman at 28 weeks has an abrupt onset of fever, chills, right flank pain, nausea and vomiting, and right costovertebral angle tenderness. Which statements about her condition are accurate? Select all that apply.
A) Pyelonephritis is the most frequent serious nonobstetric medical complication of pregnancy and a leading cause of septic shock during pregnancy
B) Infection develops only in the right kidney in more than half of all cases
C) IV antibiotics are begun as soon as cultures are collected, with ceftriaxone often prescribed
D) IV fluids are given to maintain urine output at 50 mL or more per hour
E) She may be discharged as soon as her fever begins to fall, without completing antibiotics
Answer: A, B, C, D
Rationale: Pyelonephritis presents abruptly with fever, chills, flank pain, dysuria, urgency, and frequency, with one or both costovertebral angles tender; it develops only in the right kidney in more than half of cases; IV antibiotics begin once cultures are collected; and urine output is maintained at 50 mL/h or more. Women are hospitalized 12 to 24 hours for assessment before a decision about outpatient management.
Trap: (E) contradicts the discharge criterion — "once a woman has been afebrile and asymptomatic for 24 hours" — and the 14-day outpatient course. More than 20% of women remain febrile for more than 4 days, and recurrent bacteriuria develops in 30% to 40% after treatment.

Subtopic: teaching about treatment

Q: A woman prescribed phenazopyridine along with her antibiotic should be taught which of the following?
A) That the medication colors urine and tears orange, that she should avoid wearing contact lenses while taking it, and that it will stain underwear
B) That it replaces the antibiotic once symptoms improve
C) That it must be taken on an empty stomach
D) That orange urine indicates an allergic reaction
Answer: A
Rationale: Phenazopyridine is "a urinary analgesic" given with the antibiotic for symptom relief, and the teaching is that "the medication colors urine and tears orange," so women should "avoid wearing contact lenses while taking this medication" and be "warned that it will stain underwear."
Trap: (D) turns an expected effect into an emergency, and (B) is the more dangerous error: relief of symptoms is exactly when women stop antibiotics, and "failure to complete treatment can lead to the creation of additional drug-resistant organisms."

Topic: surgery during pregnancy and appendicitis
Subtopic: why the diagnosis is harder

Q: Which statements about diagnosing appendicitis in pregnancy are accurate? Select all that apply.
A) The most common symptom is right lower quadrant abdominal pain, regardless of gestational age
B) As pregnancy progresses the appendix is pushed upward and to the right
C) The white blood cell count is not helpful because of the physiologic increase in WBCs in pregnancy
D) Loss of appetite is not a reliable indicator
E) An elevated white blood cell count confirms the diagnosis
Answer: A, B, C, D
Rationale: "The most common symptom of appendicitis in pregnant women is right lower quadrant abdominal pain, regardless of gestational age." The appendix is displaced upward and to the right, loss of appetite "is not a reliable indicator," and "because of the physiologic increase in WBCs that occurs in pregnancy, this test is not helpful in making the diagnosis." Fever, tachycardia, dry tongue, and localized tenderness are less likely indicators than in nonpregnant patients.
Trap: (E) uses a real laboratory value to say nothing, and it is the option that most feels like clinical confirmation. Rupture occurs in up to 40% of pregnant women with appendicitis, largely because the diagnosis is delayed.

Q: Which imaging sequence does the chapter recommend for suspected appendicitis in pregnancy?
A) CT first, as in nonpregnant clients
B) Ultrasonography first to avoid fetal radiation exposure from CT, with MRI as the appropriate next step if appendicitis has not been confirmed
C) MRI first, then CT
D) Plain abdominal radiography
Answer: B
Rationale: CT "is the imaging test of choice in nonpregnant clients," but "the use of ultrasonography during pregnancy is preferred to avoid fetal exposure to radiation from CT," and "magnetic resonance imaging (MRI) is the appropriate next step in a pregnant woman if appendicitis has not been confirmed by other imaging techniques." A urinalysis and chest x-ray are done to rule out UTI and right lower lobe pneumonia.
Trap: (A) applies the nonpregnant standard, which is exactly the reasoning the chapter corrects.

Subtopic: perioperative care

Q: A pregnant woman requires urgent nonobstetric surgery. Which perioperative measures does the chapter specify? Select all that apply.
A) Encouraging her to express her fears, concerns, and questions
B) A complete blood count with differential and urinalysis at minimum, with FHR and uterine activity monitored
C) Special precautions such as administering an antacid before anesthesia, because an unexpected surgical patient is in danger of vomiting and aspirating even if she has had nothing by mouth
D) Positioning on the operating table with a lateral tilt to avoid compression of the maternal vena cava
E) Withholding all fetal monitoring until 24 hours after surgery
Answer: A, B, C, D
Rationale: The chapter names expressing fears as an important aspect of preoperative care, the minimum laboratory set with fetal and uterine monitoring, the aspiration precaution for unexpected surgery, and the lateral tilt intraoperatively to improve fetal oxygenation. Continuous monitoring during surgery may be performed if the fetus is viable, using sterile gel and a sterile transducer sleeve, though many practitioners monitor before and after only.
Trap: (E) contradicts postoperative practice, where monitoring is "likely to be initiated or resumed because of the potential risk for preterm labor," with tocolysis if preterm labor occurs.

Q: Which items belong in discharge teaching after nonobstetric surgery during pregnancy, per Box 30.2? Select all that apply.
A) Care of the incision site, and diet and elimination related to gastrointestinal function
B) Signs and symptoms of wound infection, venous thromboembolism, and pneumonia
C) Equipment needed and technique for assessing temperature
D) Assessment of fetal activity with kick counts, and signs of preterm labor, if birth has not occurred
E) Instructions to resume full activity immediately
Answer: A, B, C, D
Rationale: Box 30.2 lists incision care, diet and elimination, complication signs, temperature assessment, a recommended schedule for resuming activities of daily living, treatments and medications, resource people and telephone numbers, follow-up visits, and — if birth has not occurred — kick counts and signs of preterm labor.
Trap: (E) replaces a graded schedule with an unrestricted one. Home care planning "should be completed as early as possible before discharge," with supervised practice for the woman and caregivers.

Topic: resuscitation and cpr in pregnancy
Subtopic: the priority rule

Q: A pregnant woman at 30 weeks arrives after a motor vehicle crash, unstable and bleeding. A team member moves to apply the fetal monitor first. What does the chapter direct?
A) Fetal assessment first, since the fetus is more vulnerable
B) Resuscitate the woman and stabilize her condition first, then consider fetal needs, because fetal survival depends on maternal survival
C) Simultaneous equal priority for mother and fetus
D) Immediate cesarean birth before any resuscitation
Answer: B
Rationale: The Safety Alert states: "Priorities of care for the pregnant woman after trauma must be to resuscitate the woman and stabilize her condition first and then consider fetal needs." The chapter notes that "the initial response of many trauma team members ... is to assess fetal status first," and corrects it: "fetal survival depends on maternal survival, and stabilizing the pregnant woman improves the chance of fetal survival." Pregnancy "should not result in any restriction of the usual diagnostic, pharmacologic, or resuscitative procedures."
Trap: (A) is the instinct the chapter names and then overrules, and (D) skips resuscitation entirely for a procedure that depends on it.

Q: How is the airway established in a pregnant trauma patient, and what else does the primary survey include?
A) Jaw thrust, because hyperextension of the neck is avoided and cervical spine injury is always assumed; the sequence is the CABDs — compressions, airway, breathing, and defibrillation
B) Head tilt-chin lift with full neck extension
C) Immediate intubation without cervical precautions
D) Airway management is deferred until after fetal assessment
Answer: A
Rationale: The Safety Alert directs: "Hyperextension of the neck is avoided; instead, jaw thrust is used to establish an airway for the pregnant trauma survivor." The chapter's mnemonic for the primary survey is "the initial CABDs of resuscitation: compressions, airway, breathing, and defibrillation," and "the presence of a cervical spine injury is always assumed."
Trap: (B) is the correct maneuver in the cardiac-arrest algorithm — the CPR box does direct head tilt-chin lift — but not in a trauma patient with a presumed cervical spine injury.

Q: Which measures does the chapter specify for stabilizing a pregnant trauma patient? Select all that apply.
A) Supplemental oxygen by tight-fitting nonrebreathing mask at 10 to 12 L/min
B) Two large-bore, 14- to 16-gauge intravenous lines
C) Normal saline for quick volume expansion, with low-volume resuscitation now recommended to avoid fluid overload
D) Positioning the uterus to one side after 20 weeks, or manual deflection or a wedge under one side of the backboard
E) Vasopressors as the first-line treatment for hypotension
Answer: A, B, C, D
Rationale: The chapter specifies oxygen at 10 to 12 L/min by tight-fitting nonrebreathing mask, two large-bore IV lines, normal saline with low-volume resuscitation because large crystalloid volumes cause tissue edema, ARDS, dilutional coagulopathy, and regulatory disruption, and lateral uterine positioning after 20 weeks. Blood products are given in a 1:1:1 ratio of platelets, plasma, and packed red cells.
Trap: (E) reverses two rules at once: "hypovolemia should be presumed to be the cause of hypotension until proven otherwise," and "administering vasopressor drugs to treat maternal hypotension should be avoided if possible" because they reduce uterine blood flow.

Subtopic: performing cpr

Q: Which modifications to cardiopulmonary resuscitation are specific to pregnancy? Select all that apply.
A) Left lateral uterine displacement if the fundal height is at or above the umbilicus, indicating approximately 20 weeks of gestation
B) Chest compressions performed slightly higher on the sternum if the enlarged uterus has displaced the diaphragm upward
C) Defibrillator paddles placed one rib interspace higher than usual
D) A compression rate reduced to 80/min because of the pregnancy
E) A compression-to-ventilation ratio changed to 15:2
Answer: A, B, C
Rationale: The three pregnancy-specific modifications are lateral uterine displacement at or above 20 weeks, compressions slightly higher on the sternum, and paddles one interspace higher because "the heart is displaced slightly by the enlarged uterus." Uterine displacement is critical because cardiac output from compressions, already only about 30% of normal, may be even less with aortocaval compression.
Trap: (D) and (E) alter parameters that do not change: the rate stays 100 to 120/min, the depth at least 5 cm and no more than 6 cm, the ratio 30 compressions to 2 breaths, and 1 breath every 6 seconds.

Q: According to the Emergency Treatment box, how should a pregnant woman be positioned for chest compressions, and how often is the compressor role rotated?
A) On a flat, firm surface with the uterus displaced laterally by a wedge such as a rolled towel under her hip, or manually, or in a left lateral position; the compressor role is rotated every 2 minutes if possible to prevent fatigue
B) Sitting upright, with no rotation of the compressor
C) In high Fowler position, rotating every 10 minutes
D) Prone, rotating every 5 cycles
Answer: A
Rationale: The box directs positioning "on a flat, firm surface with her uterus displaced laterally with a wedge (e.g., a rolled towel placed under her hip) or manually or place her in a left lateral position," compressions at 100 to 120/min with full chest recoil, a pulse check after 5 cycles of 30 compressions and 2 breaths or about 2 minutes, and rotating the compressor role every 2 minutes.
Trap: (D) borrows the 5-cycle interval, which is when the pulse is checked, and attaches it to compressor rotation — two different timings that happen to be about the same length.

Q: A woman at 32 weeks is choking and cannot speak or cough. What maneuver should be used?
A) Abdominal thrusts, as for any adult
B) Chest thrusts: standing behind her with arms under her armpits encircling her chest, pressing backward with quick thrusts until the foreign body is expelled
C) Back blows only
D) Immediate cricothyrotomy
Answer: B
Rationale: "Clearing an airway obstruction is usually accomplished by performing abdominal thrusts. However, during the second and third trimesters of pregnancy, chest thrusts rather than abdominal thrusts should be used." The Emergency Treatment box describes standing behind her, arms under the armpits encircling the chest, with the thumb side of the clenched fist against the middle of the sternum and the other hand over the fist.
Trap: (A) is correct for a nonpregnant adult or a woman in the first trimester, and it is the default that has to be overridden once the uterus occupies the abdomen. If she becomes unconscious, she is supported carefully to the ground, emergency services activated, and CPR begun.
