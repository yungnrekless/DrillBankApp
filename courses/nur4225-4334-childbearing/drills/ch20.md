# Chapter 20 drills — Postpartum Anatomic and Physiologic Changes

Authored from `sources/Ch20_Fact_Bank.md` (Lowdermilk, Cashion, Alden, Olshansky & Perry, 13th ed., ch. 20, pp. 417–424).

Blueprint weight is **~6 questions of 200 (3.0%), depth `standard`** — topics
*Postpartum Physiologic Changes, lochia, fluid and blood loss, vital signs,
breastfeeding*. The Week 1–4 midterm reading guide does not name this chapter,
which means the midterm does not reach it, not that it is unimportant: the
blueprint weights it at six, so the final does. Scope here comes from the
blueprint alone, and it earns **42 drills across 9 topics**.

The organizing insight the fact bank names is the spine of this set: **this
chapter is the physiology behind Chapter 21's assessment, and nearly every
"normal" it establishes looks abnormal by ordinary adult standards.** A pulse of
45, a temperature of 38°C, a white count of 30,000, 3000 mL of urine a day,
drenching night sweats, and 500 mL of blood loss are all expected. Knowing which
alarming numbers are normal here — and the exact point at which each stops being
normal — is most of what this chapter is for.

Every distinction the chapter states in both directions is drilled from both
ends: the fundus rising and then descending, afterpains in primiparas and
multiparas, lochial and nonlochial bleeding, normal fever before and after 24
hours, bradycardia as expected and tachycardia as the warning, engorgement in
breastfeeding and nonbreastfeeding women, and prolactin versus oxytocin.

**What is left out.** The vagina, perineum, and pelvic muscular support section,
and the gastrointestinal, integumentary, musculoskeletal, neurologic, and immune
systems — none of which the blueprint names. The one item there with obvious exam
potential is the **postpartum headache** discussion, whose differential includes
preeclampsia and post-dural puncture headache; both are drilled in Chapters 27
and 17.

Topic: uterine involution timeline
Subtopic: where the fundus is, hour by day

Q: Six hours after a vaginal birth, the nurse palpates the fundus one fingerbreadth above the umbilicus. How should this be interpreted?
A) As abnormal, requiring immediate provider notification
B) As expected, since within 12 hours the fundus can rise to approximately 1 cm above the umbilicus before beginning to descend
C) As a sign of a full bladder only
D) As evidence of retained placental fragments
Answer: B
Rationale: The fundus is "in the midline, approximately 2 cm below the level of the umbilicus" at the end of the third stage, and "within 12 hours the fundus can rise to approximately 1 cm above the umbilicus." After that, "the fundus descends 1 to 2 cm every 24 hours."
Trap: (A) is the reflex response to a fundus above the umbilicus, and the timing is what settles it. The same finding on postpartum day 3 would be abnormal, because by then the fundus should be several centimeters lower.

Q: Where should the fundus be on the sixth postpartum day, and when should it no longer be palpable abdominally?
A) Halfway between the umbilicus and the symphysis pubis on day 6; not palpable abdominally after 2 weeks
B) At the umbilicus on day 6; not palpable after 6 weeks
C) At the symphysis pubis on day 6; not palpable after 1 week
D) One centimeter above the umbilicus on day 6; not palpable after 4 weeks
Answer: A
Rationale: "By the sixth postpartum day the fundus is normally located halfway between the umbilicus and the symphysis pubis," and "after 2 weeks the uterus should not be palpable abdominally." By 6 weeks it "should have returned to its nonpregnant location."
Trap: (B) holds the fundus at its day-1 position for a week, which would mean no involution at all — the finding that defines subinvolution.

Subtopic: how involution happens, and when it fails

Q: A woman asks how her uterus shrinks back to its former size. Which explanation reflects the chapter?
A) The muscle cells added during pregnancy are all destroyed, returning the uterus to its exact prepregnancy size
B) Falling hormone levels cause autolysis — the self-destruction of excess hypertrophied tissue — while the additional cells laid down during pregnancy remain, which accounts for the slight increase in uterine size after each pregnancy
C) The uterus shrinks by expelling endometrial tissue only
D) Involution is complete within 1 week
Answer: B
Rationale: Pregnancy growth came from "both hyperplasia (an increase in the number of muscle cells) and hypertrophy (an enlargement of the existing cells)," and after birth "the decrease in these hormones causes autolysis," the self-destruction of excess hypertrophied tissue. "The additional cells laid down during pregnancy remain and account for the slight increase in uterine size after each pregnancy." The uterus weighs about 500 g at 1 week, 300 g at 2 weeks, and about 100 g at 4 weeks.
Trap: (A) misses the parity detail that explains why a multipara's uterus never quite returns to a nullipara's size.

Q: A woman on postpartum day 10 has a uterus that remains enlarged and boggy with continued rubra bleeding. What is this called, and what are the most common causes?
A) Subinvolution, most commonly caused by retained placental fragments and infection
B) Normal involution
C) Endometriosis
D) Uterine atony, which occurs only in the first hour
Answer: A
Rationale: "Subinvolution is the failure of the uterus to return to a nonpregnant state as a result of ineffective uterine contractions," and "the most common causes of subinvolution are retained placental fragments and infection." Persistent lochia rubra "suggests continued bleeding as a result of retained fragments of the placenta or membranes."
Trap: (D) confines atony to the immediate postpartum period, when the problem here is a failure of involution over days — which is why the two most common causes are things left behind or an infection, not a single relaxed moment.

Q: How is postpartum hemostasis primarily achieved?
A) By platelet aggregation and clot formation at the placental site
B) By compression of intramyometrial blood vessels as the uterine muscle contracts, rather than by platelet aggregation and clot formation
C) By vasoconstriction from circulating catecholamines
D) By fibrin deposition in the endometrium
Answer: B
Rationale: "Postpartum hemostasis is achieved primarily by compression of intramyometrial blood vessels as the uterine muscle contracts rather than by platelet aggregation and clot formation." Oxytocin "strengthens and coordinates these uterine contractions, compressing blood vessels and promoting hemostasis."
Trap: (A) is the ordinary mechanism of hemostasis everywhere else in the body, and it is why a boggy uterus bleeds despite a normal platelet count — and why the intervention is massage and oxytocin rather than clotting factors.

Topic: afterpains and uterine contractions
Subtopic: who has them and when

Q: A multiparous woman reports strong uterine cramping that intensifies when she breastfeeds. How should the nurse respond?
A) Explain that afterpains are more common in subsequent pregnancies and that breastfeeding intensifies them because it stimulates uterine contractions, and that they typically resolve in 3 to 7 days
B) Notify the provider, since cramping during breastfeeding is abnormal
C) Advise her to stop breastfeeding until the cramping resolves
D) Explain that afterpains are strongest in first-time mothers
Answer: A
Rationale: "In primiparous women, the uterine tone is good, the fundus generally remains firm, and the woman usually perceives only mild uterine cramping. Periodic relaxation and vigorous contractions are more common in subsequent pregnancies" and cause afterpains, which "typically resolve in 3 to 7 days." Both breastfeeding and oxytocic medication "usually intensify afterpains because both stimulate uterine contractions."
Trap: (D) reverses the parity relationship, and (C) would remove the intervention that most reduces bleeding — breastfeeding "increases the release of oxytocin, which promotes uterine contractions, therefore decreasing blood loss and reducing the risk for postpartum hemorrhage."

Q: After which births are afterpains most noticeable?
A) Births in which the uterus was overdistended, such as with a large infant, multifetal gestation, or polyhydramnios
B) Precipitous births only
C) Cesarean births only
D) First births
Answer: A
Rationale: Afterpains are "more noticeable after births in which the uterus was overdistended (e.g., large infant, multifetal gestation, polyhydramnios)."
Trap: (D) is the mirror error of the previous item. Overdistention and parity both work in the same direction, and both run opposite to the intuition that a first birth hurts most afterward.

Subtopic: why oxytocin is routine

Q: Why is exogenous oxytocin usually administered immediately after expulsion of the placenta?
A) Because during the first 1 to 2 postpartum hours uterine contractions can decrease in intensity and become uncoordinated, and it is vital that the uterus remain firm and well contracted
B) Because oxytocin prevents infection
C) Because oxytocin promotes milk production
D) Because oxytocin relieves afterpains
Answer: A
Rationale: "During the first 1 to 2 postpartum hours, uterine contractions can decrease in intensity and become uncoordinated. Because it is vital that the uterus remain firm and well contracted, exogenous oxytocin (Pitocin) is usually administered intravenously or intramuscularly immediately after expulsion of the placenta." With active third-stage management it may be given after the birth but before the placenta is expelled, and "the uterus is very sensitive to oxytocin during the first week or so after birth."
Trap: (C) assigns oxytocin the job that belongs to prolactin, and (D) reverses its effect on afterpains, which it intensifies rather than relieves.

Q: What does the chapter say about healing at the placental site?
A) Upward growth of the endometrium causes sloughing of necrotic tissue and prevents the scar formation characteristic of normal wound healing, which enables the endometrium to resume its usual cycle and allow implantation in future pregnancies
B) The site heals by scar formation like any other wound
C) The site never fully heals
D) Healing is complete within 3 days
Answer: A
Rationale: "Upward growth of the endometrium causes sloughing of necrotic tissue and prevents the scar formation characteristic of normal wound healing. This unique healing process enables the endometrium to resume its usual cycle of changes and to allow implantation and placentation in future pregnancies." Endometrial regeneration begins within 3 days and is complete by the third week, with regeneration at the placental site complete by the sixth week.
Trap: (B) applies ordinary wound healing to the one site in the body that deliberately avoids it — and a scarred placental site would prevent future implantation there.

Topic: lochia characteristics
Subtopic: the three stages

Q: A woman on postpartum day 2 has bright red vaginal discharge containing small particles of debris. How is this documented, and what does it contain?
A) Lochia rubra, present days 1 to 3, containing blood from the placental site, trophoblastic tissue debris, vernix, lanugo, and meconium
B) Lochia serosa, present days 4 to 10
C) Lochia alba, containing white blood cells only
D) Nonlochial bleeding from a laceration
Answer: A
Rationale: Table 20.1 gives lochia rubra as bright red, 1 to 3 days after birth, containing "blood from placental site; trophoblastic tissue debris, vernix, lanugo, meconium." It is the only stage whose contents include fetal debris.
Trap: (B) has the right idea and the wrong window: serosa is pinkish-brown from days 4 to 10, and alba is whitish-yellow from days 10 to 14 and can last 3 to 6 weeks.

Q: A woman is 12 days postpartum and reports whitish-yellow discharge. She asks how much longer it will last. What should the nurse say?
A) That this is lochia alba, which typically appears at 10 to 14 days and can last 3 to 6 weeks, and that most women experience lochia for 4 to 6 weeks after birth
B) That discharge at this point indicates infection
C) That lochia should have stopped by day 10
D) That this is lochia serosa and will become rubra again
Answer: A
Rationale: Table 20.1 gives lochia alba as whitish-yellow at 10 to 14 days, lasting 3 to 6 weeks, and the chapter states that "most women experience lochia for 4 to 6 weeks after birth."
Trap: (D) reverses the progression. Lochia moves rubra to serosa to alba and does not go back — a return to rubra is what suggests retained fragments.

Subtopic: what is normal variation and what is not

Q: A woman who stood up from bed reports a sudden gush of dark blood. Her fundus is firm and the flow quickly lessens to a trickle. How should the nurse interpret this?
A) As hemorrhage requiring immediate intervention
B) As lochia that pooled in the vagina while she was lying down, which should not be confused with hemorrhage
C) As a cervical laceration
D) As retained placental fragments
Answer: B
Rationale: "Lochia tends to pool in the vagina when the woman is lying in bed; the woman then can experience a gush of blood when she stands. This gush should not be confused with hemorrhage." Box 20.1 adds that a gush appearing with fundal massage, if dark, "has been pooled in the relaxed vagina, and the amount soon lessens to a trickle of bright red lochia."
Trap: (C) is what the same volume of blood would suggest if it spurted while the uterus was firmly contracted — the direction and pattern of the flow, not the volume, is the discriminator.

Q: A woman 10 days postpartum reports a sudden increase in bleeding. What does the chapter say, and what is the time limit for acting?
A) It is not uncommon to have a sudden brief increase in bleeding 7 to 14 days after birth when sloughing of eschar over the placental site occurs; if it does not subside within 1 to 2 hours, she needs evaluation for possible retained placental fragments
B) Any increase in bleeding after day 7 is abnormal and requires immediate admission
C) Increased bleeding at this point always indicates endometritis
D) She should be told to expect this for the next 2 weeks
Answer: A
Rationale: "It is not uncommon for women to experience a sudden, but brief, increase in bleeding 7 to 14 days after birth when sloughing of eschar over the placental site occurs. If this increase in bleeding does not subside within 1 to 2 hours, the woman needs to be evaluated for possible retained placental fragments."
Trap: (D) drops the time limit, which is the part that makes this teachable — the event is normal, and its persistence past 1 to 2 hours is not.

Q: Which lochia findings indicate a problem? Select all that apply.
A) Persistence of lochia rubra, which suggests continued bleeding from retained fragments of placenta or membranes
B) Continued lochia serosa or alba at 3 to 4 weeks, particularly with fever, pain, or abdominal tenderness, which can indicate endometritis
C) An offensive odor, which usually indicates infection
D) Bleeding that spurts from the vagina while the uterus is firmly contracted, which suggests a cervical or vaginal tear
E) Scant lochia in a woman receiving an oxytocic medication
Answer: A, B, C, D
Rationale: The chapter names persistent rubra, continued serosa or alba at 3 to 4 weeks with symptoms, and an offensive odor — normal lochia "should smell like normal menstrual flow." Box 20.1 identifies spurting blood with a firm uterus as a tear.
Trap: (E) has a normal explanation the chapter states directly: "if the woman receives an oxytocic medication, regardless of the route of administration, the flow of lochia is often scant until the effects of the medication wear off." Lochia is also usually less after cesarean birth, because the uterus was suctioned or wiped before closure, and flow "usually increases with ambulation and breastfeeding."

Topic: postpartum vital signs
Subtopic: temperature

Q: A woman's temperature is 37.9°C (100.2 °F) 10 hours after birth. How should the nurse interpret this?
A) As within normal limits, since during the first 24 hours the temperature can increase to 38°C as a result of the dehydrating effects of labor
B) As puerperal sepsis
C) As requiring blood cultures immediately
D) As mastitis
Answer: A
Rationale: Table 20.2 gives the normal finding: "during first 24 h, temperature can increase to 38°C (100.4 °F) as a result of dehydrating effects of labor. After 24 h, the woman should be afebrile."
Trap: (B) applies the postpartum sepsis criterion to a reading inside the normal window. The same number after 24 hours, recurring or persisting for 2 days, is what suggests puerperal sepsis — along with mastitis, endometritis, urinary tract infection, or other systemic infection.

Q: A woman's temperature is 38.2°C (100.8 °F) on postpartum day 2 and again on day 3. What does this suggest?
A) Normal postpartum thermoregulation
B) Infection: a temperature of 38°C or higher after the first 24 hours that recurs or persists for 2 days suggests puerperal sepsis, with mastitis, endometritis, urinary tract infection, or other systemic infection as possible causes
C) Engorgement alone
D) Dehydration from labor
Answer: B
Rationale: "A diagnosis of puerperal sepsis is suggested if an increase in maternal temperature to 38°C (100.4 °F) or higher is noted after the first 24 h after birth and recurs or persists for 2 days." The chapter adds that a temperature of 38°C or higher during the first 10 days postpartum can indicate infection.
Trap: (C) names a real cause of mild temperature elevation — engorged breasts "are hard and uncomfortable, and mild temperature elevation can occur" — but a recurrent fever over 2 days meets the sepsis criterion and is evaluated.

Subtopic: pulse, blood pressure, and shivering

Q: A woman's pulse is 48 beats/min on postpartum day 3. How should the nurse interpret this?
A) As bradycardia requiring cardiac evaluation
B) As puerperal bradycardia, which is common for 6 to 10 days after birth with heart rates of 40 to 50 beats/min
C) As a sign of hypovolemia
D) As an effect of the epidural
Answer: B
Rationale: "Puerperal bradycardia is common for 6 to 10 days after birth, with the heart rate decreasing to 40 to 50 beats/min." The finding that matters is the opposite: "a rapid pulse rate or one that is increasing can indicate hypovolemia due to hemorrhage."
Trap: (C) inverts the postpartum pattern. In this period tachycardia is the warning and bradycardia is the expectation, which reverses ordinary adult assessment.

Q: A woman becomes dizzy and faint when she stands for the first time after birth. Her blood pressure is unchanged. What explains this?
A) Orthostatic hypotension from the splanchnic engorgement that can occur after birth, which can develop in the first 48 hours
B) Hemorrhage
C) Preeclampsia
D) Epidural-induced hypotension persisting for days
Answer: A
Rationale: Table 20.2 describes "orthostatic hypotension, as indicated by feelings of faintness or dizziness immediately after standing up," which "can develop in the first 48 h as a result of the splanchnic engorgement that can occur after birth." Blood pressure normally shows "a transient increase of approximately 5% during the first few days."
Trap: (B) is the diagnosis to rule out rather than the explanation here, and the table warns that falling blood pressure is "a late sign" of hemorrhage, with other symptoms usually present first.

Q: A woman's blood pressure is 148/94 mm Hg on two readings 4 hours apart on postpartum day 2. How should this be interpreted?
A) As a normal transient postpartum increase
B) As possible gestational hypertension or preeclampsia, which should be evaluated
C) As an expected effect of breastfeeding
D) As orthostatic hypotension
Answer: B
Rationale: Table 20.2 states that "elevated blood pressure (>140/90 mm Hg on two occasions 4 h apart) can be a sign of gestational hypertension or preeclampsia and should be evaluated." An increased reading can also result from excessive use of vasopressor or oxytocic medications.
Trap: (A) stretches the normal 5% transient increase past the threshold the same table sets. Preeclampsia can appear for the first time after birth, which is why the postpartum blood pressure is not a formality.

Q: About half of women experience shivering episodes shortly after birth. What does the chapter say about this?
A) The exact cause is unknown and usually no treatment is needed, although pharmacologic treatment may be needed if the shivering is related to the effects of anesthesia
B) It always indicates infection
C) It indicates hemorrhage
D) It requires immediate warming measures and a provider notification
Answer: A
Rationale: "As many as 50% of women experience shivering episodes during the first few minutes up to the first hour after birth. The exact cause is unknown, and usually no treatment is needed"; if it is related to anesthesia, pharmacologic treatment may be needed.
Trap: (B) treats a common, self-limited event as a rigor. Fever criteria in this period are specific, and shivering alone does not meet them.

Topic: postpartum blood and fluid changes
Subtopic: expected blood loss

Q: How much blood loss is expected with a vaginal birth and with a cesarean birth?
A) 300 to 500 mL for a vaginal birth, about 10% of blood volume; 500 to 1000 mL for a cesarean birth, 15% to 30% of blood volume
B) 100 to 300 mL for a vaginal birth; 300 to 500 mL for a cesarean birth
C) 500 to 1000 mL for a vaginal birth; 1000 to 1500 mL for a cesarean birth
D) The same for both routes
Answer: A
Rationale: "The average blood loss for a vaginal birth of a single fetus ranges from 300 to 500 mL (10% of blood volume)," and "the typical blood loss for women who give birth by cesarean is 500 to 1000 mL (15% to 30% of blood volume)."
Trap: (D) collapses a distinction that changes the interpretation of the same number: a 700-mL loss is expected after a cesarean and excessive after a vaginal birth.

Q: Why can most women tolerate the blood loss of birth, and what compensations does the chapter name? Select all that apply.
A) Pregnancy-induced hypervolemia, an increase in blood volume of 40% to 45% above prepregnancy levels
B) Elimination of uteroplacental circulation, which reduces the size of the maternal vascular bed by 10% to 15%
C) Loss of placental endocrine function, which removes the stimulus for vasodilation
D) Mobilization of extravascular water stored during pregnancy
E) A rapid rise in erythrocyte production within the first 24 hours
Answer: A, B, C, D
Rationale: "Pregnancy-induced hypervolemia ... allows most women to tolerate considerable blood loss during birth," and the chapter lists three compensations: reduction of the vascular bed by 10% to 15%, loss of the vasodilating stimulus, and mobilization of stored extravascular water. "By the third postpartum day, the plasma volume has been replenished as extravascular fluid returns to the intravascular space."
Trap: (E) invents a marrow response on a timescale the chapter does not support: hemoglobin and hematocrit "decrease slightly during the first 24 hours, plateau by 3 to 4 days, and then begin to increase, reaching nonpregnant levels by 4 to 8 weeks."

Subtopic: cardiac output and blood components

Q: What happens to cardiac output immediately after birth?
A) It falls by half because of blood loss
B) It increases by 60% to 80% over prelabor values and returns to prelabor values within 1 hour
C) It remains unchanged for 24 hours
D) It increases gradually over 2 weeks
Answer: B
Rationale: "Typically, cardiac output is increased immediately after birth by 60% to 80% over prelabor values; it returns to prelabor values within 1 hour," because of "the compensatory influx of nearly 500 mL of blood into the maternal system from the uteroplacental bed, a rapid decrease in uterine blood flow, and mobilization of extracellular fluid." By 2 weeks it decreases by 30%, reaching prepregnant levels by 6 to 8 weeks.
Trap: (A) reasons from the blood loss alone and misses the autotransfusion — this is also the physiology behind the warning that the immediate postbirth period is the most dangerous window for a woman with cardiac disease.

Q: A postpartum woman's white blood cell count is 26,000/mm3. How should the nurse interpret this, and what consequence does the chapter draw?
A) As diagnostic of infection
B) As within the expected postpartum range, since the count can rise to 30,000/mm3; leukocytosis coupled with the normal increase in erythrocyte sedimentation rate can obscure the diagnosis of acute infection
C) As evidence of hemorrhage
D) As requiring immediate antibiotics
Answer: B
Rationale: "During labor and after birth the white blood cell (WBC) count can rise to 30,000/mm3 but returns to normal levels within 1 to 2 weeks after birth," and "leukocytosis, coupled with the increase in erythrocyte sedimentation rate that normally occurs, can obscure the diagnosis of acute infection."
Trap: (A) uses a value that is not diagnostic in this population, which is exactly the problem the chapter names — infection has to be diagnosed on other grounds here.

Q: How long does the chapter say the postpartum hypercoagulable state increases thromboembolic risk?
A) For 24 hours after birth
B) For up to 12 weeks after birth, when combined with vessel damage and immobility
C) For 1 week after birth
D) Only during the hospital stay
Answer: B
Rationale: Clotting factors and fibrinogen "remain elevated in the immediate puerperium," and "when combined with vessel damage and immobility, this hypercoagulability causes an increased risk for venous thromboembolism and pulmonary embolism for up to 12 weeks after birth."
Trap: (D) ends the risk at discharge, which is where most teaching stops and where the risk does not.

Subtopic: fluid loss

Q: A woman on postpartum night 2 wakes drenched in sweat and reports voiding large amounts. What should the nurse do?
A) Obtain blood cultures for suspected infection
B) Provide a gown change and reassurance, since urine output of 3000 mL or more per day and profuse diaphoresis, especially at night, are common for the first 2 to 3 days and account for a weight loss of 2 to 3 kg
C) Restrict fluids to reduce the diuresis
D) Insert an indwelling catheter to measure output accurately
Answer: B
Rationale: "Within 12 hours of birth, women begin to lose excess tissue fluid accumulated during pregnancy," driven by decreased estrogen levels, removal of increased venous pressure in the lower extremities, and loss of the pregnancy-induced blood volume increase. "Urine output of 3000 mL or more each day during the first 2 to 3 days after birth is common," and "profuse diaphoresis often occurs, especially at night, for the first 2 to 3 days," together accounting for "a weight loss of 2 to 3 kg (5 to 6.6 lb.) during the early puerperium."
Trap: (A) works up a normal physiologic event, and (D) adds a catheter that would raise the risk of urinary tract infection in a woman already at increased risk.

Topic: bladder and renal recovery
Subtopic: why voiding is impaired

Q: Which factors contribute to a decreased urge to void after birth? Select all that apply.
A) Birth-induced trauma
B) Increased bladder capacity after birth
C) The effects of epidural or spinal anesthesia
D) Pelvic soreness from labor, lacerations, or episiotomy, which can reduce or alter the voiding reflex
E) Decreased urine production in the first 24 hours
Answer: A, B, C, D
Rationale: "Birth-induced trauma, increased bladder capacity after birth, and the effects of conduction (e.g., epidural or spinal) anesthesia can result in a decreased urge to void. In addition, pelvic soreness caused by the forces of labor, vaginal or perineal lacerations, or episiotomy can reduce or alter the voiding reflex."
Trap: (E) reverses the fluid picture entirely. Urine production rises sharply, which is what makes the decreased urge dangerous: "decreased voiding combined with postpartal diuresis can result in bladder distention."

Q: Why does bladder distention immediately after birth cause excessive bleeding?
A) Because it increases intraabdominal pressure
B) Because a distended bladder pushes the uterus up and to the side and prevents it from contracting firmly
C) Because it compresses the renal arteries
D) It does not cause bleeding
Answer: B
Rationale: "Immediately after birth, excessive bleeding can occur if the bladder becomes distended because it pushes the uterus up and to the side and prevents it from contracting firmly."
Trap: (D) misses the single most actionable link in postpartum assessment — a fundus that is boggy and displaced laterally is a bladder problem until proven otherwise.

Q: What problem does bladder overdistention cause later in the puerperium, and when is bladder tone usually restored?
A) It makes the bladder more susceptible to infection and impedes the resumption of normal voiding; tone is usually restored by 5 to 7 days after birth
B) It causes hemorrhage; tone returns within 24 hours
C) It causes permanent incontinence; tone never fully returns
D) It has no later consequences
Answer: A
Rationale: "Later in the puerperium, overdistention can make the bladder more susceptible to infection and impede the resumption of normal voiding," and "with adequate bladder emptying, bladder tone is usually restored by 5 to 7 days after birth."
Trap: (B) applies the immediate consequence to the later period. The same distention causes bleeding early and infection later.

Q: Which statements about postpartum renal recovery are accurate? Select all that apply.
A) Renal function returns to normal by 6 weeks after birth
B) About 8 weeks are required for the pregnancy-induced hypotonia and dilation of the ureters and renal pelves to return to the nonpregnant state
C) Renal glycosuria induced by pregnancy disappears by 1 week postpartum, though lactosuria can occur in lactating women
D) The blood urea nitrogen level increases during the puerperium as autolysis of the involuting uterus occurs
E) Pregnancy-associated proteinuria persists for 6 months
Answer: A, B, C, D
Rationale: The chapter gives all four: renal function normal by 6 weeks, ureteral and renal pelvic changes resolving in about 8 weeks, glycosuria gone by 1 week with possible lactosuria, and a rising BUN from uterine autolysis. Plasma creatinine returns to normal by 6 weeks.
Trap: (E) extends a finding the chapter resolves at 6 weeks: "pregnancy-associated proteinuria resolves by 6 weeks after birth." Persistent proteinuria past that point is not expected.

Topic: postpartum breast changes
Subtopic: lactogenesis and engorgement

Q: A breastfeeding woman's breasts become fuller and heavier about 3 days after birth as the colostrum changes to mature milk. What is this called, and when does it occur?
A) Lactogenesis II, occurring by about 72 to 96 hours after birth
B) Lactogenesis I, occurring in the first 24 hours
C) Let-down, occurring with each feeding
D) Engorgement, which is always pathologic
Answer: A
Rationale: "The breasts gradually become fuller and heavier as the colostrum transitions to mature milk by about 72 to 96 hours after birth; this is known as lactogenesis II" — often called the milk coming in. True milk is "bluish white ... with a skim-milk appearance," and in the first 24 hours "there is little if any change in the breast tissue" beyond colostrum, "a clear yellow fluid."
Trap: (C) names the ejection reflex triggered by oxytocin at each feeding, which is a different event from the transition to mature milk.

Q: A breastfeeding woman has hard, uncomfortable, engorged breasts on day 3 with a mild temperature elevation, and her infant is having trouble latching. What should the nurse teach?
A) That engorgement is from an increase in blood and lymphatic fluid as milk production increases, that the fullness of the nipple tissue can make latching difficult, and that with frequent breastfeeding and proper care it typically lasts only 24 to 48 hours
B) That she should stop breastfeeding until the swelling resolves
C) That the temperature elevation indicates mastitis
D) That engorgement lasts about 2 weeks
Answer: A
Rationale: "Some women experience engorgement at this time owing to an increase in blood and lymphatic fluid as milk production increases. Engorged breasts are hard and uncomfortable, and mild temperature elevation can occur; the fullness of the nipple tissue can make it difficult for the infant to latch on and feed." And: "with frequent breastfeeding and proper care, engorgement is a temporary condition that typically lasts only 24 to 48 hours."
Trap: (B) removes the treatment. Frequent feeding is what resolves engorgement in a lactating woman, and stopping would worsen it.

Q: A woman who is not breastfeeding has distended, firm, tender breasts on postpartum day 3 and asks whether she should express some milk for relief. What should the nurse tell her?
A) Yes, expressing milk will relieve the pressure
B) No — breast distention is caused primarily by temporary congestion of veins and lymphatics rather than by an accumulation of milk; milk is present but should not be expressed, and the engorgement resolves spontaneously with discomfort decreasing usually within 24 to 36 hours
C) She should pump every 3 hours until the swelling resolves
D) She should apply heat and massage the breasts to move the milk
Answer: B
Rationale: In nonbreastfeeding women, "breast distention is caused primarily by the temporary congestion of veins and lymphatics rather than by an accumulation of milk. Milk is present but should not be expressed." Engorgement "resolves spontaneously, and discomfort decreases usually within 24 to 36 hours," and "lactation ceases within a few days to 1 week."
Trap: (A) and (C) both stimulate the breast and prolong lactation, which is the opposite of what this woman wants — and it is the most tempting advice because it works for the breastfeeding woman two items earlier.

Subtopic: assessment findings and hormones

Q: A lactating woman's breast tissue feels nodular and lumpy. How does the chapter distinguish this from a concerning finding?
A) The nodularity associated with milk production tends to shift in position, in contrast to lumps associated with fibrocystic changes or cancer, which can be palpated consistently in the same location
B) Nodularity in a lactating breast is always abnormal
C) Nodularity is distinguished only by ultrasound
D) Milk-related lumps are always painful and other lumps never are
Answer: A
Rationale: As milk ducts fill, breast tissue "can feel somewhat nodular or lumpy. In contrast to the lumps associated with fibrocystic breast changes or cancer (which can be palpated consistently in the same location), the nodularity associated with milk production tends to shift in position."
Trap: (D) substitutes pain for location, and location is the discriminator the chapter gives. Nonlactating engorgement can also involve "axillary breast tissue (the tail of Spence) and any accessory breast or nipple tissue along the milk line."

Q: Which hormone produces milk and which ejects it?
A) Prolactin from the anterior pituitary stimulates milk production; oxytocin from the posterior pituitary triggers the milk ejection or let-down reflex in response to suckling or nipple stimulation
B) Oxytocin produces milk; prolactin ejects it
C) Both are produced by the anterior pituitary
D) Estrogen produces milk and prolactin ejects it
Answer: A
Rationale: "Prolactin, produced by the anterior pituitary, is the hormone that stimulates milk production," and "the posterior pituitary produces oxytocin in response to infant suckling or nipple stimulation with milk expression. Oxytocin triggers the milk ejection or let-down reflex."
Trap: (B) reverses the pair, and the consequence matters: because oxytocin also contracts the uterus, breastfeeding reduces bleeding and intensifies afterpains at the same time.

Topic: cervix ovaries and return of fertility
Subtopic: the cervix

Q: A woman's postpartum examination notes a cervical os that appears as a jagged slit rather than a circle. How should the nurse interpret this?
A) As a laceration requiring repair
B) As the permanent change the chapter describes: the external cervical os never regains its prepregnancy appearance and instead appears as a jagged slit, often described as a fish mouth
C) As evidence of infection
D) As a temporary finding that resolves by 6 weeks
Answer: B
Rationale: "The external cervical os never regains its prepregnancy appearance; it no longer has a circular shape, but instead appears as a jagged slit often described as a 'fish mouth.'" The cervix is soft immediately after birth, shortens and firms over 12 to 18 hours, and regains its form within 2 to 3 days.
Trap: (A) treats a permanent parity marker as an injury. Dilation also closes gradually: 2 to 3 cm by the second or third day, about 1 cm by 1 week.

Subtopic: ovulation, menses, and contraception

Q: A nonlactating woman asks when she could become pregnant again. What does the chapter say?
A) Ovulation can occur as early as 27 days after birth, with a mean of about 7 to 9 weeks, and about 70% of nonlactating women resume menstruating by 12 weeks
B) Ovulation does not resume for at least 3 months
C) Ovulation resumes only after the first menstrual period
D) Ovulation cannot occur before 6 weeks
Answer: A
Rationale: In nonlactating women, ovulation can occur "as early as 27 days after birth," with a mean of "about 7 to 9 weeks," and "about 70% of nonlactating women resume menstruating by 12 weeks after birth."
Trap: (C) reverses the sequence, which is the assumption behind many unplanned pregnancies — ovulation precedes the first menses.

Q: A woman who is exclusively breastfeeding asks whether she needs contraception. What does the chapter support?
A) That breastfeeding reliably prevents pregnancy for 6 months
B) That the mean time to ovulation in lactating women is about 6 months, but breastfeeding frequency, duration of each feeding, and supplementary feedings all influence it, so discussion of contraceptive options early in the postpartum period is necessary
C) That contraception is unnecessary until menses return
D) That ovulation is suppressed by oxytocin
Answer: B
Rationale: The mean time to ovulation in lactating women is "about 6 months," and "breastfeeding frequency, duration of each feeding, and the use of supplementary feedings influence the return of ovulation," with ovulation delayed longest in exclusive breastfeeding. "Because of the uncertainty about the return of ovulation and menstruation, discussion of contraceptive options early in the postpartum period is necessary."
Trap: (D) names the wrong hormone: "the persistence of elevated serum prolactin levels in lactating women appears to be responsible for suppressing ovulation."

Q: What should a woman be told about her first menstrual period after birth?
A) That it is usually heavier than normal, and that within three or four cycles the amount returns to the prepregnancy volume
B) That it is usually lighter than normal
C) That it will be identical to her prepregnancy periods
D) That heavy first menses always indicate retained placental fragments
Answer: A
Rationale: "The first menstrual flow after birth is usually heavier than normal. Within three or four cycles, the amount of menstrual flow returns to the prepregnancy volume."
Trap: (D) converts an expected finding into a complication, and it is the sort of report that would otherwise send a woman back to triage unnecessarily.

Topic: postpartum endocrine changes
Subtopic: hormone withdrawal

Q: What happens to estrogen, progesterone, and hCG levels after birth?
A) Estrogen and progesterone drop markedly and reach their lowest levels 1 week after birth; hCG decreases rapidly but can be detected for 3 to 4 weeks after birth
B) All three remain elevated for 6 weeks
C) Estrogen rises while progesterone falls
D) hCG is undetectable within 24 hours
Answer: A
Rationale: "Estrogen and progesterone levels drop markedly after birth and reach their lowest levels 1 week after birth," and "the hCG levels decrease rapidly after birth. However, the hormone can be detected in the maternal system for 3 to 4 weeks after birth." Decreased estrogen is also associated with the diuresis of excess extracellular fluid.
Trap: (D) would make a positive pregnancy test at 2 weeks postpartum alarming, when the chapter explains it as residual hCG.

Q: A woman with type 1 diabetes has just given birth. What does the chapter say about her insulin needs?
A) They rise sharply because of the stress of birth
B) They fall significantly, because decreases in human placental lactogen, estrogens, cortisol, and placental insulinase reverse the diabetogenic effects of pregnancy — and she will likely require much less insulin for several days, especially if breastfeeding
C) They remain unchanged
D) Insulin is discontinued permanently
Answer: B
Rationale: "Decreases in human placental lactogen, estrogens, cortisol, and the placental enzyme insulinase reverse the diabetogenic effects of pregnancy, resulting in significantly lower blood glucose levels in the immediate postpartum period. Women with type 1 diabetes will likely require much less insulin for several days after birth, especially if they are breastfeeding."
Trap: (A) applies a stress response to a woman whose insulin resistance has just been removed with the placenta, and continuing the pregnancy dose is what causes severe postpartum hypoglycemia.

Q: Which statements about postpartum metabolic and prolactin changes are accurate? Select all that apply.
A) The thyroid gland gradually returns to normal by 3 months after birth, with thyroxine and triiodothyronine reaching prepregnant levels by 6 weeks
B) There is an increased risk for transient autoimmune thyroiditis in the postpartum period
C) The basal metabolic rate remains elevated for the first 1 to 2 weeks after birth
D) In nonbreastfeeding women, prolactin levels decline after birth and reach the nonpregnant range by the third postpartum week
E) Prolactin levels in breastfeeding women fall below nonpregnant levels within 6 weeks
Answer: A, B, C, D
Rationale: The chapter gives the thyroid timeline, the transient autoimmune thyroiditis risk, the elevated basal metabolic rate for 1 to 2 weeks, and the nonbreastfeeding prolactin decline by the third week.
Trap: (E) reverses the lactating pattern: prolactin is "highest during the first month after birth, gradually decreasing after 6 weeks, but remaining elevated above nonpregnant levels as long as she is breastfeeding."
