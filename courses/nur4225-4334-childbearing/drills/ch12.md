# Chapter 12 drills — Conception and Fetal Development

Authored from `sources/Ch12_Fact_Bank.md` (Lowdermilk, Cashion, Alden,
Olshansky & Perry, *Maternity & Women's Health Care*, 13th ed.).

Blueprint weight is **7 questions of 200 (3.5%), depth `standard`** — the top of
that band — topic *"Placenta, Fetal Oxygenation, Fetal System Development."*
That earns **45 questions across 10 topics**.

**Scope.** This chapter needed no scope negotiation: the blueprint topic line
maps almost exactly onto the reading guide's two assignments (*Placenta*
pp. 239–241, *Fetal maturation* pp. 241–248), so the fact bank curated the
placenta, fetal oxygenation, and system development at full depth and compressed
the chapter's conception half — meiosis, gametogenesis, fertilization,
implantation, the primary germ layers — to a cross-reference. None of that
appears below, because none of it is in the bank to draw on.

The organizing idea the fact bank names is worth restating, because five of the
ten topics hang off it: **the placenta is doing the job of four organ systems at
once**, and every fetal adaptation exists because it does the lung's job badly.
The fetus compensates with better hemoglobin, more hemoglobin, and a faster
heart rate, and routes blood around the lungs and liver with three shunts. That
is also why so much of this chapter's exam value is in what happens **at birth**,
when the placenta is cut away — which is why `fetal hepatic and renal function`
is its own topic rather than a footnote to the placenta.

Every invertible pair the fact bank flags is drilled from both ends: umbilical
artery and vein, oligohydramnios and hydramnios, hCG and hPL, estriol and
estradiol, progesterone and estrogen on uterine contractility, lecithin and
sphingomyelin, what accelerates and what inhibits lung maturity, and dizygotic
and monozygotic twins.

**What the band could not buy.** Cut, in this order: amniotic fluid volume by
gestational age and the full nine-item list of amniotic fluid functions; the
week-by-week foregut, midgut, and hindgut derivatives with their named
malformations; the forebrain, midbrain, and hindbrain cranial-nerve assignments;
the endocrine milestones other than the thyroid and the diabetes cascade;
integumentary detail beyond vernix and lanugo; most of Table 12.2's
system-by-system cells, of which only the anchors that decide a clinical
judgment are drilled; triplet zygosity; and Tables 12.1 and 12.3, which the
reading guide does not assign. Kept without exception: the cord vessel
directions, all three shunts, the three oxygenation adaptations, the four
placental hormones, all five transfer mechanisms, the lung maturity thresholds,
and the vitamin K and bilirubin reasoning chains, because each of those decides
an action or explains a newborn finding.

Topic: umbilical cord and amniotic fluid

Q: A nursing student asks which umbilical vessel carries oxygenated blood, and in which direction. Which response is accurate?
A) The single umbilical vein carries oxygenated blood from the placenta to the fetus
B) The two umbilical arteries carry oxygenated blood from the placenta to the fetus
C) The single umbilical vein carries deoxygenated blood from the fetus to the placenta
D) All three vessels carry mixed blood in both directions
Answer: A
Rationale: "Two arteries carry blood from the embryo to the chorionic villi, and one vein returns blood to the embryo" (p. 238). The figure caption states the direction: "Deoxygenated blood leaves the fetus through the umbilical arteries and enters the placenta, where it is oxygenated. Oxygenated blood leaves the placenta through the umbilical vein" (p. 239).
Trap: (B) is the single most-swapped fact in this chapter, and it is swapped because it is backwards from adult anatomy, where arteries carry oxygenated blood. In the fetus the naming follows direction of flow relative to the fetus, not oxygen content.

Q: A newborn's cord is examined after birth. Which finding is normal, and what does the abnormal finding suggest?
A) Two arteries and one vein is normal; a two-vessel cord occurs in about 1% and is associated with cardiovascular, gastrointestinal, and urinary tract anomalies
B) Two veins and one artery is normal; a three-vessel cord suggests a chromosomal abnormality
C) One artery and one vein is normal; a third vessel indicates a vascular malformation
D) The vessel count varies normally and carries no clinical significance
Answer: A
Rationale: The normal cord has two arteries and one vein. "Approximately 1% of umbilical cords contain only two vessels: one artery and one vein," associated with "fetal cardiovascular, gastrointestinal (esophageal and anal atresia), and urinary tract anomalies" (p. 238).
Trap: (B) inverts the vessel counts, which is the mirror of the previous item's error. The clinical point is that counting cord vessels at birth is a screening act: the missing artery is what prompts evaluation of three organ systems.

Q: An ultrasound reports an amniotic fluid volume of less than 300 mL at 30 weeks. Which fetal problem should the nurse anticipate?
A) A renal abnormality, since fetal urine forms a major part of amniotic fluid volume
B) A gastrointestinal malformation, since the fetus cannot swallow the fluid
C) A neural tube defect, since the fluid cushions the developing spine
D) A cardiac anomaly, since fluid volume reflects fetal cardiac output
Answer: A
Rationale: "Having less than 300 mL of amniotic fluid (oligohydramnios) is associated with fetal renal abnormalities" (p. 237). Fetal "urine is excreted into the amniotic fluid and forms a major part of the amniotic fluid volume. Oligohydramnios is indicative of renal dysfunction" (p. 247).
Trap: (B) is the mirror error, belonging to the opposite finding. The direction the fact bank gives is worth holding literally: the kidneys make the fluid and the gut removes it, so too little points at the kidneys and too much points at the gut.

Q: A different ultrasound reports more than 2 L of amniotic fluid. Which fetal problem should the nurse anticipate?
A) A gastrointestinal malformation, since fluid is removed by fetal swallowing
B) A renal abnormality, since the fetus is producing excessive urine
C) A pulmonary malformation, since lung liquid is the only source of the fluid
D) A skeletal dysplasia, since the fetus is not moving enough to displace fluid
Answer: A
Rationale: "Having more than 2 L of amniotic fluid (hydramnios or polyhydramnios) is associated with gastrointestinal and other malformations" (p. 237). From the second trimester onward, volume "is regulated by a balance between fetal fluid production (lung liquid and urine) and fluid resorption through fetal swallowing" (p. 235).
Trap: (B) is the mirror error of the previous item and completes the pair. Both findings are volume abnormalities, so only the direction tells you which side of the production-and-removal balance has failed.

Q: A nurse is explaining what protects the umbilical vessels from compression as the fetus moves. Which structure should the nurse name?
A) Wharton's jelly, a connective tissue surrounding the vessels
B) The vernix caseosa, which coats the cord along with the fetal skin
C) The chorionic villi, which anchor the cord to the placenta
D) The amnion alone, which is sufficient to prevent compression
Answer: A
Rationale: "Connective tissue called Wharton's jelly surrounds the vessels, preventing compression of the blood vessels and ensuring continued nourishment of the embryo or fetus" (p. 238). Compression is still possible if the cord lies between the fetal head and the maternal pelvis or is twisted around the fetal body.
Trap: (B) borrows a real fetal substance from the integumentary section and gives it a mechanical job it does not have. Vernix protects fetal skin; Wharton's jelly protects the cord, and the distinction matters because cord compression is a fetal oxygenation emergency and vernix is not.

Topic: placental structure and developmental milestones

Q: A patient asks at what point the placenta is structurally complete. Which response is accurate?
A) The structure of the placenta is complete by the 12th week; it then grows wider until 20 weeks and afterward grows thicker
B) The structure is complete by 20 weeks, when it covers approximately half the uterine surface
C) The structure is complete by day 17, when the maternal-placental-embryonic circulation is established
D) The structure is not complete until 28 weeks, when placental hormone production peaks
Answer: A
Rationale: "The structure of the placenta is complete by the 12th week." The placenta "continues to grow wider until 20 weeks, when it covers approximately half of the uterine surface. It then continues to grow thicker" (p. 239).
Trap: (B) is the fact bank's named error — a stem asking when the placenta is fully formed wants 12 weeks, not 20. The 20-week milestone is about surface area, and the two milestones are easy to merge because they sit in consecutive sentences.

Q: A nurse is reviewing early placental development. By which day is the maternal-placental-embryonic circulation in place, and what else happens then?
A) By day 17, when the embryonic heart starts beating
B) By day 7, at the moment of implantation
C) By day 28, when the neural tube closes
D) By the end of the eighth week, when the embryonic period ends
Answer: A
Rationale: "The maternal-placental-embryonic circulation is in place by day 17, when the embryonic heart starts beating" (p. 239). By the end of the third week, embryonic blood is circulating between the embryo and the chorionic villi.
Trap: (B) is the reflex pick because the placenta does begin to form at implantation, so implantation feels like the moment circulation begins. Formation starts there; a working circulation takes another ten days, and the two events are separated deliberately in the text.

Q: A nursing student is asked to describe the cotyledons of the placenta. Which description is accurate?
A) There are 15 to 20 cotyledons, and each one is a functional unit
B) There are 15 to 20 cotyledons, which together make up one functional unit
C) There are approximately 200 cotyledons, each containing a single chorionic villus
D) Cotyledons are the maternal blood spaces between the villi
Answer: A
Rationale: "A third layer develops into anchoring septa, dividing the projecting decidua into separate areas called cotyledons. In each of the 15 to 20 cotyledons, the chorionic villi branch out, and a complex system of fetal blood vessels forms. Each cotyledon is a functional unit" (p. 239).
Trap: (B) keeps the right count and destroys the point, which is that the placenta is built of many independent functional units rather than one. (D) names the intervillous spaces, which are where exchange happens but are not the same structure.

Q: A nurse is explaining why placental exchange becomes more efficient as pregnancy advances. Which explanation is accurate?
A) Permeability increases as the cytotrophoblast thins and disappears, leaving only the syncytium, the functional layer, by the fifth month
B) Permeability increases because additional cell layers are added to the villous membrane over time
C) Permeability is constant throughout pregnancy; only placental surface area changes
D) Permeability increases because maternal blood pressure rises through pregnancy
Answer: A
Rationale: Early exchange "is minimal at this time because the two cell layers of the villous membrane are too thick. Permeability increases as the cytotrophoblast thins and disappears." "By the fifth month, only the single layer of syncytium is left between the maternal blood and the fetal capillaries. The syncytium is the functional layer of the placenta" (p. 239).
Trap: (B) inverts the mechanism in the intuitive direction, since more tissue sounds like more capacity. The membrane gets thinner, not thicker, and the same syncytium is where the four placental hormones are produced.

Topic: placental hormones

Q: A patient's home pregnancy test is positive 10 days after ovulation. Which hormone did the test detect, and what is its primary function in early pregnancy?
A) Human chorionic gonadotropin, which preserves the function of the ovarian corpus luteum
B) Human placental lactogen, which stimulates maternal metabolism to supply fetal nutrients
C) Estriol, which reflects placental functioning
D) Progesterone, which maintains the endometrium
Answer: A
Rationale: Human chorionic gonadotropin "can be detected in the maternal serum or urine by 8 to 10 days after ovulation, shortly after implantation. This hormone is the basis for pregnancy tests," and it "preserves the function of the ovarian corpus luteum, ensuring the continued supply of estrogen and progesterone needed to maintain the pregnancy" (p. 239). Its maximum level occurs at 60 to 70 days.
Trap: (D) names a hormone with a real early-pregnancy role, but progesterone is not what a pregnancy test measures. The chain runs one step further back: the test detects the hormone that keeps the corpus luteum making progesterone.

Q: A patient at 26 weeks has a positive glucose screen. Which placental hormone increases resistance to insulin and is implicated in this finding?
A) Human placental lactogen, which is similar to a growth hormone and facilitates glucose transport across the placental membrane
B) Human chorionic gonadotropin, which peaks at 60 to 70 days and then declines
C) Estriol, the major estrogen secreted by the placenta
D) Progesterone, which decreases the contractility of the uterus
Answer: A
Rationale: Human placental lactogen, also known as human chorionic somatomammotropin, "is similar to a growth hormone" and "stimulates the maternal metabolism to supply nutrients needed for fetal growth." It "increases the resistance to insulin, facilitates glucose transport across the placental membrane, and stimulates breast development to prepare for lactation" (p. 239).
Trap: (B) is the mirror error of the previous item — the two protein hormones are easily swapped because both are placental and both are abbreviated from long names. The insulin-resistance clause is the discriminator, and it is what gestational diabetes stems are built on.

Q: A nurse is explaining why the uterus stays quiet through most of pregnancy and then does not. Which pair of statements is accurate?
A) Progesterone decreases the contractility of the uterus, and estrogen stimulates myometrial contractility
B) Progesterone stimulates myometrial contractility, and estrogen decreases it
C) Both hormones decrease contractility, and labor begins when both fall to zero
D) Neither hormone affects contractility; labor onset is entirely mechanical
Answer: A
Rationale: Progesterone "maintains the endometrium, decreases the contractility of the uterus, and stimulates maternal metabolism and development of breast alveoli," while estrogen "stimulates uterine growth and uteroplacental blood flow" and "stimulates myometrial contractility" (pp. 239–240). One stated theory of labor onset is "the decline in the ratio of circulating levels of progesterone to the increased levels of estrogen."
Trap: (C) overstates the theory in the way the fact bank flags: the book describes a shift in the ratio between the two hormones, not an absolute fall of progesterone to zero. Placental estrogen production increases greatly toward the end of pregnancy, so the ratio can shift while progesterone remains high.

Q: A provider orders a maternal estriol level to evaluate placental function. Why is estriol the appropriate measure rather than estradiol?
A) The major estrogen secreted by the placenta is estriol, whereas the ovaries produce mostly estradiol
B) The major estrogen secreted by the placenta is estradiol, and estriol is a metabolite of it
C) Estriol is produced by the corpus luteum and therefore reflects early pregnancy maintenance
D) Estriol is the only estrogen that crosses the placental membrane
Answer: A
Rationale: "The major estrogen secreted by the placenta is estriol, whereas the ovaries produce mostly estradiol. Estriol levels may be measured to determine placental functioning" (pp. 239–240). By 7 weeks the placenta is producing most of the maternal estrogens.
Trap: (B) swaps the two estrogens between their two sources, which is the whole basis of the test. If the ovaries were the placental estrogen's source, the level would say nothing about how the placenta is working.

Q: A patient at 7 weeks of gestation has a luteal phase defect and asks why this matters now rather than later. Which explanation is accurate?
A) By around 9 weeks the corpus luteum regresses as the placenta takes over; miscarriage occurs if the corpus luteum stops functioning before the placenta produces sufficient estrogen and progesterone
B) The corpus luteum supports the pregnancy until term, so the risk continues throughout
C) The placenta produces sufficient progesterone from implantation onward, so corpus luteum function is never critical
D) The corpus luteum regresses at 20 weeks, when the placenta reaches its full width
Answer: A
Rationale: Progesterone comes from the corpus luteum early, and the placenta becomes the primary source; "by around 9 weeks, the corpus luteum regresses." "Miscarriage occurs if the corpus luteum stops functioning before the placenta is producing sufficient estrogen and progesterone" (pp. 239–240).
Trap: (B) is the reflex pick for anyone who knows the corpus luteum matters but not that there is a handoff with a date. Because there is a handoff, a luteal defect is an early-pregnancy risk specifically, and the window closes rather than persisting.

Topic: placental transfer mechanisms

Q: A patient asks how her antibodies reach her baby before birth. Which transfer mechanism carries maternal immunoglobulins across the placental membrane?
A) Pinocytosis, which transfers large molecules such as albumin and gamma globulins
B) Active transport against the concentration gradient
C) Simple diffusion, as with water and small electrolytes
D) Facilitated diffusion, as with glucose
Answer: A
Rationale: Pinocytosis is "a mechanism used for transferring large molecules such as albumin and gamma globulins across the placental membrane," and it "conveys the maternal immunoglobulins that provide early passive immunity to the fetus" (p. 240).
Trap: (B) is the reflex pick because active transport sounds like the mechanism that would handle something as important as immunity. Size is what decides here, not importance: the immunoglobulins are too large for the diffusion and carrier mechanisms, so the membrane engulfs them.

Q: A nurse is sorting substances by how they cross the placenta. Select all the statements that are accurate.
A) Water and most electrolytes with a molecular weight less than 500 cross by simple diffusion
B) Glucose is transported from mother to fetus by facilitated diffusion
C) Amino acids and calcium are transported against the concentration gradient by active transport
D) The majority of medications cross by passive diffusion
E) Oxygen is actively extracted from maternal blood by the placenta
Answer: A, B, C, D
Rationale: "Water and most electrolytes with a molecular weight less than 500 readily diffuse through the membrane." "Fetal demand for glucose is high, and it is transported from mother to fetus by facilitated diffusion." "Amino acids and calcium are transported against the concentration gradient." "The majority of medications cross the placenta through passive diffusion" (p. 240).
Trap: (E) is the single wrong option, and it is the error the fact bank flags in the oxygenation section: the placenta moves gases by diffusion, not by active extraction. It is attractive because the fetus does obtain oxygen efficiently, but the efficiency comes from fetal adaptations rather than from placental work.

Q: A patient with diabetes asks why her baby's blood sugar would be lower than hers if glucose crosses freely. Which explanation is accurate?
A) The fetal concentration of glucose is lower because of rapid metabolism of glucose by the fetus
B) The fetal concentration is lower because the placenta consumes most of the glucose it transports
C) The fetal concentration is lower because glucose crosses only by active transport against a gradient
D) The fetal concentration is normally higher than maternal, so the premise is incorrect
Answer: A
Rationale: "The fetal concentration of glucose is lower than the glucose level in the maternal blood because of rapid metabolism of glucose by the fetus" (p. 240). Glucose crosses by facilitated diffusion, which follows the gradient rather than opposing it.
Trap: (B) attributes the gradient to the placenta rather than to the fetus, which is the error the fact bank names. The distinction matters for the diabetes cascade: because the gradient is driven by fetal consumption, maternal hyperglycemia raises fetal glucose directly and drives fetal hyperinsulinemia.

Q: A nursing student is asked to name the metabolic functions of the placenta. Which set is correct?
A) Respiration, nutrition, excretion, and storage
B) Respiration, circulation, filtration, and immunity
C) Nutrition, excretion, thermoregulation, and hematopoiesis
D) Respiration and nutrition only; the fetal kidneys handle excretion
Answer: A
Rationale: "The metabolic functions of the placenta are respiration, nutrition, excretion, and storage." Oxygen and carbon dioxide diffuse in opposite directions, so "the placenta functions as lungs for the fetus"; carbohydrates, proteins, calcium, and iron are stored in the placenta; and fetal metabolic wastes cross into maternal blood "for excretion by the maternal kidneys" (p. 240).
Trap: (D) is the substantive error rather than a list error, and it inverts the chapter's central point. The fetus does not need functioning kidneys in utero precisely because the placenta acts as the organ of excretion, which is why renal failure declares itself at birth rather than before it.

Topic: fetal circulation and the three shunts

Q: Which fetal shunt allows blood to bypass the liver, and where does it carry blood?
A) The ductus venosus, which carries blood from the umbilical vein into the inferior vena cava
B) The ductus arteriosus, which carries blood from the pulmonary artery into the aorta
C) The foramen ovale, which carries blood from the right atrium into the left atrium
D) The ductus venosus, which carries blood from the pulmonary artery into the aorta
Answer: A
Rationale: At the liver the umbilical vein "divides into two branches; one branch circulates some oxygenated blood through the liver. Most of the blood passes through the ductus venosus into the inferior vena cava" (p. 245). Of the three shunts, only the ductus venosus bypasses the liver.
Trap: (D) keeps the correct shunt name and attaches the ductus arteriosus's route to it, which is the harder error because naming the shunt feels like answering the question. Both ducts share a word, and only one of them has anything to do with the liver.

Q: A newborn's ductus arteriosus fails to close. Which connection has remained open, and what did it bypass in fetal life?
A) The pulmonary artery to the aorta, bypassing the lungs
B) The right atrium to the left atrium, bypassing the lungs
C) The umbilical vein to the inferior vena cava, bypassing the liver
D) The aorta to the pulmonary vein, bypassing the left ventricle
Answer: A
Rationale: The ductus arteriosus runs from the pulmonary artery to the aorta, "distal to the point of exit of the arteries supplying the head and arms." Because the fetal lungs do not function for gas exchange, "a special circulatory pathway, the ductus arteriosus, bypasses the lungs" (p. 245). A small amount of blood circulates through the resistant lung tissue, but the majority follows the lower-resistance path through the duct.
Trap: (B) describes the foramen ovale, and it is attractive because both structures bypass the lungs. The fact bank's discrimination is worth holding exactly: two shunts bypass the lungs and only one bypasses the liver, so "bypasses the lungs" cannot by itself identify a shunt.

Q: A nurse is teaching about the three fetal shunts. Select all the statements that are accurate.
A) The ductus venosus bypasses the liver
B) The foramen ovale carries blood from the right atrium to the left atrium
C) The ductus arteriosus carries blood from the pulmonary artery to the aorta
D) Both the foramen ovale and the ductus arteriosus allow blood to bypass the lungs
E) The ductus venosus bypasses the lungs
Answer: A, B, C, D
Rationale: The figure caption states that "three shunts permit most of the blood to bypass the liver and lungs: (1) ductus venosus, (2) foramen ovale, and (3) ductus arteriosus" (p. 245). The ductus venosus runs from the umbilical vein to the inferior vena cava, bypassing the liver; the foramen ovale opens from the right atrium into the left atrium; and the ductus arteriosus runs from the pulmonary artery to the aorta.
Trap: (E) is the single wrong option, and it is the one the fact bank predicts will be checked — an item naming all three shunts plus "ductus venosus bypasses the lungs" is testing precisely this. The word "ductus" appears in two shunt names, which is what makes their destinations easy to trade.

Q: Why does fetal circulation deliver the most oxygen-rich blood to the heart, head, neck, and arms rather than distributing it evenly?
A) This pattern enhances cephalocaudal, or head-to-rump, development of the fetus
B) The lower body has no arterial supply until after birth
C) The liver requires the least oxygen and is therefore supplied last
D) The pattern is incidental and has no stated developmental purpose
Answer: A
Rationale: Blood passing through the foramen ovale into the left ventricle is squeezed into the aorta, "where the arteries supplying the heart, head, neck, and arms receive most of the oxygen-rich blood." "This pattern of supplying the highest levels of oxygen and nutrients to the head, neck, and arms enhances the cephalocaudal (head-to-rump) development of the embryo/fetus" (p. 245).
Trap: (D) is the pick for a learner who has memorized the route without the reason, and the reason is what makes later material cohere — head-sparing in fetal growth restriction is the same principle under stress.

Q: Deoxygenated blood returning from the fetal head and arms enters the right atrium through the superior vena cava. Where does the majority of it go next?
A) Into the right ventricle, out the pulmonary artery, and through the ductus arteriosus into the aorta
B) Through the foramen ovale into the left atrium and out the aorta
C) Into the pulmonary circulation, where it is oxygenated by the fetal lungs
D) Directly into the umbilical vein for return to the placenta
Answer: A
Rationale: "Deoxygenated blood returning from the head and arms enters the right atrium through the superior vena cava. This blood is directed downward into the right ventricle, where it is squeezed into the pulmonary artery." "A small amount of blood circulates through the resistant lung tissue, but the majority follows the path with less resistance through the ductus arteriosus into the aorta" (p. 245).
Trap: (B) applies the foramen ovale's route to the wrong stream. Blood arriving from the inferior vena cava crosses the foramen ovale; blood arriving from the superior vena cava is directed to the right ventricle instead, and keeping the two streams separate is what the fetal heart is arranged to do. (D) reverses the umbilical vein's direction.

Topic: fetal oxygenation adaptations

Q: A nurse is explaining how the fetus obtains enough oxygen across a placenta rather than through lungs. Select all the special characteristics the chapter names.
A) Fetal hemoglobin has a high affinity for oxygen and carries 20% to 30% more oxygen than maternal hemoglobin
B) The hemoglobin concentration of the fetus is approximately 50% greater than that of the mother
C) The fetal heart rate of 110 to 160 beats per minute gives a cardiac output per unit of body weight higher than an adult's
D) The fetus has a larger lung surface area relative to body weight than an adult
Answer: A, B, C
Rationale: "The following three special characteristics enable the fetus to obtain sufficient oxygen from the maternal blood": fetal hemoglobin's higher affinity and 20% to 30% greater carrying capacity, a hemoglobin concentration approximately 50% greater than the mother's, and a fetal heart rate of 110 to 160 beats per minute producing higher cardiac output per unit of body weight (p. 246).
Trap: (D) is the single wrong option and it is wrong in a revealing way: the fetal lungs are not participating in gas exchange at all, so no lung property can be one of the adaptations. The three real adaptations are all about the blood and the pump, which is the pattern worth holding.

Q: Why is a fetal heart rate of 110 to 160 beats per minute an oxygenation advantage rather than simply a normal value?
A) It produces a cardiac output per unit of body weight that is higher than an adult's, moving more blood through the placenta per minute
B) It reflects immaturity of the fetal conduction system and confers no advantage
C) It compensates for the low oxygen affinity of fetal hemoglobin
D) It allows the fetal lungs to participate in gas exchange before birth
Answer: A
Rationale: The third named adaptation is that "the fetal heart rate is 110 to 160 beats/min, making the cardiac output per unit of body weight higher than that of an adult" (p. 246).
Trap: (C) inverts the first adaptation. Fetal hemoglobin has a high affinity for oxygen, not a low one, so a rationale built on compensating for poor affinity contradicts the item next to it in the same list.

Q: A patient asks why her baby is not harmed by having oxygen delivered through the placenta instead of lungs. Which statement about fetal hemoglobin is accurate?
A) It has a high affinity for oxygen and carries 20% to 30% more oxygen than maternal hemoglobin
B) It has a lower affinity for oxygen than maternal hemoglobin, which allows easier release to the tissues
C) It carries the same amount of oxygen as maternal hemoglobin but is present in lower concentration
D) It is identical to adult hemoglobin and confers no advantage
Answer: A
Rationale: "Fetal hemoglobin has a high affinity for oxygen and carries 20% to 30% more oxygen than maternal hemoglobin," and "the hemoglobin concentration of the fetus is approximately 50% greater than that of the mother" (p. 246).
Trap: (B) is the plausible-sounding inversion, since a lower affinity genuinely would help unload oxygen at the tissues. The fetal problem is acquiring oxygen across a membrane from maternal blood, not releasing it, so the adaptation runs toward higher affinity. (C) reverses the concentration finding as well.

Topic: fetal lung maturity

Q: An amniocentesis at 35 weeks reports a lecithin/sphingomyelin ratio of 2:1. How should the nurse interpret this?
A) The fetal lungs are deemed mature, a value reached at approximately the middle of the third trimester
B) The fetal lungs are immature, since a ratio of at least 4:1 is required
C) The result is uninterpretable without a simultaneous phosphatidylglycerol level
D) The ratio reflects renal rather than pulmonary maturity
Answer: A
Rationale: "The measure of lecithin in relation to sphingomyelin is used to determine fetal lung maturity. When the L/S ratio reaches 2:1, the fetal lungs are deemed mature," which "occurs approximately in the middle of the third trimester" (p. 246).
Trap: (B) raises a correctly remembered threshold to a wrong number, which is the standard way this item is missed. The 1.2:1 value that also appears in this chapter is the 30-to-31-week figure, not a target.

Q: A student asks why fetal lung maturity is expressed as a ratio rather than as a single surfactant level. Which explanation is accurate?
A) Lecithin is detectable at approximately 21 weeks and increases after week 24, whereas sphingomyelin remains constant, so the ratio tracks the rising component against a fixed reference
B) Sphingomyelin rises steadily while lecithin remains constant, so the ratio tracks sphingomyelin
C) Both phospholipids rise together, and the ratio corrects for amniotic fluid volume
D) The ratio corrects for maternal blood contamination of the specimen
Answer: A
Rationale: Lecithin is "the most critical alveolar surfactant required for postnatal lung expansion. It is detectable at approximately 21 weeks and increases in amount after week 24," while sphingomyelin "remains constant in amount" (p. 246).
Trap: (B) swaps which phospholipid is the variable one, and the fact bank names this as the test of whether the ratio is understood or merely memorized. If the denominator were the rising value, the ratio would move the wrong way as the lungs matured.

Q: Besides the lecithin/sphingomyelin ratio, which findings indicate fetal lung maturity?
A) Phosphatidylglycerol, detectable at about 36 weeks, indicating very low risk for respiratory distress syndrome, and a lamellar body count above 50,000
B) Phosphatidylglycerol detectable at about 24 weeks, and a lamellar body count below 50,000
C) An absent phosphatidylglycerol level with a rising sphingomyelin level
D) The presence of meconium in the amniotic fluid
Answer: A
Rationale: Phosphatidylglycerol is detectable "at about 36 weeks," and "its presence indicates very low risk for respiratory distress syndrome." A lamellar body count above 50,000 "indicates lung maturity"; lamellar bodies are "surfactant-containing particles within the alveoli" (p. 246).
Trap: (B) inverts the lamellar body threshold, turning a floor into a ceiling. (D) is a real amniotic fluid finding attached to the wrong question — meconium in the fluid points to breech presentation or fetal hypoxia, not to lung maturity.

Q: A patient with gestational diabetes at 37 weeks has an estimated fetal weight above the 90th percentile. What should the nurse anticipate about this fetus's lungs?
A) They may be immature despite the large size, because hyperinsulinemia blocks lung maturation
B) They are certainly mature, because lung maturity tracks fetal size
C) They are accelerated in maturity, because maternal diabetes stresses the fetus
D) Lung maturity cannot be affected by maternal metabolic conditions
Answer: A
Rationale: "Gestational diabetes and chronic glomerulonephritis can inhibit fetal lung maturity" (p. 246). In fetuses of mothers with uncontrolled diabetes, maternal hyperglycemia produces fetal hyperglycemia, "stimulating hyperinsulinemia and islet cell hyperplasia. This results in a macrosomic (large) fetus. Hyperinsulinemia also blocks lung maturation, placing the neonate at risk for respiratory distress and hypoglycemia when the maternal glucose source is lost at birth" (p. 247).
Trap: (B) is the trap the fact bank names outright — assuming a macrosomic infant must have mature lungs. Size and maturity are driven by the same hormone in opposite directions here, so the infant can be large and immature at once. (C) applies the acceleration rule, which belongs to maternal hypertension, placental dysfunction, infection, and corticosteroid use, not to diabetes.

Q: A neonate born by scheduled cesarean at 38 weeks without labor develops tachypnea and has crackles. Which explanation does the chapter give?
A) Neonates born by scheduled cesarean before 39 weeks without labor have an increased risk of transient tachypnea of the newborn, with crackles for the first 24 to 48 hours
B) The absence of thoracic compression during vaginal birth is now understood to be the sole cause of retained lung fluid
C) Lung fluid is produced only after birth, so early cesarean birth cannot affect it
D) Crackles at this age indicate pneumonia until proven otherwise
Answer: A
Rationale: "Neonates born by scheduled cesarean birth before 39 weeks' gestation without labor have an increased risk of transient tachypnea of the newborn and are likely to have crackles in their lungs for the first 24 to 48 hours." Lung fluid clearance "was originally attributed to the thoracic compression during vaginal birth; current thinking is that ... it begins well in advance of term birth" (p. 246).
Trap: (B) states the older explanation as though it were current, and the fact bank flags exactly this: do not attribute clearance solely to the thoracic squeeze. The distinction matters because clearance beginning well before birth is what makes gestational age, not mode of birth alone, the operative variable.

Topic: fetal system development timeline

Q: A nursing student is asked where blood formation first occurs in the developing human and how the site changes. Which sequence is correct?
A) Yolk sac in the third week, fetal liver from the sixth week, then bone marrow, spleen, thymus, and lymph nodes seeded in weeks 8 to 11
B) Fetal liver in the third week, then yolk sac, then bone marrow
C) Bone marrow from the third week onward, with no earlier site
D) Placenta in the third week, then fetal liver from the sixth week
Answer: A
Rationale: Hematopoiesis, "the formation of blood, occurs in the yolk sac ... beginning in the third week." Hematopoietic stem cells seed the fetal liver in the fifth week and hematopoiesis begins there in the sixth, which "accounts for the relatively large size of the liver between the seventh and ninth weeks." Stem cells seed the bone marrow, spleen, thymus, and lymph nodes in weeks 8 to 11 (p. 246).
Trap: (C) is the reflex pick from adult physiology, where marrow is the only answer. The sequence matters clinically because it explains the disproportionately large fetal liver at 7 to 9 weeks, which is otherwise a puzzling finding on early imaging.

Q: An Rh-negative patient has a miscarriage at 8 weeks of gestation. Is she at risk for isoimmunization?
A) Yes; blood-type antigens are present on fetal erythrocytes soon after the sixth week, so she is at risk in any pregnancy lasting longer than 6 weeks after fertilization
B) No; isoimmunization can occur only after a term birth
C) No; fetal red cells do not carry antigens until the third trimester
D) Yes, but only if the pregnancy was ectopic rather than intrauterine
Answer: A
Rationale: "The antigenic factors that determine blood type are present in the erythrocytes soon after the sixth week. For this reason an Rh-negative woman is at risk for isoimmunization in any pregnancy that lasts longer than 6 weeks after fertilization" (p. 246).
Trap: (B) is the error the fact bank names, and it is the one with real consequences — confining the risk to term birth misses every early-loss scenario, including miscarriage, ectopic pregnancy, and induced abortion, all of which are prophylaxis indications.

Q: A patient asks what "viability" means for her fetus. Which response reflects both definitions the chapter gives?
A) A standard statutory definition is 20 weeks of gestation with a birth weight of 350, 400, or 500 g and varies by state, while clinically infants at 22 to 25 weeks are on the threshold of viability
B) Viability is uniformly defined nationally as 24 weeks of gestation
C) Viability is defined solely by birth weight, without reference to gestational age
D) Viability begins at 28 weeks, when surfactant production is sufficient
Answer: A
Rationale: Viability is "the capability of the fetus to survive outside the uterus and is usually defined by fetal weight and pregnancy duration for statistical and legal purposes." "A standard definition is 20 weeks' gestation and birth weight of 350, 400, or 500 g, but this varies by state. Nurses must be aware of the laws in their states," while "infants who are 22 to 25 weeks of gestation are on the threshold of viability" (p. 241). The limits on survival are "CNS function and the oxygenation capability of the lungs."
Trap: (B) supplies the false comfort of a single national number, and the source says the opposite — the definition varies by state, which is why the chapter tells nurses to know their own state's law. The two definitions serve different purposes, and the stem decides which is being asked for.

Q: A patient at 30 weeks is exposed to a substance known to be toxic. Which type of harm is most consistent with an exposure at this stage?
A) Functional damage to the central nervous system, since from 9 weeks the fetus is less vulnerable to teratogens except those affecting CNS function
B) Major structural malformation of the limbs and heart, since these organs are still forming
C) No harm is possible after the embryonic period ends
D) Immediate fetal demise, since the fetus has no remaining adaptive capacity
Answer: A
Rationale: "The stage of the fetus begins at 9 weeks," and during this period the fetus "is less vulnerable to teratogens except for those affecting functioning of the central nervous system" (p. 241). Stressors "cause damage to the CNS long after the vulnerable embryonic time for malformations in other organ systems," resulting in "cerebral palsy, neuromuscular impairment, intellectual disability, and learning disabilities" (p. 247).
Trap: (B) applies the embryonic-period rule to a fetal-period exposure. Structural malformations belong to weeks 3 through 8, when organs are forming; a third-trimester exposure item is a functional item, and mixing the two windows is what the distinction is there to prevent.

Q: A nurse is teaching about fetal immunity. Select all the statements that are accurate.
A) Immunoglobulin G is the only immunoglobulin that crosses the placenta, providing passive acquired immunity to specific bacterial toxins
B) The fetus produces immunoglobulin M by the end of the first trimester
C) Immunoglobulin A is not produced by the fetus but is present in large amounts in colostrum
D) The normal-term neonate can fight infection, but not as effectively as an older child
E) All three immunoglobulin classes cross the placenta in the third trimester
Answer: A, B, C, D
Rationale: "The only immunoglobulin that crosses the placenta, immunoglobulin G (IgG), provides passive acquired immunity to specific bacterial toxins." The fetus "produces IgM by the end of the first trimester." "IgA is not produced by the fetus; however, colostrum, the precursor to breast milk, contains large amounts of IgA." "The normal-term neonate can fight infection but not so effectively as an older child. The preterm infant is at much greater risk for infection" (p. 248).
Trap: (E) is the single wrong option and it erases the distinction the whole section rests on. Because only immunoglobulin G crosses, an elevated immunoglobulin M level in cord blood cannot have come from the mother and therefore implies intrauterine infection — a deduction that is impossible if all three classes cross.

Q: A patient at 20 weeks reports feeling fetal movement for the first time and asks whether this is expected. Which response reflects the chapter's developmental timetable?
A) Yes; fetal movements become strong enough for the mother to feel at about 20 weeks, and the fetus responds to sound by 24 weeks
B) No; maternal perception of movement is not expected until 28 weeks
C) Yes, but it indicates advanced gestational age, since movement is usually felt at 30 weeks
D) No; muscular movements cannot be detected by any method until 24 weeks
Answer: A
Rationale: Table 12.2 places "fetal movements strong enough for mother to feel" at 20 weeks, with muscular movements detectable by examination at 16 weeks, meconium in the bowel by 16 weeks, and hearing at 24 weeks (pp. 242–245). "Fetuses respond to sound by 24 weeks," and the fetus habituates to noises heard repeatedly (p. 247).
Trap: (D) conflates two different milestones from adjacent rows of the same table — movements can be detected at 16 weeks by examination, but maternal perception of them is a separate, later event. The table lists the two separately for exactly that reason.

Topic: fetal hepatic and renal function

Q: A newborn receives prophylactic vitamin K. Which reasoning chain explains why?
A) The sterile fetal gut cannot synthesize vitamin K, so coagulation factors II, VII, IX, and X cannot be synthesized in the fetal liver, and the deficiency persists for several days after birth
B) The fetal liver is too immature to synthesize any coagulation factors until several weeks after birth
C) Vitamin K is destroyed by the placenta, so fetal stores are depleted at birth
D) Coagulation factors V and VIII cannot be synthesized because of maternal antibody transfer
Answer: A
Rationale: "Coagulation factors II, VII, IX, and X cannot be synthesized in the fetal liver because of the lack of vitamin K synthesis in the sterile fetal gut. This coagulation deficiency persists after birth for several days and is the rationale for the prophylactic administration of vitamin K to the newborn" (p. 247).
Trap: (D) names the wrong factors, and (B) attributes the deficiency to general hepatic immaturity rather than to the specific missing vitamin. The fact bank flags both: the chain has four checkable links, and a distractor that breaks any one of them is testing whether it was learned as a chain or as a conclusion.

Q: A nurse is explaining why newborns, especially preterm ones, are prone to jaundice. Which explanation is accurate?
A) In fetal life unconjugated bilirubin is cleared by the placenta, so the glucuronyl transferase enzyme needed for conjugation is present in amounts less than those required after birth
B) The fetal liver conjugates bilirubin efficiently, but the newborn gut reabsorbs it
C) Bilirubin is produced only after birth, when fetal hemoglobin begins to break down
D) The placenta produces glucuronyl transferase, which the newborn must begin making at birth
Answer: A
Rationale: "During fetal life the liver does not have to conjugate bilirubin for excretion because the unconjugated bilirubin is cleared by the placenta. Therefore the glucuronyl transferase enzyme needed for conjugation is present in amounts less than those required after birth. This predisposes the neonate, especially the preterm infant, to hyperbilirubinemia" (p. 247).
Trap: (D) relocates the enzyme to the placenta, which sounds plausible because the placenta is doing the clearing. The enzyme is fetal and simply under-produced, since there has been no demand for it — an instance of the chapter's central pattern, in which a placental function becomes a newborn problem the moment the cord is cut.

Q: A term newborn becomes hypothermic and has an increased work of breathing. Which hepatic store is the major source of energy under this stress, and how large is it?
A) Glycogen, which at term is stored at twice adult levels
B) Iron, which at term is stored at twice adult levels
C) Glycogen, which at term is stored at half adult levels
D) Brown fat, which is stored in the liver beginning at week 9
Answer: A
Rationale: The fetus stores glycogen in the liver beginning at week 9 or 10, and "at term, glycogen stores are twice those of an adult. Glycogen is the major source of energy for the fetus and neonate stressed by intrauterine hypoxia, extrauterine loss of the maternal glucose supply, the work of breathing, or cold" (p. 247).
Trap: (D) names a real store laid down for exactly this purpose, but brown fat is deposited between 26 and 30 weeks in preparation for extrauterine cold stress and is not a hepatic glycogen store. The chapter names glycogen specifically as the energy source under these four stressors, and cold is one of the four.

Q: A parent asks when their exclusively breastfed infant might need supplemental iron. Which fact from the chapter is relevant?
A) If maternal intake was sufficient, the fetus stores enough iron to last 5 months after birth
B) Fetal iron stores are depleted within the first 2 weeks after birth
C) Iron cannot be stored by the fetus and must be supplied from birth
D) Fetal iron stores last approximately 2 years after birth
Answer: A
Rationale: "If the maternal intake is sufficient, the fetus can store enough iron to last 5 months after birth" (p. 247). Iron is also among the substances stored in the placenta for ready access to meet fetal needs.
Trap: (B) and (D) bracket the correct figure on both sides, which is how a half-remembered duration is caught. The clause that carries the clinical weight is the conditional one: the 5-month store assumes sufficient maternal intake, so maternal iron status during pregnancy determines when infant supplementation becomes necessary.

Q: A nurse is explaining why fetal renal failure may not be apparent before birth. Which statement is accurate?
A) The placenta acts as the organ of excretion and maintains fetal water and electrolyte balance, so the fetus does not need functioning kidneys in utero, but the kidneys are required immediately at birth
B) The fetal kidneys are the primary organ of excretion from the end of the first trimester onward
C) Fetal renal failure always presents as hydramnios before birth
D) The newborn kidney concentrates urine as effectively as an adult kidney, so the transition is uneventful
Answer: A
Rationale: "Because the placenta acts as the organ of excretion and maintains fetal water and electrolyte balance, the fetus does not need functioning kidneys while in utero. However, at birth, the kidneys are required immediately for excretory and acid-base regulatory functions." At term "the GFR is low, and the kidneys lack the ability to concentrate urine," making the newborn "more susceptible to both overhydration and dehydration" (p. 247).
Trap: (C) inverts the amniotic fluid finding: absent or reduced fetal urine output produces oligohydramnios, not hydramnios, so renal failure that does declare itself before birth does so as too little fluid. (D) contradicts the stated limitation that makes newborn fluid management delicate.

Topic: multifetal pregnancy

Q: A patient is told she is carrying dizygotic twins. Which statement about the membranes and placentas is always true?
A) There are always two amnions, two chorions, and two placentas that may be fused
B) There is always one chorion and one placenta shared by both fetuses
C) The membrane arrangement depends on when the zygote divided
D) There is always one amnion shared by both fetuses
Answer: A
Rationale: Dizygotic twins arise when multiple mature ova are "fertilized by separate sperm," and there "are always two amnions, two chorions, and two placentas that may be fused" (p. 248). They can be the same sex or different sexes and are genetically "no more alike than siblings born at different times."
Trap: (C) applies the monozygotic rule to the dizygotic case. Membrane arrangement is a timing question only when a single zygote divides; with two separate zygotes there was never a shared structure to divide, so the arrangement is fixed rather than variable.

Q: A patient carrying monozygotic twins asks why some identical twins share a placenta and others do not. Which explanation is accurate?
A) The arrangement depends on when division occurs: soon after fertilization gives two amnions, two chorions, and two placentas, while division between 4 and 8 days, the most common timing, gives two amnions, one chorion, and one placenta
B) All monozygotic twins share one placenta and one chorion regardless of timing
C) The arrangement depends on the sex of the fetuses
D) Division after the eighth day gives two separate placentas with no shared membranes
Answer: A
Rationale: Division "soon after fertilization" produces "two embryos, two amnions, two chorions, and two placentas that can be fused"; division "between 4 and 8 days after fertilization," which is most common, produces "two embryos, two amnions, one chorion, and one placenta"; division after the eighth day produces "two embryos within a common amnion and a common chorion with one placenta," which "often causes circulatory problems because the umbilical cords may tangle together" (p. 249). Division 13 days or later produces conjoined twins.
Trap: (D) inverts the latest arrangement, and it inverts the one with the highest stakes — division after day 8 gives the most sharing, not the least, and that is the arrangement carrying cord-entanglement risk.

Q: A nurse is reviewing risk factors for twinning. Select all the statements that are accurate.
A) Dizygotic twinning occurs in families and increases with maternal age, peaking at 37 years
B) Dizygotic twinning can follow discontinuation of oral contraceptives as follicle-stimulating hormone levels rebound
C) Monozygotic twinning has no association with race, heredity, maternal age, or parity
D) Assisted reproductive technology increases the incidence of monozygotic twinning by more than tenfold
E) Monozygotic twinning runs in families in the same way dizygotic twinning does
Answer: A, B, C, D
Rationale: Dizygotic twinning "occurs in families," is more common among Black women than White women and least common among Asian women, and increases "with maternal age, peaking at 37 years; with parity; with maternal family history of twins; with maternal obesity; and with the use of assisted reproductive technology," as well as after "discontinuation of oral contraceptives as FSH levels rebound." Monozygotic twinning has "no association with race, heredity, maternal age, or parity," though assisted reproductive technology "increases the incidence ... by more than tenfold" (pp. 248–249).
Trap: (E) is the single wrong option, and it is the clause the fact bank singles out — running in families belongs only to dizygotic twinning, which makes sense mechanically, since what is inherited is the tendency to release more than one ovum. Assisted reproductive technology is the one risk factor that raises both, which is why it cannot be used to tell the two types apart.
