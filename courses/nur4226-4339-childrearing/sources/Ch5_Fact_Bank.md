## 1. What pain does besides hurt (pp. 137–138)

**Pain is not merely uncomfortable — it is harmful.** "Pain triggers a number of physiologic stress responses in the body, and these may lead to negative consequences involving multiple systems. **Long-term amplification of the sympathetic nervous system hinders healing.** Unrelieved pain, whether from trauma, surgery, or disease, may prolong stress response and adversely affect a child's recovery."

**Box 5.1 — Consequences of untreated pain in premature neonates**

| Acute consequences | Potential long-term consequences |
|---|---|
| Periventricular-intraventricular hemorrhage; increased chemical and hormone release; breakdown of fat and carbohydrate stores; prolonged hyperglycemia; higher morbidity; memory of painful events; hypersensitivity to pain; prolonged response to pain; inappropriate response to nonnoxious stimuli; lower pain threshold | Higher somatic complaints of unknown origin; greater physiologic and behavioral responses to pain; increased prevalence of neurologic deficits; psychosocial problems; neurobehavioral disorders; cognitive deficits; learning disorders; poor motor performance; behavioral problems; attention deficits; poor adaptive behavior; inability to cope with novel situations; problems with impulsivity and social control; learning deficits; emotional temperament changes in infancy or childhood; **accentuated hormonal stress responses in adult life** |

**The wind-up phenomenon** explains why this compounds: after noxious stimuli, spinal cord excitability is altered so that "normal nonpainful stimuli, such as routine nursing care and handling, [may] be perceived as noxious stimuli." **"Neonates are particularly vulnerable to acute pain responses. They have more generalized spinal reflex responses and lower thresholds to stimuli than older children."**

And the forward consequence: **"Poorly controlled acute pain also predisposes patients to chronic pain syndromes."** "When pain is unrelieved, sensory input from injured tissues causes recruitment of peripheral and spinal cord neurons to amplify pain responses even to nonpainful stimuli. **Severe and sustained pain is more difficult to control.**"

**Pain can exist purely in the brain and is still real:** "Once these pain tracks are created in the brain, it is possible for pain to exist purely in the brain. This pain, however, is real and **it feels the way the child says it feels** — regardless of the location of origin."

**Routine care hurts.** "Nurses may find it easy to overlook certain painful experiences as part of normal care and perform invasive procedures without any attempts to minimize pain. **Something as minor and ordinary as bandage removal, nasogastric tube placement, or urinary catheter placement is very painful to children.**" Premature infants "are particularly vulnerable to repeated acute pain episodes from routine and frequent procedures."

## 2. Measuring pain: the governing principles (p. 143)

Three families of tool exist: **observational pain measures, self-report rating scales, and multidimensional pain assessment tools.** Match the tool to "the child's developmental level and condition."

**Assessment is more than a number.** "Pain assessment should be more thorough than merely obtaining a pain rating. Understanding the **location, frequency, duration, and precipitating, aggravating, and alleviating factors** of the pain experienced by the child is also essential." Also assess pain's effect on "sleep, emotional function (mood, anxiety, depression), physical recovery (acute pain) or physical functioning, disability (chronic pain), role functioning (chronic pain), and satisfaction with pain treatment."

**Use the same tool consistently.** "Consistent use of the same measurement tools is necessary to standardize communication related to pain and evaluate the effectiveness of pain management strategies."

**Why scores are not interchangeable.** "A pain score of 5 may mean a lot of pain (if a 0 to 5 scale is used) or very little (if a 0 to 100 scale is used)." And the deeper reason a common metric fails: **"pain assessment tools measure different pain expressions; for example, self-report measures patients' perceived pain intensity, whereas observational pain measures are used to quantify patients' behavioral and physiologic responses to pain."**

## 3. Observational pain measures (pp. 143–144)

**When they are used:** "for children from infancy until they are able to provide a self-report of pain and distinguish increments of pain intensity, **typically around 3 to 4 years of age**." They "require a trained observer to watch and record children's behaviors, such as vocalization, facial expression, and body movements."

**Facial expression is the key indicator.** "**Facial expression is the most consistent and specific indicator of pain in infants**," evaluated through features such as "eye squeeze, brow bulge, open mouth, and taut tongue."

**Their limits, which are exactly what distractors exploit:**
- "discriminating between pain behaviors and reactions to other sources of distress, such as hunger, anxiety, or other types of discomfort, is not always easy."
- **"Observational pain measures are most reliable when used to measure short, sharp procedural pain, such as pain due to heel lance or injections. They are less reliable when measuring acute pain and not reliable or valid for assessing chronic pain or pain in older children."**
- **"Pain scores on observational measures do not correlate with children's own reports of pain intensity."**

### Special populations (pp. 144–145)

**Preterm infants.** "Early pain experiences greatly influence the developing nervous system with persistent long-term effects." Evaluation is "based on gestational age, state, condition, previous response to pain, physiologic changes, and validated observational tools."

Two facts that generate priority items:
- **"Preterm infants in awake or alert states demonstrate a more robust reaction to painful stimuli than infants in sleep states. Preterm infants' responses to pain may be behaviorally blunted or absent."**
- **"an infant receiving a muscle-paralyzing agent (vecuronium) is incapable of a behavioral or visible pain response; however, there is ample evidence that such infants are neurologically capable of feeling pain."**

The rule that follows: **"it is imperative to presume that pain exists in all situations that are usually considered painful for adults and children, even in the absence of behavioral or physiologic signs."**

**Cognitively impaired children.** They "may not be able to express pain through behaviors or self-report methods validated for developmentally similar peers," and parents report their pain "was not effectively managed." The most frequent parent-reported pain behaviors: "crying; being less active; seeking comfort; moaning; not cooperating; being irritable; being stiff, spastic, tense, or rigid; sleeping less; being difficult to satisfy or pacify; flinching or moving body part away; and being agitated or fidgety." Parents also reported that **ordinary daily activities were painful**: "assisted stretching and walking, independent standing, toileting, putting on splints, occupational therapy, range of motion, and physical therapy."

Three validated tools: **rFLACC** (Revised FLACC), **NCCPC-r** (Non-communicating Children's Pain Checklist–Revised), and **PPP** (Paediatric Pain Profile). **"When using the rFLACC, nurses must include parent-reported pain behaviors to customize the tool to use with individual children."**

**Children postanesthesia.** "nurses often use observational pain assessment tools and then transition to self-report tools when the child is more alert. **The only tool found reliable and valid for pain assessment of children 2 months to 7 years of age recovering in the postanesthesia care unit is the FLACC tool.**"

**Children in the PICU.** "It is challenging to differentiate a child's pain from distress, anxiety, and agitation due to physiologic compromise." The **COMFORT-B** is "one of the most commonly used pain scales to assess critically ill ventilated children who are nonverbal due to sedation or their condition" and "is particularly helpful in children who are mechanically ventilated." There are six COMFORT scales: "COMFORT, COMFORT plus crying, COMFORT-behavior, COMFORT-modified, COMFORT-adapted, and COMFORT-neo."

**Children with autism spectrum disorder** (p. 146). Because cognitive ability varies widely, "self-report measures should be used whenever possible. Ask the verbal child with ASD where the pain is located and to describe how it feels." Nonverbal individuals with ASD "may have an escalation of repeated self-stimulating activities (i.e., rocking or hand flapping) when in pain."

## 4. Self-report pain rating scales (pp. 145–147)

**The single most important sentence in the chapter:** **"Self-report is the single most reliable indicator of pain and must be accepted and respected by nurses as the most important pain assessment data available."**

**Age boundaries.** Self-report measures "are most often used for children older than 3 to 4 years, **but even 2-year-olds can report pain**." The cognitive skills of "measurement, classification, and seriation (the ability to accurately place items in rank order), become apparent between **7 and 10 years of age**. Therefore most school-age children have the cognitive ability required to use a numeric rating scale."

**Why faces scales exist.** "Although children at 3 or 4 years of age are able to self-report pain, they do not have the cognitive ability to understand rank order." The ability to discriminate degrees of pain in facial expressions "appears to be reasonably established by 3 years old."

**The affect trap, which is a real assessment error:** "cognitive characteristics of the preoperational stage influence their ability to separate feelings of pain and mood. It has been suggested that **smiling faces on pain assessment scales may lead younger children to rate the affective domain of pain**; or, in other words, rate their feelings about having pain rather than report the intensity of their physical pain." The mitigation: "Simple, concrete anchor words, such as the script originally used to validate the tool, should be used."

**In practice** (p. 147): "The numeric rating scales (NRSs), such as the 0 to 10 scale, are the most widely used in clinical practice because they are easy to use." The NRS "is also called the verbal numeric scale (VNS)." The VAS "is classically a 10-centimeter line."

**Beyond intensity:** "Pain assessment in the patient who can provide self-report should not be limited to pain intensity. Other dimensions (such as, pain quality, location, and spatial distribution) may change without a change in pain intensity." Pain drawings help **children over 8 years of age** describe location.

**Adolescent Pediatric Pain Tool (APPT)** — "modeled after the McGill Pain Questionnaire," a "multidimensional pain measurement instrument used with children and adolescents to assess **pain location, intensity, and quality**." It has "an anterior and posterior body outline on one side and a 100-mm word-graphing rating scale with a pain descriptor on the other side." Scoring: a template overlay with **43 body areas** counts pervasiveness; the word-graphic scale is measured in millimeters; the descriptor list totals **0 to 56** words across three categories — **evaluative (0–8), sensory (0–37), and affective (0–11)**.

**Culture** (p. 146): "Expression of pain can be greatly affected by communication barriers... Some cultures train children to withhold pain expression whereas others encourage pain expression." *Cultural Considerations:* among Spanish-speaking children with cancer, "41% of the patients were experiencing pain. Some were experiencing moderate to severe pain and **did not receive medications because they did not report their pain**."

## 5. Chronic and recurrent pain assessment (pp. 146–148)

**Why acute tools fail here:** "Tools that measure acute pain intensity may not be sensitive to the subtle changes and slow resolution characteristic of chronic and recurrent pain because pain expression becomes more complex."

| Tool | What it does |
|---|---|
| **Pediatric Pain Questionnaire (PPQ)** | Eight areas: "pain history, pain language, the colors the child associates with pain, emotions the child experiences, the worst pain experiences, the way the child copes with pain, the positive aspects of pain, and the location of the current pain." Three components: VASs, color-coded rating scales, and verbal descriptors. **"The child, parent, and physician each complete the form separately"** |
| **Functional Disability Inventory (FDI)** | "a more comprehensive evaluation of the influence of pain on physical functioning"; assesses "the child's ability to perform everyday physical activities" |
| **PedsQL** | For the child **younger than 7 years**; parent and child versions; "physical, emotional, social, and academic functioning as they relate to the child's pain" |
| **PedMIDAS** | Validated for role functioning; "specifically designed to evaluate pain caused by migraines" |
| **Pain diaries** | Used for recurrent or chronic pain; "Diary studies have included children as young as 6 years old." Paper diaries suffer "poor compliance, missing data, hoarding of responses, and back and forward filling," which electronic diaries address |

**Screen for mood too:** "Chronic and recurrent types of pain often occur with some level of depression and anxiety in children." The Children's Depression Inventory and the Revised Child Anxiety and Depression Scale "may be used together to better measure negative affect."

## 6. Prevention and treatment (pp. 148–150)

**Goals of a pain treatment program:** "The ultimate goal in the experience of any pain is **to improve function, quality of life, and a return to daily activities**," emphasizing "participation in normal activities, including daily school attendance, sports or physical activities, social life, and adequate sleep." A multidisciplinary approach is used, and "The primary care provider must first rule out and/or treat any systemic disease."

**Why nurses' knowledge is not enough:** "Nurses and other clinicians may hold onto outdated or incorrect beliefs and attitudes about pain or may work in a culture that does not support good pain management practices."

**Biobehavioral interventions.** The naming matters: the term "is preferred over nonpharmacologic because it is a term that reflects the mechanism of action. The term nonpharmacologic reflects the lack of pharmaceutical treatment as though medications are the gold standard." **"Sometimes biobehavioral interventions are as effective as, or even more effective than, pharmacologic interventions."** The preferred plan is **multimodal**: "By targeting different mechanism of pain, medications combined with biobehavioral interventions are more effective at relieving pain."

**Mechanism:** "Most biobehavioral interventions prevent and treat pain by **interrupting the pain, fear, anxiety, and stress cycle**." They "can help reduce pain perception, make pain more tolerable, decrease anxiety, and **enhance the effectiveness of analgesics or reduce the dosage required**," with "few if any side effects."

**Table 5.6 — Classification of biobehavioral approaches** (five classes, plus physical treatments):

| Class | Examples |
|---|---|
| Biologically based | "Foods, special diets, herbal or plant preparations, vitamins, other supplements" |
| Manipulative treatments | "Physical therapy, chiropractic, osteopathy, massage" |
| Energy based | "TENS, Reiki, bioelectric or magnetic treatments, pulsed fields, alternating and direct currents" |
| Mind-body techniques | "Distraction, guided imagery, hypnosis, relaxation, biofeedback, yoga, spiritual healing/prayer" |
| Alternative medical systems | "Homeopathy; naturopathy; ayurvedic; traditional Chinese medicine, including acupuncture and moxibustion" |
| Physical treatments | "Heat, ice" |

**Teaching the strategy — the timing rule:** **"Children should learn to use a specific strategy before pain occurs or before it becomes severe."** Even after learning it, "children often need help using it during a painful procedure," and it can be used afterward so "the child [has] a chance to recover, feel mastery, and cope more effectively."

**Choosing one:** if the child cannot name a familiar coping technique, describe several and let the child select. **"Parents should be involved in the selection process; they may be familiar with the child's usual coping style."** If the parent cannot assist, "other appropriate persons may include a grandparent, older sibling, nurse, or child-life specialist."

**Safety around supplements:** "About 10% of infants are given teas or botanical supplements by their parents, usually for fussiness or stomach problems," and "About 23,000 visits to the emergency department each year are associated with supplement use." Well-researched, low-risk options: "biofeedback, guided imagery, hypnosis, mindfulness, and yoga."

## 7. Pharmacologic management (pp. 150–152)

**WHO principles:** "Using a two-step strategy; Dosing at regular intervals; Using the appropriate route of administration; Adapting treatment to the individual child."

**The two-step approach replaced the old stepladder.** "For children older than 3 months of age **the first step is to administer a nonopioid analgesic. Opioids are started when pain is severe or unrelieved by nonopioids. Morphine is the medicine of choice for the second step.**"

**Nonopioids.** "NSAIDs are known for their **antipyretic, antiinflammatory, and analgesic** actions. **Acetaminophen has antipyretic and analgesic actions, but no antiinflammatory action.**" Nonopioids "are usually the first analgesics for pain related to tissue injury, also known as nociceptive pain."

| Drug | Dosing as given |
|---|---|
| Acetaminophen (Tylenol) | "10-15 mg/kg/dose q 4-6 h PO **not to exceed five doses in 24 h or 75 mg/kg/day, or 3-4000 mg/day**"; nonprescription |
| Ibuprofen (children's Motrin/Advil) | "Children **>6 months old**: 5-10 mg/kg/dose every 6-8 hours. Maximum dose 40 mg/kg/day or 3200 mg/day" |
| Choline magnesium trisalicylate (Trilisate) | "25 mg/kg every 12 hours PO. Maximum dose 3000 mg/day"; prescription |

**Codeine and CYP2D6** (p. 152). "Codeine was once a commonly used opioid in children. Children with **CYP2D6 allele combinations associated with poor metabolism** of codeine may get no analgesia, whereas children with combinations associated with **ultrarapid metabolism are at risk of morphine toxicity and death**." **"In 2017, the US Food and Drug Administration (FDA) issued a black box warning on the use of codeine in children because of reported deaths."** Genetic differences may explain variation in "an estimated 50% of currently available medications."

**Coanalgesics (adjuvants).** **"Benzodiazepines are frequently used to treat anxiety; however, these drugs do not provide analgesia and should not be used as a substitute for analgesics. The concomitant use of opioids and sedatives, such as diazepam, is associated with increased risk of death."** Coanalgesics used for chronic and, increasingly, acute pain: "Tricyclic antidepressants (e.g., amitriptyline, imipramine), antiepileptics (e.g., gabapentin, pregabalin), steroids, α2-agonists, ketamine, and intravenous (IV) lidocaine."

Amitriptyline "Provides analgesia by blocking reuptake of serotonin and norepinephrine"; note "**Analgesic effects seen earlier than antidepressant effects**," and use nortriptyline "if patient is oversedated." Gabapentin side effects: "sedation, ataxia, nystagmus, dizziness."

Drugs for opioid side effects: "peristaltics for constipation, antiemetics for nausea and vomiting, opioid antagonists for itching, and stimulants for sedation."

**Topical and transdermal** (p. 157). **EMLA** must be "administered at least an hour before the painful intervention" and used "with caution in neonates." Intradermal lidocaine stings, but "the use of **buffered lidocaine with sodium bicarbonate** reduces the stinging sensation." **The fentanyl transdermal patch "is contraindicated for acute pain management"** but is FDA approved "for use in opioid-tolerant children over 12 years of age who require chronic continuous opioid treatment," lasting 72 hours per patch.

## 8. Monitoring side effects, tolerance, and withdrawal (pp. 157–159)

**Respiratory depression "is rare, but is the most serious adverse effect from analgesics,"** and it "progresses in a predictable pattern":

> "The patient **first becomes sedated and unarousable**. Then depth of respirations decreases and rate of respirations increases. End-tidal CO2 increases, oxygen saturation decreases, and respiratory rate decreases and then ceases."

**The nursing implication:** **"Sedation from opioids is to be avoided and nurses must increase their monitoring vigilance while patients sleep."** Sedation precedes the respiratory changes, so it is the early warning, not a benign effect.

**Box 5.3 — Side effects of opioids and the signs of tolerance and withdrawal**

| General side effects | Signs of tolerance | Initial withdrawal | Later withdrawal |
|---|---|---|---|
| Nausea and vomiting; **miosis (may be sign of toxicity)**; constipation (possibly severe); sedation; euphoria; agitation; mental clouding; hallucinations; pruritus; orthostatic hypotension; respiratory depression; anaphylaxis (rare) | "Decreasing pain relief; Decreasing duration of pain relief" | **Lacrimation; rhinorrhea; yawning; sweating; restlessness** | Irritability; tremors; anorexia; dilated pupils; piloerection; nausea, vomiting; abdominal cramping, diarrhea; **seizure** |

**Managing the common ones** (p. 159):
- **Constipation:** "Prevention of constipation with peristaltic agents and laxatives is more effective than treatment once constipation occurs. **Dietary treatment, such as increased fiber and the use of stool softeners, is usually not sufficient.**" Encourage fluids and physical activity; "Gum chewing has also been used to effectively increase peristalsis."
- **Pruritus:** treated "with low-dose opioid agonists such as naloxone or nalbuphine." **"Diphenhydramine is often erroneously used to treat opioid-related pruritus; however, appreciable relief is the result of sedation rather than the antihistamine effect."** Ondansetron is also effective.
- Nausea, vomiting, pruritus, and sedation "usually subside after 2 days of regular opioid administration."

**Tolerance vs. dependence vs. addiction.** Tolerance is treated "by increasing the dose or decreasing the duration between doses." Physical dependence is "a normal, natural, physiologic state of 'neuroadaptation.'" Withdrawal shows as "neurologic excitability (irritability, tremors, increased motor tone, insomnia, seizures), gastrointestinal dysfunction (nausea, vomiting, diarrhea, abdominal cramps), and autonomic dysfunction (sweating, fever, chills, tachypnea, nasal congestion, rhinitis)."

**The prevention rule:** **"Withdrawal symptoms can be anticipated and prevented by weaning patients from opioids that were administered for more than 5 days."** The Withdrawal Assessment Tool–1 is used in children 7 months to 10 years old.

**Addiction teaching:** "Infants may be at risk for physical tolerance and physical dependence **but not psychologic dependence or addiction** when using opioids for short-term pain management. The use of opioid analgesics early in life has not been demonstrated to increase the risk for addiction later in life." But note the counterweight the chapter also states: "there is new evidence of increased risk of prescription opioid misuse in young adults who were treated with opioids as adolescents," and "Legitimately prescribed opioid use by the 12th grade is independently associated with increased risk of future opioid misuse as a young adult."

Counsel families "to secure opioids in locked medication safes or other locked cabinets" and to dispose of them promptly when no longer needed. **"Deaths from opioid misuse now outnumber deaths from motor vehicle crashes."**

## 9. Needlestick pain prevention (p. 159)

**"The most effective biobehavioral intervention to reduce needlestick pain in infants is breastfeeding."** Other effective methods: "the use of **24% sucrose or glucose** and **skin-to-skin care** in infants; **comfort holds** for toddlers and small children; and **deep breathing, distraction, hypnosis, and virtual reality** for children and adolescents."

An important distinction the chapter draws: there is strong evidence these strategies "are effective interventions to **manage distress and help children and adolescents cope** with needle pain, but there is no evidence these interventions **prevent, reduce, or relieve needle pain**." **"Local anesthetics are the most effective pharmacologic agents for preventing and reducing needlestick pain."**

**Box 5.4 — Evidence-based strategies to reduce needlestick pain**

| Area | Strategy as given |
|---|---|
| Injection technique | **"Avoid aspiration, use rapid injection technique"** |
| Vaccination order | "Administer the **least painful vaccine first** when administering multiple vaccines in one visit"; "Consider simultaneous vaccine administration if possible" |
| Positioning | **"Avoid supine position if possible"**; use a position of comfort at all ages; "use breastfeeding for small infants or at least skin-to-skin care" |
| Type of soothing | **"Caregivers and nurses should avoid verbal reassurance, empathy, and apology. Caregivers can be encouraged to use physical soothing and coaching"** |
| Topical/numbing/sucrose | "Topical numbing recommended in appropriate age groups"; **"Sucrose recommended in children under 6 months of age"** |
| Other | "Consider use of hypnosis or age-appropriate distraction" |

## 10. Trap notes

### Confusable pairs

- **Self-report vs. observational measures.** *Self-report* is "the single most reliable indicator of pain." *Observational* measures are the fallback until roughly 3 to 4 years, and their scores **do not correlate** with children's own intensity reports. A distractor that treats an observational score as more objective, and therefore more trustworthy, than a verbal child's own report inverts the chapter's central rule.
- **FLACC vs. COMFORT-B.** *Postanesthesia, 2 months to 7 years* → FLACC, the only tool validated there. *Critically ill, ventilated, nonverbal* → COMFORT-B. Score ranges differ too: FLACC 0–10, COMFORT-B 6–30 with pain considered above 17.
- **FLACC vs. rFLACC.** Plain FLACC for acute and postoperative pain; **revised** FLACC with revised descriptors, customized with parent-reported behaviors, for cognitive impairment.
- **Wong-Baker FACES vs. FPS-R.** Wong-Baker starts at a **smiling** face and is validated **from 3 years**; FPS-R starts at a **neutral** face and is for **5–17**. The starting face is the discriminator, and it matters because smiling faces may pull young children toward rating mood instead of pain intensity.
- **Oucher vs. FACES.** Oucher is **photographs plus a 0–100 vertical numeric scale**, for **3–13**, with ethnic versions. FACES is cartoon faces, from **3 years**. A child must be able to rank six geometric shapes to use Oucher's photographic scale.
- **NRS vs. VAS.** Both from about **8 years**. NRS is spoken or a numbered line and is "the most widely used in clinical practice"; VAS is a 10-cm line measured with a ruler and is the scale children **least prefer**.
- **Acetaminophen vs. NSAIDs.** Both antipyretic and analgesic; **only NSAIDs are antiinflammatory**. A stem asking for an antiinflammatory that names acetaminophen is wrong.
- **Tolerance vs. physical dependence vs. addiction.** Tolerance = needing more drug for the same effect. Physical dependence = neuroadaptation, so abrupt withdrawal produces symptoms. Neither is addiction, which is psychologic. Infants "may be at risk for physical tolerance and physical dependence but not psychologic dependence or addiction."
- **Complementary vs. alternative vs. integrative.** *Alternative* **replaces** standard care; *complementary* is used **together with** it; *integrative* is the deliberate integration of both.
- **Acute vs. chronic pain.** The cutoff is **3 months** across every chronic subtype in the definitions table.
- **Biobehavioral vs. nonpharmacologic.** Same interventions; the book prefers "biobehavioral" because it names a mechanism rather than an absence.

### Level, stage, and threshold swaps

- **Observational until 3 to 4 years; self-report from 3 to 4 years; numeric scales from 7 to 10 years** (when seriation appears) and practically **from 8**. But "even 2-year-olds can report pain."
- **Infant scale age ranges are in gestational weeks:** PIPP-R 25–40, NIPS 26–40, N-PASS 23–40, COMFORT-neo 24–42, CRIES 32–40. **Only PIPP-R and N-PASS adjust for gestational age.**
- **Two-step analgesic strategy applies to children older than 3 months**; nonopioid first, **morphine** as the second-step drug of choice.
- **Ibuprofen requires age over 6 months.** Acetaminophen max is "five doses in 24 h or 75 mg/kg/day."
- **Sucrose is recommended under 6 months of age** for needlestick pain.
- **Wean opioids given for more than 5 days** to prevent withdrawal.
- **Fentanyl patch: opioid-tolerant children over 12 years, chronic pain only** — never acute.
- **Pain diaries have been used in children as young as 6**; pain drawings from **over 8**; PedsQL for **under 7**.

### Plausible-but-adjacent facts

- **A quiet or still preterm infant is not a comfortable one.** Responses "may be behaviorally blunted or absent," and a pharmacologically paralyzed infant cannot show pain at all yet still feels it. Presume pain in any situation that would be painful for an adult.
- **A sleeping preterm infant reacts less than an awake one** — the state, not the pain, changed.
- **Sedation is the first sign of opioid-induced respiratory depression**, not a sign the pain is controlled. Respiratory *rate* initially **increases** as depth falls, before it slows and ceases.
- **Miosis may signal opioid toxicity**; dilated pupils appear in **later withdrawal**. Do not confuse the two directions.
- **Diphenhydramine does not truly treat opioid pruritus** — the relief is sedation. Low-dose naloxone or nalbuphine does.
- **Benzodiazepines are not analgesics**, and combining them with opioids raises the risk of death.
- **Verbal reassurance, empathy, and apology are listed as things to avoid** during needlesticks; physical soothing and coaching are what help. This is counterintuitive and therefore heavily tested.
- **Avoid aspiration and inject rapidly** during vaccination; give the least painful vaccine first.
- **Avoid the supine position** for needlesticks.
- **Breastfeeding beats breast milk** for needlestick pain — the act, not the substance.
- **Observational tools are invalid for chronic pain** and for older children, however convenient.
- **Distraction and virtual reality help children cope but are not shown to reduce needle pain itself**; local anesthetics do that.
- **Routine care is painful:** bandage removal, NG tube placement, and urinary catheter placement are named explicitly.

### Priority and first-action items

- **Believe the child.** Self-report outranks behavioral observation whenever the child can give one.
- **When a child cannot self-report, presume pain exists** in any situation ordinarily considered painful.
- **Increase monitoring while an opioid-treated patient sleeps**; treat unarousable sedation as an emergency, not as comfort.
- **Prevent constipation before it occurs** rather than treating it after.
- **Teach the coping strategy before the pain**, not during it.
- **Nonopioid first for a child over 3 months**, escalating to morphine when pain is severe or unrelieved.
