**The chapter has two halves that are tested very differently.** Pages 15 through 28 are
**family structure, parenting, and discipline** — concrete, enumerable, and full of named
categories that map cleanly onto answer options. Pages 28 through 38 are **sociocultural

consequences**, **time-out mechanics**, **the nine principles of implementing discipline**,

## 1. General concepts: the definition of family

**The chapter opens by refusing a single definition** (p. 15): "The term family has been
defined in many different ways according to the individual's own frame of reference, values,
or discipline. **There is no universal definition of family; a family is what an individual
considers it to be.**"

### How each discipline defines it (p. 15)

| Discipline | The chapter's words |
|---|---|
| Biology | "describes the family as fulfilling the biologic function of perpetuation of the species" |
| Psychology | "emphasizes the interpersonal aspects of the family and its responsibility for personality development" |
| Economics | "views the family as a productive unit providing for material needs" |
| Sociology | "depicts the family as a social unit interacting with the larger society, creating the context within which cultural values and identity are formed" |

### The three relationship types

"The most common type of relationships are **consanguineous (blood relationships), affinal
(marital relationships), and family of origin (family unit a person is born into).**" (p. 15)

> **Trap note — three relationship terms, three one-word definitions.** Consanguineous is
> blood, affinal is marriage, family of origin is birth (p. 15). **The two Latin terms are
> the distractor pair**, and the mnemonic is in the words: *sanguine* is blood, *affinity*
> is the tie you choose.

### How the definition has broadened

| Era | The chapter's words | p. |
|---|---|---|
| Earlier | "Earlier definitions of family emphasized that family members were related by **legal ties or genetic relationships and lived in the same household with specific roles.**" | 15 |
| Later | "Later definitions have been broadened to reflect both **structural and functional changes.**" | 15 |
| The working definition | "an institution in which individuals, related through biology or enduring commitments and representing similar or different generations and genders, participate in roles involving **mutual socialization, nurturance, and emotional commitment**" | 15 |

**And the term that absorbs the newer forms** (p. 15): "Considerable controversy has surrounded
the newer concepts of family, such as communal families, single-parent families, and
homosexual families. **To accommodate these and other varieties of family styles, the
descriptive term household is frequently used.**"

**Nursing Alert** (p. 15): "**The nurse's knowledge and the sensitivity with which he or she
assesses a household will determine the types of interventions that are appropriate to
support family members.**"

**Why it matters clinically** (p. 15): "**Family structure and dynamics can have an enduring
influence on a child, affecting the child's health and well-being.**" Therefore "nurses must
be aware of **the functions of the family, various types of family structures, and theories
that provide a foundation for understanding the changes within a family and for directing
family-oriented interventions.**"

> **Trap note — the definition question has no single right answer, and that is the
> answer.** "a family is what an individual considers it to be" (p. 15). **A distractor
> supplies a restrictive definition requiring legal ties, shared residence, or biologic
> relationship** — which the chapter explicitly labels as the *earlier* definition.

## 2. Family theories, and Table 2.1

**What a family theory is for** (p. 15): "A family theory can be used to **describe families
and how the family unit responds to events both within and outside the family.** Each family
theory makes assumptions about the family and has inherent strengths and limitations."

**And how nurses actually use them** (p. 15): "**Most nurses use a combination of theories in
their work with children and families.**"

**The three named** (p. 15): "family systems theory, family stress theory, and developmental
theory."

> theory cannot establish cause and effect *because* of circular causality; developmental
> theory struggles with stepfamilies *because* it marks stages by the oldest child's age

> **Trap note — "any event, even if positive, can be stressful." ** Family stress theory's
> first assumption (p. 15). A distractor restricts stressors to negative events. **The
> chapter names parenthood as a predictable stressor** (p. 17), and Table 2.1 lists
> "transition to parenthood" first among its applications.

## 3. Family systems theory

**Its parent theory** (p. 15): general systems theory "expanded scientific thought from **a
simplistic view of direct cause and effect (A causes B) to a more complex and interrelated
theory (A influences B, but B also affects A).**"

**The core mechanism** (p. 15): "The emphasis is on the interaction between the members; **a
change in one family member creates a change in other members, which in turn results in a new
change in the original member.**"

**And the consequence for who the patient is** (p. 15): "**a problem or dysfunction does not
lie in any one member but rather in the type of interactions used by the family.** Because the
interactions, not the individual members, are viewed as the source of the problem, **the
family becomes the patient and the focus of care.**"

**The two clinical examples** (pp. 15–16): "**nonorganic failure to thrive and child abuse.**"
(p. 15) In these, "the problem does not rest" (p. 15) "**solely with the parent or child but
with the type of interactions between the parent and the child and the factors that affect
their relationship.**" (p. 16)

> **Trap note — the family is the patient.** This is the theory's signature consequence
> (p. 15), and the two examples the chapter picks are ones a reader would instinctively
> assign to a single culpable person. **In a failure-to-thrive or abuse scenario framed by
> systems theory, the assessment target is the interaction, not the individual.**

### The whole is more than its members

**The worked arithmetic** (p. 16): "a household of parents and one child consists of **not only
three individuals but also four interactive units.** These units include **three dyads (the
marital relationship, the mother-child relationship, and the father-child relationship) and a
triangle (the mother-father-child relationship).**"

**The ecologic picture** (p. 16): "the family system functions within a larger system, with
**the family dyads in the center of a circle surrounded by the extended family, the
subculture, and the culture, with the larger society at the periphery.**"

> **Trap note — three people, four units.** The arithmetic is the item (p. 16): **three
> dyads plus one triangle.** A distractor says three, or counts only the parent-child pairs.

### Bowen, feedback, and boundaries

| Concept | The chapter's words | p. |
|---|---|---|
| Bowen's key to health | "emphasizes that **the key to healthy family function is the members' ability to distinguish themselves from one another both emotionally and intellectually**" | 16 |
| Adaptability | "The family unit has a high level of adaptability." | 16 |
| How change happens | "change occurs by **altering the interaction or feedback messages that perpetuate disruptive behavior**" | 16 |
| Feedback | "processes in the family that help identify strengths and needs and determine how well goals are accomplished" | 16 |
| The two directions | "**Positive feedback initiates change; negative feedback resists change.**" | 16 |
| Where change can start | "When the family system is disrupted, **change can occur at any point in the system.**" | 16 |
| Boundary | "**a major factor that influences a family's adaptability is its boundary, an imaginary line that exists between the family and its environment**" | 16 |

**Open versus closed boundaries** (p. 16): "one family has the capacity to **reach out for
help**, whereas another **considers help threatening.**"

**And the nursing consequence** (p. 16): "**Families with open boundaries may demonstrate a
greater receptivity to interventions, whereas families demonstrating closed boundaries often
require increased sensitivity and skill on the part of the nurse to gain their trust and
acceptance.**"

**What the nurse assesses** (p. 16): "the family's ability to **accept new ideas, information,
resources, and opportunities and to plan strategies.**"

> **Trap note — positive feedback initiates change.** In everyday speech positive feedback
> means praise; in systems theory it means **change-initiating** (p. 16), and negative
> feedback **resists** change. A distractor maps them onto approval and criticism.

> **Trap note — a closed-boundary family is not a non-compliant family.** The chapter's
> instruction is that they
> "require increased sensitivity and skill on the part of the nurse to gain their trust" (p. 16).
> **The intervention is relational, not educational.**

## 4. Family stress theory

**What it explains** (p. 16): "how families react to stressful events and **suggests factors
that promote adaptation to stress.**"

**Predictable and unpredictable stressors** (p. 17): "including those that are **predictable
(e.g., parenthood)** and those that are **unpredictable (e.g., illness, unemployment).**"

**Cumulative and simultaneous** (p. 17): "These stressors are **cumulative, involving
simultaneous demands from work, family, and community life.**"

**The threshold, with its time window** (p. 17): "**Too many stressful events occurring within
a relatively short period (usually 1 year) can overwhelm the family's ability to cope and
place it at risk for breakdown or physical and emotional health problems among its
members.**"

**What crisis is** (p. 17): "**When the family experiences too many stressors for it to cope
adequately, a state of crisis ensues.** For adaptation to occur, **a change in family structure
or interaction is necessary.**"

**The resiliency model** (p. 17): it "emphasizes that **the stressful situation is not
necessarily pathologic or detrimental to the family but demonstrates that the family needs to
make fundamental structural or systemic changes to adapt to the situation.**"

> **Trap note — the one-year window.** usually 1 year (p. 17) is the chapter's own
> quantification of cumulative stress. **A distractor omits the window or shortens it to
> weeks.**

> **Trap note — stress is not pathology.** The resiliency model's point (p. 17). A
> distractor treats a stressed family as dysfunctional. **The chapter's framing is that the
> family needs structural change, not that it is failing.**

personalities that interacts with the larger cultural social system.**"

**And the systems echo** (p. 17): "**As an interrelated system, the family does not have
changes in one part without a series of changes in other parts.**"

### The stage marker

**The rule** (p. 17): "This theory is based on the predictable changes in the family's
structure, function, and roles, **with the age of the oldest child as the marker for stage
transition.**"

**And the transition into stage II** (p. 17): "**The arrival of the first child marks the
transition from stage I to stage II.**"

**Two levels of tasks at once** (p. 17): "In every stage the family faces certain developmental
tasks. At the same time, **each family member must achieve individual developmental tasks as
part of each family life cycle stage.**"

**The application** (p. 17): "the nurse can assess **how well new parents are accomplishing the
individual and family developmental tasks associated with transition to parenthood.**"

**And the acknowledged gap** (p. 17): "**New applications should emerge as more is learned
about developmental stages for nonnuclear and nontraditional families.**"

> **Trap note — the marker is the oldest child's age, not the youngest and not the
> parents'.** Stated twice, once in Table 2.1's limitations and once in the narrative
> (p. 17). **This is also why the theory struggles with stepfamilies**, and it is the
> likeliest single item from this section.

> **Trap note — the eight stages are about the FAMILY, not the child.** This is exactly what
> the chapter's own NCLEX question 1 tests (p. 38): the keyed options describe **stages
> families progress through in adulthood** and **the journey a couple takes as their
> children mature**, while the distractors describe individual moral development and
> stage-appropriate discipline. **Duvall's stages are a marital and family life cycle, and
> they continue after the children leave.**

## 6. Family nursing interventions, and Box 2.2

**The premise** (p. 17): "In working with children, **the nurse must include family members in
their care plan.** Research confirms **parents' desire and expectation to participate in their
child's care.**"

**What assessment requires** (p. 17): "To discover **family dynamics, strengths, and
weaknesses**, a thorough family assessment is necessary."

**And the governing rule** (p. 17): "**The nurse's choice of interventions depends on the
theoretic family model that is used.**"

## 7. Family structure and function

**What alters composition** (p. 17): "When members are gained or lost through events such as
**marriage, divorce, birth, death, abandonment, or incarceration**, the family composition is
altered and **roles must be redefined or redistributed.**"

**The historical shift** (p. 17): "Traditionally, the family structure was either a nuclear or
extended family. In recent years family composition has assumed new configurations, with **the
single-parent family and blended family becoming prominent forms.**"

**And what drives the dominant pattern** (pp. 17–18): "The predominant structural pattern in
any society depends on **the mobility of families as they pursue economic goals and**" (p. 17)
"**as relationships change. It is not uncommon for children to belong to several different
family groups during their lifetime.**" (p. 18)

**Why it matters to nursing** (p. 18): "Nurses must be able to meet the needs of children from
many diverse family structures and home situations. **A family's structure affects the
direction of nursing care.**"

### The Census Bureau's four, and the others

"**The US Census Bureau uses four definitions for families: (1) the traditional nuclear family,
(2) the nuclear family, (3) the blended family or household, and (4) the extended family or
household.**" (p. 18)

**Beyond those four** (p. 18): "numerous other types of families have been defined, such as
**single-parent; binuclear; polygamous; communal; and lesbian, gay, bisexual, and transgender
(LGBT) families.**"

> **Trap note — exactly four Census definitions.** Traditional nuclear, nuclear, blended,
> extended (p. 18). **Single-parent is NOT one of the four**, despite being the most common
> alternative form — the chapter lists it among the "numerous other types." A select-all
> built on the Census set is testing that boundary.

> **Stepsiblings share no biologic parent; half-siblings share one.** That is the whole

### Extended families in practice

**The practical benefits** (p. 18): "Sharing residence with relatives also assists with **the

> **Trap note — in an extended family the decision-maker may not be the parent.** The
> chapter says the older relative holds the authority and consults the young parents

## 9. Family roles and relationships

**The framing** (p. 19): "Each individual has **a position, or status, in the family structure**
and plays **culturally and socially defined roles** in interactions within the family."

**What each family sets for itself** (p. 19): "Each family also has **its own traditions and

**When roles work, and when they do not** (p. 19): "**When family ties are strong, social

commitment. Conflicts arise when people do not fulfill their roles in ways that meet other

they choose not to meet them.**"

> **Trap note — two distinct reasons for role conflict.** **Unawareness or refusal**
> (p. 19). The nursing implication differs: unawareness is addressed by clarifying
> expectations, refusal is not. A distractor treats all role conflict as a communication
> problem.

## 11. Parenting styles

**The premise** (p. 20): "**A child's temperament heavily influences his or her response**, but
**styles of parenting have also been show to affect a child and lead to particular behavioral
responses.**"

**The three, named** (p. 20): "**Parenting styles are often classified as authoritarian,
permissive, or authoritative.**"

### The three styles, side by side

| | **Authoritarian** (pp. 20–21) | **Permissive** (p. 21) | **Authoritative** (p. 21) |
|---|---|---|---|
| Control | "try to control their children's behavior and attitudes through **unquestioned mandates**" (p. 20) | "**exert little or no control** over their children's actions" (p. 21) | "**Parental control is firm and consistent but tempered with encouragement, understanding, and security.**" (p. 21) |
| Rules | "They establish rules and" (p. 20) "**regulations or standards of conduct that they expect to be followed rigidly and unquestioningly.**" (p. 21) | "**They avoid imposing their own standards of conduct and allow their children to regulate their own activity as much as possible.**" | "They **direct their children's behavior and attitudes by emphasizing the reason for rules and negatively reinforcing deviations.**" |
| The message | "The message is: Do it because I say so." (p. 21) | "These parents consider themselves to be **resources for the children, not role models.**" | "They **respect the individuality of each child and allow the child to voice objections to family standards or regulations.**" |
| Discipline | "**Punishment need not be corporal but may be stern withdrawal of love and approval.**" | "They employ **lax, inconsistent discipline; do not set sensible limits;** and do not prevent the children from upsetting the home routine. **These parents rarely punish the children.**" | "**Control is focused on the issue, not on withdrawal of love or the fear of punishment.**" |
| If rules exist | — | "the parents **explain the underlying reason, elicit the children's opinions, and consult them in decision-making processes**" | — |
| The child produced | "**rigidly conforming behavior in the children, who tend to be sensitive, shy, self-conscious, retiring, and submissive.** They are more likely to be **courteous, loyal, honest, and dependable, but docile.**" | (not specified) | "children with **high self-esteem who are self-reliant, assertive, inquisitive, content, and highly interactive with other children**" |
| The conditional | "These behaviors are more typically observed **when close supervision and affection accompany parental authority.** **If not, this style of parenting may be associated with both defiant and antisocial behaviors.**" | — | "These parents foster **inner-directedness, a conscience that regulates behavior based on feelings of guilt or shame for wrongdoing, not on fear of being caught or punished.**" |

**Authoritative as a combination** (p. 21): "**Authoritative parents combine practices from both
of the previously described parenting styles.**"

> **Trap note — the authoritarian outcome is conditional, and the condition is the item.**
> Rigid but courteous children result **only when supervision and affection accompany the
> authority**; without affection, the same style
> "may be associated with both defiant and antisocial behaviors" (p. 21). **A distractor
> states one outcome unconditionally.**

> **Trap note — permissive parents DO explain reasons.**
> "If rules do exist, the parents explain the underlying reason, elicit the children's opinions, and consult them" (p. 21).
> So does the authoritative parent. **Explaining reasons does not distinguish them — control
> does.** The permissive parent explains and does not enforce; the authoritative parent
> explains and enforces firmly and consistently.

> **Trap note — inner-directedness versus fear of punishment.** The authoritative outcome is
> a conscience driven by
> "guilt or shame for wrongdoing, **not on fear of being caught or punished**" (p. 21),
> which is precisely what corporal punishment fails to produce:
> "when the parents are not around, children are likely to misbehave because they have not learned to behave well for their own sake" (p. 23).
> **The chapter closes that loop deliberately.**

### The chapter's cultural caveat

"**There are differing philosophies in regard to parenting. Childrearing is a culturally bound
phenomenon, and children are socialized to behave in ways that are important to their
family.**" (p. 21)

**And the instruction** (p. 21): "**When working with individual families, nurses should give
these differing styles equal respect.**"

> **Trap note — the chapter does not rank the styles for the nurse.** Authoritative produces
> the best-described outcomes, but the explicit nursing instruction is to give the styles
> equal respect (p. 21). **A distractor has the nurse tell a parent their style is wrong.**
> The teachable moments in this chapter are about *specific practices* — limits,
> consistency, timing — not about labeling a parent's style.

## 12. Limit setting and discipline

### The two terms

| Term | Definition as given | p. |
|---|---|---|
| **Discipline** | "In its broadest sense, discipline means **to teach** or refers to **a set of rules governing conduct.** In a narrower sense, it refers to **the action taken to enforce the rules after noncompliance.**" | 21 |
| **Limit setting** | "refers to **establishing the rules or guidelines for behavior**" | 21 |

**The relationship between them** (p. 21): "**The clearer the limits that are set and the more
consistently they are enforced, the less need there is for disciplinary action.**"

> **Trap note — limit setting comes first and reduces the need for discipline.** The two are
> not synonyms (p. 21): **limits are the rules, discipline is the enforcement.** A
> distractor uses them interchangeably.

### The five functions of limits and discipline (p. 21)

"Limit setting and discipline are **positive, necessary components of childrearing** and serve
several useful functions as they help children:"

| # | Function |
|---|---|
| 1 | "Test their limits of control" |
| 2 | "Achieve in areas appropriate for mastery at their level" |
| 3 | "Channel undesirable feelings into constructive activity" |
| 4 | "Protect themselves from danger" |
| 5 | "Learn socially acceptable behavior" |

**Why children need them** (p. 21): "**Children want and need limits. Unrestricted freedom is a
threat to their security and safety.** By testing the limits imposed on them, children learn
**the extent to which they can manipulate their environment and gain reassurance from knowing
that others are there to protect them from potential harm.**"

> **Trap note — testing limits is the point, not a problem.** The chapter lists
> "Test their limits of control" as the first *function* limits serve (p. 21), and explains
> that testing is how children gain reassurance. **A distractor treats limit-testing as
> misbehavior to be extinguished.**

## 13. Minimizing misbehavior

**The four reasons for misbehavior** (p. 21): "**attention, power, defiance, and a display of
inadequacy** (e.g., the child misses classes because of a fear that he or she is unable to do
the work)."

**Two more causes** (p. 21): "Children may also misbehave because **the rules are not clear or
consistently applied.**" And: "**Acting-out behavior, such as a temper tantrum, may represent
uncontrolled frustration, anger, depression, or pain.**"

**The best approach** (p. 21): "**The best approach is to structure interactions with children
to prevent or minimize unacceptable behavior.**"

> **Trap note — a tantrum may be pain.** The chapter names
> "uncontrolled frustration, anger, depression, or pain" (p. 21). **In a clinical setting,
> acting-out behavior warrants a pain assessment**, which a distractor treats purely as a
> behavioral problem.

| # | Strategy |
|---|---|
| 1 | "Set realistic goals for acceptable behavior and expected achievements." |
| 2 | "**Structure opportunities for small successes to lessen feelings of inadequacy.**" |
| 3 | "Praise children for desirable behavior with attention and verbal approval." |
| 4 | "Structure the environment to prevent unnecessary difficulties (e.g., place fragile objects in an inaccessible area)." |
| 5 | "**Set clear and reasonable rules; expect the same behavior regardless of the circumstances; if exceptions are made, clarify that the change is for one time only.**" |

| 7 | "**Review expected behavior before special or unusual events**, such as visiting a relative or having dinner in a restaurant." |
| 8 | "**Phrase requests for appropriate behavior positively**, such as Put the book down, rather than Don't touch the book." |
| 9 | "**Call attention to unacceptable behavior as soon as it begins; use distraction to change the behavior or offer alternatives to annoying actions**, such as exchanging a quiet toy for one that is too noisy." |
| 10 | "**Give advance notice or friendly reminders**, such as When the TV program is over, it is time for dinner or I'll give you to the count of three, and then we have to go." |

> **Trap note — item 15 answers item 1 of the misbehavior causes.** Misbehavior arises from
> **power**, and the last strategy is to **provide opportunities for power and control**
> (p. 21). **The chapter pairs the cause with the countermeasure**, and the same logic
> appears in Chapter 20, where a school-age child's uncooperativeness is read as a plea for
> control and power (Ch. 20, p. 612).

## 14. General guidelines for implementing discipline

**Why the principles matter** (p. 21): "**Regardless of the type of discipline used, certain
principles are essential to ensure the efficacy of the approach.** Many strategies, such as
behavior modification, can only be implemented effectively **when principles of consistency and
timing are followed.**"

**The cost of inconsistency** (p. 21): "**A pattern of intermittent or occasional enforcement of
limits actually prolongs the undesired behavior because children learn that if they are
persistent, the behavior is permitted eventually.**"

**The cost of delay** (p. 21): "**Delaying punishment weakens its intent**, and practices such
as telling the child, Wait until your father comes home **are not only ineffectual but also
convey negative messages about the other parent.**"

### Family-Centered Care: Implementing Discipline (p. 21) — complete, all nine

| # | Principle | As defined |
|---|---|---|
| 1 | **Consistency** | "Implement disciplinary action **exactly as agreed on and for each infraction.**" |
| 2 | **Timing** | "**Initiate discipline as soon as child misbehaves**; if delays are necessary, such as to avoid embarrassment, **verbally disapprove of the behavior and state that disciplinary action will be implemented.**" |
| 3 | **Commitment** | "**Follow through with the details of the discipline, such as timing of minutes; avoid distractions that may interfere with the plan**, such as telephone calls." |
| 4 | **Unity** | "**Make certain that all caregivers agree on the plan and are familiar with the details to prevent confusion and alliances between child and one parent.**" |
| 5 | **Flexibility** | "**Choose disciplinary strategies that are appropriate to child's age and temperament and the severity of the misbehavior.**" |
| 6 | **Planning** | "**Plan disciplinary strategies in advance and prepare child if feasible** (e.g., explain use of time-out); **for unexpected misbehavior, try to discipline when you are calm.**" |
| 7 | **Behavior orientation** | "**Always disapprove of the behavior, not the child**, with such statements as That was a wrong thing to do. I am unhappy when I see behavior like that." |
| 8 | **Privacy** | "**Administer discipline in private, especially with older children, who may feel ashamed in front of others.**" |
| 9 | **Termination** | "**After the discipline is administered, consider child as having a clean slate, and avoid bringing up the incident or lecturing.**" |

> **Trap note — nine named principles, and the two most tested are Timing and Termination.**
> Discipline starts **as soon as** the child misbehaves, and ends with a **clean slate** and
> no further lecturing (p. 21). **A distractor either defers the consequence to later or
> continues to reference the incident afterward.**

> **Trap note — the delay exception has a required substitute.** If delay is unavoidable,
> the parent must **verbally disapprove immediately and announce that the consequence is
> coming** (p. 21). **Silent deferral is not the option; announced deferral is.**

> **Trap note — Wait until your father comes home is doubly wrong.** It delays, and it
> "conveys negative messages about the other parent" (p. 21). This also violates Unity,
> which exists to prevent "alliances between child and one parent."

> **Trap note — behavior, not child.** Principle 7, and the Nursing Alert on p. 22 restates
> it with a technique: see §15.

## 15. Types of discipline

### Reasoning

**When it works** (p. 21): "Reasoning involves **explaining why an act is wrong** and is usually
appropriate for **older children, especially when moral issues are involved.**"

**Why it fails with young children** (p. 21): "**young children cannot be expected to see the
other side because of their egocentrism. Children in the preoperative stage of cognitive
development (toddlers and preschoolers) have a limited ability to distinguish between their
point of view and that of others.**"

**How children weaponize it** (p. 21): "Sometimes children use reasoning **as a way of gaining
attention.** For example, they may misbehave, **thinking the parents will give them a lengthy
explanation of the wrongdoing and knowing that negative attention is better than no
attention.**"

**And the scripted response** (p. 21): "parents should end the explanation by stating, **This is
the rule, and this is how I expect you to behave. I won't explain it any further.**"

**The hazard of reasoning** (p. 21): "**Unfortunately, reasoning is often combined with
scolding, which sometimes takes the form of shame or criticism.** For example, the parent may
state, You are a bad boy for hitting your brother. **Children take such remarks seriously and
personally, believing that they are bad.**"

### Nursing Alert: the I message (p. 22) — complete

"**When reprimanding children, focus only on the misbehavior, not on the child. Use of** I
**messages rather than** you **messages expresses personal feelings without accusation or
ridicule.** For example, an I message **attacks the behavior** — I am upset when Johnny is
punched; I don't like to see him hurt — **not the child.**"

> **Trap note — the I-message is the technique, and the chapter uses it twice.** Here for
> discipline (p. 22) and again for a child caught between divorcing parents, where the
> formula is given explicitly:
> "**I feel (state the feeling) when you (state the source). I would like it if you**" (p. 25).
> **A distractor phrases the correction as a you statement**, which the chapter labels
> accusation.

### Behavior modification

**The principle** (p. 22): "**Positive and negative reinforcement is the basis of behavior
modification theory — behavior that is rewarded will be repeated; behavior that is not rewarded
will be extinguished.**"

**Why it is preferred** (p. 22): "**Using rewards is a positive approach. By encouraging children
to behave in specified ways, the parents can decrease the tendency to misbehave.**"

| Age | Method | p. |
|---|---|---|
| Young children | "using **paper stars** is an effective method" | 22 |
| Older children | "the **token system** is appropriate, especially if a certain number of stars or tokens yields a special reward, such as a trip to the movies or a new book" | 22 |

**The four requirements of a reward system** (p. 22): "the parents must **explain expected
behaviors to the child** and **establish rewards that are reinforcing.** They should **use a
chart to record the stars or tokens** and **always give an earned reward promptly.**"

**And the addition that must never be dropped** (p. 22): "**Verbal approval should always
accompany extrinsic rewards.**"

> **Trap note — verbal approval accompanies the sticker; it does not replace it and is not
> optional.** always (p. 22). And rewards are given **promptly**. A distractor delays the
> reward to the end of the week or omits the verbal praise.

### Ignoring, and response burst

**The claim and the difficulty** (p. 22): "**Consistently ignoring behavior will eventually
extinguish or minimize the act. Although this approach sounds simple, it is difficult to
implement consistently.**"

**The failure mode** (p. 22): "**Parents frequently give in and resort to previous patterns of
discipline. Consequently, the behavior is actually reinforced because the child learns that
persistence gains parental attention.**"

**The four requirements for ignoring to work** (p. 22):

| # | Requirement |
|---|---|
| 1 | "understand the process" |
| 2 | "**record the undesired behavior before using ignoring** to determine whether a problem exists and to compare results after ignoring is begun" |
| 3 | "**determine whether parental attention acts as a reinforcer**" |
| 4 | "**be aware of response burst**" |

**Response burst, defined** (p. 22): "**a phenomenon that occurs when the undesired behavior
increases after ignoring is initiated because the child is testing the parents to see if they
are serious about the plan.**"

> **Trap note — the behavior gets worse before it gets better, and that is expected.**
> Response burst (p. 22) is the single most testable concept in the discipline section,
> because a parent who does not know about it concludes the plan failed and gives in — which
> the chapter says reinforces the behavior. **When a parent reports that ignoring made the
> tantrums worse, the keyed response is that this is expected and to continue.**

> **Trap note — requirement 3 is the one people skip.** Ignoring only works if **parental
> attention is actually the reinforcer** (p. 22). If the behavior is maintained by something
> else, ignoring will not extinguish it.

### Consequences — the three types

"**The strategy of consequences involves allowing children to experience the results of their
misbehavior. It includes three types:**" (p. 22)

| # | Type | Definition as given | Example as given |
|---|---|---|---|
| 1 | **Natural** | "Those that occur **without any intervention**" | "being late and having to clean up the dinner table" |
| 2 | **Logical** | "Those that are **directly related to the rule**" | "not being allowed to play with another toy until the used ones are put away" |
| 3 | **Unrelated** | "Those that are **imposed deliberately**" | "no playing until homework is completed or the use of time-out" |

**The preference** (p. 22): "**Natural or logical consequences are preferred and effective if
they are meaningful to children.**"

**The chapter's own worked qualification** (p. 22): "**the natural consequence of living in a
messy room may do little to encourage cleaning up, but allowing no friends over until the room
is neat can be motivating!**"

**Where withdrawing privileges falls** (p. 22): "**Withdrawing privileges is often an unrelated
consequence.**"

**And what the parent does afterward** (p. 22): "**After the child experiences the consequence,
the parent should refrain from any comment because the usual tendency is for the child to try
to place blame for imposing the rule.**"

> **Trap note — three types, and the chapter's own NCLEX question 3 invents a fourth.** The
> distractor is
> "Transforming: allowing the child to come to the conclusion on his or her own" (p. 38),
> which is not a type of consequence in this chapter. **Natural, logical, unrelated — and
> nothing else.**

> **Trap note — natural is not automatically best.** Preferred, yes, but
> "**effective if they are meaningful to children**" (p. 22), and the messy-room example
> shows a natural consequence failing while a logical one works. **A distractor makes
> natural consequences universally superior.**

> **Trap note — time-out is an UNRELATED consequence.** Stated twice (p. 22). A distractor
> files it as logical.

## 16. Time-out

**What it is** (p. 22): "**a refinement of the common practice of sending the child to his or
her room and is a type of unrelated consequence.**"

**Its mechanism** (p. 22): "**It is based on the premise of removing the reinforcer (i.e., the
satisfaction or attention the child is receiving from the activity).**"

**Why it works** (p. 22): "**When placed in an unstimulating and isolated place, children become
bored and consequently agree to behave in order to reenter the family group.**"

### The four problems time-out avoids (p. 22)

| # | What it avoids |
|---|---|
| 1 | "**No physical punishment is involved**" |
| 2 | "**no reasoning** ... is given" |
| 3 | "**no ... scolding is given**" |
| 4 | "**the parent does not need to be present for all of the time-out, thus facilitating consistent application of this type of discipline**" |

**The mutual benefit** (p. 23): "**Time-out offers both the child and the parent a cooling-off
time.**"

**The precondition** (p. 23): "**To be effective, however, time-out must be planned in
advance.**"

**In public** (p. 23): "**Implement time-out in a public place by selecting a suitable area, or
explain to children that time-out will be spent immediately on returning home.**"

### Family-Centered Care: Using Time-Out (p. 23) — complete

**Setup:**

| # | Step |
|---|---|
| 1 | "**Select an area for time-out that is safe, convenient, and unstimulating, but where the child can be monitored, such as the bathroom, hallway, or laundry room.**" |
| 2 | "**Determine what behaviors warrant a time-out.**" |
| 3 | "**Make certain children understand the rules and how they are expected to behave.**" |

**The process, explained to the child in advance:**

| # | Rule |
|---|---|
| 1 | "**When they misbehave, they will be given one warning. If they do not obey, they will be sent to the place designated for time-out.**" |
| 2 | "**They are to sit there for a specified period.**" |
| 3 | "**If they cry, refuse, or display any disruptive behavior, the time-out period will begin after they quiet down.**" |
| 4 | "**When they are quiet for the duration of the time, they can then leave the room.**" |

**The duration rule and the timer** (p. 23): "**A rule for the length of time-out is 1 minute
per year of age; use a kitchen timer with an audible bell to record the time rather than a
watch.**"

> **Trap note — 1 minute per year of age.** The most citable number in the chapter (p. 23).
> **A distractor scales it to the severity of the behavior**, or uses a flat duration.

> **Trap note — the audible timer is not incidental.** The chapter specifies
> "**use a kitchen timer with an audible bell to record the time rather than a watch**" (p. 23),
> because the child needs an objective, externally-signalled endpoint rather than depending
> on the parent. **A distractor has the parent watch the clock.**

> **Trap note — the clock starts when the child quiets, not when they are sent.** Rule 3
> (p. 23). This is the mechanic most often gotten wrong, and it is what makes the technique
> work for a child who screams through the first five minutes.

> **Trap note — one warning, then time-out.** Rule 1 (p. 23). Not repeated warnings, and not
> zero warnings. **The single warning is part of the published procedure.**

> **Trap note — the location is unstimulating, not punitive.** Bathroom, hallway, or laundry
> room —
> "safe, convenient, and unstimulating, but where the child can be monitored" (p. 23). **The
> child's own bedroom is a poor choice by this standard because it is stimulating**, and the
> chapter frames time-out as a refinement of, not a synonym for, sending the child to their
> room (p. 22).

## 17. Corporal punishment

**What it is** (p. 23): "**Corporal or physical punishment most often takes the form of
spanking.**"

**Its mechanism and its short-term effect** (p. 23): "**Based on the principles of aversive
therapy, inflicting pain through spanking causes a dramatic short-term decrease in the
behavior.**"

### The three serious flaws, as the chapter numbers them (p. 23)

| # | Flaw |
|---|---|
| 1 | "**it teaches children that violence is acceptable**" |
| 2 | "**it may physically harm the child if it is the result of parental rage**" |
| 3 | "**children become accustomed to spanking, requiring more severe corporal punishment over time**" |

**The additional harms** (p. 23): "**Spanking can result in severe physical and psychologic
injury, and it interferes with effective parent-child interaction.**"

**The behavioral failure** (p. 23): "**when the parents are not around, children are likely to
misbehave because they have not learned to behave well for their own sake.**"

**And the developmental cost** (p. 23): "**Parental use of corporal punishment may also
interfere with the child's development of moral reasoning.**"

> **Trap note — spanking works in the short term, and the chapter says so.**
> "a dramatic short-term decrease in the behavior" (p. 23). **That concession is what makes
> the flaws the answer**: a distractor claims spanking is ineffective, which is not this
> chapter's argument. The chapter's argument is escalation, modeled violence, risk of
> injury, no internalization, and impaired moral reasoning.

> **Trap note — flaw 3 is the escalation flaw.** Children become accustomed and require
> "more severe corporal punishment over time" (p. 23). **This is the mechanism that turns
> spanking into physical abuse**, and it is the one a distractor omits.

## 18. Special parenting situations, and parenting the adopted child

**The framing** (p. 23): "**Parenting is a demanding task under ideal circumstances, but when
parents and children face situations that deviate from the norm, the potential for family
disruption is increased.**"

**The five the chapter addresses** (p. 23): "**divorce, single parenthood, blended families,
adoption, and dual-career families.**"

**Plus immigration** (p. 23): "**as cultural diversity increases in our communities, many
immigrants are making the transition to parenthood and a new country, culture, and language
simultaneously.**"

**And three it names without treating** (p. 23): "**parental alcoholism, homelessness, and
incarceration. Although these topics are not addressed here, the reader may wish to investigate
them further.**"

### Adoption

**The definition** (p. 23): adoption "**establishes a legal relationship between a child and
parents who are not related by birth but who have the same rights and obligations that exist
between children and their biologic parents.**"

**The shift in whose rights count** (p. 23):

| Then | "In the past **the biologic mother alone made the decision to relinquish the rights to her child.**" |
|---|---|
| Now | "In recent years **the courts have acknowledged the legal rights of the biologic father** regarding this decision." |
| The concern | "**Concerned child advocates have questioned whether decisions that honor the father's rights are in the best interests of the child.**" |
| The child's own rights | "**As the child's rights have become recognized, older children have successfully dissolved their legal bond with their biologic parents to pursue adoption by adults of their choice.**" |

**And a growing group** (p. 23): "**there is a growing interest and demand within the LGBT
community to adopt.**"

**The support gap** (p. 23): "**Unlike biologic parents, who prepare for their child's birth with
prenatal classes and the support of friends and relatives, adoptive parents have fewer sources
of support and preparation for the new addition to their family.**"

**The nurse's role** (p. 23): "**Nurses can provide the information, support, and reassurance
needed to reduce parental anxiety regarding the adoptive process and refer adoptive parents to
state parental support groups.**" These "can be contacted through **a state or county welfare
office.**"

### Attachment in adoption

**The two rules, stated together** (p. 23): "**The sooner infants enter their adoptive home, the
better the chances of parent-infant attachment. However, the more caregivers the infant had
before adoption, the greater the risk for attachment problems.**"

**The mechanism** (p. 23): "**The infant must break the bond with the previous caregiver and form
a new bond with the adoptive parents.**"

**The two variables** (p. 23): difficulty depends on "**the amount of time he or she has spent
with caregivers early in life, as well as the number of caregivers** (e.g., the birth mother,
nurse, adoption agency personnel)."

**Siblings** (p. 23): "**Siblings, adopted or biologic, who are old enough to understand should
be included in decisions regarding the commitment to adopt, with reassurance that they are not
being replaced.**"

> **Trap note — two independent risk factors for attachment difficulty.** **Time spent with
> prior caregivers, and the number of them** (p. 23). A distractor names only the child's
> age at adoption.

## 19. Issues of origin, adolescence, and cross-racial adoption

### Telling the child

**The difficulty** (p. 23): "**The task of telling children that they are adopted can be a cause
of deep concern and anxiety. There are no clear-cut guidelines for parents to follow in
determining when and at what age children are ready for the information.**"

**But it must be told** (p. 23): "**it is important that parents not withhold the adoption from
the child because it is an essential component of the child's identity.**"

**The timing principle** (p. 23): "**Most authorities believe that children should be informed at
an age young enough so that, as they grow older, they do not remember a time when they did not
know they were adopted.**"

**The natural opening** (pp. 23–24): The time is highly (p. 23) "**individual, but it must be
right for both the parents and the child. It may be when children ask where babies come from, at
which time children can also be told the facts of their adoption.**" (p. 24)

**How to frame it** (p. 24): "**If they are told in a way that conveys the idea that they were
active participants in the selection process, they will be less likely to feel that they were
abandoned victims in a helpless situation.** For example, parents can tell children that **their
personal qualities drew the parents to them.**"

**The deadline** (p. 24): "**It is wise for parents who have not previously discussed adoption to
tell children that they are adopted before the children enter school to avoid having them learn
it from third parties.**"

**And the principle** (p. 24): "**Complete honesty between parents and children strengthens the
relationship.**"

> **Trap note — before school entry, and the reason is third parties.** Both halves (p. 24),
> and the reasoning is identical to Chapter 19's rule about telling siblings before a
> neighbor or other nonfamily member does (Ch. 19, p. 602). **The chapter's consistent
> position is that hearing it from outside is the harm to prevent.**

**What to expect afterward** (p. 24): "**Parents should anticipate behavior changes after the
disclosure, especially in older children. Children who are struggling with the revelation that
they are adopted may benefit from individual and family counseling.**"

**The manipulation, and its risk** (p. 24): "**Children may use the fact of their adoption as a
weapon to manipulate and threaten parents.** Statements such as **My real mother would not treat
me like this** or **You don't love me as much because I'm adopted** hurt parents and increase
their feelings of insecurity. **Such statements may also cause parents to become
overpermissive.**"

**And the corrective** (p. 24): "**Adopted children need the same undemanding love, combined with
firm discipline and limit setting, as any other child.**"

> **Trap note — the risk is overpermissiveness, not rejection.** The chapter names the
> parental failure mode precisely (p. 24). **The keyed guidance is that the adopted child
> gets the same limits as any other child** — the same same rules principle the chapter
> applies to siblings and Chapter 19 applies to chronically ill children.

### Adolescence

**Why it intensifies** (p. 24): "**The normal confrontations of adolescents and parents assume
more painful aspects in adoptive families. Adolescents may use their adoption to defy parental
authority or as a justification for aberrant behavior.**"

**The identity work** (p. 24): "**As they attempt to master the task of identity formation, they
may begin to have feelings of abandonment by their biologic parents.**"

**The search** (p. 24): "**Adopted children fantasize about their biologic parents and may feel
the need to discover their parents' identity to define themselves and their own identity.**"

**The parental stance** (p. 24): "**It is important for parents to keep the lines of communication
open and to reassure their child that they understand the need to search for their identity.**"

**The legal detail** (p. 24): "**In some states, birth certificates are made legally available to
adopted children when they come of age. Parents should be honest with questioning adolescents
and tell them of this possibility (the parents themselves are unable to obtain the birth
certificate; it is the children's responsibility if they desire it).**"

> **Trap note — the search is normal identity work, not rejection of the adoptive parents.**
> The chapter's instruction is to reassure the adolescent that the need to search is
> understood (p. 24). **A distractor has the parent discourage the search or treat it as
> ingratitude.**

### Cross-racial and international adoption

**The added task** (p. 24): "**In addition to the problems faced by adopted children in general,
children of a cross-racial adoption must deal with physical and sometimes cultural
differences.**"

**The instruction** (p. 24): "**It is advised that parents who adopt children with different
ethnic background do everything to preserve the adopted children's racial heritage.**"

**Nursing Alert, complete** (p. 24): "**As a health care provider, it is important not to ask the
wrong questions, such as** Is she yours, or is she adopted? **;** What do you know about the real
mother? **;** Do they have the same father? **or** How much did it cost to adopt him?"

**What the family may face** (p. 24): "**Bigotry may appear among relatives and friends. Strangers
may make thoughtless comments and talk about the children as though they were not members of the
family.**"

**And the family's response** (p. 24): "**It is vital that family members declare to others that
this is their child and a cherished member of the family.**"

**International adoption specifics** (p. 24):

| Issue | The chapter's words |
|---|---|
| Records | "**the medical information the parents receive may be incomplete or sketchy; weight, height, and head circumference are often the only objective information present in the child's medical record**" |
| Prematurity | "**Many internationally adopted children were born prematurely**" |
| Growth | "**common health problems such as infant diarrhea and malnutrition delay growth and development**" |
| Complexity | "**Some children have serious or multiple health problems that can be stressful for the parents.**" |

> **Trap note — the Nursing Alert is a list of questions NOT to ask.** Four of them (p. 24),
> and the phrase real mother is singled out. **In an item about communicating with an
> adoptive family, any option containing the words *real parent* or asking about cost is the
> distractor.** The chapter's preferred stance is elsewhere: ask open questions about what
> the family wants you to know (p. 38).

## 21. Impact of divorce on children, and Box 2.5

**The adversity framing** (p. 25): "**Parental divorce is an additional childhood adversity that
contributes to poor mental health outcomes, especially when combined with child abuse.**"

**The emotional signature** (p. 25): "**Even when a divorce is amicable and open, children recall
parental separation with the same emotions felt by victims of a natural disaster: loss, grief,
and vulnerability to forces beyond their control.**"

### What determines the impact

**The factors** (p. 25): "**the age and sex of the children, the outcome of the divorce, and the
quality of the parent-child relationship and parental care during the years following the
divorce.**"

**And the ranking** (p. 25): "**Family characteristics are more crucial to the child's well-being
than specific child characteristics, such as age or sex.**"

**The specific harm** (p. 25): "**High levels of ongoing family conflict are related to problems
of social development, emotional stability, and cognitive skills for the child.**"

**Age is not the differentiator it was thought to be** (p. 25): "**Previously, researchers believed
that divorce had a greater impact on younger children, but recent observations indicate that
divorce constitutes a major disruption for children of all ages.**"

**And the stress ranking** (p. 25): "**all children suffer stress second only to the stress
produced by the death of a parent.**"

**On sex differences** (p. 25): "**Although considerable research has looked at sex differences in
children's adjustments to divorce, the findings are not conclusive.**"

> **Trap note — family conflict outranks child age and sex.** The chapter states the ranking
> twice (p. 25). **A distractor makes the child's age the key determinant of outcome**,
> which is precisely the belief the chapter says has been superseded.

> **Trap note — divorce is second only to a parent's death.** A specific comparative ranking
> (p. 25) and a clean single-best-answer item.

### Research Focus: Impact of Divorce (p. 25)

**The cooperation finding** (p. 25): "**Children who reported that their divorced parents were
cooperative had better relationships with their parents, grandparents, stepparents, and
siblings.**"

**The three named complications** (p. 25): "**efforts on the part of one parent to subvert the
child's loyalties to the other, abandonment to other caregivers, and adjustment to a
stepparent.**"

**The central problem, and its fix** (p. 25): "**A major problem occurs when children are caught
in the middle between the divorced parents. They become the message bearer between the parents,
are often quizzed about the other parent's activities, and have to listen to one parent
criticize the other.**"

**The nurse's technique, with the formula** (p. 25): "**A nurse may be able to help the child get
out of the middle by stating**" I messages "**based on the formula of**" I feel (state the
feeling) when you (state the source). **An example of** an I message **is:** I do not feel
comfortable when you ask me questions about Mom; maybe you could ask her yourself.

**And why it helps** (p. 25): "**This approach enables the child to feel in control.**"

> **Trap note — the I-message appears twice in this chapter with the same structure.** For
> discipline (p. 22) and here, for a child triangulated between parents (p. 25). **Here it
> is taught to the CHILD, not used by the nurse on the parent** — the nurse teaches the
> child a script. That is unusual and therefore testable.

verbalize some feelings and understand some divorce-related changes**"; "Increased anxiety and
aggression"; "Feelings of abandonment by departing parent"

#### Middle School-Age Children (Ages 6 to 8 Years)

**Panic reactions**; "Feelings of deprivation, loss of parent, attention, money, and secure
future"; "Profound sadness, depression, fear, and insecurity"; "Feelings of abandonment and
rejection"; "Fear regarding the future"; "**Difficulty expressing anger at parents**";
"**Intense desire for reconciliation of parents**"; "Impaired capacity to play and enjoy outside
activities"; "**Decline in school performance**"; "Altered peer relationships, become bossy,
irritable, demanding, and manipulative"; "Frequent crying, loss of appetite, sleep disorders";
"Disturbed routine, forgetfulness"

#### Later School-Age Children (Ages 9 to 12 Years)

"**More realistic understanding of divorce**"; "**Intense anger directed at one or both
parents**"; "**Divided loyalties**"; "**Ability to express feelings of anger**"; "Ashamed of
parental behavior"; "**Desire for revenge; may wish to punish the parent they hold

#### Adolescents (Ages 12 to 18 Years)

"**Able to disengage themselves from parental conflict**"; "Feelings of a profound sense of loss,
of family, childhood"; Feelings of anxiety; "**Worry about themselves, parents, siblings**";
"Expression of anger, sadness, shame, embarrassment"; "**May withdraw from family and

> **Trap note — self-blame belongs to the preschool bands.** Both the 2-to-3 and the 3-to-5
> rows list blaming themselves (p. 25), and it disappears after that. **This is the same
> magical- thinking mechanism Chapter 19 describes for death** (Ch. 19, p. 611). A
> distractor assigns self-blame to a school-age or adolescent child.

> **Trap note — anger cannot be expressed at 6 to 8 and can be at 9 to 12.** The middle
> school-age row says "**Difficulty expressing anger at parents**" ; the later school-age
> row says "**Ability to express feelings of anger**" (p. 25). **That flip is the single
> cleanest age discrimination in the box.**

> **Trap note — only the adolescent row contains a positive capability.**
> "Able to disengage themselves from parental conflict" (p. 25). **Adolescents are the only
> group the box credits with the ability to step out of the middle**, which is why the
> I-message technique on p. 25 is aimed at younger children who cannot.

> **Trap note — the reconciliation fantasy peaks at 6 to 8.**
> "Intense desire for reconciliation of parents" (p. 25). A distractor places it in
> adolescence.

### Shame, and the positive outcomes

**Shame** (p. 25): "**Sometimes children see themselves as different, inferior, or unworthy of
love, especially if they feel responsible for the family dissolution.**" And: "**such feelings may
still exist in small towns or in some cultural groups and can reinforce children's negative
self-image.**"

**What determines the lasting effect** (p. 25): "**The lasting effects of divorce depend on the
children's and the parents' adjustment to the transition from an intact family to a single-parent
family and, often, to a reconstituted family.**"

**The positive outcomes, which the chapter states plainly** (pp. 25–26): "**Although most studies have
concentrated on the negative effects of divorce on youngsters, some positive outcomes of divorce
have been reported. A successful postdivorce family, either a single-parent or a reconstituted
family, can improve the quality of life for both adults and children. If conflict is resolved, a
better relationship with one or both parents may result, and some children may have less contact
with a disturbed parent. Greater stability in the home setting and the removal of arguing parents
can be a positive outcome for the child's long-term well-being.**"

> **Trap note — divorce can be the better outcome.** The chapter names three positive
> results: conflict resolution, less contact with a disturbed parent, and removal of arguing
> parents (p. 26). **A distractor treats divorce as uniformly harmful**, which contradicts
> the chapter's own emphasis that ongoing conflict, not the divorce itself, drives the harm
> (p. 25).

## 22. Telling the children

**The parental reluctance** (p. 25): "**Parents are understandably hesitant to tell children about
their decision to divorce. Most parents neglect to discuss either the divorce or its inevitable
changes with their preschool child.**"

**Why silence is worse** (p. 25): "**Without preparation, even children who remain in the family
home are confused by the parental separation. Frequently, children are already experiencing
vague, uneasy feelings that are more difficult to cope with than being told the truth about the
situation.**"

### The disclosure protocol

| Element | The chapter's words | p. |
|---|---|---|
| Who and in what order | "**If possible, the initial disclosure should include both parents and siblings, followed by individual discussions with each child.**" | 26 |
| Time | "**Sufficient time should be set aside for these discussions**" | 26 |
| When | "**they should take place during a period of calm, not after an argument**" | 26 |
| Touch | "**Parents who physically hold or touch their children provide them with a feeling of warmth and reassurance.**" | 26 |
| Content | "**The discussions should include the reason for the divorce, if age appropriate, and reassurance that the divorce is not the fault of the children.**" | 26 |
| Crying | "**Parents should not fear crying in front of the children because their crying gives the children permission to cry also.**" | 26 |

> **Trap note — group first, then individually.** The order is stated (p. 26): both parents
> and all siblings together, **followed by** individual conversations. **A distractor does
> one or the other, not both.**

> **Trap note — parental crying is permitted and framed as permission-giving.** (p. 26) The
> same principle appears in Chapter 19, which encourages parents to share their moments of
> sorrow with their children (Ch. 19, p. 625). **A distractor has the parent maintain
> composure for the child's sake.**

### What children need afterward

**The feelings to allow** (p. 26): "**Children may feel guilt, a sense of failure, or that they
are being punished for misbehavior. They normally feel anger and resentment and should be allowed
to communicate these feelings without punishment. They also have feelings of terror and
abandonment.**"

**What they need** (p. 26): "**They need consistency and order in their lives.**"

**The concrete questions children have, quoted complete** (p. 26): "**They want to know where they
will live, who will take care of them, if they will be with their siblings, and if there will be
enough money to live on. Children may also wonder what will happen on special days such as
birthdays and holidays, whether both parents will come to school events, and whether they will
still have the same friends.**"

**And the underlying fear** (p. 26): "**Children fear that if their parents stopped loving each
other, they could stop loving them. Their need for love and reassurance is tremendous at this
time.**"

> **Trap note — the child's questions are logistical, and answering them is the
> intervention.** Where will I live, who will care for me, will I be with my siblings, will
> there be money (p. 26). **A distractor responds only to the emotional content.** The
> chapter's list is concrete because the child's need is for "consistency and order."

> **Trap note — the core fear is transferable loss of love.**
> "if their parents stopped loving each other, they could stop loving them" (p. 26). **That
> is the reassurance to give**, and it is more specific than general comfort.

| Type | Definition as given | p. |

## 27. Sociocultural influences: the social ecologic model

**The layered picture** (p. 28): "**A child and his or her immediate family are nested within a
local community of school, peers, and extended family and within a larger community that may be
bound by common geography, background, traditions, and an even broader community that
incorporates the social, political, and economic elements that influence many aspects of family
life.**"

**Why the outer layer matters** (p. 28): "**the sociopolitical context, though an outer layer of a
social ecologic model, can exert enormous influence on a child's daily life, opportunities, and
outcomes.**"

**And what it directs attention to** (p. 28): "**the sociopolitical context draws our attention to
factors and health policies at the local, regional, state, and federal levels that can serve as
barriers to health equity.**"

### The model itself

**Its lineage** (p. 28): "**The social ecologic model, rooted in Bronfenbrenner's ecologic model,
offers a perspective of viewing children and their families within the context of various
circles of influence, called an ecologic framework.**"

**Its central claim** (p. 28): "**This framework posits that individuals adapt in response to
changes in their surrounding environments, whether that be the environment of the immediate
family, the school, the neighborhood in which the family lives, or the socioeconomic forces that
may shape job availability in their geographic area.**"

**Kazak's addition** (p. 28): "**a person's behavior results from the interaction of his or her
traits and abilities with the environment.**"

**And the anti-reductionist rule** (p. 28): "**No single factor can explain the totality of a
child and his or her family's health behaviors.**"

### The four nested levels (p. 28)

| Level | The chapter's words |
|---|---|
| 1. The individual | "**Children possess their own factors that influence their behavior (i.e., personal history or biologic factors).**" |
| 2. Relationships | "**they are surrounded by relationships with family, friends, and peers who influence their behavior**" |
| 3. Community | "**Children and their families are then situated within a community that establishes the context in which social relationships develop.**" |
| 4. Societal | "**wider sociocultural factors exist that influence whether a behavior is encouraged or prohibited** (e.g., social policy on smoking, cultural norms of mothers as primary caregivers of young children, media that can influence how a teen thinks he or she should look)" |

**The nursing requirement** (p. 28): "**Promoting the health of children requires a nurse to
understand social, cultural, and religious influences on children and their families.**"

**The consequence of ignoring them** (p. 28): "**Patients experience negative health outcomes when
social, cultural, and religious factors are not considered as influencing their health care.**"

**And one remedy** (p. 28): "**Educating health care providers is one way to reduce disparities in
health care.**"

> **Trap note — four levels, individual outward.** Individual, relationships, community,
> societal (p. 28). **A distractor collapses relationships and community, or omits the
> societal layer** — which is the one the chapter argues is most often overlooked and most
> consequential for policy.

## 28. School communities: school health and school connectedness

### Schools as health promotion sites

**The framing** (p. 29): "**Within communities, schools are important sites for health
promotion.**"

**Why early education matters** (p. 29): "**High-quality early childhood education is especially
beneficial to set children up for success in early grades. This is especially true for children
from disadvantaged backgrounds.**"

**And the access problem** (p. 29): "**Access to this high-quality education is key, yet also
occurs along a socioeconomic gradient, with more highly resourced children attending
higher-quality preschool programs.**"

**The named initiative** (p. 29): the Centers for Disease Control and Prevention sponsored
"**Whole Child, Whole School, Whole Community**", which "**emphasize and build on this critical
connection between youth academic performance and health.**"

**Its elements, quoted complete** (p. 29): "**physical education and activity; nutritional
environment; counseling; psychologic and social services; social and emotional climate; physical
environment; family engagement; and community involvement.**"

**The dual role of school** (p. 29): "**It situates school not only as a place to learn but also a
place to practice positive health behaviors and health promotion skills.**"

**The worked example** (p. 29): a nutritional health environment "**gives youth a chance to live
out these skills by making healthy food choices available, by weaving nutrition education into
the health curriculum, and by including supportive messaging about healthy nutrition in the
school environment.**"

**Place-based care** (p. 29): "**schools and early childhood centers can contribute to the health
of children by teaching about health and healthy behaviors and also offering ways to enact
healthy behaviors.**" And: "**place-based care can improve access for many children and emphasizes
coordination among services in a given neighborhood.**"

### School connectedness

**The definition** (p. 29): "**School connectedness is defined as students' perception that they
and their learning matter to the adults at their school and their peers.**"

**Restated** (p. 29): "**It is when youth believe that their teachers, staff, and peers care about
them academically and personally.**"

**Why the concept exists** (p. 29): "**research indicating greater impact on youth health through
bolstering protective factors that help children and adolescents avoid risky or unhealthy
behaviors.**"

**And its buffering role** (p. 29): "**This focus on strengths and promoting positive choices may
also help buffer the impact of negative events on children and their health.**"

### What connectedness protects against

| Outcome | The chapter's words | p. |
|---|---|---|
| General | "**Children and youth who feel more connected are more likely to engage in healthy behaviors and do well academically.**" | 29 |
| Specifically protective | "**School connectedness has been found particularly protective against substance abuse, early sexual debut, and violence in both boys and girls.**" | 29 |
| The ranking | "**school connectedness was second only to family connectedness in protection against emotional distress, disordered eating, and suicidal ideation and attempts**" | 29 |
| Academic | "**it also promotes academic outcomes in addition to health outcomes, including improved grades, decreased absence, and delayed dropout**" | 29 |

> **Trap note — second only to family connectedness.** A specific comparative ranking
> (p. 29), and the outcomes it protects against are named: emotional distress, disordered
> eating, suicidal ideation and attempts. **A distractor makes school connectedness the
> strongest protective factor**, or the peer group.

### The four contributors to connectedness (p. 29)

"Important factors include **supportive teachers and school staff, membership in a positive peer
group, school environment, and overall commitment to education.**"

| Contributor | The chapter's elaboration |
|---|---|
| Teachers and staff | "**Teachers and staff play a pivotal role in promoting a child's sense of being part of a school community. Youth perceptions about themselves are shaped by the degree to which they believe the adults in their community care about them and are involved in their lives.**" |
| Peer group | "**A positive, stable peer group can protect youth against victimization, whereas a peer group composed of youth participating in negative behaviors can have a detrimental effect of health and academic outcomes.**" |
| Commitment to education | "**the extent to which students believe their teachers, staff, and peers value education influences the overall commitment to education**"; youth who internalize it "**exhibit persistence, effort, and sustained attention to tasks**" |
| School climate | "characterized by **respectful relationships and disciplinary practices, clean and physically safe spaces for learning, and broad opportunities for student and family participation**" |

**The three recommended strategies** (p. 29): "**effective training for teachers and staff to
create positive learning environments, opportunities for families to become involved in the
process and associated training, and provision of important academic, emotional, and social
skills to students (and addressing of barriers to achieving these skills) so that students can
feel engaged and have a stake in their education.**"

> **Trap note — connectedness is a perception, not a program.** The definition turns on
> "students' **perception** that they and their learning matter" (p. 29). **A distractor
> defines it by participation in activities or attendance.** What the strategies target is
> the belief.

## 29. Schools, peer cultures, and community

### Schools

### School as a socializing institution

### Peer cultures

**When peers matter most** (p. 30): "**Peer relationships become increasingly important and
influential as children proceed through school.**"

**The children's own culture** (p. 30): "In school, children have **what can be regarded as a
culture of their own. This is even more apparent in unsupervised playgroups because the culture
in school is partly produced by adults.**"

**Why peer values compel** (p. 30): "**The values imposed by the peer group are especially
compelling because children must accept and conform to them to be accepted as members of the

**The useful function of mild conflict** (p. 30): "**When the peer values are not too different
from those of family and teachers, the mild conflict created by these small differences serves to

**What determines the group's influence** (p. 30): "**The kind of socialization provided by the

**The source of conflict** (p. 30): "**Many conflicts between teachers and students and between

**And two more functions** (p. 30): "**to relieve boredom and to provide recognition that
individual members do not receive from teachers and other authority figures.**"

> **Trap note — mild peer-family value conflict is developmentally useful.** The chapter
> says it
> "**serves to separate children from the adults in their lives and to strengthen the feeling of belonging to the peer group**" (p. 30).
> **A distractor treats any divergence from family values as a warning sign.** The qualifier
> is not too different.

> **Trap note — the peer group is where sex and drug information comes from.** Stated
> plainly (p. 30), and it is the argument for the schools' role in offering
> "timely, accurate sexuality and drug education" (p. 31).

**The school consequences** (p. 30): "**affected neighborhoods are more likely to have

**And the intervention this implies** (p. 30): "**Thus we see an important need for two-generation

**Adolescent mortality** (p. 30): "**over 70% of teen deaths are attributed to accidents, homicide,

> **Trap note — two-generation programs.** The chapter names the intervention type that
> follows from the intergenerational chain (p. 30). **A distractor targets the child
> alone**, which the chapter's own reasoning says will not break the loop.

> **Trap note — over 70% of teen deaths are accidents, homicide, and suicide.** (p. 30)
> Chapter 19 gives the same three plus cancer in a ranked order for ages 1 to 19 (Ch. 19, p.
> 605). **Here the point is that all three are community-influenced**, which is why the

## 31. Race, ethnicity, racism, and ethnocentrism

### The two terms

| Term | Definition as given | p. |
|---|---|---|
| **Race** | "a term that groups together people by their **outward, physical appearance**" | 31 |
| **Ethnicity** | "a classification aimed at grouping **individuals who consider themselves, or are considered by others, to**" (p. 31) "**share common characteristics that differentiate them from the other collectivities in a society, and from which they develop their distinctive cultural behavior**" (p. 32) |

**Both are constructed** (p. 31): "**Race and ethnicity are socially constructed terms used to
group people who share similar characteristics, traditions, or historical experience
together.**"

**What differentiates ethnicities** (p. 32): "**Ethnicities may be differentiated from one another
by customs and language and may influence family structure, food preferences, and expressions of
emotion.**"

**And their fluidity** (p. 32): "**The composition and definition of ethnic groups can be fluid in
response to changes in geography (i.e., moving from one country to another).**"

**How they affect health** (p. 32): "**Race and ethnicity influence a family's health when they are
used as criteria by which a child or family is discriminated against.**"

> **Trap note — race is appearance, ethnicity is shared characteristics and culture.**
> (pp. 31–32) **The discriminating word in the ethnicity definition is
> self-identification**: "individuals who consider themselves, or are considered by others."
> Race in this chapter carries no self-identification clause.

### Racism as a social determinant

**The claim** (p. 32): "**Racism remains an important social determinant of health.**"

**The mechanism** (p. 32): for groups who experience stigmatization, "**inequalities in health are
created by larger inequalities in society**", meaning "**prevailing social conditions and obstacles
to equal opportunities for all influences the health of all individuals.**"

**The evidence** (p. 32):

| Finding | The chapter's words |
|---|---|
| Child mortality | "**from birth forward, African American and Native American children have a higher mortality rate than Caucasian children in general**" |
| Infant mortality | "**There is also a higher death rate for babies of African American and Hispanic women versus Caucasian women.**" |
| Education does not close it | "**Even when controlling for maternal levels of education, the infant mortality rate for college-educated African American women is 2.5 times higher than Hispanic and Caucasian women of similar education level.**" |

**Perceived racism** (p. 32): "in a study of more than 5000 fifth-graders, **15% of Hispanic youth
and 20% of African American youth reported that they had experienced racial discrimination.**
**Such experiences were then associated with a higher risk of mental health symptoms.**"

**And online** (p. 32): "**Teens also report racial discrimination through online communities,
social networking sites, and texting, which is related to increased anxiety and depression.**"

> **Trap note — the 2.5-fold figure survives controlling for education.** That is the point
> of the statistic (p. 32): **education does not eliminate the disparity**, which is what
> makes racism itself the determinant rather than socioeconomic status alone. A distractor
> attributes the gap to education or income.

### Ethnocentrism

**The definition** (p. 32): "**the emotional attitude that one's own ethnic group is superior to
others; that one's values, beliefs, and perceptions are the correct ones; and that the group's
ways of living and behaving are the best.**"

**Its implication** (p. 32): "**Ethnocentrism implies that all other groups are inferior.**"

**What it produces** (p. 32): "**Stereotyping or labeling stems from ethnocentric beliefs.**"

**Who holds it** (p. 32): "**It is a common attitude among the dominant ethnic group and strongly
influences a person's ability to evaluate objectively the beliefs and behaviors of others.**"

**And the nursing instruction** (p. 32): "**Nurses must overcome the natural tendency to have
ethnocentric attitudes when caring for people from backgrounds different from their own.**"

> **Trap note — the chapter calls ethnocentrism a natural tendency.** (p. 32) It is not
> framed as a moral failing to be denied but as a default to be actively overcome — which is
> exactly the premise of cultural humility's "deliberate reflection" (p. 37). **A distractor
> has the nurse assert they have no biases.**

## 32. Social class and poverty

### Social class

**What it comprises** (p. 32): "**This relates to the family's economic and educational levels and
their ability to access resources needed to thrive in daily life.**"

**And the claim that matters most** (p. 32): "**Strength of family relationships is not tied to
social class.**"

**The chapter's two-way illustration** (p. 32):

| Group | The chapter's words |
|---|---|
| Lower socioeconomic status | "**A family of lower socioeconomic status may have fewer resources, but they may be well connected to the broader family network and rely on them for support to meet physical and emotional needs.**" |
| Higher socioeconomic groups | "**Families in higher socioeconomic groups may have access to resources that reach beyond their extended family but may be disconnected because of pressures of work and outside obligations.**" |

> **Trap note — family strength does not track social class, and the chapter shows it in
> both directions.** (p. 32) **A distractor equates resources with functioning**, which the
> chapter refutes with the well-connected poor family and the disconnected affluent one.
> Compare Chapter 19's functional-burden principle, where resources and demands are weighed
> separately (Ch. 19, p. 596).

### Health disparities and health inequities

| Term | Definition as given | p. |
|---|---|---|
| **Child health disparities** | "discrepancies that are based in **avoidable differences** and are concerning because of **the immense and foundational development that occurs in early childhood**" | 32 |
| **Health inequities** | "differences in health states between populations that are **socially produced and systemic in unequal distribution across populations that are avoidable and unfair**" | 32 |

**The social gradient** (p. 32): "**Health follows a social gradient — better health outcomes with
increased socioeconomic status and further improvements with movements up in socioeconomic status.
Similarly, compromised health outcomes are associated with lower economic status.**"

**The inverse care problem** (p. 32): "**Inequitable access to services maintains or perpetuates
health inequities in early childhood because the children most in need of services are most
likely to not receive them.**"

**The three-part response** (p. 32): "**policies that improve access to high-quality services and
connect parents to secure, stable, flexible workplaces; service systems that deliver inclusive
programs rooted in research and evidence; and timely assistance for parents so that they can feel
successful in their parenting role.**"

> **Trap note — the children who most need services are least likely to get them.** The
> chapter states this as a mechanism that *perpetuates* inequity (p. 32). **A distractor
> treats access as randomly distributed** or as solely a matter of family motivation.

> **Trap note — avoidable is in both definitions.** Disparities are "avoidable differences"
> ; inequities are "avoidable and unfair" (p. 32). **The added word in inequities is
> *unfair*** — that is the distinction, and it is what makes inequities a policy target
> rather than a statistical observation.

### Economy and poverty: the numbers

| Fact | The chapter's words | p. |
|---|---|---|
| Below 100% FPL | "**Nearly one fifth of all American children live in families with annual incomes below 100% of the federal poverty level (approximately $22,000)**" | 32 |
| Below 200% FPL | "**almost one-half of children live 200% below the federal poverty limit ($44,000 for a family of four)**" | 32 |
| Geography | "**Poverty affects children and their families across all geographic areas, including urban, suburban, and rural.**" | 32 |

> "urban, suburban, and rural" (p. 32). **A distractor localizes it.**

### Poverty as chronic stress

**The mechanism** (p. 32): "**Poverty itself is a major cause of acute and chronic stress for
adults and children. The daily stressors associated with poverty are overwhelming, and other
circumstances, such as trauma or violence, can add to the stress experienced by parents and
children.**"

**Its effect on coping** (p. 32): "**This overwhelming stress can undermine skills in dealing with
challenging situations.**"

**And the supports that are missing** (p. 32): "**supports to deal with this depletion may not be
available, such as adequate mental health services, high-quality and affordable child care, or
social supports.**"

### The brain

**Why the brain is the place to look** (p. 33): "**Children's brains are dynamic and plastic and
change according to what they experience. This impact is greatest in young children and
adolescence because their brains go through such rapid development.**"

**The prenatal start** (p. 33): "**Even as early as the prenatal period, children are affected by
the stressors of their surrounding environments**", and poverty's stressors include "**housing
instability, poor or compromised nutrition, unsafe living conditions, lack of insurance, or
financial hardship related to insurance copayments or medications that inhibit use of prenatal
care.**"

**The caregiver cycle, quoted as the chapter builds it** (p. 33): "**parents and caregivers who are
overwhelmed by the stressors associated with poverty or trauma may be less able to participate in
reciprocal communication with an infant or young child. Affected caregivers may not respond or
infant crying or cues as needed or may become irritable, which then propels the infant toward a
dysregulated state. Parent and infant are caught in a stressful cycle, and it may then be harder
for the parent to prevent a harsh response in this challenging situation.**"

**The neurologic consequences** (p. 33): "**Serious, early, adverse events can affect development
of the prefrontal cortex, influencing later self-regulation, emotional responsiveness, and
reactivity. Severe, chronic adversity early in life can trigger toxic stress responses that affect
brain development and response and diminish mental health.**"

**And the cognitive costs** (p. 33): "**Early exposure to stressful events is associated with
challenges to working memory, attention, emotional regulation skills, and perception of new
situations as potential threats that require quick action.**"

**Who is most affected** (p. 33): "**Infants and toddlers are more likely to live in poverty than
older children.** Thus some of the most profound effects of poverty are demonstrated in young
children, such as **increased prevalence of low birth weight and infant mortality and delayed
language development.**"

> **Trap note — toxic stress is the named mechanism, and the target organ is the prefrontal
> cortex.** (p. 33) The downstream deficits are **self-regulation, emotional responsiveness,
> and reactivity**, plus working memory and attention. **A distractor attributes poverty's
> effect on school performance to missed schooling alone.**

> **Trap note — the parent-infant cycle is bidirectional and self-reinforcing.** Stressed
> caregiver misses cues → infant dysregulates → harder for the parent to avoid a harsh
> response (p. 33). **This is family systems theory's circular causality applied to
> poverty**, and it is why the chapter calls for two-generation programs (p. 30).

|---|---|---|
| The scale | "**Nearly one fifth of US children are underinsured because of the inability to pay more than one health-related cost or service in the past year.**" | 33 |
| The parental perception link | "**approximately 60% and 90% of parents who reported that their child's health had suffered in the past several years were underinsured**" | 33 |
| **Underserved children** | "**children who live in families who cannot afford clinician recommended health care despite having insurance coverage for children**" | 33 |
| The insured-but-struggling | "**two-thirds of families that have problems paying health care bills have health insurance**" | 33 |

**And the conclusion the chapter states twice** (p. 33): "**This reminds us that having insurance
does not automatically negate financial distress or impact on families**", and "**Underinsurance

> **Trap note — insurance status is not a proxy for affordability.** The chapter says it
> twice and gives the two-thirds figure (p. 33). **A distractor screens for financial
> hardship by asking whether the family has insurance.** The chapter's own screening

## 33. Parental education and screening for poverty

**The chain** (p. 33): "**Child economic status is directly tied to parental earnings. Parental

**The structural problem** (p. 33): "**Income inequality is pervasive and has become worse in

**And the mobility problem** (p. 33): "**Gaps in income undermine a family's ability to access
social mobility and move permanently out of poverty.**"

### Screening

**The mandate** (p. 33): "**The high incidence and pervasive nature of child poverty demands that

hardship into everyday practice.**"

**Its universality** (p. 33): "**Pediatric nurses, despite geographic context, will likely care for

**The AAP recommendation, with its content** (p. 33): "**The American Academy of Pediatrics
recommends that this screening be done at each well visit by asking about ability to meet basic
needs, such as heat, shelter, and food.**"

**The AAP's two-part response recommendation** (p. 33): "**build on family strengths such as
cohesion, humor, support, and spiritual beliefs, and advocate for creation of programs to address
the multiple facets of childhood poverty that are rooted in research.**"

**The named referral resources** (p. 33): "**Head Start, United Way, and the Nurse Family
Partnership.**"

### The four barriers to screening (p. 33)

| # | Barrier |
|---|---|
| 1 | "**not recognizing the impact of poverty or the measurable outcomes associated with it**" |
| 2 | **lack of time** |
| 3 | "**limited and insufficient training in assessment, and limited familiarity with assessment tools**" |
| 4 | "**limited knowledge about available community resources**" |

**The four screening functions** (p. 33): "**initiate surveillance and screening to elicit and
address parent concerns; identify risk and protective factors; screen for specific issues; and

**How to tailor it** (p. 33): "**screening can be tailored to the most sensitive or sustained

**The three-part practice charge** (p. 33): "**imbue their practice with an understanding of the

**And the closing quotation** (p. 33): "**it is a reasonable conclusion that poverty reduction will

> **Trap note — screen at EVERY well visit, by asking about heat, shelter, and food.** Three
> concrete items and a frequency (p. 33). **A distractor screens only families who appear
> poor**, or asks about income directly. **The chapter's question is about the ability to
> meet basic needs, not about earnings.**

> **Trap note — build on strengths, and the strengths are named.**
> "cohesion, humor, support, and spiritual beliefs" (p. 33). **Humor is on the list**, which
> is unexpected and therefore a good distractor discriminator.

## 34. Land of origin and immigration status

in the domain of the home; thus calls for universal preschool are not heeded.**" (p. 34)

**The demographic shift** (p. 34): "**Children of color are disproportionately represented in
impoverished communities and schools, yet they will comprise the majority of children in 13 states
by 2020.**"

**And why the state matters** (p. 34): "**a child's chances for thriving depend not only on child,
family, and community characteristics, but also on the state in which the child lives because of
variations in health and social services available. State policy choices and investments can have
a tremendous impact on child health.**"

### Refugee and displaced children

**The scale** (p. 34): "**Nearly half of the world's displaced people are children, amounting to
over 20 million children.**"

**Who is included** (p. 34): "**both children and families who have fled their home countries and
those who are internally displaced within their home country.**"

**The double danger** (p. 34): "**they may have experienced danger and violence in both staying in
their homes and fleeing.**"

**Dangers on the journey** (p. 34): "**inclement weather, discrimination in destination countries,
potential loss of loved ones along the journey, further threats to safety in refugee camps, and
lack of food and water.**"

**For unaccompanied minors** (p. 34): "**they face heightened threats of abduction, trafficking,
sexual violence and exploitation, or abuse.**"

**The three-country pattern** (p. 34): "**children and families will have already fled one country,
arrived in a second country, and are resettled in a third country.**"

**And what that means for trauma** (p. 34): "**trauma and psychologic distress may come not only
from extraordinary threats of violence, but also from constant disruption to family life.**"

### The health picture

| Domain | The chapter's words | p. |
|---|---|---|
| Mental health | "**psychologic distress and mental illness are the major causes of disability among refugee children as they frequently experience posttraumatic stress, grief, anxiety, and depression**" | 34 |
| Physical health | "**the greatest risks stem from infectious diseases and malnutrition**" | 34 |
| The three infections | "**The leading causes of infectious disease related death among refugees are tuberculosis, malaria, and intestinal parasites**" | 34 |
| Why | "**often due to overcrowding, poor sanitation, or inability to institute standing transmission-prevention measures in campsites**" | 34 |

**The nursing adaptations** (p. 34): "**their assessment and treatment procedures may need to be
accommodated because of trauma and stress children have already experienced.**"

**The trafficking indicators** (p. 34): "**Nurses must also be aware of signs of exploitation or
abuse, indicating that a child or adolescent may be a victim of trafficking, such as elevated fear
or unexplained injuries.**"

**And the timeline for disclosure** (p. 34): "**The process of disentangling the child and family's
story may unfold over time after trust has been established.**"

> **Trap note — mental illness is the leading cause of disability, infection the leading
> cause of death.** Two different rankings for two different outcomes (p. 34). **A
> distractor merges them.** The three infections named are tuberculosis, malaria, and
> intestinal parasites.

> **Trap note — the story comes out over time, not at intake.** (p. 34) A distractor obtains
> a full trauma history at the first visit. **Trust precedes disclosure in this chapter**,
> exactly as with the closed-boundary family (p. 16).

### Undocumented immigrants

**The definition** (p. 34): "**foreign-born, non-citizens residing in a country, who do not have
legal documentation.**"

**The vulnerability** (p. 34): "**Because of their own or their parents' undocumented status,
children may be particularly vulnerable to limited access to health care.**"

**The associated harms** (p. 34): "**poverty, poor living and working conditions, food insecurity,
chronic stress, and ongoing fear of deportation and arrest.**"

**The utilization paradox** (p. 34): "**Despite the negative health effects, research demonstrates
that undocumented immigrants seek health care at lower rates than other groups.**"

**The named program and its barriers** (p. 34): "**Although programs such as the Migrant Health
Program exist to provide access to low-cost primary care, barriers families face in accessing this
care include language barriers, lack of transportation, difficulty arranging for time off from
work, and limited ability to pay.**"

### The health care system as a barrier

**The dual role** (p. 34): "**Although this can be a site of health promotion, health maintenance,
and illness management, it may also erect barriers for families to enact the care prescribed.**"

**The referral finding** (p. 34): "**Although child and family factors accounted for some influence
(child care, transport, inadequate insurance), health care system factors had a major impact on
completion of referrals.**"

**The three system factors** (p. 34): "**ability to get an appointment quickly, communication style
of front-desk staff, and interpreter services.**"

**And the opportunity** (p. 34): "**health care system factors may be most amenable to intervention
such as extended evening hours, clear directions to clinic site, and a unit navigator.**"

> **Trap note — the front desk is named as a determinant of referral completion.** (p. 34)
> So are appointment wait times and interpreter services. **A distractor attributes
> non-completion to family motivation or health literacy**; the chapter's finding is that
> **system factors dominate and are the most fixable.**

## 35. Religion and spiritual identity

### The two terms, and why the chapter separates them

**The framing** (p. 34): "**Religion and spirituality are powerful forces in the lives of
individuals, families, and communities. Spirituality and religion are important principles around
which families organize their responses to illness experiences and life transitions.**"

**The prevalence** (p. 34): "**nearly 80% of people report that religion is fairly important to
them.**"

**And the correction** (p. 34): "**The terms, however, are often conflated. Religion and
spirituality, although frequently grouped together, denote different entities.**"

| Term | Definition as given | p. |
|---|---|---|
| **Religion** | "a specific set of beliefs **enacted through a practice (religiosity)**" | 34 |
| **Spirituality** | "described as a **unique awareness, belief, practice, and experience starting in childhood and is rooted over time**" | 34 |

**What both do for children** (p. 34): "**Religion and spirituality are important avenues through
which children participate in meaning-making around important life events, such as illness in
themselves or loved ones and are shaped by the developmental stage of the child.**"

**Why assessment is required** (p. 34): "**spiritual assessment is necessary to provide
family-centered care that is aligned with spiritual and religious needs.**"

**And who requires it** (p. 34): "**many regulatory bodies that guide practice call for assessment
of and intervention for spiritual concerns, including the American Nurses Association, the
International Council of Nurses, and The Joint Commission.**"

> **Trap note — religion is a set of beliefs plus a practice; spirituality is an individual
> awareness.** (p. 34) **A distractor treats them as synonyms**, which the chapter
> explicitly corrects. Note also that spiritual assessment is a **regulatory requirement**,
> not an optional courtesy — three bodies are named.

### The individual and communal experience

**Both at once** (p. 34): "**children experience them from their own, individualized experience as
someone situated within a community, and they also experience what the community as a whole
experiences.**"

**The chapter's worked example** (p. 34): a child whose family practices Islam experiences "**the
religion within the home and what their own relationship to Allah means**", "**from the perspective
of being part of a community when attending mosque or participating in collective prayer**", and
"**being part of a faith community that may face stigma or bias or whose tenets are
misunderstood.**"

**The common thread** (p. 34): "**Most of the world's religions share a common thread of upholding
humility, charity, and honesty.**"

### Religion in parenting

**Why parents draw on it** (p. 34): "**When individuals become parents, they may connect to their
religion as a guide in raising their children, to enhance their well-being, and to provide a moral
and social context for their childrearing.**"

**And why practice varies so much** (pp. 34–35): "how this is enacted or operationalized varies
dramatically because of **variability in beliefs about parenting**" (p. 34) "**goals and roles, the
parent-child relationship, and child discipline and punishment.**" (p. 35)

### When families are isolated from their faith community

**The situation** (p. 35): "**children and families are often isolated from their faith communities
and experience crises for which they have no prior experience to draw on or that cause existential
questioning and meaning-seeking.**"

**The nursing action** (p. 35): "**Children and families receiving a new or life-threatening
diagnosis often experience fear and anxiety and may benefit from health care providers helping
them connect to those spiritual and religious practices that are important to them.**"

**The two concrete examples** (p. 35): "**nurses can help families find a suitable place to pray
and asking families which of their health care practices may be most helpful in addressing their
spiritual needs.**"

### Box 2.7, Guidelines for Integrating Spiritual Care Into Pediatric Nursing Practice (p. 35) — complete

| # | Guideline |
|---|---|
| 1 | "**Respect the child's and family's religious beliefs and practices.**" |
| 2 | "**Consider the child's development when talking about spiritual concerns.**" |
| 3 | "**Contact the institution's chaplaincy department for patients and families who have symptoms of spiritual distress or ask for specific religious rituals.**" |
| 4 | "**Become knowledgeable about the religious worldviews of cultural groups found in the patients you care for.**" |
| 5 | "**Encourage visitation with family members, members of the patient's spiritual community, and spiritual leaders.**" |
| 6 | "**Allow children and families to teach you about the specifics of their religious beliefs.**" |
| 7 | "**Develop awareness of your own spiritual perspective.**" |
| 8 | "**Listen for understanding rather than agreement or disagreement.**" |

> **Trap note — item 8 is the keyed stance.**
> "Listen for understanding rather than agreement or disagreement" (p. 35). **A distractor
> has the nurse agree with the family's beliefs so as not to offend** — which is exactly the
> distractor in the chapter's own NCLEX question 5, option C (p. 38). **Agreement is not
> required and is not the goal.**

> **Trap note — item 6 inverts the expertise.** The family teaches the nurse (p. 35). **A
> distractor has the nurse demonstrate knowledge of the family's religion**, which is the
> cultural-competence posture the chapter later criticizes for perpetuating stereotypes
> (p. 37). Item 4 asks the nurse to become knowledgeable *and* item 6 asks the nurse to let
> the family specify — both, in that order.

### What spirituality does for children

**In transition** (p. 35): "**Children who experience major life transitions, such as being
diagnosed with a life-threatening illness or the death of a loved one, often experience
spirituality as a source of hope and comfort that promotes their resilience and helps them discern
the meaning of the life transition for them.**"

**The research gap** (p. 35): "**there is a paucity of research on children's spiritual development
in a contemporary context.**"

**Adolescents and young adults** (p. 35): "**adolescents and young adults may be a greater risk for
spiritual distress because of their more sophisticated understanding of the impact and risk of
illness; diagnosis of a life-threatening illness can cause an internal spiritual struggle and
questioning of beliefs. They may engage less with institutionalized religion, but still use
spirituality as a means of finding meaning and connecting with family and loved ones.**"

### The key practices (p. 35)

| # | Practice |
|---|---|
| 1 | "**avoiding assumptions about spiritual beliefs and practices**" |
| 2 | "**asking how the child and family understand the illness's or life event's spiritual impact on the affected individual, other family members, and the family unit**" |
| 3 | "**taking time to explore one's own spiritual and religious perspective so that one's values can be clarified and judgments evaluated**" |
| 4 | "**active listening that is compassionate and keeps the child's spiritual needs at the center, without false reassurances**" |
| 5 | "**incorporating arts or storytelling into spiritual assessment and intervention**" |

**And the physical environment** (p. 35): "**Providing space in the physical environment for
children to participate in their spirituality in ways that bring them comfort and provide for
meaning-making opportunities is essential as it can provide a glimpse into their spiritual
distress and sources of hope.**"

### What children actually ask

**The finding** (p. 35): children facing life-threatening illnesses "**most often ask questions
about the time-limited nature of their illness, why this has happened to them, and why their
divine would allow this to happen to them.**"

**And how they describe what follows** (p. 35): "**children often described the after-life without
fear, sadness, or pain and with angels or predeceased loved ones caring for them in the
after-life.**"

**The nurse's position** (p. 35): nurses are in an "**ideal position to facilitate spiritual care
and communication between child and parents and child, family, and chaplain or some other
spiritual care provider.**"

**The two components any nurse can provide** (p. 35): "**supportive, caring presence to the child
and family and taking time to reflect on one's own spirituality or beliefs in order to meet the
needs of others.**"

**And the reassurance to nurses** (p. 35): "**Nurses need not share the belief system of a child or
family to sit with and support them in times of spiritual questioning or reflection.**"

**The four skills required** (p. 35): "**supportive, therapeutic listening, answering children's
questions about death and the afterlife, facilitating discussion between parents and children, and
preserving mindfulness and family relationships.**"

> **Trap note — you do not need to share the belief to provide the care.** Stated directly
> (p. 35). **A distractor has the nurse hand off all spiritual care to the chaplain**
> because of a difference in belief. Chaplaincy referral has specific triggers (Box 2.7,
> item 3); outside them, presence is the intervention.

> **Trap note — the three questions children ask are specific.** How long the illness will
> last, why it happened to them, and why their divine allowed it (p. 35). **A distractor
> supplies questions about treatment or about the parents.**

> **Trap note — the equity consequence of not asking.** If nurses do not listen and
> facilitate, "**they may inadvertently contribute to health inequities because**" (p. 35)
> "**nonwhite children and families may rely more on religion and spirituality during crises of life-threatening illness**" (p. 36).
> **Skipping spiritual care is framed as a disparity issue, not just a gap in comfort.**

### Parents' spiritual needs

**The primary concern** (p. 36): "**Often their primary spiritual concern is maintaining connection
to the sick child.**"

**What PICU parents reported mattered** (p. 36): "**access to clergy, prayers, and support of
religious practices in caring for their child's body after death were important.**"

**The enduring bond** (p. 36): "**the recognition and cultivation of the idea that the parent-child
relationship transcends death and is an enduring bond.**"

**And what prevents moral distress** (p. 36): "**open, honest, clear communication from their
child's health care providers can prevent moral or spiritual distress because they will believe
they had all of the information needed to make the right decisions for their child.**"

### Religion's mixed effects on children

**The domains affected** (p. 36): "**physical health, healthy behaviors, mental and emotional
health, participation in risky behaviors or demonstrating externalizing problems, and
academic/cognitive functioning.**"

| Direction | The chapter's words | p. |
|---|---|---|
| Positive, physical | "**positive effects resulting from the emphasis on healthy behaviors and respect for others**"; "**Religiosity and spirituality have been associated with adolescent substance use and older age at sexual debut**" | 36 |
| Negative, physical | "**deleterious effects may be associated with some religions that are more authoritarian and prohibit health care interventions, such as cases of religiously motivated medical neglect, when a child suffers injury or dies because parents did not seek treatment for a particular ailment, or when immunizations or screenings are declined**" | 36 |
| The contraception paradox | "**adolescents who identified as highly religious were less likely to use contraception at sexual debut, despite being older than nonreligious adolescents, putting them at risk for unplanned pregnancy or sexually transmitted infections**" | 36 |
| Positive, mental | "**connection to religion or spirituality has been associated with less adolescent depression, anxiety, and suicidality, as well as lower rates of criminal or delinquent behavior**" | 36 |
| Negative, mental | "**religion or spirituality can contribute to psychologic distress if youth are subjected to negative relationships or experiences that are imbued with demandingness and criticism**" | 36 |
| The reconciling principle | "**the social context in which the religion is practiced may be more important than the religion itself**" | 36 |
| Misuse | "**Religion can be misused as a way to inflict maltreatment, in forms of physical abuse, emotional abuse, medical neglect, and sexual abuse by those in positions of authority, though this is not the norm.**" | 36 |

**Why parents include religion** (p. 36): "**generally because it was an important part of their
lives and because they believe it will promote their child's well-being.**" And: "**Religion and
spirituality can help parents cope with the demands of parenting and help parents find meaning in
the role.**"

> **Trap note — the contraception paradox is the chapter's sharpest single finding here.**
> Highly religious adolescents debut later **and** use contraception less (p. 36), which
> nets out to risk. **A distractor treats religiosity as uniformly protective for sexual
> health.**

> **Trap note — the social context matters more than the religion.** (p. 36) This is the
> chapter's own reconciliation of its mixed findings and the safest general answer when an
> item asks whether religion helps or harms.

### Religious objection to medical care

**The AAP position** (p. 36): the AAP "**cites the intersection of religion, spirituality, and
health care as potentially harmful to children based on situations in which parents refuse medical
care for their children, and religious exemptions to child abuse and neglect laws.**"

**The governing principle** (p. 36): "**Although parents' refusal of medical care for their children
falls under the scope of parental authority, the child's best interest is priority. Failure to
provide essential health care is increasingly viewed as a form of neglect.**"

**The range of refusal** (p. 36): "**from refusal of all treatment to refusal of select
interventions or therapeutics, such as blood products.**"

**The balance** (p. 36): "**free exercise of religion should be balanced against protecting children
from harm.**"

**The future-autonomy argument** (p. 36): "**in a case where parents and health care providers
differ on the proposed benefit or burden of a treatment and the primary benefit is spiritual or
religious, children's future ability to decide the issue for themselves should be protected and
privileged.**"

**The Supreme Court's position** (p. 36): "**the free exercise of religion does not include the
liberty to subject communities or children to disease or children, in particular, to diminished
health or death.**"

**Where it gets murky** (p. 36): "**These arguments and considerations become murky when the
conditions are not life threatening or when the proposed treatment has significant adverse
effects, or the efficacy is limited.**"

**And the variation across states** (p. 36): "**Complicating this debate is variation in religious
exemption laws across states, creating additional uncertainty among families, health care
providers, and child protection agencies about when and why to intervene and potential
penalties.**"

**The chapter's caution to providers** (p. 36): "**we must walk this line carefully, as health care
providers are often in positions of power and families may feel vulnerable, limit their sharing
with health care providers, and ultimately withdraw from care. This hampers spiritual assessment
and diminishes communication between child, parent, and health care providers.**"

> **Trap note — the child's best interest is the priority, and refusal of essential care is
> increasingly neglect.** Both stated (p. 36). **But the chapter immediately warns against a
> confrontational posture**, because families withdraw from care. **The keyed nursing
> behavior is continued engagement and assessment, not immediate escalation** — escalation
> is a systems and legal question the chapter deliberately leaves unresolved.

### BELIEF, and the state of the evidence

**The screening approach** (p. 36): a focused and tiered screening "**allows for all children to
be screened for spiritual distress and identify immediate needs for support to conducting in-depth
spiritual assessment or accessing someone who can.**"

**The BELIEF mnemonic, complete** (p. 36):

| Letter | Element |
|---|---|
| B | Belief system |
| E | Ethics or values |
| L | Lifestyle |
| I | Involvement |
| E | Education |
| F | Future |

**Context matters** (p. 37): "**the effect of religion or spirituality may be more pronounced in an
isolated rural area versus a major metropolitan area with more heterogeneity in its population.**"

**The five limitations of the research** (p. 37): "**there are fewer studies about children and
religion versus adults and religion; it is hard to disentangle whether other variables interact
with or mediate the effect of religion on child health; whether the results are the same across
levels or doses of religiosity; what the meaning other participation in a religious or spiritual
community is for a child and how this translates into thoughts, beliefs, and behaviors ascertained
through qualitative or mixed-methods research; and greater emphasis on Judeo-Christian samples.**"

> **Trap note — BELIEF has six letters and two Es.** Belief system, Ethics or values,
> Lifestyle, Involvement, Education, Future (p. 36). **The two easiest to lose are
> Involvement and Education**, and a distractor substitutes something like *Faith* for the
> F, which is actually *Future*.

## 36. Cultural humility, and Box 2.8

### The problem with cultural competence

**Its stated goal** (p. 37): "**to improve the ability of health care providers and health care
systems to communicate effectively with and provide quality health care to patients from diverse
social backgrounds.**"

**Why communication matters** (p. 37): "**Effective communication with patients and families is
critical to positive health outcomes, yet many trainees do not feel prepared to engage with
patients and families whose backgrounds are different than their own.**"

**And the chapter's critique** (p. 37): "**Cultural competence is too limiting for this discussion
because it can perpetuate stereotypes, is often limited to race/ethnicity differences, and treats
those different from the white provider as other.**"

**The concrete failure** (p. 37): "**it is not helpful to demonstrate competence by lumping a group
of people together and applying an assumed set of beliefs onto them, such as Latinas/Latinos have
strong family support, as these lump understandings can become stereotypical and not illuminate
what is important for a particular patient and family in a particular context.**"

### Cultural humility

| Aspect | The chapter's words | p. |
|---|---|---|
| Definition | "**the process of self-reflection to promote honest and trustworthy relationships**" — "**knowing one's self better in order to know others**" | 37 |
| Not an endpoint | "**best defined not as a discrete end point but as a commitment and active engagement in a lifelong process that individuals enter into on an ongoing basis with patients, communities, colleagues, and with themselves**" | 37 |
| What it requires of the nurse | "**This critical consciousness leads the nurse to step back and examine his or her own beliefs, cultures, and positions in the world, as well as assumptions, biases, and values.**" | 37 |
| Not a class | "**Cultural humility cannot be collapsed into a class or education offering; rather it is viewed as an ongoing process.**" | 37 |
| Its posture | "**It emphasizes approaching clinical encounters with flexibility, courage, and openness versus competence or confidence.**" | 37 |
| Its scope | "**culture is not only race and ethnicity, but also social class, age, sexual orientation, sexuality, gender, ability, and professional discipline**" | 37 |
| Power | "**Assessing cultural impact through the lens of cultural humility also reminds us of power differentials that influence health care encounters.**" | 37 |
| Its core | "**Core to any work on cultural humility is the deliberate reflection on their values and biases.**" | 37 |
| Who is the expert | "**people are often the experts in their own experiences**" | 37 |

**Its named attributes** (p. 37): "**openness, self-awareness, egoless, supportive interactions,
self-reflection and critique.**"

**Its named consequences** (p. 37): "**mutual empowerment, partnership, respect, optimal care,
[and] lifelong learning.**"

**Where it applies** (p. 37): "**across nursing expertise levels and functions, from student nurse,
to nurse at the bedside, to advanced practice nurse, to professor and researcher.**"

**Mindfulness** (p. 37): "**Mindfulness can facilitate cultural humility by pulling nurses and
health care providers out of autopilot.**" And: "**Such mindful relationships can break down
stereotypes.**"

**And the historical acknowledgment** (p. 37): "**Cultural humility also attends to and
acknowledges the systemic maltreatment of certain groups of people at the hands of health care
providers/systems.**"

> **Trap note — competence versus humility is the cleanest confusable pair in the second
> half of the chapter.** **Competence: a skill set, an endpoint, knowledge about groups.
> Humility: a process, lifelong, self-reflective, and it locates expertise in the patient**
> (p. 37). This is exactly what the chapter's own NCLEX question 4 tests, where the
> *competence* option is keyed **wrong** (p. 38).

> **Trap note — culture is not only race and ethnicity.** The chapter's list adds
> "social class, age, sexual orientation, sexuality, gender, ability, and professional discipline" (p. 37).
> **A distractor limits culture to race, ethnicity, and religion.**

### Eliciting health beliefs

**The avenues of influence** (pp. 37–38): "**health beliefs (e.g., children should not know the
severity of their illness); health customs (e.g., care of the dead); ethnic customs (e.g., gender
roles and division of labor); religious beliefs (e.g., use of blood products); dietary customs
(e.g., maintaining a kosher diet); and interpersonal customs (e.g., how children address parents
or how touch is used in**" (p. 37) **communication).** (p. 38)

**The three opening questions** (p. 38): "**Is there anything you would like for me to know about
your child or your family so that I can take the best possible care of you all?**"; "**Can you tell
me about your condition (your child's condition)?**"; "**Do you have any special beliefs or things
you do or people you talk to when it comes to your (your child's) health?**"

**Their quality** (p. 38): "**These are example questions that are open-ended and can be adapted to
anyone the nurse is caring for at any time in any given location or context.**"

### Box 2.8, Exploring a Family's Culture, Illness, and Care (p. 38) — complete

| # | Question |
|---|---|
| 1 | "**What do you think caused your child's health problem?**" |
| 2 | "**Why do you think it started when it did?**" |
| 3 | "**How severe is your child's sickness? Will it have a short or long course?**" |
| 4 | "**How do you think your child's sickness affects your family?**" |
| 5 | "**What are the chief problems your child's sickness has caused?**" |
| 6 | "**What kind of treatment do you think your child should receive?**" |
| 7 | "**What are the most important results you hope to receive from your child's treatment?**" |

> **Trap note — every question asks what the FAMILY thinks.** All eight (p. 38). **None asks
> the nurse to supply information**, and none is answerable yes or no. This is the
> operational form of cultural humility, and it matches Chapter 20's keyed answer that
> cultural understanding is best accomplished by
> "Understanding the parents' perception of the seriousness or severity of the illness" (Ch.
> 20, p. 654).

## 38. The chapter's own NCLEX Review Questions, with rationales

Five questions, p. 38. The answer key is printed on p. 38 as "1. B, C, E; 2. C; 3. C; 4. B, C, D,
E; 5. D". **The source supplies no rationales**; the rationales below are built from the chapter's
own text with page cites, and where a keyed answer is not supported by this chapter, that is said
plainly.

### Question 1 (p. 38) — keyed **B, C, E**

**Stem:** "Duvall's Developmental Stages of the Family include which of the following? Select all
that apply."

| Option | Text as printed | Verdict |
|---|---|---|
| A | "Stages an individual progresses through in his or her moral and spiritual development" (p. 38) | **Wrong.** Duvall's stages are family stages, not individual moral development. The chapter attributes them to "**eight developmental tasks of the family**" (p. 17) |
| **B** | "Stages families progress through in adulthood" (p. 38) | **Correct.** Stages I through VIII run from marriage to aging families (p. 17), entirely in adulthood |
| **C** | "Stages that designate how parenting progresses as a child develops" (p. 38) | **Correct.** Stages II through VI are named by the children's stage: infants, preschoolers, schoolchildren, teenagers, launching (p. 17) |
| D | "Stages that designate appropriate discipline related to developmental stages" (p. 38) | **Wrong.** No stage in Box 2.1 concerns discipline (p. 17); discipline is a separate section (pp. 21–23) |
| **E** | "Stages that describe the journey a couple will take as their children mature" (p. 38) | **Correct.** Stage I is "Reestablish couple identity" and stage VII is "Reinvest in couple identity"; the couple thread runs throughout (p. 17) |

> **Trap note — the couple is present in the first and last stages.** Options B and E both
> key on the fact that **Duvall's cycle begins before children and continues after they
> leave** (p. 17). A distractor treats the stages as a child-development schedule, which is
> exactly what options A and D do.

### Question 2 (p. 38) — keyed **C**

**Stem:** "Family systems theory includes:"

| Option | Text as printed | Verdict |
|---|---|---|
| A | "Direct causality, meaning each change affects the whole family" (p. 38) | **Wrong.** The theory rests on **circular**, not direct, causality (p. 15). General systems theory specifically "expanded scientific thought from a simplistic view of direct cause and effect" (p. 15) |
| B | "Family systems react to changes as they take place, not initiate it" (p. 38) | **Wrong.** Table 2.1 states the opposite: "**Family systems can initiate change, as well as react to it.**" (p. 15) |
| **C** | "A balance between morphogenesis and morphostasis is necessary" (p. 38) | **Correct**, near-verbatim from Table 2.1 (p. 15) |
| D | "Theory is used primarily for family dysfunction and pathology" (p. 38) | **Wrong.** Table 2.1 lists as a strength that it is "**Applicable for family in normal everyday life, as well as for family dysfunction and pathology**" (p. 15) — not primarily the latter |

> **Trap note — every distractor is a one-word inversion of a Table 2.1 line.** Direct for
> circular, react for initiate, primarily for as-well-as (p. 15). **This is the cleanest
> example in the chapter of a question written directly off a table.**

### Question 3 (p. 38) — keyed **C**

**Stem:** "The nurse is explaining the strategy of consequences to a parent he is working with.
Which response by the parent indicates that more teaching is needed when he describes the types of
consequences?"

| Option | Text as printed | Verdict |
|---|---|---|
| A | "Natural: those that occur without any intervention" (p. 38) | **Correct definition**, verbatim from p. 22, so not the answer to a stem asking where more teaching is needed |
| B | "Logical: those that are directly related to the rule" (p. 38) | **Correct definition**, verbatim from p. 22 |
| **C** | "Transforming: allowing the child to come to the conclusion on his or her own" (p. 38) | **This is the keyed answer.** There is **no such type** in this chapter; the strategy "includes three types" and transforming is not one of them (p. 22) |
| D | "Unrelated: those that are imposed deliberately" (p. 38) | **Correct definition**, verbatim from p. 22 |

> **Trap note — read the stem direction.** This is a **negative-polarity** item: the keyed
> answer is the *incorrect* statement. Three options are verbatim-correct definitions; the
> fourth is an invented fourth category. **Knowing there are exactly three types is
> sufficient to answer it.**

### Question 4 (p. 38) — keyed **B, C, D, E**

**Stem:** "Culture includes which of the following? Select all that apply."

| Option | Text as printed | Verdict |
|---|---|---|
| A | "Cultural competence, which includes building skills in the health care provider, such as offering lists of common foods, health care beliefs, and important rituals" (p. 38) | **Wrong**, and the chapter says why: this list-based approach "**can perpetuate stereotypes**" and amounts to "lumping a group of people together and applying an assumed set of beliefs onto them" (p. 37) |
| **B** | "Cultural humility, which requires that health care providers participate in a continual process of self-reflection and self-critique" (p. 38) | **Correct**, near-verbatim from p. 37 |
| **C** | "Recognizing the power of the health care provider role that views the patient and family as full members of the health care team" (p. 38) | **Correct.** The chapter names "power differentials that influence health care encounters" and says "people are often the experts in their own experiences" (p. 37) |
| **D** | "A particular group with its values, beliefs, norms, patterns, and practices that are learned, shared, and transmitted from one generation to another" (p. 38) | **Keyed correct, but this definition does not appear in Chapter 2.** See the GAP register |
| **E** | "A complex whole in which each part is interrelated, including beliefs, tradition, lifeways, and heritage" (p. 38) | **Keyed correct, but this definition does not appear in Chapter 2.** See the GAP register |

> **Trap note — option A is the distractor precisely because it sounds like preparation.**
> Offering providers lists of common foods and rituals is the **cultural competence**
> posture the chapter criticizes (p. 37). **Any option that equips the nurse with
> group-level facts in advance is suspect in this chapter; options that put the family in
> the expert role are keyed.**

### Question 5 (p. 38) — keyed **D**

**Stem:** "Ways to integrate spiritual practices into nursing care include:"

| Option | Text as printed | Verdict |
|---|---|---|
| A | "Explaining the religious practices you personally take part in" (p. 38) | **Wrong.** Box 2.7 asks the nurse to "**Develop awareness of your own spiritual perspective**" (p. 35) — awareness, not disclosure. The chapter's general stance is that the nurse withholds personal opinion |
| B | "Realizing that young children have little understanding regarding their spirituality" (p. 38) | **Wrong.** Spirituality is described as "starting in childhood and is rooted over time" (p. 34), and Box 2.7 says to "**Consider the child's development when talking about spiritual concerns**" (p. 35) — consider it, not dismiss it |
| C | "Agreeing with children and their families when they explain their religious beliefs so they are not offended" (p. 38) | **Wrong**, and it is directly contradicted: "**Listen for understanding rather than agreement or disagreement.**" (p. 35) |
| **D** | "Becoming knowledgeable about the religious worldviews of cultural groups found in the patients you care for" (p. 38) | **Correct**, verbatim from Box 2.7 (p. 35) |

> **Trap note — option D and question 4's option A look like the same posture but are keyed
> oppositely.** Both involve the provider acquiring knowledge about groups. **The difference
> is what the knowledge is for**: Box 2.7 item 4 asks the nurse to become knowledgeable
> *and* item 6 asks the nurse to let families teach the specifics (p. 35), whereas question
> 4's option A substitutes a list for the family's own account. **Knowledge as background is
> endorsed; knowledge as a substitute for asking is not.**

> **Trap note — the chapter's spiritual-care stance in one line.** Know yourself, learn the
> background, ask the family, listen without agreeing or disagreeing, refer when there is
> distress or a ritual request (p. 35). **Every distractor in question 5 violates exactly
> one of those.**
