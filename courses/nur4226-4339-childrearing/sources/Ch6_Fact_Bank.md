## 1. Infection control: HAIs and Standard Precautions

### The scale of the problem

| Fact | The chapter's words | p. |
|---|---|---|
| Incidence | "**approximately 2 million patients each year develop hospital-acquired infections (HAIs)**" | 169 |
| Cost | "**The overall cost of HAIs to US hospitals is $96 billion to $147 billion annually.**" | 169 |
| Mechanism | "**These infections occur when there is interaction among patients, health care personnel, equipment, and bacteria.**" | 169 |
| Preventability | "**HAIs are preventable if caregivers practice meticulous hand washing, cleaning, and disposal techniques.**" | 169 |

### Standard Precautions

**What they combine** (p. 169): they "synthesize the major features of **universal (i.e., blood
and body fluid) precautions (designed to reduce the risk of transmission of blood-borne
pathogens) and body substance isolation (designed to reduce the risk of transmission of
pathogens from moist body substances).**"

**What they involve** (p. 169): "the use of **barrier protection (personal protective equipment
[PPE]), such as gloves, goggles, gowns, and masks**, to prevent contamination from **blood; all
body fluids, secretions, and excretions, except sweat, regardless of whether they contain
visible blood; nonintact skin; and mucous membranes.**"

**Who they are for** (p. 169): "**Standard Precautions are designed for the care of all patients
to reduce the risk of transmission of microorganisms from both recognized and unrecognized
sources of infection.**"

> **Trap note — the one exception is sweat.** Standard Precautions cover every body fluid,
> secretion, and excretion **except sweat** (p. 169). That single exclusion is the detail an
> item tests.

> **Trap note — all patients and "unrecognized sources" are the whole point.** This sentence
> is the answer to the chapter's own NCLEX question 1 (p. 194), which quotes it almost
> verbatim. **Standard Precautions are not reserved for known infections.**

### The 2007 additions

"In 2007 the Centers for Disease Control and Prevention recommended adding **Respiratory
Hygiene/Cough Etiquette and safe injection practices** to Standard Precautions." (p. 169)

| Addition | What it does | p. |
|---|---|---|
| Respiratory Hygiene/Cough Etiquette | "stresses the importance of **source control measures to contain respiratory secretions to prevent droplet and fomite transmission of viral respiratory tract infections such as respiratory syncytial virus, influenza, and adenovirus**" | 169 |
| Safe injection practices | "involve the use of **safety-engineered sharp devices to prevent sharps injury**" | 169 |

### Hand hygiene

**The ranking** (p. 169): "**Hand hygiene continues to be the single most important practice to
reduce the transmission of infectious diseases in health care settings.**"

**What it includes** (p. 169): "**hand washing with soap and water, as well as the use of
alcohol-based products for hand disinfection.**"

**And restated for resistant organisms** (p. 170): "**Hand washing is the most critical infection
control practice.**"

> **Trap note — hand hygiene is named the single most important practice, twice.** Once
> generally (p. 169) and once specifically for antimicrobial-resistant organisms (p. 170).
> **In any infection-control priority item in this chapter, hand hygiene is the default
> answer.**

## 2. Transmission-Based Precautions, and Box 6.1

**Who they are for** (p. 169): Transmission-Based Precautions are "**designed for patients with
documented or suspected infection or colonization**" and specifically for pathogens that are
"**highly transmissible or epidemiologically important**."

**The three types** (p. 169): "**(1) Airborne Precautions, (2) Droplet Precautions, and (3)
Contact Precautions.**"

**Two rules about how they are used** (p. 169):

1. "**They may be combined for diseases that have multiple routes of transmission.**"
2. "**They are to be used in addition to Standard Precautions.**"

> **Trap note — Transmission-Based Precautions are additive, never a substitute.** Stated
> explicitly (p. 169), and Box 6.1 repeats "In addition to Standard Precautions" at the head
> of every category. **A distractor replaces Standard Precautions with a transmission-based
> type.**

### Box 6.1, Types of Precautions and Patients Requiring Them (p. 169) — complete

#### Respiratory Hygiene/Cough Etiquette

**The measures** (p. 169): "**covering the mouth and nose during coughing and sneezing; offering
a surgical mask to persons who are coughing; using tissues to contain respiratory secretions;
turning the head away from others and keeping a space of 3 feet or more when coughing.**"

**When they start** (p. 169): "**These measures should be used on entry to the health care
institution for patients and visitors or family members who have symptoms of respiratory
infection.**"

#### Airborne Precautions

**What is required** (p. 169): "use Airborne Precautions and **an airborne infection isolation
room (AIIR)** for patients known or suspected to have serious illnesses transmitted by
**airborne droplet nuclei.**"

**The three named examples** (p. 169): "**measles, varicella (including disseminated zoster), and
tuberculosis.**"

#### Droplet Precautions

**For illnesses transmitted by** (p. 169) "**large particle droplets**". The examples:

| Category | Examples as given |
|---|---|
| Bacterial, invasive | "**Invasive Haemophilus influenzae type b disease, including meningitis, pneumonia, epiglottitis, and sepsis**"; "**Invasive Neisseria meningitidis disease, including meningitis, pneumonia, and sepsis**" |
| Bacterial, respiratory | "**diphtheria (pharyngeal), mycoplasmal pneumonia, pertussis, pneumonic plague, streptococcal pharyngitis, pneumonia, or scarlet fever in infants and young children**" |
| Viral | "**adenovirus, influenza, mumps, human parvovirus B19, and rubella**" |

#### Contact Precautions

**For illnesses** (p. 169) "**easily transmitted by direct patient contact or by contact with
items in the patient's environment.**" The examples:

| Category | Examples as given |
|---|---|
| Multidrug-resistant | "**Gastrointestinal, respiratory, skin, or wound infections or colonization with multidrug-resistant bacteria**" |
| Enteric | "**Clostridium difficile**"; and for diapered or incontinent patients, "**enterohemorrhagic Escherichia coli 0157:H7, Shigella organisms, hepatitis A, or rotavirus**" |
| Viral respiratory in young children | "**Respiratory syncytial virus, parainfluenza virus, or enteroviral infections in infants and young children**" |
| Skin | "**diphtheria (cutaneous), herpes simplex virus (neonatal or mucocutaneous), impetigo, major (noncontained) abscesses, cellulitis or decubitus, pediculosis, scabies, staphylococcal furunculosis in infants and young children, zoster (disseminated or in the immunocompromised host)**" |
| Eye | "**Viral or hemorrhagic conjunctivitis**" |
| Hemorrhagic fevers | "**Viral hemorrhagic infections (Ebola, Lassa, or Marburg)**" |

> **Trap note — diphtheria appears under two different precaution types, split by site.**
> **Pharyngeal diphtheria is Droplet; cutaneous diphtheria is Contact** (p. 169). Table 6.2
> repeats the split:
> "Follow Standard and Droplet Precautions until two cultures are negative for C. diphtheriae; **use Contact Precautions with cutaneous manifestations**" (p. 179).
> This is the cleanest site-dependent precaution item in the chapter.

> **Trap note — varicella and zoster are both Airborne, and zoster is qualified.** Airborne
> covers "varicella (**including disseminated zoster**)" while Contact covers
> "zoster (**disseminated or in the immunocompromised host**)" (p. 169). **Disseminated
> zoster appears in both lists**, which is consistent: varicella in Table 6.2 requires
> "Standard, Airborne, and Contact Precautions" together (p. 179).

## 3. The three routes, and the mechanisms behind them

Learning the mechanism makes every precaution assignment in the chapter derivable.

| | **Airborne** | **Droplet** | **Contact** |
|---|---|---|---|
| What travels | "**airborne droplet nuclei** (i.e., small-particle residue [≤5 mm] of evaporated droplets that may remain suspended in the air for long periods) or **dust particles containing the infectious agent**" (p. 169) | "**large-particle droplets (>5 mm) containing microorganisms**" (p. 169) | microorganisms transmitted "**by direct or indirect contact**" (p. 169) |
| How far | "can be **dispersed widely by air currents** and may become inhaled by or deposited on a susceptible host **within the same room or over a longer distance** from the source patient" (p. 169) | "droplets **do not remain suspended in the air and generally travel only short distances, usually 3 feet or less**, through the air" (p. 169) | requires contact, not distance |
| Special air handling | "**Special air handling and ventilation are required**" (p. 169) | "**special air handling and ventilation are not required**" (p. 169) | not applicable |
| How generated | evaporated droplets and dust | "**primarily during coughing, sneezing, or talking and during procedures such as suctioning and bronchoscopy**" (p. 169) | "**skin-to-skin contact and physical transfer of microorganisms**" (p. 169) |
| Direct versus indirect | — | — | **Direct:** "**skin-to-skin contact and physical transfer of microorganisms to a susceptible host from an infected or colonized person, such as occurs when turning or bathing patients**"; it "**also can occur between two patients**" (p. 169). **Indirect:** "**contact of a susceptible host with a contaminated intermediate object, usually inanimate, in the patient's environment**" (p. 170) |

> **Trap note — 5 mm is the dividing line, and the direction matters.** **Droplet nuclei are
> ≤5 mm and stay airborne; droplets are >5 mm and fall within about 3 feet** (p. 169). Every
> other difference follows: only the airborne route needs special ventilation, and only the
> airborne route reaches beyond the room.

> **Trap note — 3 feet is the number, and it appears twice.** As the droplet travel distance
> (p. 169) and as the cough-etiquette spacing,
> "keeping a space of 3 feet or more when coughing" (p. 169). **Same number, two uses.**

> **Trap note — direct contact can be patient to patient.** The chapter names it (p. 169),
> not just staff to patient. In a scenario about two children sharing a room, that is the
> transmission route in play.

## 4. Practical infection control with young children

**The exposure** (p. 170): "**Nurses caring for young children are frequently in contact with body
substances, especially urine, feces, and vomitus.**"

**The judgment call, stated as one** (p. 170): "**Nurses need to exercise judgment concerning
those situations when gloves, gowns, or masks are necessary.**"

**The worked example** (p. 170): "**wear gloves and possibly gowns for changing diapers when there
are loose or explosive stools. Otherwise, the plastic lining of disposable diapers provides a
sufficient barrier between the hands and body substances and gloves are adequate.**"

**During feedings** (p. 170): "**wear gowns if the child is likely to vomit or spit up, which often
occurs during burping.**"

**The glove caveat** (p. 170): "**When wearing gloves, wash the hands thoroughly after removing the
gloves because gloves fail to provide complete protection. The absence of visible leaks does not
indicate that gloves are intact.**"

> **Trap note — gloves do not replace hand washing, and intact-looking gloves may not be
> intact.** Both halves (p. 170). **A distractor treats glove use as sufficient.**

### Resistant organisms

**The trend** (p. 170): "**Antimicrobial-resistant organisms are causing increasing numbers of
HAIs.**"

**MRSA specifics** (p. 170): "**In hospitals, patients are the most significant sources of
methicillin-resistant Staphylococcus aureus (MRSA), and the main mode of transmission is
patient-to-patient transmission via the hands of a health care provider.**"

> **Trap note — the source is the patient; the vehicle is the provider's hands.** The
> chapter separates them (p. 170). **A distractor names the environment or equipment as the
> main mode.**

### Sharps

**The rule** (p. 170): "**all needles (uncapped and unbroken) are disposed of in a rigid,
puncture-resistant container located near the site of use.**"

**Where the containers go, and the pediatric complication** (p. 170): "these containers are
installed in each patient's room. **Because children are naturally curious, extra attention is
needed in selecting a suitable type of container and a location that prevents access to the
discarded needles.**"

**Needleless systems** (p. 170): they allow "**secure syringe or intravenous (IV) tubing
attachment to vascular access devices without the risk of needle stick injury to the child or
nurse.**"

**Nursing Alert on equipment** (p. 170): "**The most common piece of medical equipment, the
stethoscope, can be a potent source of harmful microorganisms and nosocomial infections. Consider
also the keyboard and desktop as potential sources.**"

> **Trap note — uncapped and unbroken.** Needles go into the container **without recapping
> and without breaking** (p. 170). A distractor recaps before disposal.

> **Trap note — the container location is a pediatric safety problem, not just a
> convenience.** Near the point of use **and** out of a curious child's reach (p. 170).
> **Both constraints must be satisfied at once**, which is what makes it a
> pediatric-specific item.

## 5. Immunizations: scope, and Box 6.2

**The achievement** (p. 170): "**One of the most dramatic advances in pediatrics has been the
decline of infectious diseases during the twentieth century because of the widespread use of
immunization for preventable diseases.**"

**The schedule shape** (p. 171): "**Although many of the immunizations can be given to individuals
of any age, the recommended primary schedule begins during infancy and, with the exception of
boosters, is completed during early childhood.**"

**The vaccines this section covers** (p. 171): "**diphtheria, tetanus, and acellular pertussis
(DTaP); poliovirus; measles, mumps, and rubella (MMR); Haemophilus influenzae type b (Hib);
hepatitis B virus (HBV); hepatitis A virus (HAV); meningococcal; pneumococcal conjugate vaccine
(PCV); influenza (and H1N1); varicella-zoster virus (VZV; chickenpox); rotavirus; and human
papillomaviruses.**"

### The vaccination-versus-immunization distinction

**The chapter's own caution** (p. 171): "**Although in this discussion the terms vaccination and
immunization are used interchangeably in reference to active immunization, they are not synonymous
because the administration of an immunobiologic such as a vaccine cannot automatically be equated
with the development of adequate immunity.**"

> **Trap note — giving the vaccine is not the same as producing immunity.** The chapter says
> so explicitly (p. 171), and Box 6.2's definitions preserve the distinction: **vaccination
> is "the physical act of administering" ; immunization is
> "the process of inducing or providing" immunity** (p. 171). A distractor treats them as
> synonyms, which the chapter does in casual use and then disavows.

### The term pairs worth holding

Box 6.2 is reproduced in full in the key-terms table above. **These are the pairs an item will
build on**, all on p. 171.

| Pair | The discriminator |
|---|---|
| **Natural vs. acquired immunity** | Innate resistance vs. immunity from exposure to the agent |
| **Active vs. passive immunity** | Immune bodies actively formed by the person vs. "Temporary immunity obtained by transfusing immunoglobulins or antitoxins" |
| **Vaccine vs. toxoid** | A suspension of live or inactivated organisms vs. "A modified bacterial toxin that has been made nontoxic" |
| **Antitoxin vs. toxoid** | Antibodies from immunized animal serum, conferring passive immunity vs. a modified toxin producing active immunity |
| **Immunoglobulin vs. specific immunoglobulin** | Antibodies from large pools of plasma vs. pools "preselected for a high antibody content against a specific antigen" |
| **Monovalent vs. polyvalent vs. combination** | One antigen vs. two or more antigens vs. multiple vaccines in one parenteral form |
| **Conjugate vaccine** | A carrier protein joined to "a less antigenic polysaccharide antigen to enhance the type and magnitude of the immune response" |

> **Trap note — active immunity can be natural or artificial, and so can passive.** Both
> definitions carry both routes (p. 171): active naturally by having the disease or
> artificially by vaccine; passive artificially by transfusion or **naturally from the
> mother to the fetus via the placenta.** **Maternal antibody is natural passive immunity**,
> and a distractor calls it active or purely artificial.

> **Trap note — herd immunity protects the unvaccinated.** The definition names them
> explicitly:
> "the population that has been vaccinated protects those in the same population who are unvaccinated" (p. 171).
> **A distractor defines it as universal vaccination or as individual protection.**

> **Trap note — cocooning is defined in Box 6.2 and is pertussis-specific.**
> "protecting infants from pertussis by vaccinating all persons who come in close contact with the infant, including the mother, grandparents, and health care workers" (p. 171).
> **The infant is not the one vaccinated** — that is the whole idea, and it is why the
> strategy exists for a disease whose vaccine cannot be given early enough.

## 8. Hepatitis B and hepatitis A

### Hepatitis B

**Why it matters in childhood** (p. 172): "**HBV is a significant pediatric disease because HBV
infections that occur during childhood and adolescence can lead to fatal consequences from
cirrhosis or liver cancer during adulthood.**"

**The chronicity figures** (p. 172): "**Up to 90% of infants infected perinatally and 25% to 50% of
children infected before age 5 years become chronically infected.**"

### The four maternal and weight scenarios

| Scenario | What is given | p. |
|---|---|---|
| Mother HBsAg **negative**, term infant | "**newborns receive HepB before hospital discharge**" | 172 |
| Mother HBsAg **positive or unknown**, term or preterm | "**should receive HepB and hepatitis B immune globulin (HBIG), 0.5 ml, within 12 hours of birth at two different injection sites**" | 172 |
| Infant **under 2000 grams**, mother HBsAg negative | "**the first HepB dose should be given to such infants at a chronologic age of 1 month**" | 172 |
| Preterm infant already given a birth dose | "**the infant be given the full series (three additional doses) at 1, 2, and 6 months of age**" | 172 |

**Why the weight matters** (p. 172): "**the immune response to HepB is not optimum in newborns
weighing less than 2000 grams (4.4 pounds).**"

**Which formulation when** (p. 172): "**Monovalent HepB should be given as the birth dose, whereas
combination vaccine containing HepB may be given for subsequent doses in the series.**"

**Site** (p. 172): "**intramuscularly in the vastus lateralis in newborns or in the deltoid for
older infants and children. Regardless of age, avoid the dorsogluteal site because it has been
associated with low antibody seroconversion rates, indicating a reduced immune response.**" And:
"**No data exist regarding the seroconversion when the ventrogluteal site is used.**"

> **Trap note — HBIG plus vaccine, within 12 hours, at two different sites.** Four specifics
> in one sentence (p. 172): the product, the dose (0.5 ml), the window (12 hours), and the
> two-site rule. **This is the highest-yield newborn item in the chapter.**

> **Trap note — the dorsogluteal site is avoided for an immunologic reason here, not a
> neurologic one.** "low antibody seroconversion rates" (p. 172). The Atraumatic Care box
> gives the other reason: "**the potential for sciatic nerve damage**" (p. 176). **Both
> reasons appear in this chapter, on different pages.**

> **Trap note — under 2000 g the birth dose waits, but only if the mother is HBsAg
> negative.** The condition is explicit (p. 172). **A positive or unknown maternal status
> overrides the weight rule** and the infant gets vaccine plus HBIG within 12 hours
> regardless.

### Hepatitis A

**Transmission** (p. 172): "**HAV is spread by the fecal-oral route and from person-to-person
contact, by ingestion of contaminated food or water, and, rarely, by blood transfusion.**"

**Presentation** (p. 172): "**The illness has an abrupt onset, with fever, malaise, anorexia,
nausea, abdominal discomfort, dark urine, and jaundice being the most common clinical signs.**"

**In young children** (p. 172): "**In children under 6 years of age, who represent approximately
one-third of all cases of hepatitis A, the disease may be asymptomatic, and jaundice is rarely
evident.**"

**The result of vaccination** (p. 172): "**Since the implementation of widespread childhood HepA
vaccination, infection rates among children ages 5 to 14 years have declined significantly.**"

> **Trap note — young children with hepatitis A are often asymptomatic and rarely
> jaundiced.** And they are a third of all cases (p. 172). **That combination is what makes
> them efficient transmitters**, and it is the argument for universal childhood vaccination.

## 9. Diphtheria, tetanus, pertussis, and cocooning

### Diphtheria

**Its rarity and its severity** (p. 172): "**Although cases of diphtheria are rare in the United
States, the disease can result in significant morbidity.**"

**Manifestations** (p. 172): respiratory ones include "**respiratory nasopharyngitis or obstructive
laryngotracheitis with upper airway obstruction**"; cutaneous ones "**include vaginal, otic,
conjunctival, or cutaneous lesions, which are primarily seen in urban homeless persons and in the
tropics.**"

**The treatment, and its urgency** (p. 172): "**Administer a single dose of equine antitoxin
intravenously to the child with clinical symptoms because of the often fulminant progression of
the disease.**"

**The five formulations diphtheria vaccine comes in** (p. 172):

| # | Formulation |
|---|---|
| 1 | "**in combination with tetanus and pertussis vaccines (DTaP) or DTaP and Hib vaccines for children younger than 7 years of age**" |
| 2 | "**in combination with a conjugate H. influenzae type B vaccine**" |
| 3 | "**in a combined vaccine with tetanus (DT) for children younger than 7 years of age who have some contraindication to receiving pertussis vaccine**" |
| 4 | "**in combination with tetanus and acellular pertussis (Tdap) for children 11 years and older**" |
| 5 | "**as a single antigen when combined antigen preparations are not indicated**" |

**Duration and boosters** (p. 172): "**Although the diphtheria vaccine does not produce absolute
immunity, protective antitoxin persists for 10 years or more when given according to the
recommended schedule, and boosters are given every 10 years for life.**"

**And the crucial caveat** (p. 172): "**Several vaccines contain diphtheria toxoid (e.g., Hib,
meningococcal, pneumococcal), but this does not confer immunity to the disease.**"

> **Trap note — diphtheria toxoid as a carrier protein does not immunize against
> diphtheria.** Stated on p. 172 and repeated as a Nursing Alert on p. 174:
> "**The use of meningococcal and diphtheria proteins in combination vaccines does not mean the child has received adequate immunization for meningococcal or diphtheria illnesses; the child must be given the appropriate vaccine for that specific disease.**"
> **The chapter makes this point twice**, which marks it as a known misconception.

> **Trap note — DT versus DTaP.** **DT is for children under 7 who have a contraindication
> to pertussis** (p. 172). The lowercase letters signal reduced antigen content in adult and
> adolescent formulations (Td, Tdap). **A distractor gives DTaP to a child with a pertussis
> contraindication.**

### Tetanus

**The three forms, and the one that is gone** (p. 172): "**tetanus toxoid, tetanus immunoglobulin
(TIG) (human), and tetanus antitoxin (equine antitoxin) — are available; however, tetanus
antitoxin is no longer available in the United States.**"

**Routine use** (p. 172): "**Tetanus toxoid is used for routine primary immunization, usually in one
of the combinations listed for diphtheria, and provides protective antitoxin levels for
approximately 10 years.**"

**Wound management** (p. 173): "**For wound management, passive immunity is available with TIG.
Persons with a history of two previous doses of tetanus toxoid can receive a booster dose of the
toxoid. Separate syringes and different sites are used when tetanus toxoid and TIG are given
concurrently.**"

**For children over 7** (p. 173): "**tetanus immunization may be accomplished by administering Td
(adult-type diphtheria and tetanus toxoids).**"

> **Trap note — toxoid and immunoglobulin go in separate syringes at separate sites.**
> Stated for tetanus (p. 173) and repeated for the equine antitoxin. **The same principle
> governs HepB and HBIG** (p. 172) and any vaccine given with immunoglobulin (p. 177).
> **When active and passive products are given together, they are always separated.**

### The Tdap age rules

These are the most intricate age rules in the chapter. All on **pp. 172–173**.

| Group | Rule |
|---|---|
| Ages **11 to 12** | "**Tetanus and diphtheria toxoids along with acellular pertussis vaccine (Tdap) are now recommended for persons ages 11 to 12 years who have completed the recommended DTaP/DTP vaccine series but have not received the tetanus (Td) booster dose.**" (p. 172) |
| Ages **13 to 18** | "**Adolescents 13 to 18 years**" (p. 172) "**of age who have not received the Td/Tdap booster should receive a single Tdap booster, provided the routine DTaP/DTP childhood immunization series has been previously received.**" (p. 173) |
| Ages **7 to 10** | "**children ages 7 to 10 years who are not fully vaccinated for pertussis (i.e., did not receive 5 doses of DTaP or 4 doses of DTaP with the fourth dose being administered on or after the fourth birthday) should receive a dose of Tdap.**" (p. 173) |
| Thereafter | "**children receive subsequent Td boosters every 10 years.**" (p. 173) |
| Interval | "**When the Tdap is used as a booster dose, it may be administered regardless of the interval from the previous tetanus, diphtheria, and pertussis-containing vaccine.**" (p. 173) |

**The two licensed products and their age ranges** (p. 173): "**Boostrix (Tdap) is currently
licensed for persons 10 years of age (including those ≥65 years of age) and older, whereas Adacel
(Tdap) is licensed for individuals 10 to 64 years of age.**"

> **Trap note — Tdap needs no minimum interval from the last tetanus-containing vaccine.**
> (p. 173) This is counterintuitive and is what allows a booster to be given at an
> unscheduled visit. **A distractor imposes a waiting period.**

> **Trap note — "fully vaccinated for pertussis" has a precise definition.**
> "**5 doses of DTaP or 4 doses of DTaP with the fourth dose being administered on or after the fourth birthday**" (p. 173).
> The chapter states it twice. **A four-dose child whose fourth dose came before the fourth
> birthday is NOT fully vaccinated.**

### Pertussis

**The age window for the pediatric vaccine** (p. 173): "**Pertussis vaccine is recommended for all
children 6 weeks through 6 years of age (up to the seventh birthday) who have no neurologic
contraindications to its use.**"

**Where the cases are** (p. 173): "**Many cases of pertussis have occurred in children less than 6
months or persons over 7 years, both groups falling in the category for which pertussis
immunization previously was not recommended.**"

### Cocooning

| Element | The chapter's words | p. |
|---|---|---|
| The pregnancy recommendation | "**pregnant adolescents and women who are not protected against pertussis receive the Tdap vaccine optimally between 27 and 36 weeks of gestation or postpartum before discharge from the hospital**" | 173 |
| Breastfeeding | "**breastfeeding is not a contraindication to Tdap vaccination**" | 173 |
| Since when | "**The concept of cocooning has been promoted since 2006 to reduce the spread of pertussis to vulnerable infants.**" | 173 |
| Who is vaccinated | "**vaccinating pregnant women during or after pregnancy, as well as all persons who will have close contact with the infants (including health care workers, fathers, and adults [especially those ages 65 years and older])**" | 173 |
| Its limitation | "**Cocooning can prevent pertussis in vulnerable infants; however, actual implantation of the cocooning strategy among all family members is difficult.**" | 173 |

> **Trap note — 27 to 36 weeks of gestation.** The specific window (p. 173), and it is keyed
> in the chapter's own NCLEX question 3 (p. 194). **The answer is Tdap, not Td and not
> DTaP.**

### Whole-cell versus acellular

| | Whole-cell | Acellular |
|---|---|---|
| Composition | "**prepared from inactivated cells of Bordetella pertussis and contains multiple antigens**" (p. 173) | "**contains one or more immunogens derived from the B. pertussis organism**" (p. 173) |
| Reactions | more | "**associated with fewer local and systemic reactions**" (p. 173) |
| Use | may be used for the fourth and fifth doses | "**recommended by the American Academy of Pediatrics (2015) for the first three immunizations**", "usually given at 2, 4, and 6 months" (p. 173) |

**The manufacturer rule** (p. 173): "**It is also recommended that the first three DTaP vaccinations
be from the same manufacturer. The fourth dose may be from a different manufacturer.**"

**And mixed series are acceptable** (p. 173): "**The child who has received one or more whole-cell
vaccines may complete the series of five with the acellular vaccine.**"

**In unvaccinated infants** (p. 173): "**The diagnosis of pertussis may be missed or delayed in
unvaccinated infants, who often are seen with respiratory distress and apnea without the typical
cough.**"

> **Trap note — same manufacturer for the first three, any manufacturer for the fourth.**
> (p. 173) A specific, checkable rule that a distractor generalizes to all five doses.

> **Trap note — the infant with pertussis may have no cough at all.** Respiratory distress
> and apnea instead (p. 173), and Table 6.2 confirms: infants under 6 months
> "**may not have characteristic whoop cough**" (p. 182). **The absence of the whoop does
> not rule out pertussis.**

## 10. Polio, measles, mumps, and rubella

### Polio

**The schedule** (p. 173): "**All children should receive four doses of IPV at 2 months, 4 months,
6 to 18 months, and 4 to 6 years of age.**"

**Why the change from OPV** (p. 173): "**The change from the exclusive use of oral polio vaccine
(OPV) to the exclusive use of IPV is related to the rare risk of vaccine-associated polio paralysis
(VAPP) from OPV.**"

**The trade-off, stated honestly** (p. 173): "**The exclusive use of IPV eliminates the risk of VAPP
but is associated with an increased number of injections and increased cost.**"

**The result** (p. 173): "**Since IPV usage was instituted in the United States in 2000, no new
indigenously acquired cases of VAPP have occurred.**"

### The combination vaccines containing IPV

| Product | Contents | Licensed for | p. |
|---|---|---|---|
| **Pediarix** | "**DTaP, hepatitis B, and IPV**" | "**as young as 6 weeks**"; "may be used as the primary immunization beginning at 2 months of age" | 173 |
| **Kinrix** | **DTaP and IPV** | "**the fifth dose in the DTaP series and the fourth dose in the IPV series in children ages 4 to 6 years**" | 173 |
| **Pentacel** | "**DTaP, Hib, and inactivated poliovirus**" | "**infants 4 weeks old and older**" | 173 |

> **Trap note — three combination products, three different contents.** Pediarix has
> hepatitis B; Pentacel has Hib; Kinrix has neither and is a 4-to-6-year product only
> (p. 173). **The discriminator is what is bundled with DTaP and IPV.**

### Measles

| Rule | The chapter's words | p. |
|---|---|---|
| Routine first dose | "**The measles (rubeola) vaccine is given at 12 to 15 months of age.**" | 173 |
| During outbreaks | "**the vaccine can be given at 6 to 11 months of age, followed by a second inoculation after age 12 months**" | 173 |
| Second dose | "**recommended at 4 to 6 years of age (at school entry) but may be given earlier provided that 4 weeks have elapsed since the administration of the previous dose**" | 173 |
| If missed | "**Revaccination should occur by 11 to 12 years of age if the measles**" (p. 173) "**vaccine was not administered at school entry (4 to 6 years).**" (p. 174) |
| Vaccinated before 12 months | "**Any child who is vaccinated before 12 months of age should receive two additional doses beginning at 12 to 15 months and separated by at least 4 weeks.**" | 174 |
| The 1956 line | "**Revaccination should include all individuals born after 1956 who have not received two doses of measles vaccine after 12 months of age. Individuals born before this date are thought to be immune from exposure to natural measles virus.**" | 174 |
| Postexposure | "**one dose of MMR may be administered within 72 hours of exposure in vaccine-eligible individuals ≥12 months of age and is preferable to immunoglobulin**" | 174 |

**Vitamin A** (p. 174): "**Vitamin A supplementation has been effective in decreasing the morbidity
and mortality risks associated with measles in developing countries.**"

> **Trap note — a dose given before 12 months does not count.** Two additional doses are
> still needed, starting at 12 to 15 months (p. 174). **The same principle appears for
> mumps**: the vaccine
> "should not be administered to infants younger than 12 months because persisting maternal antibodies can interfere with the immune response" (p. 174).
> **Maternal antibody is the mechanism**, and it explains both rules.

> **Trap note — 72 hours for postexposure MMR, and it beats immunoglobulin.** Both halves
> (p. 174). A distractor gives immunoglobulin first or extends the window.

> **Trap note — two different birth-year cutoffs in adjacent sections.** **Measles: born
> after 1956** (p. 174). **Mumps: born after 1957** (p. 174). They are one year apart and
> easy to conflate.

### MMRV

**What it is** (p. 174): "**The measles, mumps, rubella, and varicella (MMRV) vaccine is an
attenuated live virus vaccine and may be given to children 12 months to 15 months of age or at 4
through 6 years of age concurrent with other vaccines.**"

**The HIV contraindication** (p. 174): "**Children with HIV should not receive the MMRV vaccine
because of a lack of evidence of its safety in this population.**"

**The febrile seizure risk, and how the chapter frames the decision** (p. 174): "**the risk for a
febrile seizure at 5 to 12 days in children 12 to 23 months old remains relatively low and should
be weighed with the benefit of one fewer intramuscular injection.**"

**The AAP preference by age** (p. 174): "**either the MMR or MMRV vaccine be given as the first dose
of measles, mumps, rubella, and varicella vaccine at ages 12 through 47 months; for children 48
months and older, the first dose with MMRV is recommended to decrease the number of injections; for
the second dose at any age (15 months through 12 years), MMRV is also recommended for the same
reason.**"

> **Trap note — the febrile seizure window is 5 to 12 days after MMRV, in
> 12-to-23-month-olds.** Two specifics (p. 174). **The risk is described as relatively low
> and is weighed against one fewer injection** — the chapter does not forbid MMRV, it asks
> for informed discussion.

### Mumps

| Rule | The chapter's words | p. |
|---|---|---|
| Age | "**recommended for children at 12 to 15 months of age and is typically given in combination with measles and rubella**" | 174 |
| The under-12-month rule | "**It should not be administered to infants younger than 12 months because persisting maternal antibodies can interfere with the immune response.**" | 174 |
| Who else | "**mumps immunization is recommended for all individuals born after 1957 who may be susceptible**" | 174 |
| Where the disease is | "**especially in children 10 to 19 years of age**" | 174 |

### Rubella

**Whom the vaccine actually protects** (p. 174): "**Rubella is a relatively mild infection in
children, but in a pregnant woman the actual infection presents serious risks to the developing
fetus. Therefore the aim of rubella immunization is actually protection of the unborn child rather
than the recipient of the immunization.**"

| Rule | The chapter's words | p. |
|---|---|---|
| Schedule | "**recommended for all children at 12 to 15 months of age and at age of school entry or 4 to 6 years of age**" | 174 |
| Postpubertal females | "**Postpubertal females without evidence of rubella immunity should be immunized unless they are pregnant; they should be counseled not to become pregnant for 28 days after receiving the rubella-containing vaccine.**" | 174 |
| Pregnancy | "**Because the live attenuated virus may cross the placenta and theoretically present a risk to the developing fetus, rubella vaccine is currently not given to any pregnant woman.**" | 174 |
| The evidence | "**current evidence from women who received the vaccine while pregnant and delivered unaffected offspring indicates that the risk to the fetus is negligible**" | 174 |
| The child of a pregnant mother | "**there is no reported danger of administering rubella vaccine to a child if the mother is pregnant**" | 174 |

> **Trap note — the vaccine's target is the fetus, not the child receiving it.** This is the
> chapter's own framing (p. 174) and the most distinctive idea in the rubella section.

> **Trap note — 28 days, not 3 months.** The pregnancy-avoidance interval after a
> rubella-containing vaccine is **28 days** (p. 174). Do not confuse it with the **3-month**
> interval after immunoglobulin or blood transfusion before giving MMR or varicella
> (p. 177). **Two different intervals for two different situations.**

> **Trap note — a pregnant mother in the household is not a contraindication for the
> child.** Stated explicitly (p. 174). **A distractor defers the child's MMR because a
> parent is pregnant.**

## 11. Haemophilus influenzae type b

**What it protects against** (p. 174): "**bacterial meningitis, epiglottitis, bacterial pneumonia,
septic arthritis, and sepsis.**"

**And the name confusion the chapter pre-empts** (p. 174): "**Hib is not associated with the viruses
that cause influenza, or flu.**"

**The products** (p. 174): "**PedvaxHIB, Pentacel, and Comvax, which are combination vaccines, and
Hiberix and ActHib.**"

**MenHibrix** (p. 174): "**licensed for administration to children ages 6 weeks to 18 months and
provides protection against meningococcal (groups A, C, Y, and W-135), as well as Hib**", given
"**in a four-dose series at 2, 4, 6, and 12 to 15 months of age.**"

**How conjugate vaccines work here** (p. 174): "**These conjugate vaccines connect Hib to a nontoxic
form of another organism, such as meningococcal protein, tetanus toxoid, or diphtheria protein.
There is no antibody response to these nontoxic proteins, but they significantly improve the
antibody response to Hib, especially in infants.**"

**Hiberix** (p. 174): "**a conjugate vaccine licensed for use as the booster (final) dose of the Hib
vaccine series for children ages 15 months to 4 years.**"

**The 2013 clarification** (p. 174): "**only one dose of Hib should be given to children 15 months
old or older who have not been previously vaccinated.**"

**Consistency and administration** (p. 174): "**When possible, the Hib conjugate vaccine used at the
first vaccination should be used for all subsequent vaccinations in the primary series. All Hib
vaccines are administered by intramuscular injection using a separate syringe and at a site separate
from any concurrent vaccinations.**"

> **Trap note — Hib is not influenza.** The chapter says so in a parenthesis (p. 174)
> because the name invites the error. **Hib prevents meningitis, epiglottitis, and bacterial
> pneumonia** — which is the chapter's own NCLEX question 2 (p. 194), keyed to Hib.

> **Trap note — no antibody response to the carrier protein.** Stated directly (p. 174),
> which is why the Nursing Alert insists the child still needs the specific meningococcal
> and diphtheria vaccines. **The carrier improves the response to Hib; it does not immunize
> against itself.**

## 12. Varicella

**Who should get it** (p. 174): "**any susceptible child (i.e., one who lacks proof of varicella
vaccination or has a reliable history of varicella infection).**"

| Rule | The chapter's words | p. |
|---|---|---|
| Dose and route | "**A single dose of 0.5 ml should be given by subcutaneous injection.**" | 174 |
| First dose | "**for children ages 12 to 15 months**" | 174 |
| Second dose | "**for children at 4 to 6 years of age**" | 174 |
| Earlier second dose | "**may be administered before 4 years of age as long as a period of 3 months occurs between the first and second doses**" | 174 |
| Age 13 and older | "**Children 13 years of age or older who are susceptible should receive two doses administered at least 4 weeks apart.**" | 174 |

> **Trap note — the two-dose regimen is not about waning immunity.** The chapter is explicit

## 13. Pneumococcal disease

**What it causes** (p. 175): "generalized infections, such as **septicemia and meningitis**, or
localized infections, such as **otitis media, sinusitis, and pneumonia.**"

**Who is at risk** (p. 175): "**These illnesses are particularly problematic in children who attend

**PCV13** (p. 175): "**A 13-valent pneumococcal vaccine (PCV13 [Prevnar13]) has been licensed for use

**The schedule** (p. 175): "**administered at 2, 4, and 6 months of age, with a fourth dose at 12 to

**The supplemental dose** (p. 175): "**A single supplemental dose of PCV13 is recommended for children

## 17. Reactions, atraumatic care, and anaphylaxis

**The safety framing** (p. 176): "**Vaccines for routine immunizations are among the safest and most
reliable drugs available. However, minor side effects do occur after many of the immunizations and,
rarely, a serious reaction may result from the vaccine.**"

### Reactions to components rather than antigens

**The inactive components** (p. 176): "**preservatives, stabilizers, adjuvants, antibiotics (e.g.,
neomycin), and purified culture medium proteins (e.g., egg).**"

**The key insight** (p. 176): "**A child may react to the preservative in the vaccine rather than the
vaccine component; an example of this is the hepatitis B vaccine, which is prepared from yeast
cultures. Yeast hypersensitivity therefore would preclude one from receiving that particular vaccine
without consulting an allergist.**"

**Neomycin** (p. 176): "**Trace amounts of neomycin are used to decrease bacterial growth within
certain vaccine preparations, and persons with documented anaphylactic reactions to neomycin should
avoid those vaccines.**"

### Thimerosal

| Point | The chapter's words | p. |
|---|---|---|
| What it is | "**Some vaccines contain a preservative, thimerosal, which contains ethyl mercury.**" | 176 |
| The history | "**Concerns regarding possible mercury poisoning in the 1990s prompted many to put off vaccination of infants and small children for fear of childhood developmental problems, such as autism.**" | 176 |
| The response | "**A number of manufacturers have since stopped producing vaccines containing thimerosal.**" | 176 |
| Local reactions | "**No local hypersensitivity reactions to thimerosal have been recorded**" | 176 |
| The evidence | studies "**failed to establish a causal relationship between the two**" | 176 |
| The IOM conclusion | "**The Institute of Medicine (2004), following an in-depth 3-year study, concluded that there was no link between autism and the MMR vaccine or vaccines containing the preservative thimerosal.**" | 176 |

> **Trap note — this chapter and Chapter 20 reach the same conclusion from different
> evidence bases.** Here it is the IOM's 3-year study (p. 176); in Chapter 20 it is a full
> Translating-Evidence-Into-Practice box (Ch. 20, p. 650). **Both conclude there is no
> link.**

### Reactions to inactivated antigens

**Timing and character** (p. 176): with inactivated antigens such as DTaP, "**side effects are most
likely to occur within a few hours or days of administration and are usually limited to local
tenderness, erythema, and swelling at the injection site; low-grade fever; and behavioral changes
(e.g., drowsiness, eating less, prolonged or unusual cry).**"

**And the technique that reduces them** (p. 176): "**Local reactions tend to be less severe when a
needle of sufficient length to deposit the vaccine in the muscle is used.**"

**If epinephrine is given** (p. 176): "**observe for adverse reactions such as tachycardia,

## 18. Contraindications and precautions

**Why the distinction matters** (p. 176): "**Nurses need to be aware of the reasons for withholding
immunizations — both for the child's safety in terms of avoiding reactions and for the child's maximum
benefit from receiving the vaccine.**"

**And the cost of getting it wrong** (p. 176): "**Unfounded fears and lack of knowledge regarding
contraindications can needlessly prevent a child from having protection from life-threatening
diseases.**"

### The two definitions

| Term | Definition as given | What follows | p. |
|---|---|---|---|
| **Contraindication** | "a condition in an individual that **increases the risk for a serious adverse reaction**" | "**one would not administer a vaccine when a contraindication is present**" | 177 |
| **Precaution** | "a condition in a recipient that **might increase the risk for a serious adverse reaction or that might compromise the ability of the vaccine to produce immunity**" | "**If conditions are such that the benefit of receiving the vaccine would outweigh the risk of an adverse event or incomplete response, a precaution would not prevent vaccine administration.**" | 177 |

> **Trap note — a precaution does not stop the vaccine; a contraindication does.** That is
> the entire distinction (p. 177), and it hinges on **whether benefit outweighs risk**. A
> distractor treats both as absolute bars.

### The contraindications, in the chapter's order

| # | Contraindication | The chapter's words | p. |
|---|---|---|---|
| 1 | **Severe febrile illness** | "**The general contraindication for all immunizations is a severe febrile illness.**" The reason: "**This precaution avoids adding the risk of adverse side effects from the vaccine to an already ill child or mistakenly identifying a symptom of the disease as having been caused by the vaccine.**" And: "**The presence of minor illnesses, such as the common cold, is not a contraindication.**" | 177 |
| 2 | **Altered immunity, for live vaccines** | "**Live virus vaccines are generally not administered to anyone with an altered immune system because multiplication of the virus may be enhanced, causing a severe vaccine-induced illness.**" And: "**live virus vaccines should not be administered to persons who are severely immunocompromised or among persons whose immune function is not known.**" | 177 |
| 3 | **Recent passive immunity, for live vaccines** | "**Another contraindication to live virus vaccines (e.g., MMR, varicella, and rotavirus) is the presence of recently acquired passive immunity through blood transfusions, immunoglobulin, or maternal antibodies.**" | 177 |
| 4 | **Known allergic response** | "**A final contraindication is a known allergic response to a previously administered vaccine or a substance in the vaccine. An anaphylactic reaction to a vaccine or its component is a true contraindication.**" | 177 |
| 5 | **Pregnancy, for MMR** | "**Pregnancy is a contraindication to MMR vaccines, although the risk of fetal damage is primarily theoretic.**" | 177 |

### The passive-immunity interval

**The rule** (p. 177): "**Administration of MMR and varicella should be postponed for a minimum of 3
months after passive immunization with immunoglobulins and blood transfusions (except washed red
blood cells, which do not interfere with the immune response).**"

**The interval varies** (p. 177): "**Suggested intervals between administration of immunoglobulin
preparations and MMR and varicella depend on the type of immune product and dosage.**"

**If both must be given** (p. 177): "**If the vaccine and immunoglobulin are given simultaneously
because of imminent exposure to disease, the two preparations are injected at sites far from each
other. Vaccination should be repeated after the suggested intervals unless there is serologic
evidence of antibody production.**"

> **Trap note — washed red blood cells are the exception.** They
> "**do not interfere with the immune response**" (p. 177), so the 3-month postponement does
> not apply. **A distractor applies the delay to all blood products.**

> **Trap note — 3 months after immunoglobulin, 1 month between two live vaccines, 28 days
> before pregnancy after rubella.** Three different intervals in this chapter
> (pp. 174, 175, 177). **Match the interval to the situation, not to a general rule.**

> child** (p. 173).

### The parental concerns the chapter names

**The misconceptions** (p. 176): "**the misconception that administering combination vaccines may
overload the child's immune system**", and the concern "**that vaccines are not a part of the
individual's natural immunity and that administering too many vaccines may decrease the child's
immunity to such diseases.**"

**The chapter's answer to the first** (p. 176): "**the combined vaccines have undergone rigorous study
in relation to side effects and immunogenicity rates following administration.**"

**What parents actually post about** (p. 176): "**parents were concerned about adverse reactions, such

## 19. Communicating with parents about immunizations

**The nurse's position** (p. 178): "**Nurses are at the forefront in providing parents with appropriate
information regarding childhood immunization benefits, contraindications, and side effects and the
effects of nonvaccination on the child's health.**"

### The nine suggestions, complete (p. 178)

| # | Suggestion |
|---|---|
| 1 | "**Provide accurate and user-friendly information on vaccines (the necessity for each one, the disease each prevents, potential adverse effects).**" |
| 2 | "**Realize that the parent is expressing concern for the child's health.**" |
| 3 | "**Acknowledge the parent's concerns in a genuine, empathetic manner.**" |
| 4 | "**Tailor the discussion to the needs of the parent; avoid judgmental or threatening language.**" |
| 5 | "**Be knowledgeable about the benefits of individual vaccines, the common adverse effects, and how to minimize those effects.**" |

| 7 | "**Help the parent make an informed decision regarding the administration of each vaccine.**" |

> **Trap note — the vaccine-hesitant parent is framed as a concerned parent.** Item 2

> non-adherent. **Respecting the parent's ultimate wishes is the closing instruction**
> (p. 178).

> **Trap note — acetaminophen 45 minutes before, as warranted.** A specific interval
> (p. 178).

## 20. Administration, documentation, and the legal framework

### Storage and technique

**The two principal precautions** (p. 178): "**proper storage of the vaccine to protect its potency and
institution of recommended procedures for injection.**"

**Refrigeration detail** (p. 178): "**if the vaccine is to be refrigerated, it should be stored on a
center shelf, not in the door, where frequent temperature increases from opening the refrigerator can
alter the vaccine's potency.**"

**Light protection** (p. 178): "**For protection against light, the vial can be wrapped in aluminum
foil.**"

**Expiration** (p. 178): "**Periodic checks are established to ensure that no vaccine is used after its
expiration date.**"

**The adjuvant problem** (p. 178): "**The DTP (or DTaP) vaccines contain an adjuvant to retain the
antigen at the injection site and prolong the stimulatory effect. Because subcutaneous or
intracutaneous injection of the adjuvant can cause local irritation, inflammation, or abscess
formation, excellent intramuscular injection technique must be used.**"

**Needle length evidence** (p. 178): "**In two studies, the use of longer needles significantly
decreased the incidence of localized edema and tenderness when vaccines were administered to a group
of infants.**"

> **Trap note — center shelf, not the door.** A concrete, checkable storage rule (p. 178).
> **A distractor stores vaccine in the refrigerator door**, where temperature swings degrade
> potency.

> **Trap note — the adjuvant is why DTaP must go deep.** Subcutaneous deposit of an
> adjuvanted vaccine can cause "irritation, inflammation, or abscess formation" (p. 178).
> **The injection-depth rule has a specific pharmacologic reason here**, not just a comfort
> one.

**Additional data** (p. 179): "**the site and route of administration and evidence that the parent or
legal guardian gave informed consent before the immunization was administered.**"

**Adverse events** (p. 179): "**Report any adverse reactions after the administration of a vaccine to
the Vaccine Adverse Event Reporting System.**"

**The parent's own record** (p. 178): "**Each child should have an immunization record for parents to
keep, especially for families who move frequently.**"

**The registry** (p. 179): "**Many states and territories participate in the Immunization Information
System (IIS), which provides immunization information for parents and health professionals, as well as
a local or regional registry of immunizations children have received. In addition the IIS can provide
parents with information about missed scheduled immunizations.**"

> **Trap note — five required data elements, and the lot number is the one that gets
> forgotten.** Date, manufacturer, **lot number**, and the administering person's name,

## 21. Table 6.2, Communicable Diseases of Childhood

**The framing** (p. 179): "**The incidence of childhood communicable diseases has declined

**Ten diseases, reproduced complete.** The source separates each label from its value with an em

### Chickenpox (Varicella) — p. 179

| Field | As printed |
|---|---|
| Agent | "**Varicella-zoster virus (VZV)**" |
| Source | "**Primary secretions of respiratory tract of infected persons; to a lesser degree, skin lesions (scabs not infectious)**" |
| Transmission | "**Direct contact, droplet (airborne) spread, and contaminated objects**" |
| Incubation | "**2-3 weeks, usually 14-16 days**" |

| # | Action |
|---|---|
| 1 | "**Maintain Standard, Airborne, and Contact Precautions if hospitalized until all lesions are crusted; for immunized child with mild breakthrough varicella, isolate until no new lesions are seen.**" |

> **Trap note — no aspirin, because of Reye syndrome.** (p. 179) The chapter repeats the

> "**aspirin and other salicylates should not be administered to children to avoid Reye syndrome**" (p. 190).

followed by sudden inspiration associated with a high-pitched crowing sound**" — the source calls this
a whoop — "**during paroxysms, cheeks become flushed or cyanotic, eyes bulge, and tongue protrudes;
paroxysm may continue until thick mucus plug is dislodged; vomiting frequently follows attack; stage
generally lasts 4-6 weeks, followed by convalescent stage.**"

**In infants under 6 months** (p. 182): they "**may not have characteristic whoop cough, but have
difficulty maintaining adequate oxygenation with amount of secretions, frequent vomiting of mucus and
formula or breast milk.**"

**In adolescents and adults** (p. 182): "**cough and whoop may be absent, however, as many as 50% of
adolescents may have a cough for up to 10 weeks**"; additional symptoms are "**difficulty breathing and
posttussive vomiting.**"

**Management** (p. 182): "**Antimicrobial therapy (e.g., erythromycin, clarithromycin,
azithromycin)**"; supportive care includes "**Increased oxygen intake and humidity**", **Adequate
fluids**, and "**Intensive care and mechanical ventilation if needed for infants <6 months old.**"

**Complications** (p. 182): "**Pneumonia (usual cause of death in younger children)**";
**Atelectasis**; **Otitis media**; **Seizures**; "**Hemorrhage (scleral, conjunctival, epistaxis;
pulmonary hemorrhage in neonate)**"; "**Weight loss and dehydration**"; "**Hernias (umbilical and
inguinal)**"; "**Prolapsed rectum**". In adolescents: "**syncope, sleep disturbance, rib fractures,
incontinence, and pneumonia.**"

**Nursing care** (p. 182):

| # | Action |
|---|---|
| 1 | "**Maintain isolation during catarrhal stage; if child is hospitalized, institute Standard and Droplet Precautions.**" |
| 2 | "**Obtain nasopharyngeal culture for diagnosis.**" |
| 3 | "**Encourage oral fluids; offer small amount of fluids frequently.**" |
| 4 | "**Ensure adequate oxygenation during paroxysms; position infant on side to decrease chance of aspiration with vomiting.**" |
| 5 | "**Provide humidified oxygen; suction as needed to prevent choking on secretions.**" |
| 6 | "**Observe for signs of airway obstruction (e.g., increased restlessness, apprehension, retractions, cyanosis).**" |
| 7 | "**Encourage compliance with antibiotic therapy for household contacts.**" |
| 8 | "**Encourage adolescents to obtain pertussis booster (Tdap).**" |
| 9 | "**Use Standard Precautions and Droplet in health care workers exposed to children with persistent cough and high suspicion of pertussis.**" |

> **Trap note — pneumonia is the usual cause of death in younger children.** Stated in the
> complications column (p. 182). **A distractor names the apnea or the paroxysm itself.**

> **Trap note — hernias and rectal prolapse are pertussis complications.** Both are
> mechanical consequences of violent coughing (p. 182), and both are unexpected enough to
> make good distractor discriminators. **Rib fractures appear in the adolescent list** for
> the same reason.

> **Trap note — position the infant on the side during paroxysms.** To prevent aspiration
> with the vomiting that follows (p. 182). **A distractor positions supine or upright.**

> **Trap note — treat the household contacts.** Item 7 (p. 182), and the immunization
> section adds that exposed health care workers should receive a Tdap dose if not previously
> vaccinated (p. 173). **Pertussis management extends beyond the patient in both
> directions.**

### Poliomyelitis — p. 183

| Field | As printed |
|---|---|
| Agent | "**Enteroviruses, three types: type 1, most frequent cause of paralysis, both epidemic and endemic; type 2, least frequently associated with paralysis; type 3, second most frequently associated with paralysis**" |
| Source | "**Feces and oropharyngeal secretions of infected persons, especially young children**" |
| Transmission | "**Direct contact with persons with apparent or inapparent active infection; spread via fecal-oral and pharyngeal-oropharyngeal routes; vaccine-acquired paralytic polio may occur as a result of the live oral polio vaccination (no longer available in the United States)**" |
| Incubation | "**Usually 7-14 days, with range of 5-35 days**" |
| Communicability | "**Not exactly known; virus present in throat and feces shortly after infection and persists for about 1 week in throat and 4-6 weeks in feces**" |

**The three forms** (p. 183):

| Form | As printed |
|---|---|
| "**Abortive or inapparent**" | "**Fever, uneasiness, sore throat, headache, anorexia, vomiting, abdominal pain; lasts a few hours to a few days**" |
| **Nonparalytic** | "**Same manifestations as abortive but more severe, with pain and stiffness in neck, back, and legs**" |
| **Paralytic** | "**Initial course similar to nonparalytic type, followed by recovery and then signs of central nervous system paralysis**" |

**Management** (p. 183): "**Complete bed rest during acute phase**"; "**Mechanical or assisted
ventilation in case of respiratory paralysis**"; "**Physical therapy for muscles after acute stage.**"

**Complications** (p. 183): "**Permanent paralysis**"; "**Respiratory arrest**"; **Hypertension**;
"**Kidney stones from demineralization of bone during prolonged immobility.**"

**Nursing care** (p. 183):

| # | Action |
|---|---|
| 1 | "**Institute Contact Precautions.**" |
| 2 | "**Administer mild sedatives as necessary to relieve anxiety and promote rest.**" |
| 3 | "**Participate in physical therapy procedures (use of moist hot packs and range-of-motion exercises).**" |
| 4 | "**Position child to maintain body alignment and prevent contractures or skin breakdown; use footboard or appropriate orthoses to prevent footdrop; use pressure mattress for prolonged immobility.**" |
| 5 | "**Encourage child to perform activities of daily living to capability; promote early ambulation with assistive devices; administer analgesics for maximum comfort during physical activity; give high-protein diet and bowel management for prolonged immobility.**" |
| 6 | "**Observe for respiratory paralysis (e.g., difficulty talking, ineffective cough, inability to hold breath, shallow and rapid respirations); report such signs and symptoms to practitioner.**" |

> **Trap note — the virus persists far longer in feces than in the throat.** About 1 week in
> the throat, **4 to 6 weeks in feces** (p. 183). **That is why polio requires Contact
> Precautions** rather than a respiratory precaution, and why the fecal-oral route
> dominates.

> **Trap note — the paralytic form recovers first, then paralyzes.**
> "followed by recovery and then signs of central nervous system paralysis" (p. 183). **An
> apparent recovery is not reassurance in polio.**

> **Trap note — four signs of respiratory paralysis.** Difficulty talking, ineffective
> cough, inability to hold breath, shallow and rapid respirations (p. 183). **Difficulty
> talking is the one most often missed**, and it is the earliest.

### Rubella (German Measles) — p. 184

| Field | As printed |
|---|---|
| Agent | **Rubella virus** |
| Source | "**Primarily nasopharyngeal secretions of person with apparent or inapparent infection; virus also present in blood, stool, and urine**" |
| Incubation | **14-21 days** |
| Communicability | "**7 days before to about 5 days after appearance of rash**" |
| Constitutional signs | "**Occasionally low-grade fever, headache, malaise, and lymphadenopathy**" |

**The prodromal stage** (p. 184): "**Absent in children, present in adults and adolescents; consists of
low-grade fever, headache, malaise, anorexia, mild conjunctivitis, coryza, sore throat, cough, and
lymphadenopathy; lasts 1-5 days, subsides 1 day after appearance of rash.**"

**The rash** (p. 184): "**First appears on face and rapidly spreads downward to neck, arms, trunk, and
legs; by end of first day, body is covered with discrete, pinkish-red maculopapular exanthema;
disappears in same order as it began and is usually gone by third day.**"

**Management** (p. 184): "**No treatment necessary other than antipyretics for low-grade fever and
analgesics for discomfort.**"

**Complications** (p. 184): "**Rare (arthritis, encephalitis, or purpura); most benign of all childhood
communicable diseases; greatest danger is teratogenic effect on fetus.**"

**Nursing care** (p. 184): "**Institute Droplet Precautions.**"; "**Reassure parents of benign nature of
illness in affected child.**"; "**Use comfort measures as necessary.**"; "**Avoid contact with pregnant
woman.**"; "**Monitor rubella titer in pregnant adolescent.**"

> **Trap note — the most benign childhood communicable disease, and the most dangerous to a
> fetus.** Both statements are in one complications cell (p. 184). **That paradox is the
> whole point of the rubella vaccine**, which the chapter says aims at
> "**protection of the unborn child rather than the recipient of the immunization**" (p. 174).

> **Trap note — the prodrome is absent in children.** Present only in adults and adolescents
> (p. 184). **A distractor describes a prodromal stage in a young child with rubella.**

> **Trap note — measles versus rubella, side by side.** Both rashes start on the face and
> spread downward. **Measles: 10-20 day incubation, Koplik spots, confluent rash, brownish
> then desquamating, Airborne Precautions, serious complications** (p. 182). **Rubella:
> 14-21 day incubation, no Koplik spots, discrete rash, gone by day 3, Droplet Precautions,
> benign except in pregnancy** (p. 184). **The discriminators are Koplik spots, confluence,
> and duration.**

### Scarlet Fever — p. 185

| Field | As printed |
|---|---|
| Agent | "**Group A β-hemolytic streptococci**" |
| Source | "**Usually from nasopharyngeal secretions of infected persons and carriers**" |
| Transmission | "**Direct contact with infected person or droplet spread; indirectly by contact with contaminated articles or ingestion of contaminated milk or other food**" |
| Incubation | "**2-5 days, with range of 1-7 days**" |
| Communicability | "**During incubation period and clinical illness, approximately 10 days; during first 2 weeks of carrier phase, although may persist for months**" |

**The prodromal stage** (p. 185): "**Abrupt high fever, pulse increased out of proportion to fever,
vomiting, headache, chills, malaise, abdominal pain, halitosis.**"

**The enanthem, including both strawberry tongues** (p. 185): "**Tonsils enlarged, edematous, reddened,
and covered with patches of exudates; in severe cases appearance resembles membrane seen in
diphtheria; pharynx is edematous and beefy red; during first 1-2 days tongue is coated and papillae
become red and swollen (white strawberry tongue); by fourth or fifth day white coat sloughs off,
leaving prominent papillae (red strawberry tongue); palate is covered with erythematous punctate
lesions.**"

**The exanthem** (p. 185): "**Rash appears within 12 hours after prodromal signs; red pinhead-sized
punctate lesions rapidly become generalized but are absent on face, which becomes flushed with
striking circumoral pallor; rash more intense in folds of joints; by end of first week desquamation
begins (fine, sandpaper-like on torso; sheetlike sloughing on palms and soles), which may be complete
by 3 weeks or longer.**"

**Management** (p. 185): "**Full course of penicillin (or erythromycin in penicillin-sensitive
children) or oral cephalosporin**"; "**Antibiotic therapy for newly diagnosed carriers (nose or throat
cultures positive for streptococci)**"; supportive care is "**Rest during febrile phase, analgesics for
sore throat; antipruritics for rash if bothersome.**"

**Complications** (p. 185): "**Peritonsillar and retropharyngeal abscess**"; **Sinusitis**; **Otitis
media**; "**Acute glomerulonephritis**"; "**Acute rheumatic fever**"; "**Polyarthritis
(uncommon).**"

**Nursing care** (p. 185):

| # | Action |
|---|---|
| 1 | "**Institute Standard and Droplet Precautions until 24 hours after initiation of treatment.**" |
| 2 | "**Ensure compliance with oral antibiotic therapy; intramuscular benzathine penicillin G [Bicillin] may be given.**" |
| 3 | "**Encourage rest during febrile phase; provide quiet activity during convalescent period.**" |
| 4 | "**Relieve discomfort of sore throat with analgesics, gargles, lozenges, antiseptic throat sprays, and inhalation of cool mist.**" |
| 5 | "**Encourage fluids during febrile phase; avoid irritating liquids (e.g., citrus juices) or rough foods (e.g., chips); when child is able to eat, begin with soft diet.**" |
| 6 | "**Advise parents to consult practitioner if fever persists after beginning therapy.**" |
| 7 | "**Discuss procedures for preventing spread of infection**" — "**discard toothbrush; avoid sharing drinking and eating utensils.**" |

> **Trap note — white strawberry tongue first, red strawberry tongue by day 4 or 5.** The
> sequence and the timing (p. 185). **A distractor reverses them or merges them into one
> finding.**

> **Trap note — the rash is absent on the face, with circumoral pallor.** (p. 185) **That
> negative finding is the classic descriptor**, alongside the sandpaper texture and the
> sheetlike peeling of palms and soles.

> **Trap note — 24 hours of treatment ends precautions.** (p. 185) **This is the shortest
> isolation period in Table 6.2** and reflects the bacterial, antibiotic-responsive nature
> of the illness.

> **Trap note — two complications are immune-mediated and late.** **Acute glomerulonephritis
> and acute rheumatic fever** (p. 185). They are the reason the full antibiotic course
> matters, which the chapter reinforces:
> "**Prevention of complications from diseases such as diphtheria, pertussis, and scarlet fever requires compliance with antibiotic therapy**" (p. 187),
> and "**educate the importance to complete the entire course of therapy**" (p. 188).

> **Trap note — discard the toothbrush.** A specific, memorable instruction (p. 185).

## 23. Nursing care management: assessment

**The first priority** (p. 185): "**Identification of the infectious agent is of primary importance to
prevent exposure to susceptible individuals.**"

**Who sees it first** (p. 186): "**Nurses in ambulatory care**" (p. 185) "**settings, child care
centers, and schools are often the first persons to see signs of a communicable disease, such as a
rash or sore throat.**" (p. 186)

**The stance required** (p. 186): "**The nurse must operate under a high index of suspicion for common
childhood diseases to identify potentially infectious cases and to recognize diseases that require
medical intervention.**"

**The worked example** (p. 186): "**An example is the common complaint of sore throat. Although most
often a symptom of a minor viral infection, it can signal diphtheria or a streptococcal infection,
such as scarlet fever. Each of these bacterial conditions requires appropriate medical treatment to
prevent serious complications.**"

### The four assessment items (p. 187)

"When the nurse suspects a communicable disease, it is important to assess the following:"

| # | Item |
|---|---|
| 1 | "**Recent exposure to a known case**" |
| 2 | "**Prodromal symptoms (symptoms that occur between early manifestations of the disease and its overt clinical syndrome) or evidence of constitutional symptoms, such as a fever or rash**" |
| 3 | "**Immunization history**" |
| 4 | "**History of having the disease**" |

**Why the last two matter** (p. 187): "**Immunizations are available for many diseases, and
infection usually confers lifelong immunity; therefore the possibility of many infectious agents can
be eliminated based on these two criteria.**"

> **Trap note — a sore throat is the chapter's own example of a symptom that must not be
> dismissed.** It can be diphtheria or scarlet fever (p. 186), and both are bacterial and
> both have serious late complications. **In a triage scenario, a sore throat with fever
> earns a culture, not reassurance.**

> **Trap note — immunization history and past disease are used to RULE OUT.** The chapter
> says they "eliminate the possibility of many infectious agents" (p. 187). **That is the
> diagnostic function of those two questions**, and it is why they are on a four-item list.

## 24. Prevent spread

**The two components** (p. 187): "**Prevention consists of two components: prevention of the disease
and control of its spread to others. Primary prevention rests almost exclusively on immunization.**"

**What control measures must do** (p. 187): "**reduce risk of cross-transmission of infectious
organisms between patients and to protect health care workers from organisms harbored by
patients.**"

**The most important procedure** (p. 187): "**The most important procedure is hand washing. Persons
directly caring for the child or handling contaminated articles must wash their hands and practice
effective Standard Precautions in care of their patients.**"

**Teaching the child** (p. 187): "**Instruct the child to practice good hand-washing technique after
toileting and before eating.**"

**For droplet-spread diseases** (p. 187): "**The child who is old enough should use a tissue to cover
the face during coughing or sneezing; otherwise the parent should cover the child's mouth with a
tissue and then discard it.**"

**And the household rule** (p. 187): "**Stress the usual hygiene measures of not sharing eating and
drinking utensils to the family.**"

### Nursing Alert: the undiagnosed exanthem (p. 187) — complete

"**If a child is admitted to the hospital with an undiagnosed exanthema, institute strict
Transmission-Based Precautions (contact, airborne, and droplet) and Standard Precautions until a
diagnosis is confirmed.**"

**The diseases requiring these precautions** (p. 187): "**diphtheria, varicella-zoster virus (VZV;
chickenpox), measles, tuberculosis, adenovirus, Haemophilus influenzae type b (Hib), influenza, mumps,
Neisseria meningitides, Mycoplasma pneumoniae infection, pertussis, plague, rhinovirus, group A
streptococcal pharyngitis, severe acute respiratory syndrome, pneumonia, or scarlet fever.**"

> **Trap note — an undiagnosed rash gets ALL THREE transmission-based precautions plus
> Standard.** Contact, airborne, and droplet together, until diagnosis (p. 187). **This is
> the single most likely precautions item in the chapter**, because it inverts the usual
> logic: you do not pick the precaution until you know the disease, so you use all of them.

## 25. Prevent complications

**Who is at risk** (p. 187): "**certain groups are at risk for serious, even fatal, complications from
communicable diseases, especially the viral diseases chickenpox and erythema infectiosum (fifth
disease) caused by human parvovirus B19.**"

### Immunocompromised children and varicella

**Who** (p. 187): "**those receiving steroid or other immunosuppressive therapy, those with a
generalized malignancy such as leukemia or lymphoma, or those with an immunologic disorder.**"

**The risk** (p. 187): "**at risk for viremia from replication of the varicella-zoster virus (VZV) in
the blood.**"

**The two diseases one virus causes** (p. 187): "**VZV is so named because it causes two distinct
diseases: varicella (chickenpox) and zoster (herpes zoster or shingles).**"

**Herpes zoster defined** (p. 187): "**an intensely painful varicella that is localized to a single
dermatome (body area innervated by a particular segment of the spinal cord).**"

**Who reactivates** (p. 187): "**Immunocompromised patients and healthy infants younger than 1 year of
age (who also have reduced immunity) are at a higher risk for reactivation of VZV causing herpes
zoster, probably as a result of a deficiency in cellular immunity.**"

**Complications of zoster in children** (p. 187): "**secondary bacterial infection, depigmentation, and
scarring. Postherpetic neuralgia in children is uncommon.**"

### The prophylaxis and treatment

| Product | Who for | p. |
|---|---|---|
| **VariZIG or IVIG** | "**recommended for children who are immunocompromised, who have no previous history of varicella, and who are likely to contract the disease and have complications as a result**" | 187 |
| **Acyclovir or valacyclovir** | "**may be used to treat varicella infections in susceptible immunocompromised persons**" | 187 |

**What acyclovir does** (p. 187): "**It is effective in decreasing the number of lesions; shortening
the duration of fever; and decreasing itching, lethargy, and anorexia.**"

**Who to consider valacyclovir for** (p. 187): "**immunocompromised children without a history of
varicella disease, newborns whose mother had varicella within 5 days before delivery or within 48
hours after delivery, and hospitalized preterm infants with significant varicella exposure.**"

> **Trap note — the maternal varicella window is 5 days before to 48 hours after delivery.**
> Two different intervals on either side of birth (p. 187). **A distractor makes them
> symmetric.**

> **Trap note — three conditions must all be met for VariZIG.** Immunocompromised, **no
> previous varicella**, and likely to contract it with complications (p. 187). **A
> distractor gives it to any exposed immunocompromised child**, including one with a history
> of the disease.

> **Trap note — healthy infants under 1 year are also at higher risk of zoster
> reactivation.** Not only the immunocompromised (p. 187), and the stated reason is reduced
> immunity and "a deficiency in cellular immunity."

### Hemolytic disease and fifth disease

**The mechanism** (p. 187): "**Human parvovirus B19 infects and lyses red blood cell precursors, thus
interrupting the production of red blood cells. Therefore the virus may precipitate a severe aplastic
crisis in patients who need increased red blood cell production to maintain normal red blood cell
volumes.**"

**Other cytopenias** (p. 187): "**Thrombocytopenia and neutropenia may also occur as a result of
human parvovirus B19 infection.**"

**The fetal risk** (p. 187): "**The fetus has a relatively high rate of red blood cell production and
an immature immune system; it may develop severe anemia and hydrops as a result of maternal human
parvovirus infection.**"

**The figures** (p. 187): "**Fetal death rates as a result of human parvovirus B19 have been estimated
to be between 2% and 6%, with the greatest risk appearing to be in the first 20 weeks.**"

**Nursing Alert** (p. 187): "**Refer children at risk for contracting these communicable diseases to
the practitioner immediately in case of known exposure or outbreaks.**"

> **Trap note — the mechanism explains who is at risk.** B19 lyses red cell precursors, so
> anyone **dependent on high red cell turnover** decompensates: children with sickle cell
> disease and other hemolytic diseases, and the fetus (p. 187). **A distractor names
> immunosuppression as the risk factor for aplastic crisis**, which belongs to varicella.

> **Trap note — 2% to 6% fetal death, greatest in the first 20 weeks.** (p. 187) Note that
> Table 6.2 gives the highest risk as "primarily second trimester" (p. 181). **The two
> statements are adjacent in emphasis but not identical**; both are quoted to their own
> pages.

### Pertussis

**The trend** (p. 187): "**In the past decade the incidence of pertussis has increased, particularly in
infants less than 6 months old and in children 10 to 14 years of age.**"

**Infant presentation** (p. 187): "**gagging and gasping, followed by posttussive emesis, apnea, and
cyanosis; the typical whoop associated with the disease is absent.**"

**Older children** (p. 187): "**the disease may manifest as a common cold, but a prolonged cough (at
least 21 days) is common in adolescents.**"

**The antibiotics, by age** (p. 187): "**Azithromycin (for infants younger than 1 month old) and
erythromycin, clarithromycin, or azithromycin are administered to infants and children with
pertussis.**"

> **Trap note — azithromycin is specified for infants under 1 month.** (p. 187) **The other
> macrolides are for older infants and children.** This age-specific drug choice is unusual
> enough to be tested.

> **Trap note — at least 21 days of cough in adolescents.** (p. 187) **A prolonged cough in
> a teenager is the presentation**, not a whoop, and Table 6.2 adds that up to 50% may cough
> for 10 weeks (p. 182).

### Vitamin A in measles

**The recommendation** (p. 188): "**Evidence suggests that vitamin A supplementation reduces both
morbidity and mortality in measles and that all children with severe measles should receive vitamin A
supplements.**"

**The dose** (p. 188): "**A single oral dose of 200,000 international units for children at least 1
year old is recommended (use half that dose for children 6 to 12 months of age).**"

**Side effects** (p. 188): "**The higher dose may be associated with vomiting and headache for a few
hours.**"

**Repeat dosing** (p. 188): "**The dose should be repeated the next day and at 4 weeks for children
with ophthalmologic evidence of vitamin A deficiency.**"

**Nursing Alert on toxicity** (p. 188): "**Although the risk of vitamin A toxicity from these doses
(they are 100 to 200 times the recommended dietary allowance) is relatively low, nurses should
instruct parents on safe storage of the drug. Ideally, vitamin A should be dispensed in the
age-appropriate unit dose to prevent excessive administration and possible toxicity.**"

> **Trap note — 100 to 200 times the RDA, with low toxicity risk but a storage warning.**
> All three elements (p. 188). **The safety intervention is unit-dose dispensing**, which is
> a concrete, testable action.

## 26. Provide comfort, and support the child and family

### Itching

**The chief discomfort** (p. 188): "**The chief discomfort from most rashes is itching, and measures
such as cool baths (usually without soap) and lotions (e.g., calamine) are helpful.**"

**A refinement** (p. 188): "**Cooling the lotion in the refrigerator beforehand often makes it more
soothing on the skin than at room temperature.**"

**Nursing Alert on topical antihistamines** (p. 188): "**When lotions with active ingredients such as
diphenhydramine in Caladryl are used, they are applied sparingly, especially over open lesions, where
excessive absorption can lead to drug toxicity. Use these lotions with caution in children who are
simultaneously receiving an oral antihistamine.**"

**Overheating** (p. 188): "**To avoid overheating, which increases itching, children should wear
lightweight, loose, nonirritating clothing and keep out of the sun.**"

**Scratching** (p. 188): "**If the child persists in scratching, keep the nails short and smooth or use
mittens and clothes with long sleeves or legs.**"

**Systemic antipruritics** (p. 188): "**For severe itching, antipruritic medication, such as
diphenhydramine (Benadryl) or hydroxyzine (Atarax), may be required, especially when the child has
trouble sleeping because of itching. Loratadine, cetirizine, and fexofenadine do not cause drowsiness
and may be preferred for urticaria during the day.**"

> **Trap note — topical plus oral antihistamine is a toxicity risk.** Stated explicitly
> (p. 188). **A distractor combines Caladryl with oral Benadryl**, which the chapter
> cautions against.

> **Trap note — sedating at night, non-sedating by day.** Diphenhydramine or hydroxyzine
> when itching disturbs sleep; loratadine, cetirizine, or fexofenadine during the day
> (p. 188). **The drowsiness profile is the selection criterion.**

### Fever, sore throat, and activity

**Fever** (p. 188): "**both antipyretic medicine (acetaminophen or ibuprofen) and environmental
manipulation are implemented.**" And the limit: "**Acetaminophen is effective in lowering the fever but
does not significantly reduce the symptoms of itching, anorexia, abdominal pain, fussiness, or
vomiting.**"

**Sore throat** (p. 188): "**managed with lozenges, saline rinses (if the child is old enough to
cooperate), and analgesics.**"

**Diet** (p. 188): "**Because most children are anorectic during an illness, bland foods and increased
liquids are usually preferred.**"

**Activity** (p. 188): "**During the early stages of the disease, children voluntarily curtail their
activity, and although bed rest is beneficial, it should not be imposed unless specifically
indicated.**"

**And distraction** (p. 188): "**During periods of irritability, quiet activity (e.g., reading, music,
television, video games, puzzles, coloring) helps distract children from the discomfort.**"

> **Trap note — do not impose bed rest.** Children curtail activity on their own, and bed
> rest "should not be imposed unless specifically indicated" (p. 188). **A distractor
> prescribes strict bed rest for a febrile child with a rash.**

> **Trap note — acetaminophen treats the fever and nothing else.** The chapter lists five
> symptoms it does not help (p. 188). **A distractor expects antipyretics to relieve the
> itching or the abdominal pain.**

### Support

**The parental experience** (p. 188): "**Most communicable diseases are benign but may produce
considerable concern and anxiety for parents. Often the occurrence of a disease, such as chickenpox,
is the first time the child is acutely uncomfortable.**"

**What families need** (p. 188): "**Parents need assistance to cope with manifestations of the illness,
such as intense itching. The family and child need reassurance that recovery is generally rapid.

**Nursing Alert on the opportunity** (p. 188): "**The occurrence of a communicable disease provides the
opportunity to ask parents about the child's immunization status and reinforce the benefits of
vaccines for children.**"

> **Trap note — the rash outlasts the illness.**
> "**visible signs of the dermatosis may be present for some time after the child is well enough to resume usual activities**" (p. 188).
> **A distractor keeps the child home until the skin clears**, which is not the criterion in
> any Table 6.2 entry.

## 27. Conjunctivitis, and Box 6.3

**The definition and the age pattern** (p. 188): "**Acute conjunctivitis (inflammation of the
conjunctiva) occurs from a variety of causes that are typically age related.**"

### In newborns

**The organisms** (p. 188): "**In newborns conjunctivitis can occur from infection during birth, most
often from Chlamydia trachomatis (inclusion conjunctivitis) or Neisseria gonorrhoeae.**"

**The urgency** (p. 188): "**Conjunctivitis in a neonate is a serious condition and can potentially lead
to blindness; all signs of conjunctivitis require prompt reporting and a comprehensive
evaluation.**"

### Timing as a diagnostic clue

| Onset | Cause | p. |
|---|---|---|
| "**within 24 hours of instillation of neonatal ophthalmic prophylaxis**" | "**A chemical conjunctivitis**" | 188 |
| "**within 2 to 5 days after birth**" | **N. gonorrhoeae** | 188 |
| "**5 to 14 days after birth**" | **C. trachomatis** | 188 |

> **Trap note — the three newborn timings are the item.** Chemical within 24 hours,
> gonococcal at 2 to 5 days, chlamydial at 5 to 14 days (p. 188). **Timing alone
> distinguishes them**, because
> "The clinical signs of conjunctivitis are similar regardless of the cause" (p. 188).

**A different cause in infants** (p. 188): "**In infants recurrent conjunctivitis may be a sign of
nasolacrimal (tear) duct obstruction or dacryocystitis, an infection of the lacrimal sac.**"

### In children

**The causes** (p. 188): "**viral, bacterial, allergic, or related to a foreign body.**"

**The most common** (p. 188): "**Bacterial infection accounts for most instances of acute conjunctivitis
in children.**"

**Diagnosis** (p. 188): "**made primarily from the clinical manifestations, although cultures of purulent
drainage may be needed to identify the specific cause.**"

### Box 6.3, Clinical Manifestations of Conjunctivitis (p. 188) — complete

| Type | Manifestations as printed |
|---|---|
| **Bacterial** (the source calls it pink eye) | "**Purulent drainage**"; "**Crusting of eyelids, especially on awakening**"; "**Inflamed conjunctiva**"; **Swollen eyelids** |
| **Viral** | "**Usually occurs with upper respiratory tract infection**"; "**Serous (watery) drainage**"; "**Inflamed conjunctiva**"; **Swollen eyelids** |
| **Allergic** | **Itching**; "**Watery to thick, stringy discharge**"; "**Inflamed conjunctiva**"; **Swollen eyelids** |
| **Foreign body** | **Tearing**; **Pain**; "**Inflamed conjunctiva**"; "**Usually only one eye affected**" |

> **Trap note — all four share inflamed conjunctiva; three share swollen eyelids.** The
> discriminators are the **discharge and the associated feature**: purulent with morning
> crusting equals bacterial; watery with a cold equals viral; itching with stringy discharge
> equals allergic; pain in **one eye** equals foreign body (p. 188). **Unilateral
> involvement is the foreign-body cue.**

### Therapeutic management

| Type | Treatment | p. |
|---|---|---|
| Viral | "**self-limiting, and treatment is limited to removal of the accumulated secretions**" | 188 |
| Bacterial | "**topical antibacterial agents such as polymyxin and bacitracin (Polysporin), sodium sulfacetamide (Sulamyd), or trimethoprim and polymyxin (Polytrim)**" | 188 |
| Bacterial in infants | "**Infants with bacterial conjunctivitis may require systemic antibiotics.**" | 188 |
| Children 1 year and older | "**fluoroquinolones and aminoglycosides are commonly used ophthalmic antimicrobial agents**"; fourth-generation fluoroquinolones "**provide broad-spectrum coverage, are bactericidal, and are generally well tolerated**" | 188 |

**Drops versus ointment** (p. 188): "**Drops may be used during the day and an ointment at bedtime
because the ointment preparation remains in the eye longer but blurs the vision.**"

**The corticosteroid caution** (p. 188): "**Corticosteroids are avoided because they reduce ocular
resistance to bacteria.**"

> **Trap note — drops by day, ointment at bedtime, and the reason is blurring.** (p. 188)
> **A distractor uses ointment during school hours.**

> **Trap note — corticosteroids are avoided in conjunctivitis.** Because they
> "reduce ocular resistance to bacteria" (p. 188). Chapter 20 gives a related but distinct
> reason for its eye section: steroids "exacerbate viral infections such as herpes simplex"
> (Ch. 20, p. 647). **Two chapters, two reasons, same caution.**

### Nursing care management

**Cleaning direction** (pp. 188–189): "**Remove accumulated secretions by wiping from**" (p. 188) "**the
inner canthus downward and outward, away from the opposite eye.**" (p. 189)

**Compresses** (p. 189): "**Warm, moist compresses, such as a clean washcloth wrung out with hot tap
water, are helpful in removing the crusts. Compresses are not kept on the eye because an occlusive
covering promotes bacterial growth.**"

**Medication timing** (p. 189): "**Instill medication immediately after the eyes have been cleaned and
according to correct procedure.**"

**Preventing family spread** (p. 189): "**Keep the child's washcloth and towel separate from those used
by others. Discard tissues used to clean the eye. Instruct the child to refrain from rubbing the eye
and to use good hand-washing technique.**"

**Nursing Alert: signs of serious conjunctivitis** (p. 189) — complete: "**reduction or loss of vision,
ocular pain, photophobia, exophthalmos (bulging eyeball), decreased ocular mobility, corneal
ulceration, and unusual patterns of inflammation (e.g., the perilimbal flush associated with iritis or
localized inflammation associated with scleritis). If a patient has any of these signs, refer him or
her immediately to an ophthalmologist.**"

> **Trap note — inner canthus, downward and outward, away from the other eye.** Three
> elements of one direction (pp. 188–189). **A distractor wipes toward the nose**, which
> carries organisms to the other eye.

> **Trap note — compresses remove crusts but are not left on.** Because an occlusive
> covering "promotes bacterial growth" (p. 189). **A distractor applies a patch or leaves a
> warm compress in place.**

> **Trap note — seven red flags for immediate ophthalmology referral.** (p. 189) **Pain,
> photophobia, and vision loss are the three most likely to appear in a stem**, and none of
> them belongs to uncomplicated conjunctivitis.

## 28. Stomatitis

**The definition** (p. 189): "**inflammation of the oral mucosa, which may include the buccal (cheek)
and labial (lip) mucosa, tongue, gingiva, palate, and floor of the mouth. It may be infectious or
noninfectious and may be caused by local or systemic factors.**"

**The two types seen in children** (p. 189): "**In children, aphthous stomatitis and herpetic
stomatitis are typically seen.**"

**Who is at high risk** (p. 189): "**Children with immunosuppression and those receiving chemotherapy or
head and neck radiotherapy are at high risk for developing mucosal ulceration and herpetic
stomatitis.**"

### Aphthous stomatitis

| Feature | The chapter's words | p. |
|---|---|---|
| Names | "**aphthous ulcer, canker sore**" | 189 |
| Nature | "**a benign but painful condition whose cause is unknown**" | 189 |
| Triggers | "**mild traumatic injury (e.g., biting the cheek, hitting the mucosa with a toothbrush, or a mouth appliance rubbing on the mucosa), allergy, or emotional stress**" | 189 |
| Appearance | "**painful, small, whitish ulcerations surrounded by a red border**" | 189 |
| How distinguished | "**by healthy adjacent tissues, absence of vesicles, and no systemic illness**" | 189 |
| Course | "**The ulcers persist for 4 to 12 days and heal uneventfully.**" | 189 |

### Herpetic gingivostomatitis

| Feature | The chapter's words | p. |
|---|---|---|
| Cause | "**caused by HSV, most often type 1**" | 189 |
| Forms | "**may occur as a primary infection or recur in a less severe form known as recurrent herpes labialis (commonly called cold sores or fever blisters)**" | 189 |
| Primary presentation | "**usually begins with a fever; the pharynx becomes edematous and erythematous; and vesicles erupt on the mucosa, causing severe pain**" | 189 |
| Associated findings | "**Cervical lymphadenitis often occurs, and the breath has a distinctly foul odor.**" | 189 |
| Recurrent form | "**the vesicles appear on the lips, usually singly or in groups**" | 189 |
| Precipitants of recurrence | "**emotional stress, trauma (often related to dental procedures), immunosuppression, or exposure to excessive sunlight**" | 189 |
| Duration | "**The disease can last 5 to 14 days, with varying degrees of severity.**" | 189 |

> **Trap note — the three discriminators for aphthous versus herpetic.** **Healthy adjacent
> tissue, no vesicles, no systemic illness** equals aphthous (p. 189). Herpetic has **fever,
> vesicles, cervical lymphadenitis, and foul breath.** That four-versus-three contrast is
> the cleanest oral item in the chapter.

### HFMD and herpangina

**What they share** (p. 189): "**Stomatitis may occur as a manifestation of hand-foot-and-mouth disease
(HFMD) and herpangina; both manifest with scattered vesicles on the buccal mucosa and are commonly
caused by the nonpolio enteroviruses (primarily coxsackieviruses).**"

**The feeding problem** (p. 189): "**Children with either HFMD or herpangina often have poor intake as a
result of the mouth sores; infants may refuse to nurse or take a bottle or may pull away and cry after
a few seconds of nursing.**"

> **Trap note — the infant who latches and then pulls away crying.** A specific,
> recognizable behavior (p. 189) pointing to oral pain rather than to a feeding or supply
> problem.

### Therapeutic management

**The aim** (p. 189): "**Treatment for all types of stomatitis is aimed at relief of symptoms, primarily
pain.**"

| Measure | The chapter's words | p. |
|---|---|---|
| Mild cases | "**Acetaminophen and ibuprofen are usually sufficient**" | 189 |
| Severe HGS | "**stronger analgesics such as codeine may be needed**" | 189 |
| Topical over the counter | "**Orabase, Anbesol, and Kank-A**" | 189 |
| Viscous lidocaine | "**Lidocaine (Xylocaine Viscous) can be prescribed for the child who can keep 1 tsp of the solution in the mouth for 2 to 3 minutes and then expectorate the drug.**" | 189 |
| The mixture | "**A mixture of equal parts of diphenhydramine elixir and Maalox (aluminum and magnesium hydroxide) provides mild analgesia, antiinflammatory properties, and a protective coating for the lesions.**" | 189 |
| Coating agent | "**Sucralfate can also be used as a coating agent for oral mucous membranes.**" | 189 |
| Antivirals | "**Treatment for children with severe cases of HGS includes the use of antiviral agents such as acyclovir.**" | 189 |

> **Trap note — viscous lidocaine requires the child to hold and spit.** 1 teaspoon, 2 to 3
> minutes, then expectorate (p. 189). **The child must be able to cooperate**, which is why
> it is prescribed selectively and why the younger child gets the diphenhydramine-and-Maalox
> mixture applied with an applicator instead (p. 190).

### Nursing care management

**The two goals** (p. 189): "**relief of pain and prevention of spread of the herpes virus.**"

**Timing of analgesia** (p. 189): "**Analgesics and topical anesthetics are used as needed to provide
relief, especially before meals to encourage food and fluid intake.**"

**For infants and toddlers** (pp. 189–190): "**For younger infants and toddlers who cannot swish and
swallow, apply the diphenhydramine and Maalox**" (p. 189) "**solution with a cotton-tipped applicator
before feedings to minimize pain.**" (p. 190)

**Why it matters** (p. 190): "**Educating parents regarding the use of these medications is important to
maintain adequate hydration in the child whose mouth is too sore to take liquids.**"

**A practical aid** (p. 190): "**Drinking bland fluids through a straw is helpful in avoiding the
painful lesions.**"

**Mouth care** (p. 190): "**Encourage mouth care; the use of a very soft bristle toothbrush or disposable
foam-tipped toothbrush provides gentle cleaning near ulcerated areas.**"

**Preventing spread** (p. 190): "**Careful hand washing is essential when caring for children with HGS.
Because the infection is autoinoculable, children should keep their fingers out of the mouth;
contaminated hands can infect other body parts. Very young children may require elbow restraints to
ensure compliance. Articles placed in the mouth are cleaned thoroughly. Newborns and individuals with
immunosuppression should not be exposed to infected children.**"

**Nursing Alert** (p. 190): "**When examining herpetic lesions, wear gloves. The virus easily enters
breaks in the skin and can cause herpetic whitlow of the fingers.**"

**And the reassurance to give parents** (p. 190): "**Because herpes infection is often associated with
sexual transmission, explain to parents and older children that HGS is usually caused by type 1 HSV,
the type not associated with sexual activity.**"

> **Trap note — analgesia before meals, not after.** The stated purpose is
> "to encourage food and fluid intake" (p. 189). **A distractor medicates for pain after the
> child refuses to eat.**

> **Trap note — autoinoculable means the child can spread it to themselves.** Which is why
> fingers stay out of the mouth and why gloves protect the nurse from herpetic whitlow
> (p. 190). **The nurse's own risk is a named, specific complication.**

> **Trap note — the type 1 versus type 2 reassurance.** Parents may assume sexual
> transmission (p. 190). **Explaining that HGS is usually type 1 is a listed nursing
> action**, not an optional aside.

> preferred** and albendazole is explicitly less effective.

> Two different parasitic and viral anemias in one chapter (pp. 187, 190). **The mechanism
> differs: blood loss versus arrested red cell production.**

### Diagnosis

**The methods** (p. 192): "**microscopic examination of stool specimens or duodenal fluid or by
identification of G. intestinalis antigens in these specimens by techniques such as enzyme immunoassay
(EIA) and direct fluorescence antibody (DFA) assays.**"

**Why repeat testing may be needed** (p. 192): "**Because the Giardia organisms live in the upper
intestine and are excreted in a highly variable pattern, repeated microscopic examination of stool
specimens may be required to identify trophozoites (active parasites) or cysts.**"

**Duodenal sampling and the string test** (p. 192): "**Duodenal specimens are obtained by direct
aspiration, biopsy, or the string test. In the string test, the child swallows a gelatin capsule with a
nylon string attached. Several hours later, the string is withdrawn, and the contents are sent for
laboratory analysis.**"

**The trend** (p. 192): "**With the availability of EIA techniques to identify Giardia antigens in stool
specimens, other tests are being used less often.**"

| Drug | The chapter's words | p. |
|---|---|---|
| The three of choice | "**metronidazole (Flagyl), tinidazole (Tindamax), and nitazoxanide (Alinia)**" | 192 |

| Metronidazole and tinidazole | "**have a metallic taste and gastrointestinal side effects, including nausea and vomiting**" | 192 |

> **Trap note — nitazoxanide is the palatable one, and it goes with food.** Both features
> (p. 192), and they matter because the other two taste metallic and cause nausea. **In a

**The priority** (p. 192): "**The most important nursing consideration is prevention of giardiasis and

**The key practice** (p. 192): "**Attention to meticulous sanitary practices, especially during diaper

**The swimming rules** (p. 193):

| # | Rule |
|---|---|
| 1 | "**discourage young children who are infected or who have diarrhea from swimming in community or private pools until they have been infection free for 2 weeks**" |

> **Trap note — 2 weeks infection-free before swimming.** A specific exclusion period
> (p. 193).

| Fact | The chapter's words | p. |
|---|---|---|

| Prevalence | "**It is universally present in temperate climatic zones and may infect more than 30% of all children at any one time.**" | 193 |

### Box 6.5, Clinical Manifestations of Pinworms (p. 193) — complete

**The principal symptom** (p. 193): "**Intense perianal itching is the principal symptom.**"

**Evidence of itching in young children:**

| # | Sign |
|---|---|
| 1 | "**General irritability**" |
| 2 | **Restlessness** |
| 3 | **Poor sleep** |
| 4 | **Bed-wetting** |
| 5 | **Distractibility** |
| 6 | "**Short attention span**" |
| 7 | "**Perianal dermatitis and excoriation secondary to itching**" |

> **Trap note — the pinworm presentation looks behavioral.** Irritability, restlessness,
> poor sleep, **bed-wetting, distractibility, and short attention span** (p. 193). **A
> distractor attributes them to a sleep or attention disorder.** The discriminating symptom
> is the perianal itching, and the chapter says the rest are "evidence of itching."

### Diagnosis: the tape test

**When** (p. 193): "**Repeated tests to collect eggs may be necessary (3 consecutive days in the early
morning before the child washes are recommended for testing), and if there is a possibility that other
family members may be infected, a tape test should be performed on them.**"

**The technique, quoted complete** (p. 193): "**A loop of transparent (not frosted or magic) tape, sticky
side out, is placed around the end of a tongue depressor, which is then firmly pressed against the
child's perianal area.**"

**The timing** (p. 193): "**Pinworm specimens are collected in the morning as soon as the child awakens
and before the child has a bowel movement or bathes.**"

**How many times** (p. 193): "**The procedure may need to be performed on 3 or more consecutive days
before eggs are collected.**"

**Transport** (p. 193): "**Parents are instructed to place the tongue blade in a glass jar or loosely in
a plastic bag so it can be brought in for microscopic examination.**" For specimens collected in a
clinical setting, "**place the tape smoothly on a glass slide, sticky side down, for examination.**"

> **Trap note — transparent tape, not frosted.** The chapter specifies it (p. 193), because
> frosted tape is opaque under a microscope.

> **Trap note — three conditions on timing, all of which a distractor breaks.** **In the
> morning, as soon as the child awakens, before a bowel movement, and before bathing**
> (p. 193). **An evening or post-bath specimen is worthless.**

> **Trap note — sticky side OUT on the depressor, sticky side DOWN on the slide.** Both
> orientations (p. 193), and they are opposite because the tape is inverted when mounted.

| Point | The chapter's words | p. |
|---|---|---|
| The drugs | "**pyrantel pamoate (Pin-Rid, Antiminth) and albendazole**" | 193 |
| The age restriction | "**Mebendazole is not recommended for children younger than 2 years of age.**" | 193 |
| Who is treated | "**Because pinworms are easily transmitted, all household members should be treated.**" | 193 |
| The repeat dose | "**The dose of antiparasitic medication should be repeated in 2 weeks to completely eradicate the parasite and prevent reinfection.**" | 193 |
| Adherence | "**Adherence to the drug regimen is usually excellent because only one or two doses are needed.**" | 193 |

**The environmental measures, with the chapter's own caveat** (p. 193): "**To prevent reinfection,
washing all clothes and bed linens in hot water and vacuuming the house may be recommended. However,
there is little documentation on the effectiveness of these measures because pinworms survive on many
surfaces.**"

**The measures that help** (p. 193): "**hand washing after toileting and before eating, keeping the
child's fingernails short to minimize the chance of ova collecting under the nails, dressing children in
one-piece sleeping outfits, and daily showering rather than tub bathing.**"

**And the expectation to set** (p. 193): "**Inform families that recurrence is common. Treat repeated
infections in the same manner as the first one.**"

> **Trap note — treat the whole household, and repeat in 2 weeks.** Both (p. 193). **A
> distractor treats only the symptomatic child**, or gives a single dose.

> **Trap note — showers rather than tub baths, and one-piece sleepwear.** Two specific,
> unusual measures (p. 193): the shower avoids re-depositing eggs in bathwater, and
> one-piece sleepwear limits nighttime scratching.

> **Trap note — the chapter doubts the laundry-and-vacuum ritual.**
> "little documentation on the effectiveness of these measures" (p. 193). **A distractor
> makes environmental cleaning the primary intervention**; the chapter's primary
> interventions are the medication, the household-wide treatment, and the hand and nail
> measures.

### Management

**The three-part focus** (p. 193): "**proper identification, treatment of the symptoms, and
eradication.**"

**Identification** (p. 193): "**Bedbugs can be identified on bedding at night because of their nighttime
activity. They tend to hide in dark crevices (e.g., floor, walls, furniture) during the daytime and do
not stay on the human host.**"

**Two myths corrected** (p. 193): "**Contrary to several myths, bedbugs do not fly or jump.**"

**Misdiagnosis** (p. 193): "**It is not uncommon for bedbug bites to be misdiagnosed as scabies,
chickenpox, spider or mosquito bites, and even food anaphylaxis in some cases.**"

**Treatment** (p. 193): "**There is no specific treatment for bedbugs; topical steroids and systemic
antihistamines may be used to treat the urticaria. Secondary skin infections are treated with
antibiotics.**"

**Eradication** (p. 193): "**Eradication of bedbugs is complex and must be handled by professional
exterminators; multiple chemical applications are often required to completely eradicate the
insects.**"

**Travel advice** (p. 193): "**inspecting the mattresses for signs of infestation; encasing mattress
covers may be helpful. Thorough washing of all clothing and bed linens may also help minimize
exposure.**"

> **Trap note — bedbugs do not live on the host.** They
> "do not stay on the human host" (p. 193), which distinguishes them from lice and scabies
> and explains why treatment is environmental rather than topical to the child.

> **Trap note — no evidence of disease transmission.** (p. 193) **The health problems are
> secondary: infection from scratching, allergic reactions, and sleep loss.** A distractor
> names bedbugs as a vector.

> **Trap note — professional extermination, not a home remedy.** Stated as a must (p. 193).

### Question 1 (p. 194) — keyed **C**

**Stem:** "**Which of the following should be used in the care of all pediatric patients to reduce the

|---|---|
| A. "Transmission-Based Precautions" | **Wrong.** These are "designed for patients with documented or suspected infection or colonization" with specific pathogens (p. 169), not for all patients |
| B. "Airborne Precautions" | **Wrong.** One of the three transmission-based types, for droplet nuclei (p. 169) |

> **Trap note — the stem quotes the definition.** all patients and
> "recognized and unrecognized" are the two phrases that identify Standard Precautions
> (p. 169). **Recognizing a definitional stem is faster than reasoning about the options.**

### Question 2 (p. 194) — keyed **A**

**Stem:** "**Which childhood vaccine provides some protection against bacterial meningitis,
epiglottitis, and bacterial pneumonia?**"

| Option | Verdict |
|---|---|
| **A. Hib vaccine** | **Correct.** p. 174 lists exactly these: Hib conjugate vaccines "protect against a number of serious infections caused by H. influenzae type b, especially bacterial meningitis, epiglottitis, bacterial pneumonia, septic arthritis, and sepsis" |

> **Trap note — epiglottitis is the giveaway.** It appears in the Hib list and nowhere else
> in the chapter (p. 174). **Option D exists because of the name similarity the chapter

> "not protected against pertussis," and **only Tdap contains a pertussis component**

### Question 5 (p. 194) — keyed **B**

**Stem:** "**One of the most common intestinal parasitic pathogens in the United States acquired from a
contaminated water source such as a lake or swimming pool is:**"

| Option | Verdict |
|---|---|
| A. Tinea capitis | **Wrong**, and **this organism is not discussed anywhere in Chapter 6** (see the GAP register). It is a fungal scalp infection, not an intestinal parasite |
| **B. "Giardia intestinalis"** | **Correct.** It is "the most common intestinal parasitic pathogen in the United States" (p. 190), and "Contaminated water, especially in lakes and streams, and swimming or wading pools frequented by diapered infants are common sources of transmission" (p. 192) |
