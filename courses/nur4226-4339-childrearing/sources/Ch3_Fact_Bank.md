## Curation note

This is the curated layer over that raw pull: definitions quoted as the source gives them,
each with the printed page it sits on, plus the comparisons and traps the raw text holds
only implicitly. The blueprint weights Chapter 3 at **~7 questions**, the heaviest chapter
in its batch and the top of the standard band: a full key-terms table plus every named
blueprint topic covered.

This chapter is unusual for the book in that it is **almost entirely conceptual**. There is
no disease to nurse and no procedure to perform for most of its length. What it teaches is a
**vocabulary and four inheritance patterns**, and the exam questions come out of exactly
that: given a described family, which pattern is it, what is the recurrence risk, and who
can be a carrier.

The chapter is organized here around that spine. **Sections 9 through 13 are the core**:
autosomal dominant, autosomal recessive, X-linked recessive, X-linked dominant, and the
variations that break those rules. Everything before them is vocabulary and mechanism;
everything after is application, screening, counseling, and the nurse's role.

Emphasis went where item writers go: **the recurrence-risk numbers for each pattern**, **the
carrier-state question** (which patterns have carriers and which do not), **the three
configurations of Down syndrome and their very different recurrence risks**, **PKU** (this
chapter's own NCLEX question 4), **the purposes of genetic screening** (question 3), **Box
3.7's assessment clues** (question 5), **sex chromosome aneuploidies** (question 2), and
**the malformation-versus-disruption distinction** (question 1). Table 3.4's 30-plus
disorders are given in a compressed but complete form, because that table is a lookup
resource rather than something to memorize whole.

**One conceptual warning worth stating up front.** The chapter uses *dominant* and
*recessive* to describe **alleles**, not diseases, and it uses *carrier* in two different
senses: the ordinary heterozygous carrier of a recessive allele, and the much rarer
"carrier of the reduced penetrant allele" in autosomal dominant disease (p. 44). Keeping
those separate resolves most of the confusion this chapter generates.

## 2. Genetics, genomics, and what a gene actually does (pp. 43, 42)

### The human genome (p. 43)

"A human genome consists of 22 nuclear autosome chromosomes and a sex chromosome (either an X
or a Y), as well as a single circular molecule of DNA within the mitochondria" (p. 43).

| Cell | Contents as given (p. 43) |
|---|---|
| Any body cell but egg or sperm | "contains a maternally inherited and a paternally inherited copy of the genome in its nucleus, as well as the maternally inherited mitochondrial DNA" |
| Ovum | "a single nuclear genome (22 autosomes and the X chromosome), as well as the circular DNA strand within the mitochondria" |
| Sperm | "a single nuclear genome (22 autosomes and either the X or the Y chromosome) but does not contain mitochondria" |

**The sperm has no mitochondria. That one sentence is the entire explanation for maternal
mitochondrial inheritance** (pp. 43, 60), and it is the mechanism behind every mitochondrial
disorder in §16.

### Exons, introns, and what a mutation does (p. 43)

"Genes are segments of DNA that code for structural or functional proteins" (p. 43). "Areas of
DNA that do not code for proteins still have essential regulatory functions that are beginning
to be better understood" (p. 43).

| Element | As given (p. 43) |
|---|---|
| Locus | "The locations of genes or noncoding regulatory elements are often referred to as sites, or loci, indicating a physical or" geographic "location on a chromosome" |
| Exons | "Exons are the portions of a gene that contain the code for a specific protein" |
| Introns | "Introns may have important regulatory elements or function as spacers between exons to improve the efficiency of the cellular molecules that transcribe the gene" |
| What is removed | "Although the entire gene is transcribed, the intron portions of the mRNA are removed so that only the exonic codes remain in the mature mRNA that leaves the nucleus and enters the cytoplasm where it is translated into an eventual protein" |
| Effect of mutation | "Mutations in genes may have significant qualitative and quantitative effects on the synthesis of the corresponding protein, with potential clinical consequences" |

**Two protein classes (p. 43):** "Proteins can be classified as structural (or constitutive)
proteins, and functional, those that affect the metabolism of other molecules or substrates
(enzymes)."

**And the sentence that undoes a common assumption (p. 43):** "Such alterations in an
individual genome may have been inherited from a parent or may represent an event that is new
to that person and may be the first case in that family (new mutation). **It is therefore
erroneous to consider all genetic disorders as having a positive family history.**"

**A negative family history does not rule out a genetic disorder.** That is the chapter's own
emphasis and it recurs under autosomal dominant new mutations (p. 44) and VCFS (p. 50).

### Table 3.1, protein to disease (p. 43)

The table's whole point is that the *kind* of protein determines the *kind* of disease.

| Protein | Altered mechanism as given | Resulting disorder |
|---|---|---|
| **Constitutive proteins** | | |
| Globins | "Altered oxygen transport" | hemoglobinopathies: Sickle cell disease and thalassemias |
| Dystrophin | Muscle cell defect | "Muscular dystrophies" |
| Coagulation factors VIII and IX | "Abnormal clotting activity" | Hemophilia A and B |
| **Enzymes** | | |
| Phenylalanine hydroxylase | "Interrupted metabolism of phenylalanine and accumulation of toxic precursors (phenylalanine and phenylketones)" | phenylketonuria |
| Hexosaminidase A | "Interrupted metabolism and accumulation of precursors (GM2 ganglioside)" | Tay-Sachs disease |
| Hypoxanthine-guanine phosphoribosyltransferase | "Disruption of metabolic feedback mechanism and accumulation of end product (uric acid)" | "Lesch-Nyhan syndrome" |
| HMG-CoA reductase | "Disruption of metabolic feedback mechanism and accumulation of end product (cholesterol)" | "Familial hypercholesterolemia" |

**Note the two enzyme failure modes, because §17 builds its whole model on them:** either a
*precursor accumulates behind a block*, or an *end product accumulates because feedback
failed*. Tay-Sachs and PKU are the first kind; Lesch-Nyhan and familial hypercholesterolemia
are the second.

### Genetics versus genomics (p. 43)

| Term | As given (p. 43) |
|---|---|
| Genetics | "When referring to the study of individual genes and their impact on distinct disorders we use the term genetics" |
| Genomics | "the term genomics refers to the study of all the genes in the human genome and the multiple interactions between many genes" |
| The caveat | "Although often used interchangeably" |

**One gene versus all of them.** Box 3.1 adds that genetics concerns "relatively rare
single-gene disorders" (p. 41) while genomics includes "their interactions with each other and

## 3. Genes and environment together (pp. 43, 42)

**The organizing claim (p. 43):** "Whether considering single genes or the entire genome's
influence on disease, environmental factors must also be considered. Most diseases are
influenced by a genetic/genomic predisposition that can be activated, modified, or suppressed
by environmental factors."

| Example | The genetic half | The environmental trigger as given |
|---|---|---|
| PKU (p. 43) | "the (genetically determined) absence of an enzyme that metabolizes the amino acid phenylalanine" | "the deleterious effects in the infant are expressed only after sufficient ingestion of phenylalanine-containing substances, such as milk (environmental trigger)" |
| Sickle cell disease (p. 43) | a classic single-gene condition | "its acute signs and symptoms are precipitated by certain conditions such as lowered oxygen tension, infection, or dehydration" |
| Cancer (p. 42) | inherited or somatic mutation | "environmental insult, such as tobacco smoking, sun exposure, and radiation, can be carcinogenic" |
| Neural tube defects (p. 43) | multifactorial susceptibility | folic acid intake (pp. 67, 69) |

**PKU is the cleanest illustration in the whole chapter: the gene is broken from conception,
and nothing happens until the baby is fed** (p. 43).

### Inherited versus somatic cancer (p. 42)

"Cancer is another example of genetic-environment interplay and explains the difference between
inherited conditions and somatic cell genetic disorders" (p. 42).

| | Somatic (sporadic) | Inherited predisposition |
|---|---|---|
| Where the change is | "A normal somatic cell (any body cell other than the ova and sperm) may become a cancer cell after acquiring a series of gene changes. This process is the typical genetic cause of cancer" (p. 42) | "In a small subset of families, a mutation in a gene normally involved in regulation of cell growth, DNA repair, or cell death (apoptosis) is transmitted through the germ cells (ova and sperm)" (p. 42) |
| Consequence | confined to that cell line | "Children who inherit the genetic mutation will have it in all of their somatic cells, making them more susceptible to subsequent genetic changes in one or more cells that may transform into cancer cells" (p. 42) |
| Heritable to offspring | no | yes |

**Inheriting a cancer gene does not mean inheriting cancer; it means starting with the first
step already taken** (p. 42). That framing returns under predisposition testing in §21, where
some BRCA carriers "may die in their eighties without ever developing cancer" (p. 67).

### Genes and infection: the CCR5 story (p. 42)

"Evidence is growing that genes play an important role in human susceptibility and resistance
to infection even in cases with a clear environmental cause of the infectious disease"
(p. 42).

| Finding | As given (p. 42) |
|---|---|
| The adults | "Researchers identified adults with a specific deletion in both copies of their CCR5 genes who did not become infected with human immunodeficiency virus (HIV) despite repeated exposure" |
| The children | "children exposed to HIV in utero typically had a significantly delayed onset of disease if at least one of their CCR5 genes had the specific mutation" |
| The payoff | "Understanding the mechanism of resistance associated with CCR5 mutation led to a novel molecular therapy" |
| Why it is in this chapter | "This type of drug discovery or development informed by a gene is a newer aspect of pharmacogenetics" |

**Two copies of the deletion gave resistance; one copy delayed the disease.** That gene-dose
relationship is the same logic as heterozygote versus homozygote in §9 and §10 (p. 42).

### Pharmacogenetics and pharmacogenomics (p. 42)

"The study of how genes influence a person's response to medication is called
pharmacogenetics or pharmacogenomics" (p. 42).

| Term | As given (p. 42) |
|---|---|
| Pharmacogenetics | "the study of single genes and their respective proteins that influence a medication's intended effect and the risk for adverse effects from the medication" |
| Pharmacogenomics | "the study of the portion of the genome that contributes to medication response" |
| Current usage | "These terms are often used interchangeably, and a trend to simply use the term pharmacogenomics is becoming apparent" |

**The mechanism (p. 42):** "Genes involved in drug response may not be expressed until a
medication is given. Variations in the genes predispose a person to experiencing unintended
effects from normal doses of certain medications."

**Both directions of failure, as given (p. 42):** "Unintended effects include absent or reduced
therapeutic effect resulting from subtherapeutic levels of the active drug or adverse drug
reactions caused by toxicity from abnormally high levels of the active drug after given a
normal dose."

**A normal dose can be too little or too much depending on the child's genes.** Not just
toxicity, and that is the half people forget (p. 42).

**The one-to-one mapping worth holding: genetics is to single genes as pharmacogenetics is to
single drug-response genes.** The narrower term is always the single-gene one (pp. 41, 42, 43).

## 4. Congenital anomalies (pp. 42, 45)

### The four mechanisms (p. 42)

"Congenital anomalies, or birth defects, occur in 2% to 4% of all live-born children and are
often classified as deformations, disruptions, dysplasias, or malformations" (p. 42).

**This four-way classification is the chapter's first testable set, and it is the subject of
this chapter's own NCLEX question 1.**

| Type | Definition as given (p. 42) | Example as given |
|---|---|---|
| **Deformation** | "Deformations are often caused by extrinsic mechanical forces on normally developing tissue" | "Club foot is an example of a deformation often caused by uterine constraint" |
| **Disruption** | "Disruptions result from the breakdown of previously normal tissue" | "Congenital amputations caused by amniotic bands (fibrous strands of amnion that wrap around different body parts during development) are examples of disruption anomalies" |
| **Dysplasia** | "Dysplasias result from abnormal organization of cells into a particular tissue type" | "Congenital abnormalities of the teeth, hair, nails, or sweat glands may be manifestations of one of the more than 100 different ectodermal dysplasia syndromes" |
| **Malformation** | "Malformations are abnormal formations of organs or body parts resulting from an abnormal developmental process" | cleft lip |

**The discriminator in one line each: deformation is normal tissue pushed out of shape,
disruption is normal tissue destroyed, dysplasia is tissue built wrong, and malformation is an
organ that never formed correctly** (p. 42).

**Timing (p. 42):** "Most malformations occur before 12 weeks of gestation."

**The cleft lip worked example (p. 42), which is the source of NCLEX question 1:** "Cleft lip,
an example of a malformation, occurs at approximately 5 weeks of gestation when the developing
embryo naturally has two clefts in the area. Normally between 5 and 7 weeks, cells rapidly
divide and migrate to fill in those clefts. If there is an abnormality in this developmental
process, the embryo is left with either a unilateral or bilateral cleft lip that may also
involve the palate."

**Cleft lip is a malformation, not a disruption.** The tissue never came together; nothing was
broken down. Any answer option calling it a disruption is wrong on the chapter's own
definitions (p. 42).

**Why development is so vulnerable (p. 42):** "Embryogenesis and fetal development are an
intricate and precisely timed series of events in which all parts must be properly integrated
to ensure a coordinated whole. Insults during development or abnormalities in differentiation
or in the proper timing of organogenesis may result in a variety of congenital anomalies."

### Major versus minor, and isolated versus patterned (p. 45)

"The types of anomalies that can result from genetic or prenatal environmental agents can be
major structural abnormalities with serious medical, surgical, or quality-of-life
consequences, or they can be minor anomalies or normal variants with no serious consequences,
such as a sacral dimple, an extra nipple, or a café-au-lait spot" (p. 45).

"Congenital anomalies can occur in isolation, such as congenital heart defect, or multiple
anomalies may be present" (p. 45).

### Syndrome, association, and sequence (p. 45)

**This is the chapter's second confusable set, and the three are separated by whether the cause
is known and whether the defects are causally chained.**

| Term | Definition as given (p. 45) | Example |
|---|---|---|
| **Syndrome** | "A recognized pattern of anomalies resulting from a single specific cause is called a syndrome" | "Down syndrome or fetal alcohol syndrome" |
| **Association** | "A nonrandom pattern of malformations for which a cause has not been determined is called an association" | "VACTERL [vertebral defects, anal atresia, cardiac defect, tracheoesophageal fistula, and renal and limb defects] association" |
| **Sequence** | "When a single anomaly leads to a cascade of additional anomalies, the pattern of defects is referred to as a sequence" | Pierre Robin sequence |

**Syndrome has one known cause; association has a pattern with no known cause; sequence has one
first defect that causes the rest** (p. 45).

**The Pierre Robin worked example (p. 45):** "Pierre Robin sequence begins with the abnormal
development of the mandible, resulting in abnormal placement of the tongue during development.
The normal developmental process for the palate is prevented because the tongue obstructs the
migration of the palatal shelves toward the midline, and a cleft palate remains. Consequently,
infants born with Pierre Robin sequence have a recessed mandible and an abnormally placed
tongue and are at risk for obstructive apnea."

**The airway risk is the nursing point.** Recessed mandible plus displaced tongue equals
obstructive apnea (p. 45), and that is what makes this a sequence rather than a coincidence.

**The same defect can arrive by several routes (p. 45):** "NTDs, cleft lip and palate,
deafness, congenital heart defects, and cognitive impairment are examples of congenital
malformations that can occur in isolation or as part of a syndrome, association, or sequence
and can have different causes, such as single-gene or chromosome abnormalities, prenatal
exposures, or multifactorial causes."

## 5. The four causes of genetic disorders (p. 45)

"Genetic disorders can be caused by chromosome abnormalities as seen in Turner syndrome, Down
syndrome, or velo-cardio-facial syndrome (VCFS); single-gene mutations as seen in sickle cell
anemia, neurofibromatosis, or Duchenne muscular dystrophy; a combination of genetic and
environmental factors as seen in NTDs or maturity-onset diabetes in the young; and
mitochondrial DNA (mtDNA) mutations as seen in nonsyndromic deafness susceptibility due to
aminoglycoside sensitivity" (p. 45).

**Four causes, four example sets, and each gets its own section below:** chromosome
abnormalities in §6 through §8, single-gene disorders in §9 through §13, multifactorial in
§17, and mitochondrial in §16.

| Scale | As given (p. 45) |
|---|---|
| Chromosome level | "numeric or structural chromosome aberrations automatically involve large groups of genes" |
| Gene level | "a small gene mutation does not alter chromosome structure and number" |
| Why it is invisible | "Alterations in single genes (single-gene disorders) or in many genes (polygenic disorders) may represent a lesion too small to cause an identifiable alteration in chromosomal structure" |
| Gene count | "Human nucleated somatic cells contain approximately 25,000 genes distributed across 46 chromosomes" |
| Size and gene number | "Because human chromosomes vary in size, the larger the chromosome, the greater the number of genes carried" |

**That last sentence explains the severity gradient of the trisomies.** Trisomy 13 "carries more
severe malformations than the previous two trisomies discussed, consistent with the increased
size of the extra chromosome and greater gene imbalance" (p. 48).

**What alerts the nurse (p. 45):** "Nurses often note dysmorphic facial features, behavioral
characteristics such as an unusual cry and poor feeding behavior, and other neurologic
manifestations such as hypotonia or abnormal reflex responses, which may alert them to these
and other chromosome abnormalities."

## 6. Numeric chromosome abnormalities (pp. 45–46)

### Ploidy vocabulary (p. 45)

| Term | As given (p. 45) |
|---|---|
| Diploid | "human somatic cells are diploid (a cell that contains two copies of each chromosome). A diploid chromosome number in humans is represented by the notation 2n = 46" |
| Haploid | "A haploid chromosome constitution (n = 23) is found in germ cells, the male and female gametes (sperm and ova)" |
| Autosomes and sex chromosomes | "Somatic cells contain 44 autosomes (the 22 pairs of chromosomes that do not greatly influence sex determination at conception) and 2 sex chromosomes: XX in females and XY in males" |
| Karyotype | "the laboratory-made arrangement of specially prepared chromosomes according to their size, banding patterns, and centromere position" |
| Centromere classification | "The location of the centromere allows the classification of human chromosomes as acrocentric, submetacentric, and metacentric chromosomes" |

### Euploidy versus aneuploidy (p. 45)

**The distinction: whole extra SETS versus one extra or missing chromosome.**

| | Euploidy | Aneuploidy |
|---|---|---|
| Definition | "a euploid cell being one whose chromosome number is a multiple of 23" (p. 45) | "one chromosome may be added to or lost from one of the pairs, creating a condition of aneuploidy" (p. 45) |
| Arithmetic | "triploid cells with 69 chromosomes (46 + 23), or tetraploid cells with 92 chromosomes (46 + 23 + 23)" (p. 45) | 47 for a trisomy, 45 for a monosomy (p. 45) |
| Survival | "Individuals who are triploid (3n = 69) have a genetic imbalance of such magnitude that the few who are carried to term have severe multiple abnormalities that limit their life span to a few hours or days" (p. 45) | variable by chromosome |

| Aneuploidy type | As given (p. 45) |
|---|---|
| Trisomy | "When one chromosome is added to a pair, the embryo, fetus, or child is described as having a trisomy, and the total chromosome number is 47" |
| Which trisomies survive | "Most fetuses that have an autosomal trisomy are not live born. Fetuses with trisomy 21, trisomy 18, and trisomy 13 may be live born" |
| Monosomy | "When one chromosome is lost from the pair, the fetus is described as having a monosomy, and the total chromosome number in somatic cells is 45" |
| **The asymmetry** | "The loss of a chromosome and its related complement of genes is overall more detrimental than the addition of a chromosome" |
| The only survivable monosomy | "The only monosomy compatible with life is monosomy X (Turner syndrome), yet most 45,X pregnancies spontaneously miscarry" |

**Losing a chromosome is worse than gaining one.** Only three autosomal trisomies reach live
birth and only one monosomy does, and even that one usually miscarries (p. 45).

### Disjunction and nondisjunction (pp. 45–46)

"The most common cause of alteration in the number of chromosomes is a misdistribution of
chromosomes during mitosis or meiosis" (p. 45).

**Normal (p. 45):** "As somatic cells multiply by mitosis, each daughter cell receives the same
chromosome number as the mother cell. This equitable chromosome sharing in anaphase is due to
a phenomenon termed disjunction, by which chromatids of each chromosome separate and migrate
to opposite poles of the cell."

**Abnormal (p. 46):** "Disruption of this orderly chromosome distribution occurs in
nondisjunction, which can occur during both mitosis and meiosis." (The sentence begins on
p. 45 and completes on p. 46.)

### Mitotic versus meiotic nondisjunction: the pair that produces two different results

| | Mitotic nondisjunction | Meiotic nondisjunction |
|---|---|---|
| When | after fertilization, "during embryonic development" (p. 46) | during gametogenesis, "during meiosis I and II, during both oogenesis and spermatogenesis" (p. 46) |
| What it produces | **mosaicism** | **aneuploidy in every cell** |
| Mechanism | "failure of chromatids to separate properly during anaphase will result in daughter cells with different chromosome numbers (e.g., 45 and 47, instead of 46 and 46)" (p. 46) | "resulting in gametes with an aneuploid chromosome number (e.g., 22 or 24, instead of 23)" (p. 46) |
| Which cells survive | "the 45-chromosome monosomic cells will tend to degenerate and die, but those with the extra chromosome (trisomic) may continue to divide and generate a complete line of trisomic cells" (p. 46) | "gametes lacking a chromosome are not likely to survive, but gametes with an extra chromosome are more often viable" (p. 46) |
| Result | "the trisomic cell line proliferates concomitantly with the normal cell line and results in an individual with mosaicism for that particular chromosome" (p. 46) | "Fertilization of an aneuploid gamete with a normal gamete will produce an aneuploid zygote" (p. 46) |
| Frequency | rarer | "Meiotic nondisjunction is a major cause of aneuploidy" (p. 46); "The most common aneuploidies in humans are trisomies" (p. 46) |

**Before conception gives a whole affected child; after conception gives a mosaic.** That is
the cleanest statement of the pair (p. 46).

### Mosaicism in detail (p. 46)

"Mosaicism therefore results in an individual (mosaic) with two or more genetically different
cell populations" (p. 46).

**The notation, as printed (p. 46):** "The chromosomal notation for a male with mosaic type of
Down syndrome, for example, is 46,XY/47,XY,+21). The slash (/) indicates a dual cell population
in which one has the normal chromosomal constitution (46,XY), whereas the other carries an
extra chromosome 21 and has a total chromosome number of 47."

**The slash is the mosaicism marker.** Any karyotype in this chapter with a slash in it
describes two cell lines (pp. 46, 48).

| Factor | As given (p. 46) |
|---|---|
| What sets the level | "The percentage, or level, of mosaicism depends on the stage of embryonic development in which the cell division error occurs" |
| Earliest error | "If it occurs at the first cell division after fertilization, the level of mosaicism may be as high as 50%" |
| Later error | "If the cell division error occurs in later development, the abnormal cells may be localized to one cell type, such as the brain tissue or germ cell line (ovaries or testes)" |
| What determines severity | "The extent of clinical manifestations is determined by the type of tissues that contain cells with abnormal chromosome numbers and the percentage of affected cells, and may vary from near normal to a fully manifested syndrome" |

**Earlier error, more mosaicism.** And the tissue distribution matters as much as the
percentage, which is why mosaicism does not reliably mean milder disease (pp. 46, 48).

### Trisomy 21 (pp. 46–48)

| Fact | As given (p. 46) |

| Variability | "Physical and cognitive abnormalities vary" |

**The three configurations, which are the most examinable content in this chapter.**
"The chromosomal constitution of Down syndrome is variable, with three possible
configurations" (p. 46).

| | **1. Free trisomy** | **2. Translocation** | **3. Mosaic** |

**The maternal-age paradox, stated outright (p. 47):** "however, because young women have more
babies, about 75% of babies with trisomy 21 are born to younger mothers."

**Risk per pregnancy rises with maternal age; the absolute number of affected babies is higher
in younger mothers.** Both are true and a distractor is built by presenting either alone
(p. 47).

**Translocation recurrence risks, which are the numbers to memorize (pp. 47–48):**

| Situation | Risk as given |
|---|---|
| Theoretic risk, carrier parent with a normal partner | "their theoretic chances of producing a live-born child with Down syndrome are 33%" (p. 47) |
| **Observed risk, mother is the carrier** | approximately 15% (p. 48) |
| **Observed risk, father is the carrier** | less than 10% (p. 48) |
| Producing another balanced carrier | "The observed chance of producing a live-born child who is a balanced translocation carrier approaches 50%" (p. 48) |
| **21;21 translocation carrier** | "A carrier mother [45,XX,t(21;21)] or father [45,XY,t(21;21)] of the translocation would have a 100% chance of producing a pregnancy with Down syndrome because the other parent normally would contribute one chromosome 21" (p. 48) |

**The observed risk is lower than the theoretic risk, and it differs by which parent carries
the translocation.** Theoretic 33%, observed about 15% from the mother and under 10% from the
father (pp. 47, 48). **A distractor that gives 33% as the counseling figure has used the wrong
number.**

**And the 21;21 case is one of the few absolutes in genetics (p. 48):** "This latter situation
is one of the rare examples in genetics where an abnormality is passed on to all living
progeny."

**The mosaicism warning, which contradicts intuition (p. 48):** "Contrary to what one might
expect, children with mosaic Down syndrome do not necessarily have a better developmental
outcome than those with free trisomy type. The proportion of trisomic cells in various tissues
and organs play a role in the child's developmental potential and syndrome-associated potential
health problems."

**Mosaic does not reliably mean milder.** Reassuring a family on that basis is contradicted by
the text (p. 48).

### Trisomy 18 and trisomy 13 (p. 48)

| | Trisomy 18 (Edwards) | Trisomy 13 (Patau) |
|---|---|---|
| As given | "Edwards syndrome is a fairly common trisomy in fetuses. **The severity order follows chromosome size: 21 is the smallest autosome, then 18, then 13**
(pp. 45, 48). Down syndrome is compatible with adulthood; the other two are not.

## 8. Sex chromosome aneuploidies (pp. 48–49)

**The general rule, and it is the answer to a comparison question (p. 48):** "An alteration in
the number of sex chromosomes usually does not produce the profound effects that are associated
with the autosomal trisomies. Intelligence may be normal or low normal, or the child may have
some learning disabilities, but moderate or severe cognitive impairment is less common."

"Some of the most common genetic disorders caused by sex chromosome aneuploidies are
Klinefelter, XYY, triple-X female, and Turner syndromes" (p. 48).

**The mechanism is the same as for autosomes (p. 48):** "prefertilization nondisjunction during
one of the meiotic divisions of gametogenesis in either parent or in the early postfertilization
divisions of the zygote."

### The four, side by side

| | **47,XXY Klinefelter** | **47,XYY** | **45,XO Turner** | **47,XXX triple-X** |

| Sex | male | male | female | female |
| Physical | "decreased masculinization, such as gynecomastia; hypogonadism (with sterility resulting from degeneration of seminiferous tubules); and increased pubis-to-sole length, reflecting elongated lower limbs" (p. 48) | not described | "ovarian dysgenesis (with gonads consisting of streaks of connective tissue and devoid of germ cells)" (p. 48) | a normal phenotype (p. 49) |

| Mosaic form | "Chromosome mosaicism (46,XY/47,XXY) rarely occurs and results in individuals with milder manifestations than their trisomic counterparts" (p. 48) | not given | "Mosaicism also occurs in Turner syndrome (e.g., 46,XX/45,XO), resulting in milder expression of the phenotype" (p. 48) | not given |

**Note the mosaicism inconsistency across the chapter.** For the sex chromosome aneuploidies,
mosaicism is stated to give **milder** disease (p. 48). For mosaic Down syndrome, the chapter
explicitly warns that it does **not necessarily** give a better outcome (p. 48). Both statements
are in the source, one page apart; see the GAP register.

**Klinefelter's diagnostic difficulty (p. 48):** "Overall, the phenotype of Klinefelter syndrome
is highly variable, making it difficult, in the absence of chromosome studies, to make a
prepubertal clinical diagnosis."

**The XYY history lesson, which is the reason the chapter includes this karyotype (p. 48):**
"This genotype was reported in the early 1960s by Patricia Jacobs, a Scottish cytogeneticist who
detected an increased frequency of double-Y men among inmates of penal institutions in Great
Britain. **This and subsequent early studies that linked this karyotype with criminal behavior
were significantly flawed by selection bias.**"

**XYY is not a criminality karyotype.** The chapter names the study, names the flaw, and names
the actual current association, which is autism spectrum disorder risk (p. 48). Any answer
option linking XYY to criminal behavior is repeating a retracted claim.

**The extra Y is paternal (p. 48):** "The extra Y is paternally derived. Although XYY fathers
have an increased risk for offspring with abnormal Y chromosome complement, the majority of
live births have a normal number of sex chromosomes."

### Turner syndrome in detail (pp. 48–49)

| Element | As given |
|---|---|
| Growth pattern | "There is no prepubertal growth spurt" (p. 48); "The child's growth is usually normal until 3 years of age and then slows, gradually drifting away from the normal growth curve" (p. 48) |
| **Hormone therapy trade-off** | "It is common practice to administer female hormones around the time that puberty would occur to provide the girl with Turner syndrome some secondary sex characteristics; however, the female hormones may further stunt growth and must be used judiciously" (p. 48) |

**No prepubertal growth spurt, and the hormones that give secondary sex characteristics cost

says the hormones "must be used judiciously" (p. 48).

**A distractor to watch: this chapter's own NCLEX question 2 offers the statement that
"Turner syndrome girls have a prepubertal growth spurt and then mostly stop growing" (p. 77),
which reverses the text** (p. 48).

### Triple-X (p. 49)

"females with triple-X display a normal phenotype, with an increased risk of learning
disabilities compared with their euploid sisters. **Gynecologic complications include delayed
menarche and premature menopause.** As with XYY men, the offspring of XXX women is largely

**Delayed menarche and premature menopause, in that order.** This chapter's own NCLEX question

## 9. Structural chromosome abnormalities (pp. 49–50)

### Breakage (p. 49)

"Chromosomes are subject to structural alterations resulting from breakage and rearrangement.
Chromosome breakage has long been recognized as a significant source of genetic abnormalities"
(p. 49).

| Element | As given (p. 49) |
|---|---|
| Clastogens | "Many clastogens (chromosome-breaking agents) have been identified, including physical (e.g., ionizing radiation), chemical (e.g., chlorpromazine), and biologic (e.g., viral infections) agents" |
| Nonspecific causes | "Chromosome breakage can also result from many nonspecific causes, such as influenza. These breaks usually are restricted to somatic cells and are temporary" |
| **When it matters** | "Chromosome breakage becomes significant when it is permanent (or long lasting) and when these permanent changes, in addition to appearing in somatic cells, are also present in germ cells and thus have the potential of being transmitted to the offspring" |

**A break only matters genetically if it is permanent and in a germ cell.** Somatic, temporary
breakage from an ordinary infection is not transmitted (p. 49).

### Deletions and ring chromosomes (p. 49)

| Type | As given (p. 49) |
|---|---|
| Deletion | "A chromosome deletion occurs when chromosome breakage results in loss of the broken fragment at a chromosome's terminal end or within the chromosome" |
| Clinical impact | "Chromosome deletions often have significant clinical impact, as in a chromosome 5 terminal deletion that results in cri du chat syndrome" |
| Sticky ends | "Chromosome breakage can create unstable end points", which "predispose the chromosomes to a variety of rearrangements of the fragments" |
| Ring chromosome | "If a break occurs in the terminal end of both arms of a chromosome, the ends may fuse together, forming a circle" |
| What determines severity | "Like any structural alteration of a chromosome, the clinical manifestations depend on which genes are lost" |

### Translocations, the pair (p. 49)

"A more common rearrangement resulting from chromosome breakage is a translocation, which
occurs when a chromosomal fragment reunites with another, nonhomologous chromosome. Two types
of translocations have clinical significance: reciprocal translocations and robertsonian
translocations" (p. 49).

| | Reciprocal | Robertsonian |
|---|---|---|
| Mechanism | "breaks occur in two different chromosomes and the fragments are mutually exchanged, resulting in derivative chromosomes" (p. 49) | "occur when the short arms of two acrocentric chromosomes (pairs 13 to 15 and pairs 21 and 22) break off and the remaining long arms fuse at the centromere" (p. 49) |
| Which chromosomes | any two | **only acrocentric ones: 13, 14, 15, 21, 22** |
| Result in the carrier | balanced, no clinical manifestations | balanced, no clinical manifestations |

**Robertsonian translocations happen only between acrocentric chromosomes, which is why Down
syndrome translocations involve 14 and 21 or 21 and 21** (pp. 47, 48, 49).

**The balanced carrier (p. 49):** "Both types result in individuals who have the correct amount
of genetic information (although rearranged), and therefore no clinical manifestations are
expected. These persons are termed balanced translocation carriers."

| Consequence | As given (p. 49) |
|---|---|
| Transmission | "These asymptomatic, balanced translocation carriers (either male or female) may pass the translocation to their offspring in a balanced or unbalanced form, depending on how the chromosomes segregate to the gametes" |
| Unbalanced outcome | "If it is passed in the unbalanced form, the combination is often lethal, and an early spontaneous abortion occurs" |
| What decides the risk | "The chance of having a live-born child with birth defects associated with the unbalanced translocation depends on the quantity and role of the missing or additional genetic material" |
| **The recurrent-loss statistic** | "Approximately 5% of cases of repeated spontaneous abortion (two or more) can be attributed to a balanced translocation carrier parent" |

**Recurrent miscarriage is a karyotype indication.** 5% of couples with two or more losses have
a balanced translocation carrier (p. 49), and that is why Box 3.6 lists "Balanced translocation
in mother or father" as an indication for prenatal testing (p. 70).

### Cri du chat syndrome (p. 49)

"Cri du chat, or cat's cry, syndrome is a rare (1 : 50,000 live births) chromosome deletion
syndrome resulting from loss of the small arm of chromosome 5" (p. 49).

| Feature | As given (p. 49) |
|---|---|
| Early face | "In early infancy this syndrome manifests with a typical but nondistinctive facial appearance, often a" moon-shaped "face with wide-spaced eyes (hypertelorism)" |
| **The face fades** | "As the child grows, this feature is progressively diluted, and by age 2 years the child is indistinguishable from age-matched controls" |
| Cognition | "Profound cognitive impairment persists throughout their short life; many die in infancy" |
| The cry | "Typical of this disease is a crying pattern that is abnormal and catlike. At times it sounds like an angry cat, and at others like a soft mewing sound" |
| Its cause | "This is a result of a laryngeal atrophy that improves with age" |
| **The cry fades too** | "By age 3 years the crying pattern is still abnormal, but it acquires a normal pitch and loses its catlike quality" |

**Both diagnostic signs disappear with age, and the cognitive impairment does not.** The face
is gone by 2 and the catlike cry by 3 (p. 49). **A stem describing a 4-year-old with cri du chat
should not offer the catlike cry as a current finding.**

### Fragile X syndrome (p. 50)

"Fragile X syndrome acquired its name from the fact that, in special cell culture conditions,
the affected X chromosome may display a gap in its terminal portion. However, it is an X-linked
condition caused by an unstable expansion" (p. 50).

**"Fragile X is the most common cause of inherited cognitive impairment"** (p. 50).

**Clinical features (p. 50):** "a typical facial appearance, with an elongated face and large
ears; macroorchidism in adolescent males; connective tissue dysplasia; and behavioral problems,
including autism spectrum disorder."

**Most common inherited cause, which is not the same as most common cause.** Down syndrome is
more common overall but is usually not inherited (pp. 46, 50).

### Velo-cardio-facial syndrome (p. 50)

"Velo-cardio-facial syndrome (VCFS), sometimes called DiGeorge syndrome or CATCH22, is a common
(1 : 4000) microdeletion syndrome. It is caused by a specific microdeletion within the long arm
of chromosome 22 (22q11.2 deletion)" (p. 50).

| Element | As given (p. 50) |
|---|---|
| Variability | "Manifestations of this condition are variable, with nearly 200 different possible clinical features described. Although no one feature is found in every patient, cognitive impairment is common" |
| Range | "can range from full-scale measured IQ in the borderline low-normal range with characteristic learning disabilities to mild cognitive impairment" |
| **Inheritance** | "Although most patients' deletion is caused by a sporadic event, those with the condition can transmit the microdeletion in an autosomal dominant manner. Therefore their chances of producing a child with VCFS are 50% with each pregnancy" |

**A chromosome deletion that is inherited as an autosomal dominant.** VCFS is the chapter's

cardiac defect and subsequently found to have VCFS requires surgical intervention for the
congenital malformations. Such an infant also benefits from early intervention services and
eventually an individualized education plan in school because developmental delay and eventual
learning problems are common."

### Chromosome instability syndromes (p. 50)

"Chromosome instability syndromes are a heterogeneous group of genetic disorders characterized
by a high frequency of chromosome breakage observed in vitro. They include ataxia
telangiectasia, Fanconi anemia, and xeroderma pigmentosum. **These syndromes are associated with
decreased immune function and an increased incidence of cancer**" (p. 50).

**Three names, one consequence: broken chromosomes mean immune deficiency and cancer risk**

## 10. Single-gene disorders and how they are detected (p. 50)

"Chromosome anomalies typically affect large numbers of genes; however, a single-gene disorder
is caused by an abnormality within a gene or in a gene's regulatory region. Single-gene
disorders can affect all body systems and may have mild to severe expression" (p. 50).

| Element | As given (p. 50) |
|---|---|
| Pattern | "These disorders display a mendelian pattern of dominant or recessive inheritance that was first delineated in the mid" nineteenth century "by Gregor Mendel's experiments with plants" |
| **Detection** | "Single-gene disorders cannot be detected by chromosome analysis and demand specific and sophisticated molecular detection methods, such as DNA-based techniques" |

**A normal karyotype does not rule out a single-gene disorder.** That sentence is the reason
Table 3.3 exists and the reason §20 distinguishes cytogenetic from molecular testing (pp. 50,
66).

### Penetrance and expressivity, the pair (p. 50)

"Mendelian inheritance laws allow for risk prediction in single-gene disorders; however,
phenotypic expression may be altered by incomplete penetrance or variable expressivity of the
responsible allele" (p. 50).

| | Incomplete penetrance | Variable expressivity |
|---|---|---|
| Definition | "An allele is said to have reduced or incomplete penetrance in a population when a proportion of persons who possess that allele do not express the phenotype" (p. 50) | "An allele is said to have variable expressivity when individuals possessing that allele display the features of the syndrome in various degrees, from mild to severe" (p. 50) |
| The question it answers | **whether** the phenotype appears at all | **how much** phenotype appears |
| Applies to | a population | individuals |
| The boundary | "If a person expresses even the mildest possible phenotype, the allele is penetrant in that individual" (p. 50) | |

**Penetrance is all or nothing; expressivity is a matter of degree.** And the boundary sentence
settles the overlap: the faintest expression still counts as penetrant (p. 50).

**The worked example of variable expressivity (p. 59):** "a parent of a child with classic
neurofibromatosis may exhibit only a few" birthmarks "that a medical geneticist, genetics
advanced practice nurse, or genetic counselor would recognize as café-au-lait spots, one of the
manifestations of neurofibromatosis."

**A parent who looks unaffected may not be.** Box 3.2 makes the same point as a caution:
"due to variable expressivity, careful clinical examination and laboratory studies may be
necessary before concluding that a person is truly phenotypically normal" (p. 44).

### Table 3.3, DNA analysis techniques (p. 50)

| Technique | Purpose as given | Application as given |
|---|---|---|
| **DNA extraction** | "Extracted DNA can be immediately analyzed or stored for future use" | "DNA extracted from variety of cells (amniocytes, lymphoblasts, single cell from eight-cell stage embryo) and tissues (skin, cheek cells, hair root, chorionic villi, archived pathology specimens)" |
| **Polymerase chain reaction** | "Rapid amplification of small quantities of genetic material (DNA or RNA). Short lengths of DNA (primers) bind to specific DNA regions and initiate their replication" | "Essential technique for many molecular genetic diagnostic tests, but limited to already sequenced DNA genes or regions" |
| **Full gene sequencing** | "As cost decreases, this is becoming preferred test for genes that have many possible disease-associated mutations throughout the gene" | "BRCA1, BRCA2, familial adenomatous polyposis"; "Picks up all mutations except large gene deletions, but may pick up a mutation not previously described and may not be able to tell if it has any pathologic significance" |

**Southern blotting is the non-PCR method and it is the one used for repeat expansions**
(p. 50). Fragile X uses CGG repeats; Huntington uses CAG. Both reappear in §15.

**PCR's limit is stated plainly: it is "limited to already sequenced DNA genes or regions"**
(p. 50). You cannot amplify what has not been mapped, which is why whole exome sequencing
exists (p. 67).

## 11. Autosomal dominant inheritance (pp. 50, 44, 52)

### The two prerequisites the chapter insists on (p. 50)

"A clear understanding of transmission of autosomal inheritance patterns requires the
understanding of a few basic facts."

| Fact | As given (p. 50) |
|---|---|
| **First** | "most genetic diseases are rare. The probability that two affected persons will mate is very low for most genetic disorders (with the exception of societal selection, as in the case of achondroplasia)" |
| **Second** | "depending on the disease, if one parent is affected, he or she is much more likely to be heterozygous (have one mutant allele) than homozygous (have two mutant alleles)" |
| Why it matters | "Usually, an individual with two dominant mutant alleles will experience physical or cognitive abnormalities at a much more severe level" |

**Assume heterozygous unless told otherwise.** Every 50% figure in this section depends on that
assumption, and the chapter states it before giving the number (p. 50).

### Box 3.2, autosomal dominant characteristics (p. 44)

The six characteristics, as printed. Note the footnote: "All partners of affected persons here
are considered genotypically normal."

| # | As given (p. 44) |
|---|---|
| 1 | "Males and females are equally likely to be affected." |
| 2 | "A single copy of the mutant allele is typically sufficient to cause the phenotype to express, and therefore a carrier state does not exist. However, if the mutant allele is not fully penetrant (some individuals in a population with the same mutant allele do not express the trait), the unaffected person with the mutant allele may be referred to as a carrier of the reduced penetrant allele." |
| 3 | "The homozygote for the mutant allele is usually more severely affected than the heterozygote." |
| 4 | "The phenotype appears in consecutive generations unless the condition was due to a new dominant mutation." |
| 5 | **"Children of an affected parent have a 50% chance of inheriting the mutant allele and being affected."** |
| 6 | "If the mutant allele is 100% penetrant, phenotypically normal persons in a family pedigree are free of the mutant allele and do not transmit the phenotype to their offspring." |

**Characteristic 2 is the one to read twice.** In autosomal dominant disease **a carrier state
does not exist**, except in the specific case of reduced penetrance (p. 44). That is the
opposite of autosomal recessive disease, where the carrier state is the whole point.

**Characteristic 4 gives the pedigree signature: the disease appears in every generation.**
Skipped generations point away from autosomal dominant unless a new mutation or reduced
penetrance explains them (p. 44).

### The three genotypes and the two matings (p. 44)

"If a gene has only two alleles, one normal and one mutant, three possible allele combinations
exist: normal/normal, normal/mutant, and mutant/mutant. In autosomal dominant conditions, only
persons with normal/normal combination will be disease free, assuming that the mutant allele is
100% penetrant" (p. 44).

| Mating | Outcome as given (p. 44) |
|---|---|
| Affected heterozygote with genotypically normal partner | the usual case, 50% per pregnancy |
| Affected homozygote with genotypically normal partner | "The result of these matings is one of the few instances in medical genetics in which all progeny have a 100% chance of being affected, assuming that the mutant allele they receive is 100% penetrant. These matings, however, are extremely rare" |

**Two 100% situations exist in this chapter and it is worth holding both:** the affected
homozygote in autosomal dominant disease (p. 44) and the 21;21 translocation carrier (p. 48).

### New mutations and germline mosaicism (p. 44)

"Many children diagnosed with an autosomal dominant disorder have a positive family history of
the disease. In other instances, that child may represent the first occurrence of that condition
in the family" (p. 44).

| Explanation | As given (p. 44) |
|---|---|
| The two possibilities | "the event may be due to a new mutation (de novo mutation) in that child or to the presence of the mutation only in a subset of the germ cells of a healthy parent" |
| **How to tell them apart** | "The birth of other affected children indicates the second possibility" |

**A second affected child born to unaffected parents means germline mosaicism, not coincidence**
(p. 44). That distinction changes the recurrence counseling completely.

### Why dominant disease alleles persist (pp. 44, 52)

"The range of expression of autosomal dominant gene mutations is highly variable, from minor
manifestations (e.g., polydactyly), to severe, debilitating, and life-threatening disease"
(p. 44), for example neurofibromatosis (p. 52).

| Mechanism | As given (p. 52) |
|---|---|
| Selection | "Depending on the degree of disability the condition imposes on the individual and the ability to procreate, the mutated gene either will be eliminated or will continue to be passed on through several generations" |
| **Late onset** | "in diseases that have a late age of onset (e.g., Huntington disease), a person with a disease-associated mutation may be healthy and asymptomatic during childbearing years and be unaware of the risk of passing on the mutant allele to offspring. Consequently, the mutant allele continues to be passed on through several generations" |

**A lethal dominant disease survives in a population if it kills after reproduction.** Huntington
disease is the chapter's example and its "mean age of onset in third to fourth decade of life"
is in Table 3.4 (p. 54).

**Examples named (p. 52):** "achondroplasia, neurofibromatosis, and Marfan syndrome."

**The achondroplasia pedigree, and the point it makes (p. 52):** "The gene mutation associated
with achondroplasia is considered 100% penetrant. The pedigree in the figure demonstrates that
although an affected parent has a 50% chance with each pregnancy of transmitting the gene
mutation associated with achondroplasia, 50% of offspring do not necessarily inherit the gene
mutation."

**50% is a per-pregnancy probability, not a quota.** Four children of an affected parent can all
be affected or all be unaffected. That is the same misconception the chapter's Nursing Alert
addresses for recessive disease: "Chance has no memory" (p. 72).

## 12. Autosomal recessive inheritance (pp. 56, 53, 57)

### The genotype requirement (p. 56)

"Children who display an autosomal recessive disorder are always homozygous for that trait (both
the maternally and paternally inherited alleles contain disease-associated mutations). This is
due to the fact that a recessive allele is one whose phenotypic expression occurs only when both
genes have disease-associated mutations" (p. 56).

**The refinement most people miss (p. 56):** "Although this makes the alleles homozygous because
both alleles are recessive, the disease-associated mutation may be different in each allele.
When this is the case, the pair of alleles is more accurately referred to as compound
heterozygous for the recessive trait."

**Two broken copies, not necessarily two identical broken copies.** Compound heterozygosity is
why cystic fibrosis carrier panels miss cases: "carrier testing for CF typically analyzes less
than 100 of the possible CFTR mutations" out of more than 1000 (p. 69).

**The third outcome that dominant inheritance does not have (p. 53):** "in autosomal recessive
inheritance a third possibility arises: that of a heterozygous carrier. These are individuals
who are clinically normal (or nearly normal) but who are at risk of having offspring who are
affected."

### Box 3.3, autosomal recessive characteristics (p. 53)

| # | As given (p. 53) |
|---|---|
| 1 | "Males and females are equally likely to be affected." |
| 2 | **"A carrier state exists. Both males and females can be carriers."** |
| 3 | "The disease rarely appears in multiple generations unless there is consanguinity in the family or the family is part of a racial, ethnic, or cultural group in which the frequency of the mutated allele is high. Members of the generation who have affected children are usually asymptomatic (heterozygous) carriers." |
| 4 | **"Carrier parents have a 25% chance of producing an affected child and a 50% chance of producing a carrier child in each and every pregnancy."** |
| 5 | "Parent consanguinity may be a factor when a child is affected with a rare recessive disease." |

**The pedigree signature is the opposite of dominant: the disease appears in ONE generation, not
consecutive ones** (pp. 44, 53).

**The three numbers per pregnancy from two carrier parents: 25% affected, 50% carrier, 25%
unaffected noncarrier** (p. 53). The chapter states the first two explicitly; the third follows.

### Identifying carriers (pp. 53, 57)

| Situation | As given |
|---|---|
| **Obligate carriers** | "In the case of an unaffected couple who produce a child with a recessive disease, identification is straightforward, assuming lack of misattributed paternity; because they each must contribute a mutant allele, they are considered obligate carriers, even in the absence of specific carrier testing for that gene" (p. 53) |
| Why not screen everyone | "Specific tests to detect heterozygous carriers of a variety of genetic diseases are available, but it would be impractical and certainly not cost-effective to use all available tests to screen all prospective parents without specific risk factors" (the sentence begins on p. 53 and completes on p. 57) |
| Who is screened | "Genetic screening for carriers usually is limited to populations at risk, either because they belong to a high-risk group for a certain disorder (e.g., Ashkenazi Jews and Tay-Sachs disease) or because of positive family history" (p. 57) |
| When | "Carriers for some specific disorders can be identified by specific tests before conception by means of routine screening or after conception by means of prenatal diagnosis" (p. 57) |

**Everyone carries something (p. 57):** "It is estimated that each person carries from three to
eight mutated genes for a severe recessive disease."

**The carrier frequencies to know, all from p. 57:**

| Population and disorder | Carrier frequency as given |
|---|---|
| Cystic fibrosis, US and Northern European | "1 in 25 persons in the United States and Northern European populations carries a recessive gene mutation for CF" |
| Sickle cell, African American | "In the African American population, 1 in 10 persons is a carrier for the sickle cell gene mutation" |
| PKU, general population | "For PKU, the carrier rate in the general population is 1 in 50" |
| Tay-Sachs, Ashkenazi Jewish | "1 in 30 Ashkenazi Jews is a carrier of a gene mutation associated with Tay-Sachs disease" |

**And the disease frequencies that follow from them (p. 57):** Tay-Sachs affects "1 in 3600 live
births" among Ashkenazi Jews "without preconception screening," while "the frequency of
Tay-Sachs disease outside this population is 100 times smaller (i.e., 1 in 360,000 live
births)."

**A hundredfold difference by ancestry.** That single comparison is the argument for targeted
rather than universal carrier screening (p. 57).

### Consanguinity (p. 57)

"because genetic diseases are rare, the probability of the mating of two persons who carry the
same gene in the same allelic configuration is very small. The chances are increased if mating
occurs among persons who select a mate because of geographic, ethnic, or religious restrictions
or blood relationship (consanguinity)" (p. 57).

**The first-cousin figure (p. 74):** "The first-cousin risk for birth defects is about 5%
compared with the general population risk of 2% to 3%. **The primary risk increase is for
autosomal recessive disorders, in which the chance that two individuals carry the same rare
mutation is increased if they are related.**"

**And how far the concern extends (p. 74):** "It is important at this point to determine whether
the couple might be related in any way, although contrary to popular belief, this is usually a
concern only for first cousins or closer relatives."

**5% versus 2% to 3% is roughly double, not catastrophic**, and only first cousins or closer
matter (p. 74). Both halves of that are worth saying to a family.

**Two matings, two outcomes (p. 57):** the mating of two carriers gives "each pregnancy carrying
a 25% chance of producing an affected child," and the mating of an affected parent with a
genotypically normal partner is one "in which 100% of the offspring will be carriers."

**An affected parent plus a noncarrier partner produces zero affected children and 100%
carriers** (p. 57). That is a common stem and the answer is counterintuitive.

**Examples named (p. 57):** "the thalassemias, congenital adrenal hyperplasia, and
galactosemia," plus PKU, sickle cell disease, cystic fibrosis, and Tay-Sachs elsewhere in the
chapter.

## 13. Sex-linked inheritance (pp. 57–59)

### Sex-linked versus X-linked (p. 57)

"The transmission of genes located on one of the sex chromosomes (either X or Y) is termed
sex-linked inheritance. However, few genes have been found on the Y chromosome, so frequently
the terms sex-linked and X-linked are interchangeably (and incorrectly) used" (p. 57).

| The Y chromosome | As given (p. 57) |
|---|---|
| What it does | "The testicular organizing region of the Y chromosome determines the formation of testes and the development of male sexual structures during embryonic growth" |
| The other known gene | "A gene for hairy ears, with high prevalence in southern Asia, has also been located on the Y chromosome" |
| Its pattern | "Inheritance of Y-linked genes follows a father-to-son, or male-to-male, pattern" |

**The single most useful pedigree rule in the chapter (p. 57):** "It is important to remember
that men give their X chromosomes to their daughters (and their Y to their sons), so in
analyzing a family pedigree, **male-to-male transmission of a gene rules out X-linked
inheritance.**"

**Father to son means it is not X-linked.** One observation, one conclusion, and it appears in
nearly every pedigree question (p. 57).

### Why males are different (p. 58)

"Women have two X chromosomes; therefore inheritance of genes located on the X chromosome
(X-linked inheritance) follows the same pattern as that for autosomal genes" (p. 58).

"However, in the case of males, the X and Y chromosomes have small areas of homology and
therefore do not pair side-by-side during meiosis. Because of this, men are hemizygous for all
genes on the X chromosome that do not have a homologous site on the Y chromosome, and their
alleles are represented as single copies" (p. 58).

**The consequence (p. 58):** "in the inheritance of an X-linked gene, the single-copy presence
of its normal, or its mutant, allele will result in the expression of the normal or mutant
phenotype, respectively. **This is true for both dominant and recessive X-linked diseases in
males.**"

**There are no male carriers of X-linked disease.** Box 3.4's figure legend says it directly:
"there are no carrier males because the phenotype is determined solely by the characteristic of
X-linked allele. Hemizygous males are either normal or affected" (p. 58).

**For a male, dominant and recessive stop mattering on the X chromosome.** One copy is all he
has, so it is expressed either way (p. 58).

### X inactivation, and why some females are affected (p. 58)

"In females the alleles of an X-linked recessive gene behave as the alleles of any autosomal
recessive gene: the effect of the abnormal allele is hidden by the normal (dominant) allele.
Therefore females who have a disease-associated mutation in both members of the gene pair will
express the phenotype. Although it is rare for females to express the phenotype if only one
member of the gene pair carries a disease-associated mutation, it is possible due to X
inactivation" (p. 58).

| Step | As given (p. 58) |
|---|---|
| When | "Soon after fertilization, it is normal for one X chromosome in females to be inactivated through a natural process called methylation" |
| Where | "This occurs in each cell in the early blastocyst stage" |
| **Randomness** | "whether the paternal or maternally inherited X is inactivated is random in each cell" |
| Permanence | "At the time this occurs, the X that has been inactivated will remain so through all subsequent cell divisions" |
| Why a carrier can be symptomatic | "Females who express a phenotype may have a greater proportion of X chromosome with the normal allele inactivated within the tissues or organs associated with the disorder" |

**A carrier female can be symptomatic if chance silenced too many of her good X chromosomes in
the wrong tissue** (p. 58). This is the mechanism, not an exception to the rule.

### Box 3.4, X-linked recessive characteristics (p. 58)

| # | As given (p. 58) |
|---|---|
| 1 | "Most affected persons are male. Affected females are extremely rare." |
| 2 | "Males transmit their Y chromosome to their sons; therefore father-to-son transmission of X-linked traits is not possible except in the rare instance of nondisjunction when a sperm carries both an X and Y chromosome." |
| 3 | **"All daughters of an affected male are (heterozygous) carriers; none is affected (homozygous)."** |
| 4 | **"Sons of carrier females have a 50% chance of inheriting the mutant allele and being affected (hemizygous). Daughters have a 50% risk of inheriting the mutant allele and being (heterozygous) carriers."** |

Footnote: "Unless otherwise specified, all partners of affected or carrier persons here are
genotypically normal."

**Examples (p. 58):** "hemophilia types A and B and Duchenne muscular dystrophy."

**The two matings, memorized as a pair:**

| Mating | Sons | Daughters |
|---|---|---|
| Affected father, normal mother | all unaffected, all noncarriers | **all carriers, none affected** |
| Normal father, carrier mother | **50% affected** | **50% carriers** |

**An affected father cannot have an affected son and cannot have a noncarrier daughter**
(p. 58). Those two absolutes make the affected-father pedigree instantly recognizable.

### X-linked dominant inheritance (pp. 58–59)

The chapter states that X-linked dominant inheritance "is rare" (p. 58). The three characteristics, as given:

| # | As given |
|---|---|
| 1 | the sentence straddles the break: "The main characteristics of X-linked dominant inheritance are (1) both males and" (p. 58) continues "females can be affected, but females, because of the random nature of X inactivation, are usually less severely affected than males" (p. 59) |
| 2 | "affected men do not transmit the mutant allele to their sons" (p. 59) |
| 3 | **"all daughters of an affected man are affected and have a 50% chance of passing on the mutant allele to their sons and daughters"** (p. 59) |

**Examples (p. 59):** "hypophosphatemic vitamin D" resistant "rickets and incontinentia
pigmenti."

**And the severity note from Table 3.4 (p. 55):** incontinentia pigmenti is "Often prenatally
lethal in males" (p. 55).

| Carrier mother's sons | not applicable | 25% affected | **50% affected** (p. 58) | not applicable |
| Male-to-male transmission | possible | possible | **impossible** (p. 57) | **impossible** (p. 59) |
| Examples | achondroplasia, neurofibromatosis, Marfan syndrome (p. 52) | thalassemias, congenital adrenal hyperplasia, galactosemia (p. 57), PKU, CF, sickle cell, Tay-Sachs | hemophilia A and B, Duchenne muscular dystrophy (p. 58) | hypophosphatemic rickets, incontinentia pigmenti (p. 59) |

**Three questions identify the pattern from a pedigree:** Does it appear in every generation, or
skip? Are affected people mostly male? Is there any father-to-son transmission? (pp. 44, 53,
57, 58).

## 15. Variations that bend the mendelian rules (pp. 59–60)

"A number of variables have been observed that explain or modify basic inheritance patterns and
the effects of chromosome abnormalities" (p. 59).

### Locus heterogeneity (p. 59)

"some disorders have been reported to follow more than one inheritance pattern in different
families (e.g., a classically recessive disorder occasionally may be reported to be following a
dominant or X-linked pattern in other families). This phenomenon is known as locus
heterogeneity" (p. 59).

**Table 3.4 shows it directly:** Fanconi anemia is "Most forms AR; at least one form XL"
(p. 52).

### Mutation (p. 59)

| Element | As given (p. 59) |
|---|---|
| Definition | "mutations are heritable changes in the DNA sequence of a gene" |
| Types | "Mutations can result from a substitution of bases (point mutations) or the insertion or deletion of bases" |
| Rates vary | "Some genes have a high mutation rate, and various forms of the resulting disease may have varying expression"; "Mutation rates are not constant for all genes, so some diseases may occur with much greater frequency than others" |
| The example | "the CFTR gene, in which more than 1000 different CF-associated mutations have been identified (the vast majority being point mutations or small deletions)" |

### Expansion mutations and anticipation (p. 59)

"Some genetic disorders are caused by an abnormal expansion of a short DNA sequence that
naturally repeats. **The size of an expansion mutation can influence the age of onset and
severity of the disorder**" (p. 59).

**The fragile X worked example, which gives the three repeat ranges (p. 59):**

| State | Repeats as given |
|---|---|
| Normal | "the FMR1 gene on the X chromosome usually has about 5 to 40 repeats of three nucleotides: cytosine, guanine, guanine (CGG)" |
| **Premutation** | "When FMR1 has 59 to 200 CGG repeats, that area of the gene can become unstable and gain further repeats during meiosis. Females who have this number of repeats are considered carriers of a premutation for fragile X syndrome" |
| **Full mutation** | "A person with fragile X has a full mutation allele with hundreds to thousands of repeats" |

**Why females are less affected (p. 59):** "Because fragile X syndrome is an X-linked disorder,
females are less likely to be affected because of the presence of a normal allele on the
homologous X chromosome."

**Where else expansions occur (p. 59):** "These expanding repeats have also been found to occur
in autosomal dominant disorders such as myotonic dystrophy and in autosomal recessive disorders
such as Friedreich ataxia."

**Anticipation, defined (p. 59):** "Expansion mutations that have a tendency to further expand
when transmitted from one generation to the next can display phenotypic anticipation. Pedigrees
display anticipation when individuals in successive generations develop the disorder at an
earlier age and/or with more severe manifestations."

**Earlier and worse in each generation.** A pedigree where the grandmother had mild adult-onset
disease, the mother had it in her twenties, and the child has it at birth is describing
anticipation (p. 59), and myotonic dystrophy's congenital form is exactly that pattern (p. 55).

### Genomic imprinting (pp. 59–60)

"Genomic imprinting and uniparental disomy are two genetic phenomena that consider the parental
origin of genetic information (i.e., maternally or paternally derived)" (p. 59).

| Element | As given (p. 60) |
|---|---|
| The mechanism | "Methyl groups are naturally added to (an in some cases removed from) certain regions of the genome to control the expression of select genes. **Methylation patterns can be specific to parent of origin**" |
| The worked example | "during sperm formation, methyl groups are removed from the father's chromosomes that were inherited from his mother and methyl groups added to areas of those chromosomes to assure that genes that should be turned on or off in paternally inherited chromosomes are transmitted to future offspring" |
| In pregnancy | "Genomic imprinting is exhibited during pregnancy, when paternally derived chromosomes seem to positively influence placental development and maternally derived chromosomes seem to positively influence fetal development" |

**Father builds the placenta, mother builds the fetus.** That is the chapter's own summary of
imprinting in pregnancy (p. 60).

### Prader-Willi versus Angelman, the chapter's headline pair (p. 60)

"In both these disorders about two-thirds of affected individuals have a deletion of the same
segment of chromosome 15 that is normally methylated. **However, the clinical manifestations of
Prader-Willi and Angelman syndromes are markedly different**" (p. 60).

| | **Prader-Willi syndrome** | **Angelman syndrome** |
|---|---|---|
| Which parent's copy is deleted | "If the deletion occurs on the paternally derived chromosome 15 so only the mother's genes are expressed, the child exhibits Prader-Willi syndrome" (p. 60) | "if the deletion occurs on the maternally derived chromosome 15 so that only the father's genes are expressed, the child manifests Angelman syndrome" (p. 60) |
| Early infancy | "failure to thrive and central hypotonia in the newborn and infancy period" (p. 60) | not given |
| Later | "later insatiable hunger that can lead to morbid obesity during childhood" (p. 60) | "abnormal (puppetlike) gait, and paroxysms of inappropriate laughter" (p. 60) |
| Cognition | "cognitive dysfunction" (p. 60) | "severe cognitive impairment" (p. 60) |
| Other features | "typical dysmorphic features, behavioral disturbances, hypothalamic hypogonadism, and short stature" (p. 60) | "characteristic facies" (p. 60) |
| Speech | not given | "Children with Angelman syndrome are usually nonverbal" (p. 60) |
| Table 3.2 summary | "Severe hypotonia, failure to thrive in early infancy; after 1-2 years of age, excessive eating"; morbid obesity; hypogonadism (p. 46) | "Significant motor, cognitive, and speech delays; microcephaly; ataxia" (p. 46) |

**Same chromosome, same deletion, opposite syndromes, and the only variable is which parent it
came from** (p. 60). **The mnemonic that follows from the text: PAter for Prader-Willi.** Lose
the father's copy and you get Prader-Willi.

**The clinical arc of Prader-Willi is itself a trap: hypotonia and failure to thrive first, then
insatiable hunger and obesity after 1 to 2 years** (pp. 46, 60). A stem describing a floppy,
poorly feeding newborn and a stem describing an obese 5-year-old who eats nonfood items can be
the same diagnosis.

### Uniparental disomy (p. 60)

"In some cases both copies of a chromosome pair are discovered to have come from one parent,
either the mother or the father, instead of one from each; this phenomenon is called uniparental
disomy" (p. 60).

| Element | As given (p. 60) |
|---|---|
| The CF example | "both chromosomes, each with a mutant recessive gene, came from the carrier mother; the father was not a carrier" |
| **Why it matters clinically** | "In cases that appear to be misattributed paternity, uniparental disomy may be a factor" |
| One proposed mechanism | "the chromosome pair was originally a trisomy and the father's chromosome was randomly eliminated, leaving two copies of the mother's chromosome" |
| Why it is hard to see | "Because the chromosomes appear as a normal pair, diagnosis of this situation is only possible with molecular (DNA) techniques" |
| Other associations | "Uniparental disomy has also been reported with Beckwith-Wiedemann syndrome, which is characterized by overgrowth and hypoglycemia at birth" |

**A recessive disease in a child of one carrier parent is not automatically misattributed
paternity.** Uniparental disomy explains it, and saying so protects a family from an accusation
(p. 60). That is a genuinely consequential piece of counseling.

**Chromosomal microarray can detect it (p. 66):** "If the identical area is in a segment
contained within a single chromosome, it may indicate uniparental disomy."

## 16. Mitochondrial disorders (p. 60)

"Mitochondrial disorders can be caused by mutations in the nuclear genes and exhibit mendelian
inheritance. However, mitochondrial disorders can also be caused by DNA found in a cytoplasmic
cellular organelle, the mitochondrion, whose primary function in cellular metabolism is the
production of energy" (p. 60).

**The inheritance rule (p. 60):** "Inheritance of traits contained in mtDNA is exclusively
maternal because only the mitochondria from the ovum are transmitted to the zygote.
Mitochondria are not contained within the sperm head."

**Mothers pass it to all children; fathers pass it to none.** That is the pedigree signature of
mitochondrial inheritance and the reason it is called nonmendelian (p. 60).

### Heteroplasmy (p. 60)

| Element | As given (p. 60) |
|---|---|
| Definition | "during mitosis, mitochondria are randomly distributed among the daughter cells, so both normal and mutated mitochondria may be found in the same cell, a phenomenon known as heteroplasmy" |
| Consequence | "This leads to variable dosages of mutated mtDNA between tissues and organs" |
| Clinical result | "This variation in mutation load leads to a highly variable spectrum of clinical manifestations, and individuals with the same mtDNA mutation may range from symptom free, to mildly affected, to severely impaired" |
| Symptoms named | "seizures, pancreatitis, and metabolic disease" |
| Within one family | "Different manifestations may be seen in different members of the same family" |
| **Why prenatal testing fails** | "the mutation load identified in sampled fetal tissue (chorionic villi or amniocytes) may not correspond to other fetal tissues, the mutational load of which will continue to change during development due to random mitotic segregation of cytoplasmic organelles. **Consequently, it is not possible to predict the unborn child's phenotype based on prenatal test results**" |

**You can find the mutation prenatally and still not be able to say what it will do.** That is
the one disorder class in this chapter where a positive prenatal test carries no prognosis
(p. 60).

### When to suspect one (p. 60)

"Because mitochondrial disorders have such variability of expression, determining the diagnosis
can be confusing. However, **when a child has an unexplained constellation of abnormal findings
in tissue or organs that require high energy (e.g., heart, skeletal muscle, eyes), a
mitochondrial disorder should be considered**" (p. 60).

**High-energy organs, unexplained multisystem findings.** Heart, skeletal muscle, and eye, and
the brain by extension of Table 3.5 (p. 60).

### Table 3.5, mitochondrial genetic disorders (p. 60)

| Disorder | Recognition | Findings as given |
|---|---|---|
| **Kearns-Sayre syndrome** | "Infancy; before 20 years of age" | "multisystem disease primarily affecting CNS, endocrine system, skeletal muscles, heart, retina" |
| **Leigh syndrome / NARP** | Leigh in infancy, NARP in childhood | "Symptoms depend on mutation load and tissue distribution of disease causing mtDNA mutation; progressive, neurodegenerative disorder"; "Leigh onset typically after viral infection; 75% die by 3 years of age"; NARP gives "ataxia, learning difficulties, episodic deterioration possible following viral infections" |
| **MELAS** | Early childhood | "Encephalopathy, seizures, recurrent vomiting, recurrent headaches, exercise intolerance, strokelike episodes" |
| **MERRF** | Childhood | "Myoclonus, generalized epilepsy, ataxia, dementia" |

**Kearns-Sayre's inheritance note is a counseling number worth having (p. 60):** "Maternal
inheritance but affected women only have approximately 1 in 24 risk of having affected
offspring; maternal transmission to more than one child has not been reported."

**Viral infection is a stated trigger for two of these** (p. 60): Leigh syndrome onset "typically
after viral infection" (p. 60), and NARP's "episodic deterioration possible following viral infections."
That makes ordinary childhood illness a decompensation risk.

**Leigh syndrome's full description from the narrative (p. 60):** "movement disorder, respiratory
dyskinesia, regression, hypotonia, seizures, and failure to thrive."

## 17. Hereditary cancer predisposition genes (p. 60)

"The process of carcinogenesis implies permanent changes in the DNA of the targeted cell"
(p. 60).

**The four early observations, as given (p. 60):**

1. "certain types of cancer occur more frequently within certain families (breast, colon,
   ovarian, some leukemias)"
2. "some well-defined genetic disorders show a predisposition to various malignancies (familial
   adenomatous polyposis and colon cancer, Bloom syndrome, and lymphomas)"
3. "chromosome abnormalities occur frequently with malignancies (Philadelphia chromosome in
   chronic myelogenous leukemia)"
4. "certain chromosome aneuploidies predispose the person to cancer (Down syndrome and acute
   leukemias)"

### The three gene classes

| Class | Normal function as given | What a mutation does |
|---|---|---|
| **Oncogenes** | "oncogenes are normally involved in cell growth and division" (p. 60) | "A mutation in an oncogene can disrupt this normal process and transform a normal cell into one that has uncontrolled cell growth or division, predisposing the cell to further mutations that eventually transform the cell into one that is malignant" (p. 60) |
| **Tumor suppressor genes** | "Tumor suppressor genes normally inhibit cell growth and division" (p. 60) | "A mutation in these genes can interfere with this normal function and lead to uninhibited cell growth and division" (p. 60) |
| **Mismatch repair genes** | "normally function by recognizing and repairing DNA errors that occur during replication or mutations that are caused by external agents such as ultraviolet light or chemical exposure" (p. 60) | the errors are not repaired |

**Oncogenes are gas pedals stuck down; tumor suppressors are brakes that failed; mismatch repair
genes are the proofreader who stopped reading.** All three normal functions are stated in the
source (p. 60).

**The history correction (p. 60):** "Initially thought to be carried exclusively by
retroviruses, oncogenes were later identified as natural genes that existed in all mammals.
Early investigations suggested that oncogenes were found in inactive form (i.e., no correlation
with cancer could be identified in this state). **In reality, oncogenes are normally involved in
cell growth and division.**"

**An oncogene is a normal gene, not a foreign one.** It becomes dangerous when mutated (p. 60).

**Box 3.5, cancer genetics current knowledge (p. 60):**

- "Genetic predispositions influence risk for malignancies."
- "Environmental agents act as triggers, increasing the inherited risk factor."
- "Retroviruses can cause cancer by transmitting an oncogene or by insertional mutagenesis."
- "Cancers may be caused by somatic mutations of genes that regulate cell growth" as "a
  localized process leading to sporadic (nonhereditary) tumors."
- "Germ cell gene mutations are transmitted to the offspring" as hereditary cancers.
- "Tumor suppressor genes (e.g., p53) normally suppress cell growth. Mutations can disrupt
  suppression, allowing for uncontrolled cell growth that leads to cancer. Examples of other
  genes that suppress cell growth include BRCA1 (female breast and ovarian cancer), BRCA2 (male
  and female breast, prostate cancer), and APC (colon cancer)."

**Note that BRCA2 is listed as male and female breast cancer plus prostate; BRCA1 as female
breast and ovarian** (p. 60). That split is examinable.

**p53 and Li-Fraumeni (p. 60):** "Among this group is p53 (sometimes called TP53), whose germ
cell (or hereditary) mutations are associated with Li-Fraumeni syndrome and whose somatic cell
mutations (sporadic) result in various malignancies, such as bladder cancer. Li-Fraumeni
syndrome is inherited as an autosomal dominant trait that predisposes children and young adults
to the development of various tumors, including osteosarcoma; soft tissue sarcomas; breast,
brain, and adrenocortical carcinomas; and leukemia."

**One gene, two diseases, depending on which cell it mutated in.** Germline p53 gives
Li-Fraumeni; somatic p53 gives sporadic cancers (p. 60). That is the inherited-versus-somatic
distinction from §3 applied to a specific gene.

**Mismatch repair and xeroderma pigmentosum (p. 60):** "Xeroderma pigmentosum is a classic
example of inherited predisposition to cancers caused by a germline mutation in one of several
possible mismatch repair genes. Infants and children with xeroderma pigmentosum are at
considerable risk for skin cancers triggered by sun and ultraviolet light exposure."

## 18. Multifactorial (complex) disorders (p. 61)

"A number of frequently encountered diseases and defects show an increased incidence than would
be expected by chance, yet no specific mode of inheritance can be identified" (p. 61).

"These conditions are classified as multifactorial disorders, **in which a genetic
susceptibility and specific environmental agents interact to produce a disease state**"
(p. 61).

**The list, which is worth memorizing whole because it is short and recurring (p. 61):**
"Multifactorial disorders include NTDs, cleft lip and cleft palate, many congenital heart
defects, congenital hip dislocation, and pyloric stenosis."

### Recurrence risk (p. 61)

| Element | As given (p. 61) |
|---|---|
| How the number is derived | "Recurrence risks for multifactorial conditions are empirically based on observed recurrence within a population" |
| **The general figure** | "In general, recurrence risk is usually low (<10%)" |
| The worked example | "NTDs occur in 1 out of 1000 births in the general population. **The recurrence risk after the first affected child is 2 or 3 out of 100 births**" |

**A 20- to 30-fold increase that is still under 3%.** Both halves of that matter in counseling:
the relative risk rises sharply and the absolute risk stays low (p. 61).

**Empiric versus theoretic, defined later (p. 72):** "For disorders with an unknown or
multifactorial cause, recurrence risks are termed empiric, meaning they are based on
observations of recurrence in similar situations, rather than theoretic, meaning they are based
on mendelian inheritance patterns."

**Mendelian risks are calculated; multifactorial risks are counted.** That is why a
multifactorial recurrence figure comes with a population attached and a mendelian one does not
(pp. 61, 72).

## 19. The intrauterine environment and teratogens (pp. 61–62)

"The intrauterine environment can have a profound and permanent effect on the developing fetus,
with or without chromosome or single-gene abnormalities" (p. 61).

| Element | As given |
|---|---|
| Growth restriction, genetic causes | "Intrauterine growth retardation, for example, can occur with many genetic syndromes, such as Down, Russell-Silver, Prader-Willi, and Turner syndromes" (the sentence begins on p. 61 and completes on p. 62) |

**Cerebral palsy is increasingly a placental story rather than a delivery story** (p. 62). That
is the same revision Chapter 34 makes about the origins of CP, and it matters because it moves
the cause before labor.

### Teratogens (p. 62)

"Teratogens, agents that cause birth defects when present in the prenatal environment, account

**The five categories with their named examples (p. 62):**

| Category | Examples as given |

**Maternal PKU is a teratogen.** The mother's own metabolic disease is the exposure, which is

## 20. Inborn errors of metabolism: the model (pp. 62–63)

"Inborn errors of metabolism (IEMs) include a large number of inherited diseases caused by
interruptions in the various pathways involved in the metabolism of protein, carbohydrates, or
lipids" (p. 62).

### What a metabolic pathway is (p. 62)

| Element | As given (p. 62) |
|---|---|
| Definition | "Metabolic pathways are series of biochemical reactions by which substrates are sequentially converted into other by-products, aiming at a final end-product that can be successfully used or eliminated by the body" |
| Control | "Each of these transformations is mediated by an enzyme, which is under the control of a specific structural gene" |
| Regulation | "A feedback loop ensures physiologic levels" of the end product |
| What a mutation does | "A gene mutation may result in qualitative or quantitative changes in the enzyme, resulting in a different (and therefore ineffective) enzyme, or in the decreased synthesis of the enzyme, including its total absence" |
| **The two consequences** | "This ineffective (or missing) enzyme will interrupt the pathway, resulting in accumulation of the by-product that immediately precedes the blockage, as well as lack of the by-products beyond the blockage" |
| The definition that follows | "IEMs can occur as a result of such accumulations or absences of an essential by-product" |

**A block in a pathway causes two problems at once: too much of what is behind it and too little
of what is past it** (p. 62). Every disease below is one or the other, or both.

### The three failure patterns, with their prototype diseases (p. 62)

| Pattern | Mechanism as given | Prototype |
|---|---|---|
| **Precursor accumulates behind the block** | "Assuming a continuous uptake" of the substrate, "two outcomes ensue: accumulation" of the preceding by-product "and depletion of all products beyond the block" | **Tay-Sachs disease.** "a mutation in the HexA gene" causes "the lack of the enzyme hexosaminidase A (HexA)"; absence of it "creates an accumulation of ganglioside GM2" as "lipids, in nerve cells, resulting in the clinical manifestations of this progressive neurologic disorder because the lipids are not broken down by the cellular liposomes" |
| **An alternative pathway opens and its product accumulates** | "In this instance an alternative pathway" is opened, and If accumulation of the alternative product "reaches toxic levels, a disease process may occur" | **PKU.** The missing enzyme, from a mutation "in the phenylalanine hydroxylase (PAH) gene", "is phenylalanine hydroxylase. The alternative pathway leads to the formation and accumulation of phenylketones" |
| **Feedback control fails and the end product accumulates** | "a genetic alteration of the enzyme" involved "in the feedback control of synthesis" of the end product, so that it "may accumulate to toxic levels" | **Lesch-Nyhan syndrome.** "A deficiency in HGPRT and accumulation of uric acid to extremely high levels will result in the development of cognitive impairment and self-mutilation tendency" |

**Tay-Sachs stores what it cannot break down; PKU diverts into a toxic side road; Lesch-Nyhan
never stops making the end product** (p. 62). Those three sentences cover every IEM mechanism in
this chapter and they match Table 3.1's enzyme rows exactly (p. 43).

**One outcome is benign, and the chapter says so (p. 62):** an alternative pathway product "may
eventually be converted into" the normal end product, "with no significant clinical
consequences." Not every rerouting causes disease.

### Inheritance, and why carriers are well (p. 62)

**"The mode of inheritance in IEMs is almost always autosomal recessive"** (p. 62).

"The heterozygote has one gene with a normal effect and is still able to produce the enzyme in
sufficient amounts to carry out the metabolic function under normal circumstances. Therefore the
heterozygote does not exhibit symptoms of the disorder. The homozygote, who inherits a defective
gene from both parents, has no functioning enzyme and thus is clinically affected" (p. 62).

**Half the normal enzyme is enough.** That is the general reason recessive carriers are
asymptomatic, stated in metabolic terms (p. 62).

### Why nurses matter here (p. 63)

| Point | As given (p. 63) |
|---|---|
| Collectively common | "Individually, different IEMs are rare; collectively they account for a significant proportion of health problems in children" |
| **Timing is everything** | "With most IEMs, early diagnosis and prompt treatment are essential to prevent a relentless course of physical and mental deterioration" |
| Prenatal value | "Prenatal diagnosis provides for special care of the infant immediately after birth" |
| Screening limits | "Neonatal screening is useful in detecting many disorders after a few days of life, but it is less helpful in detecting symptoms early in the neonatal period" |
| **Who can fall through** | "Nurses caring for neonates must be certain that screening is performed, especially in infants who are discharged early, are born at home, or are in neonatal intensive care units" |

**Three groups miss newborn screening: early discharge, home birth, and the NICU** (p. 63). The
PKU section adds a fourth: "infants adopted internationally" (p. 64).

**The nurse's documentation tasks (p. 63):** "Nurses also need to make certain that the neonate
has a primary care provider and current home address and telephone number documented on the
newborn screening blood spot card. Nurses should instruct parents to ask about newborn screening
test results at their newborn's first well-child visit."

**A screening result is useless if nobody can find the family.** The address and phone number on
the card are the nursing intervention (p. 63).

**Tandem mass spectrometry (p. 63):** "A new screening test, tandem mass spectrometry, has the
potential to identify up to 40 IEMs. With tandem mass spectrometry, earlier identification of
IEMs may prevent further developmental delays and morbidities in affected children."

### Recognizing an IEM clinically (p. 63)

**The nonspecific set, which is exactly why they are missed (p. 63):** "Some nonspecific
manifestations, including lethargy, poor feeding, vomiting, diarrhea, hypoglycemia, metabolic
acidosis, respiratory distress, apnea, hypothermia, coma, and seizures, occur in a wide variety
of genetic and acquired disorders."

**The timing clue (p. 63):** "The time of onset may be important. **Most IEMs produce no symptoms
during the first 24 hours of life.**"

**A sick newborn in the first day is probably not an IEM; a newborn who was well and then
deteriorated might be** (p. 63).

**The more specific set (p. 63):** "jaundice, hepatomegaly, unusual odor (e.g., sweat, urine,
feces), abnormal eating patterns (e.g., food aversions, vomiting after eating certain foods),
coarse facial features, macroglossia (enlarged tongue), abnormal hair, dysmorphic features, and
abnormal eye findings (e.g., cataracts, retinal changes)."

**And the family history clue (p. 63):** "A family history of neonatal deaths (within the same
sibling group, or among family members) alerts the observer to the possibility of an IEM."

**Unexplained infant deaths in a sibship is an IEM red flag** (p. 63), and it is why the pedigree
section insists on asking about "death and causes of death, including early infant deaths"
(p. 74).

**Whose job it is (p. 63):** "The initial recognition of signs that might indicate an IEM is the
responsibility of health professionals, including nurses."

**Which three the chapter covers, and why (p. 63):** "only three are discussed here because they
can be identified in the neonatal period and because treatment has been reasonably successful.
They are examples of disorders of protein metabolism (PKU), disorders of carbohydrate metabolism
(galactosemia), and disorders of hormone synthesis (congenital hypothyroidism)."

**Note that congenital hypothyroidism is named as the third example and then never discussed in
this chapter.** See the GAP register.

## 21. Phenylketonuria (pp. 63–65)

### The disorder (p. 63)

"Phenylketonuria (PKU), a genetic disease inherited as an autosomal recessive trait, is caused
by an absence of the enzyme phenylalanine hydroxylase needed to metabolize the essential amino
acid phenylalanine" (p. 63).

| Fact | As given (p. 63) |
|---|---|
| Incidence | "The reported figures for PKU range from 1 per 10,000 to 15,000 live births" |
| Why the range | "The prevalence of PKU varies widely in the United States because different states have different definition criteria for what constitutes hyperphenylalaninemia and PKU" |
| **Highest frequency** | "The disease is most prevalent among individuals of Northern European ancestry, American Indians, and Alaskan Natives" |
| **Lowest frequency** | "African American, Hispanic, Jewish, and Asian individuals account for the lowest frequencies" |

### Pathophysiology (p. 63)

"In PKU the hepatic enzyme phenylalanine hydroxylase, which controls the conversion of
phenylalanine to tyrosine, is absent. This results in the accumulation of phenylalanine in the
bloodstream and urinary excretion of abnormal amounts of its metabolites, the phenyl acids"
(p. 63).

| Consequence | As given (p. 63) |
|---|---|
| The name and the smell | "One of these phenyl ketones, phenylpyruvic acid, gives urine the characteristic musty odor associated with this disease and is responsible for the term phenylketonuria" |
| What is missing downstream | "Amino acids produced by the metabolism of phenylalanine are absent in PKU. One of these, tyrosine, is needed to form the pigment melanin and the hormones epinephrine and thyroxine" |
| **The phenotype that follows** | "Decreased melanin production results in similar phenotypes of most children with PKU: blond hair, blue eyes, and fair skin that is particularly susceptible to eczema and other dermatologic problems" |
| In darker-skinned children | "Children with a genetically darker skin color may be red haired or brunette" |
| Brain damage | "Severe hyperphenylalaninemia (>360 to 600 mmol/L) causes progressive damage to the developing brain with severe consequences: defective myelination, cystic degeneration of the gray and white matter, and disturbances in cortical lamination" |
| **The timing warning** | "Cognitive impairment occurs before the metabolites are detected in the urine and will progress if ingested phenylalanine levels are not lowered" |

**Brain damage precedes the urine finding.** That single sentence is the argument for blood
screening at birth rather than waiting for symptoms or for a positive urine test (p. 63).

**The blond, blue-eyed, fair, eczematous phenotype is a tyrosine deficiency, not a coincidence**
(p. 63). Tyrosine makes melanin, and the blocked pathway never produces it.

### Clinical manifestations (p. 63)

"Clinical manifestations of PKU include growth failure (failure to thrive); frequent vomiting;
irritability; hyperactivity; and unpredictable, erratic behavior" (p. 63).

"Older children commonly display bizarre or schizoid behavior patterns such as fright reactions,
screaming episodes, head banging, arm biting, disorientation, failure to respond to strong
stimuli, and spasticity or catatonia-like positions" (p. 63).

"Many of the severely cognitively impaired children have seizures, and approximately 80% of
untreated persons with PKU demonstrate abnormal electroencephalographs, regardless of whether
overt seizures occur" (p. 63).

### Diagnosis: the Guthrie test and the screening rules (pp. 63–64)

**The objective, stated first (p. 63):** "The objective in diagnosing or treating the disorder is
to prevent cognitive impairment."

| Element | As given (p. 63) |
|---|---|
| The test | "The most commonly used test for screening newborns is the Guthrie bacterial inhibition assay for phenylalanine in the blood" |
| How it works | "Bacillus subtilis, present in the culture medium, grows if the blood contains an excessive amount of phenylalanine" |
| Normal newborn range | "The normal range of blood phenylalanine concentration in newborns is 0.5 to 1 mg/dl" |
| Detection threshold | "The Guthrie test detects serum phenylalanine levels greater than 4 mg/dl (normal value is 1.6 mg/dl)" |
| **The specimen rule** | "Only fresh heel blood, not cord blood, can be used for the test" |
| Mandate | "Newborn screening tests are mandatory in all 50 US states" |
| Reliability | "The screening test is most reliable if the blood sample is taken after the infant has ingested a source of protein" |

**Heel blood, not cord blood, and after a protein feeding.** Both requirements exist because the
test measures a substance that only accumulates once the baby eats (pp. 43, 63).

**The three timing recommendations, as printed (p. 63):** "(1) collecting the initial specimen as
close as possible to discharge and no later than 7 days after birth, (2) **obtaining a subsequent
sample by 2 weeks of age if the initial specimen is collected before the newborn is 24 hours
old**, and (3) designating a primary care provider to all newborns before discharge for adequate
newborn follow-up screening."

**Recommendation 2 is this chapter's own NCLEX question 4.** If the first sample was drawn before
24 hours of age, the repeat is due **by 2 weeks**, not by 4 weeks (pp. 63, 78).

"In several states, a second newborn screening is performed when the infant is 1 to 2 weeks old,
on the basis that a maximum number of children with genetic disorders will be identified"
(p. 63). "After a positive screen, diagnostic testing must be performed promptly" (p. 63).

**The specimen technique (p. 64):** "When collecting the specimen, avoid layering the blood
specimen on the special Guthrie paper. **Layering is placing one drop of blood on top of the
other or overlapping the specimen. Best results are obtained by collecting the specimen with a
pipette from the heel stick and spreading the blood uniformly over the blot paper.**"

**Uniform spread, not stacked drops.** That is a concrete, testable technique error (p. 64).

**Who gets missed (p. 64):** "A major concern is that a significant number of infants are not
rescreened for PKU after early discharge and are at risk for a missed or delayed diagnosis. Give
special consideration to screening infants born at home who have no hospital contact, as well as
infants adopted internationally."

**Confirming the diagnosis (p. 64):** "Because of the possibility of variant forms of
hyperphenylalaninemia, a natural dietary protein challenge test is recommended after
approximately 3 months of dietary treatment to confirm the diagnosis of classic PKU."

### Therapeutic management (pp. 64–65)

**Why an enzyme cannot simply be given (p. 64):** "As with most genetic disorders, because the
genetic enzyme is intracellular, systemic administration of phenylalanine hydroxylase is of no
value."

**The two competing criteria (p. 64):** "Phenylalanine cannot be totally eliminated because it is
an essential amino acid in tissue growth. Therefore dietary management must meet two criteria:
**meet the child's nutritional need for optimum growth and maintain phenylalanine levels within
a safe range.**"

**Restrict but never eliminate.** Phenylalanine is essential; a diet with none of it starves the
child (p. 64).

| Element | As given (p. 64) |
|---|---|
| Treatment threshold | "Treatment for infants with sustained blood phenylalanine levels greater than 360" micromoles per liter is recommended |
| **When not to treat** | "treatment of phenylalanine levels between 120 and 360" micromoles per liter "is not recommended, but close follow-up for the first few years of life is important" |
| Duration | "a restricted phenylalanine diet, including medical foods and low-protein products, most likely will be medically required for virtually all individuals with classic PKU for their entire life" |
| Why lifelong | "Such a lifetime reduction of phenylalanine intake is necessary to prevent neuropsychologic and cognitive deficits" |
| Monitoring | "To evaluate the effectiveness of dietary treatment, frequent monitoring of blood phenylalanine and tyrosine levels is necessary" |
| **The dose** | "The diet is calculated to allow 20 to 30 mg of phenylalanine per kilogram of body weight per day" |
| **When to start** | "For optimum growth to occur, the diet begins no later than 3 weeks of age" |
| Monitoring schedule | "Phenylalanine levels should be carefully monitored throughout the first 12 years of age, and once stable, monthly assessments are still recommended" |

**Both phenylalanine and tyrosine are monitored.** Tyrosine because the pathway that makes it is
blocked (pp. 63, 64).

**Formula and feeding (pp. 64–65):**

| Element | As given |
|---|---|
| Why formula is needed | "Because all natural food proteins contain approximately 15% phenylalanine, specially prepared milk substitutes are prescribed for the infant" (p. 64) |
| What they contain | products "made from specially treated enzymatic casein hydrolysate, which provides only 0.4% phenylalanine (28.5 mg/8 oz)"; "They also contain minerals and vitamins to provide a balanced nutritional formula. Tyrosine and several other amino acids are supplied in the formula" (p. 64) |
| **Breastfeeding** | "Because of the low phenylalanine content of breast milk, total or partial breastfeeding may be possible with close monitoring of phenylalanine levels" (p. 64) |
| Older children | "Diet substitutes for older children, such as Phenyl-Free 2 (Mead Johnson) and Phenex-2 (Ross), contain no phenylalanine and allow for greater exchanges with natural low-phenylalanine foods in the diet, leading to a more normal diet" (the sentence begins on p. 64 and completes on p. 65) |
| Lifelong | "A low-phenylalanine diet begins as soon as possible after birth and continues throughout life. Adherence to this diet can be especially challenging in adolescence and adulthood" (p. 65) |

**Breast milk is allowed and is low in phenylalanine.** This chapter's own NCLEX question 4
offers that statement as a *correct* parent statement (pp. 64, 77).

**Maternal PKU (p. 65):** "Because phenylalanine levels greater than or equal to 20 mg/dl in
mothers with PKU affect the normal embryologic development of the fetus, women with PKU who are
not on a lifelong diet must resume a low-phenylalanine diet before pregnancy."

### Prognosis (p. 65)

| Finding | As given (p. 65) |
|---|---|
| Cognitive outcome | "Although many individuals with treated PKU manifest no cognitive and behavioral deficits, many comparisons of individuals with PKU to controls show lower performance on IQ tests, with larger differences in other cognitive domains. However, their performance is still in the average range" |
| Behavior | "Evidence for differences in behavioral adjustment is inconsistent despite anecdotal reports suggesting greater risk for internalizing psychopathology and attention disorders" |
| **What is unknown** | "there are insufficient data on the effects of phenylalanine restriction over many decades of life" |
| **Bone** | "Total bone mineral density is considerably lower in children who are on a low-phenylalanine diet, even though calcium, phosphorus, and magnesium intakes are higher than normal" |

**The diet has its own cost: lower bone mineral density despite adequate mineral intake**
(p. 65). That is an honest limitation the chapter states rather than glossing.

**The conceptual framing the chapter closes with (p. 65):** "In the case of PKU, early childhood
treatment with a reduced phenylalanine diet is highly successful at removing the possibility of
cognitive impairment. However, **PKU-rescued adults (i.e., those treated with reduction of
phenylalanine in early childhood) remain unable to metabolize the amino acid and will always
exhibit phenylalaninemia.**"

**Treatment changes the phenotype, not the genotype.** The enzyme is still missing at 30 as it
was at 3 days (p. 65), which is the whole basis of §25's phenotype-modification framework.

**Maternal PKU restated (p. 65):** "PKU-rescued women who do not maintain low phenylalanine
levels tend to produce children with cognitive and developmental impairments. **Although
indirectly related to the maternal PKU genotype, this is due to the fact that embryos are
developing in a uterine environment with excessive concentrations of blood phenylalanine.**"

**The fetus is damaged by the mother's blood, not by inheriting her genes.** A child of a
PKU-rescued mother is at risk even if the child does not have PKU (p. 65).

### Nursing care management (p. 65)

"The principal nursing considerations involve teaching the family regarding the dietary
restrictions. Although the treatment may sound simple, the task of maintaining such a strict
dietary regimen is demanding, especially for older children and adolescents" (p. 65).

| Food group | As given (p. 65) |
|---|---|
| Allowed with measuring | "Foods with low phenylalanine levels (e.g., some vegetables [except legumes]; fruits; juices; and some cereals, breads, and starches) must be measured to provide the prescribed amount of phenylalanine" |
| Restricted | "Most high-protein foods, such as meat and dairy products, are either eliminated or restricted to small amounts" |

**The developmental arc of nonadherence, which is what makes this a nursing problem (p. 65):**

| Stage | As given (p. 65) |
|---|---|
| Infancy | "Maintaining the diet during infancy presents few problems. Parents can introduce solid foods such as cereal, fruits, and vegetables as usual to the infant" |
| Toddler and preschool | "A decreased appetite and refusal to eat may reduce intake of the calculated phenylalanine requirement. The child's increasing independence may inhibit absolute control of what he or she eats. Either factor can result in decreased or increased phenylalanine levels" |
| School age | "peer pressure becomes a major force in deterring the child from eating the prescribed foods or abstaining from high-protein foods such as milkshakes or ice cream" |
| Adolescence | "Adolescence is a particularly difficult period, and limiting foods containing phenylalanine in adolescents with PKU is challenging" |
| Illness and growth | "Illness and growth spurts increase the body's need for this essential amino acid" |

**The number that makes the diet real (p. 65):** "a quarter-pound hamburger may provide a 2-day
phenylalanine allowance for a school-age child."

**Support (p. 65):** "The assistance of a registered dietitian is essential. Parents need a basic
understanding of the disorder and practical suggestions regarding food selection and
preparation."

**Involving the child (p. 65):** "As soon as children are old enough, usually by early preschool,
they should be involved in the daily calculation, menu planning, and formula preparation. A
computer voice-activated calculator, cards, or colored beads can help children keep track of the
daily allowance of phenylalanine foods. **A system of goal setting, self-monitoring, contracts,
and rewards can promote compliance in adolescents.**"

**Early preschool is when the child starts participating.** Not school age, not adolescence
(p. 65).

## 22. Galactosemia (pp. 65–66)

"Galactosemia is a rare autosomal recessive disorder that results from various gene mutations
leading to three distinct enzymatic deficiencies" (p. 65).

| Element | As given (p. 65) |
|---|---|
| The common form | "The most common type of galactosemia (classic galactosemia) results from a deficiency of a hepatic enzyme, galactose 1-phosphate uridyltransferase (GALT), and affects approximately 1 of 16,000 to 60,000 births" |
| The rare forms | "The other two varieties of galactosemia involve deficiencies in the enzymes galactokinase (GALK) and galactose 4" epimerase; "these are extremely rare disorders" |
| What all three do | "GALT, GALK, and GALE are all involved in the conversion of galactose into glucose" |

### Pathophysiology and course (pp. 65–66)

"Accumulation of activated 1-phosphate metabolites of galactose (and fructose) is extremely
toxic to various tissues, especially in the kidneys, liver, and nervous system" (p. 65).

**The timeline, which is the most examinable part of this section:**

| Timing | As given |
|---|---|
| At birth | "Infants with galactosemia appear normal at birth" (p. 66) |
| **On feeding** | "on ingestion of milk (which has a high lactose content) they begin to show progressive symptoms, including vomiting, diarrhea, and weight loss" (p. 66) |
| Second week | "Hepatic dysfunction leads to cirrhosis, resulting in jaundice in the infant by the second week of life" (the sentence begins on p. 65 and completes on p. 66) |
| Then | "The spleen subsequently becomes enlarged as a result of portal hypertension" (p. 66) |
| 1 to 2 months | "Cataracts are usually recognizable by 1 or 2 months of age" (p. 66) |
| Soon after | "cerebral damage, manifested by the symptoms of lethargy and hypotonia, is evident soon afterward" (p. 66) |
| Untreated | "Death during the first month of life is frequent in untreated infants" (p. 66) |

**Normal at birth, sick after the first feeding.** Exactly the same trigger structure as PKU:
the gene is broken from conception and the disease starts with milk (pp. 43, 66).

**Jaundice at 2 weeks, cataracts at 1 to 2 months.** Those two intervals separate galactosemia
from physiologic jaundice and from congenital cataracts of other causes (p. 66).

### Diagnosis (p. 66)

"Diagnosis is made on the basis of the infant's history, physical examination, galactosuria,
increased levels of galactose in the blood, or decreased levels of uridine diphosphate" galactose
"transferase activity in erythrocytes" (p. 66).

| Element | As given (p. 66) |
|---|---|
| Appearance | "The infant may display characteristics of malnutrition and dehydration; decreased muscle mass and body fat may be evident" |
| Screening | "Newborn screening for this disease is required in all states" |
| **Heterozygotes** | "Heterozygotes can also be identified because they have significantly lower levels of the essential enzyme" |
| Their behavior | "Although asymptomatic, such individuals have been noted to spontaneously dislike and therefore limit the ingestion of galactose-containing foods" |

**Carriers spontaneously avoid milk products.** A striking, memorable detail and the only place
in this chapter where a heterozygote has an observable behavior (p. 66).

### Management (p. 66)

| Element | As given (p. 66) |
|---|---|
| Infancy | "treatment consists of eliminating all milk and lactose-containing formula, **including breast milk**" |
| Formula of choice | "Traditionally, lactose-free formulas are used, with soy-protein formula being the feeding of choice. The American Academy of Pediatrics (2015) recommends the use of soy formula for infants diagnosed with galactosemia" |
| Solids | "As the infant progresses to solids, only foods low in galactose should be consumed. Certain fruits are high in galactose, and some dietitians recommend that they be avoided" |
| Nursing action | "Nurses should give food lists to the family to ensure appropriate foods are chosen" |
| **If suspected** | "If galactosemia is suspected, implement supportive treatment and care, including monitoring for hypoglycemia, liver failure, bleeding disorders, and Escherichia coli sepsis" |

**Breastfeeding is contraindicated in galactosemia and permitted in PKU.** That is the single
most likely thing to be swapped between these two disorders (pp. 64, 66).

**E. coli sepsis is on the monitoring list.** An unexpected but specific association (p. 66).

### Prognosis, and the honest limitation (p. 66)

"Follow-up studies of children treated from birth or within the first 2 months of life after
symptoms appear have found long-term complications, such as ovarian dysfunction, cataracts,
abnormal speech, cognitive impairment, growth restriction, and motor delay" (p. 66).

**"These findings have revealed that eliminating sources of galactose does not significantly
improve the outcome. New therapeutic strategies, such as enhancing residual transferase
activity, replacing depleted metabolites, or using gene replacement therapy, are needed to
improve the prognosis for these children"** (p. 66).

**Diet works for PKU and does not fully work for galactosemia.** The chapter says so directly
(pp. 65, 66), and that contrast is the reason both diseases are in the same section.

### Nursing care (p. 66)

"Nursing interventions are similar to those for PKU and other IEMs, except that dietary
restrictions are easier to maintain because many more foods are allowed" (p. 66).

| Point | As given (p. 66) |
|---|---|
| Labels | "reading food labels carefully for the presence of any form of lactose, especially dairy products, is mandatory" |
| **The hidden source** | "Many drugs, such as some of the penicillin preparations, contain lactose as filler and also must be avoided. Unfortunately, lactose is an unlabeled ingredient in many pharmaceuticals" |
| The action | "instruct parents to ask their local pharmacist about galactose content of any over-the-counter or prescription medication" |

**Lactose is a drug filler and is often unlabeled.** Ask the pharmacist about every medication,
prescription or not (p. 66). That is the one instruction unique to galactosemia.

## 23. Cytogenetic and molecular diagnostic techniques (pp. 66–67)

### Karyotype, and what replaced it (p. 66)

"Chromosomes have traditionally been studied under light microscopy" (p. 66). "The most common
human cells used for chromosomes studies are peripheral blood leukocytes obtained by
venipuncture and cells obtained by biopsy from a variety of tissues, such as skin (including
fetal skin cells), chorionic villi, and bone marrow" (p. 66).

"Robotic cell harvesters and computerized imaging systems have drastically cut down on manual
labor; however, expert technicians and cytogeneticists are still necessary to analyze and
interpret karyotypes" (p. 66).

**The change in practice, stated plainly (p. 66):** "Although karyotypes still have an important
role in identifying large chromosomal rearrangements such as translocations, **they have been
replaced by chromosomal microarray testing as the preferred tool for evaluating children with
congenital malformations and intellectual or global developmental delays and for whom a clinical
diagnosis is uncertain.**"

**Karyotype for translocations; microarray for the undiagnosed child with anomalies or delays**
(p. 66). That is the current-practice sentence in this section.

### What a chromosomal microarray finds (p. 66)

| Capability | As given (p. 66) |
|---|---|
| Core function | "Chromosomal microarray technology is used to identify submicroscopic as well as larger segments of DNA that have more or fewer copy number than normal" |
| Scale | "Thousands to millions of DNA segments throughout the genome are examined for clinically relevant copy number variations (CNVs)" |
| Known syndromes | "Conditions caused by deletions such as velo-cardio-facial syndrome (VCFS) and cri du chat can be diagnosed" |
| **Unknown syndromes** | "as well as very rare or previously unrecognized syndromes caused by submicroscopic deletions or duplications" |
| Relatedness | "If the identical areas are scattered throughout the genome, it is possible to estimate relatedness between the biologic parents" |
| Uniparental disomy | "If the identical area is in a segment contained within a single chromosome, it may indicate uniparental disomy" |
| Recessive clues | "An area of identical DNA between both chromosomes may offer diagnostic clues for autosomal recessive disorders" |

**A microarray can reveal consanguinity the family did not disclose.** That is a real
confidentiality issue attached to a routine test (p. 66).

### FISH, the targeted alternative (p. 66)

"When clinical examination, health history, and developmental history are strongly suggestive of
a known condition, **a less expensive targeted test can be used** to identify the specific
microdeletion, microduplication, or fragile site" (p. 66).

"Fluorescent in situ hybridization (FISH) uses fluorescent-labeled single-stranded DNA probes
designed to attach to specific areas of chromosomes" (p. 66).

**The VCFS worked example (p. 66):** "to diagnose VCFS, probes that adhere to 22q11.2 are used,
as well as probes to identify chromosome 22. A positive FISH test for persons with VCFS
identifies both copies of chromosome 22, but the probe specific to the q11.2 region only
attaches to one of the chromosomes because the region of interest is deleted in the other
chromosome 22."

**A positive FISH here means a signal is MISSING from one chromosome.** Two chromosome 22
signals, one q11.2 signal (p. 66). That inversion is what makes FISH results confusing to read.

**FISH answers a question you already have; microarray finds a question you did not ask**
(p. 66).

### Molecular techniques and sequencing (pp. 66–67)

"Identification of single-gene mutations responsible for a growing number of genetic disorders is
now possible with a variety of molecular tests" (p. 66).

| Level | As given |
|---|---|
| Single known gene | "In some conditions, such as CF, sickle cell disease, myotonic dystrophy, and fragile X syndrome, the specific gene is known and tests are available that target many of the known disease-associated mutations or that sequence the coding portions of the gene associated with the specific condition" (p. 66) |
| Gene panels | "Gene panels for some disorders have become available as technology improves to make it easier and less expensive to analyze a number of genes at one time" (the sentence begins on p. 66 and completes on p. 67) |
| **Whole exome** | "When exons in the entire genome are sequenced, it is called whole exome sequencing" (p. 67) |
| **Whole genome** | "When all accessible DNA within a genome is sequenced, it is called whole genome sequencing" (p. 67) |
| When to use them | "Whole exome or whole genome sequencing is used to diagnose rare disorders for which specific testing is not available or when a clinically diagnosed condition can be caused by a mutation in one of many possible genes" (p. 67) |
| Research frontier | "The use of whole exome or whole genome sequencing in healthy newborns is being studied" (p. 67) |

**Exome is the coding parts; genome is everything.** Exons "contain the code for a specific
protein" (p. 43), so exome sequencing reads only the protein-coding fraction (p. 67).

**The escalation ladder in this chapter: targeted mutation test, then gene panel, then exome,
then genome** (pp. 66, 67). Each step costs more and answers a broader question.

## 24. Predisposition genetic testing (p. 67)

"Molecular diagnostic techniques have enabled predisposition testing, which is the
identification of gene mutations associated with genetic disorders in asymptomatic individuals.
**Depending on the penetrance of the allele(s), these may be considered presymptomatic or
susceptibility tests**" (p. 67).

### The two kinds, and the penetrance that separates them

| | **Presymptomatic testing** | **Susceptibility testing** |
|---|---|---|
| Example | familial adenomatous polyposis, APC gene (p. 67) | hereditary breast and ovarian cancer, BRCA1 and BRCA2 (p. 67) |
| Penetrance | "These mutations are considered virtually 100% penetrant" (p. 67) | "Penetrance of mutations in these genes has been reported to be as high as 85% for breast cancer and lower for other associated cancers (ovarian, prostate, pancreatic, gastrointestinal, and melanoma)" (p. 67) |
| What a positive means | it **will** happen: "resulting in hundreds to thousands of adenomatous polyps and eventual colon cancer" (p. 67) | it **might**: "although some members may develop one or more of the associated cancers, other mutation-carrying members may die in their eighties without ever developing cancer" (p. 67) |
| Why test a child | "APC mutation testing in at-risk children is done to identify those who need colon screening and to identify polyp formation early so cancer-preventive surgical decisions can be made" (p. 67) | not tested in childhood |
| Variability | | "Onset and type of cancer vary among members of a family identified with a mutation" (p. 67) |

**Penetrance decides the name of the test.** Near-100% penetrance makes it presymptomatic;
partial penetrance makes it susceptibility (p. 67). That is a direct application of §10's
penetrance definition (p. 50).

**Both require a known family mutation first (p. 67):** "Once the APC mutation has been
identified in an affected family member, at-risk family members can be tested for the same
mutation" (p. 67). The same sentence structure is used for BRCA. **This is the same constraint as Table
3.4's prenatal-testing footnote** (p. 56).

### Testing children for adult-onset disease (p. 67)

**"Predisposition genetic testing for adult onset cancers is discouraged in at-risk children
until they are cognitively and emotionally able to make an informed decision about testing for
themselves"** (p. 67).

**The exception, and the ethical problem it creates (p. 67):** "However, if a child has whole
exome or whole genome testing to determine the cause of his or her genetic disorder, the child's
parents are given the option to have their child's DNA additionally analyzed for a set of genes
that include BRCA1, BRCA2, and other genes associated with adult-onset conditions."

**"Purposely analyzing the genes compromises the child's future autonomy to decide whether or not
the information is desired"** (p. 67).

**The child's right not to know is the principle.** The chapter names it twice, here and under
carrier testing: "Carrier testing for children is generally not recommended because the
information is for reproductive purposes"; "parents are counseled that the child should make a
decision about carrier testing when she or he is old enough to make an informed choice"
(p. 69).

**But APC testing in children IS recommended** (p. 67), because a preventable, childhood-onset
consequence exists. **The discriminator is whether the result changes the child's care now.**

## 25. Therapeutic management of genetic disease (pp. 67–68)

**The framing sentence for this whole section (p. 67):** "Therapy for genetic disease is
currently aimed at correcting the phenotypic expression of a variant gene, and therefore the
major goal of therapy is **modification of the internal or external environment** to correct or
minimize the effects of the variant gene or pair of variant genes."

"However, genomic advances have opened doors to genotype intervention, and clinical applications
of gene manipulation techniques are currently being tested" (p. 67).

**We treat the phenotype, not the genotype.** Everything below except gene transfer is a way of
changing the environment around an unchanged gene (p. 67).

### The nine modalities

| # | Modality | What it does, as given |
|---|---|---|
| 1 | **Surgical management** (p. 67) | "Surgical repair of structural defects has made it possible to prolong life in a number of multifactorial disorders, such as congenital heart disease and NTDs" |
| 2 | **Diet modification** (p. 67) | "restricting the intake of foods containing that substance may prevent irreversible damage from the improper metabolism of these compounds" |
| 3 | **Metabolic manipulation** (pp. 67–68) | "supplying the missing product that cannot be synthesized prevents undesirable effects"; also removal of accumulated toxins and cofactor supplementation |
| 4 | **Avoidance of drugs or other harmful substances** (p. 68) | "avoidance of the drugs that precipitate a reaction is a simple preventive measure" |
| 5 | **Immunologic prevention** (p. 68) | "The administration of immunoglobulin to Rh-negative mothers after the birth of an Rh-positive infant is effective in preventing Rh-antibody formation, which causes hemolytic disease of the newborn in subsequent births" |
| 6 | **Transplantation** (p. 68) | "Better control of tissue incompatibility problems is improving the survival of children undergoing replacement of nonfunctioning organs with normal organs" |
| 7 | **Gene product replacement** (p. 68) | "Recombinant DNA technology makes it possible to produce large quantities of certain gene products" |
| 8 | **Gene transfer** (p. 68) | "an experimental technique used to introduce a normally functioning gene into the cells of a patient with a genetic disorder" |
| 9 | **Environmental modification** (p. 68) | "Inherited diseases or defects with no therapeutic modality can be modified to enhance the quality of life for the affected individual" |

### Surgical examples (p. 67)

"Numerous facial and limb deformities can be corrected by plastic and reconstructive
techniques." "In the case of familial adenomatous polyposis, the colon is surgically removed to
prevent colon cancer that would eventually develop in one or more of the countless polyps that
line the colon." "The use of splenectomy in several hereditary disorders of blood cells prevents
the trapping and destruction of abnormal blood cells in that organ." "Early diagnosis and
enucleation in retinoblastoma have reduced the mortality rate from this malignant eye tumor."
"Fetal surgery may also be performed for some life-threatening anomalies such a diaphragmatic
hernia."

**And the one the chapter flags as contested (p. 67):** "Corrective surgery for children born
with ambiguous genitalia (e.g., girls with congenital adrenal hyperplasia), although widely
used, has been recently the focus of controversy that involves the issue of parental versus
affected individual (future) preferences."

**The same autonomy principle as predisposition testing.** A decision made for a child that the
adult would have made differently (pp. 67, 67).

### Diet modification and folic acid (p. 67)

**The dietary examples (p. 67):** "the low-phenylalanine diet prescribed for PKU, elimination of
dairy products containing lactose for hereditary lactase deficiency, avoidance of foods
containing or producing galactose for galactosemia, and a diet low in branched-chain amino acids
for maple syrup urine disease." "This dietary control is lifelong and requires a high level of
adherence."

**Maternal PKU restated a third time (p. 67):** "Women with PKU who have not maintained dietary
control must reinstitute a strict low-phenylalanine diet before conception and maintain it
throughout pregnancy to prevent a high risk of adverse fetal effects."

**Folic acid, with all four numbers (p. 67):**

| Element | As given (p. 67) |
|---|---|
| The finding | "Preconception and prenatal folic acid supplementation has been shown to reduce the incidence or recurrence of NTDs" |
| **The general recommendation** | "in 1992 the US Public Health Service recommended that all women capable of becoming pregnant should consume 0.4 mg of folic acid per day" |
| Fortification | "As of 1998 the US Food and Drug Administration mandated that folic acid be added to enriched grain products. This would add 0.1 mg folic acid to the daily diet of the average person" |
| **The high-risk dose** | "Women who have previously had a pregnancy affected by an NTD are advised to take 4.0 mg of folic acid per day beginning 1 month before conception and continuing throughout the first trimester of pregnancy" |
| The effect | "The risk of recurrence of an NTD in another pregnancy is reduced by 72% in women following this regimen" |

**0.4 mg for everyone, 4.0 mg after an affected pregnancy: a tenfold difference.** And the
high-risk dose starts **1 month before conception** and runs through the first trimester
(p. 67). **A 72% reduction in recurrence** is the payoff.

**All women capable of becoming pregnant, not all pregnant women.** The neural tube closes before
most women know they are pregnant (pp. 42, 67).

### Metabolic manipulation, in three directions (pp. 67–68)

| Direction | Examples as given |
|---|---|
| **Supply what is missing** | "thyroid hormone is prescribed to prevent the damaging effects of hypothyroidism, and providing the missing blood factors prevents life-threatening and debilitating hemorrhages in persons with different types of hemophilia"; "insulin for diabetes mellitus, growth hormone for growth hormone deficiency, and corticosteroids for adrenogenital syndrome" (the sentence begins on p. 67 and completes on p. 68) |
| **Remove what accumulates** | "Some of the deleterious effects of hemochromatosis, a hereditary disorder characterized by an excess accumulation of iron in the liver, heart, and pancreas, can be reduced with the removal of iron by periodic therapeutic phlebotomies or administration of chelating agents" (p. 68) |
| **Supply a cofactor** | "the use of vitamin B12 in persons with pernicious anemia, whose absorption of this vitamin is impaired. Vitamin and cofactor supplementation may be used to treat mitochondrial disease. Phenobarbitone can be used to enhance hepatic metabolism of unconjugated bilirubin in children with Crigler-Najjar syndrome type 2, thus helping prevent brain damage (kernicterus) and jaundice" (p. 68) |

### Drug avoidance (p. 68)

"In drug-induced disease, such as glucose-6-phosphate dehydrogenase (G6PD) deficiency and the
porphyrias, avoidance of the drugs that precipitate a reaction is a simple preventive measure"
(p. 68).

| Disorder | Drugs to avoid as given (p. 68) |
|---|---|
| G6PD deficiency | "aspirin and quinine-based antimalarial medications" |
| Acute intermittent porphyria | "ethanol, barbiturates, anticonvulsants, sulfonamides, and oral contraceptives" |
| Malignant hyperthermia | "Some anesthetic agents may precipitate symptoms of malignant hyperthermia" |

**Three pharmacogenomic disorders in one paragraph.** This is §2's pharmacogenomics made
concrete (pp. 42, 68), and Table 3.4 adds fava beans and certain infections for G6PD (p. 54).

### Gene product replacement, and its three limits (p. 68)

"The administration of gene products is especially applicable when the missing product is a
circulatory peptide or protein, as in coagulation factor VIII or IX in hemophilia type A and
type B, respectively" (p. 68). Recombinant technology "avoids the infectious contamination risk
that accompanied extraction of gene products from mass quantities of human blood or tissue"
(p. 68).

| Limit | As given (p. 68) |
|---|---|
| **Membrane-bound products** | "If the gene product is membrane bound, as is the case for enzymes deficient in various lysosomal storage diseases, the natural enzyme product may need to be purposefully altered so that it can enter the cell through a naturally occurring cell receptor" |
| The success story | "Gaucher disease was the first lysosomal disorder to be successfully treated in this manner, and lessons learned in the process have led to more recent enzyme replacement therapies for disorders such as Pompe disease and Hurler syndrome" |
| **Protected compartments** | "If the missing gene product is a substance that acts within a metabolic pathway of a specific organ protected by a barrier, such as the enzyme hexosaminidase A in Tay-Sachs disease (blood-brain barrier), injectable products become ineffective and replacement is more problematic" |
| **Immune response** | "The introduction of a foreign gene product may eventually trigger an immune reaction, as has been observed in hemophilia when the recipient develops antibody against the clotting factor, greatly reducing the efficiency of an otherwise successful therapy" |

**Circulating protein: replaceable. Inside a cell: needs engineering. Behind the blood-brain
barrier: not reachable.** That three-step limit explains why hemophilia is treatable and
Tay-Sachs is not (p. 68), and it is the same intracellular argument PKU makes about giving
phenylalanine hydroxylase (p. 64).

### Gene transfer (p. 68)

| Element | As given (p. 68) |
|---|---|
| Purpose | "to introduce a gene that will produce a protein that can function in a patient and treat or prevent disease" |
| The other direction | "Researchers are also working on strategies to turn off genes that are not functioning properly and cannot be fixed by simply adding a functioning gene into patients' cells" |
| **Where it is used** | "These approaches involve considerable risk and are focused on treatment of genetic disorders that have no other therapies" |
| Cancer | "Gene transfer technology is also being studied for the treatment of cancer by targeting the tumor cells" |

### Environmental modification (p. 68)

"Some examples of environmental manipulation include hearing aids for children with congenital
hearing loss, glasses or vision enhancers such as books in enlarged print or Braille for the
visually impaired, mobilizing devices such as braces and wheelchairs for persons with muscle and
bone impairment, prosthetic devices for those with limb deficiencies, and infant stimulation
programs to maximize the potential of children who are developmentally delayed" (p. 68).

"Environmental manipulation also includes protection from the damaging effects of the
environment, such as exposure to sunlight, which can cause skin fragility and blistering in
persons with porphyrias and increases the risk of skin cancers in patients with xeroderma
pigmentosum and oculocutaneous albinism" (p. 68).

**Sun avoidance is a genetic therapy for three disorders in this chapter** (p. 68): porphyria,
xeroderma pigmentosum, and oculocutaneous albinism.

## 26. Genetic testing and the purposes of screening (pp. 68–69)

### The two categories (p. 68)

"Genetic testing can be broadly divided into two categories: diagnostic testing and screening"
(p. 68). "Tests to detect a disease-associated gene mutation or chromosome abnormality in
symptomatic individuals are rapidly assuming greater importance in management of genetic
disorders as more genes and mutations are identified and techniques are developed for easy
application of the tests" (p. 68).

**The caution attached (p. 68):** "to be truly effective, screening programs depend on thorough
education of both health professionals and the public regarding these programs and the
limitations and implications of testing. **The religious, moral, ethical, and legal issues
revolving around screening and prenatal diagnosis are extensive and change over time.**"

### Screening defined, and its four preconditions (p. 68)

"Genetic screening is presumptive identification of an unrecognized genetic predisposition for
future disease in individuals or their progeny for which preventive or disease course" altering
interventions exist (p. 68).

**"In general, genetic screening targets populations, whereas genetic diagnostic testing targets
individuals"** (p. 68).

**Population versus individual is the distinction.** Same test, different purpose (p. 68).

**The four requirements, as given (p. 68):** "The first corollaries of any genetic screening
intervention must be **voluntary participation, equal access to all, and confidentiality** (both
in conducting the tests and in handling records and results). In addition, **education and
counseling about tests and procedures must be an integral part of any screening program.**
Attention must be paid to quality control of all aspects of testing and laboratory procedures."

### The three purposes (p. 69)

**This list is this chapter's own NCLEX question 3.** "Genetic screening has three purposes:
(1) to provide for early recognition of a disease, before signs and symptoms occur, for which
effective intervention and therapy exist (e.g., PKU); (2) to identify carriers of a genetic
disease for the purpose of maximizing parenthood planning options (e.g., Tay-Sachs disease); and
(3) to obtain population data on frequency, spectrum, and natural history of genetic variations
not currently known to be associated with disease" (p. 69).

**Treat it, plan around it, or study it.** Purposes 1 and 2 benefit the individual; purpose 3
benefits knowledge (p. 69).

### The four times screening can occur (p. 69)

"(1) preconception screening of selected populations for heterozygous carriers (e.g., Ashkenazi
Jewish carrier screening panel); (2) screening of relatives of known carrier or affected
individuals within a family, for the purpose of reproductive decision making; (3)
postconceptional (prenatal) screening (e.g., maternal serum screen for identifying risks for
NTDs and chromosome abnormalities); and (4) newborn screening" (p. 69).

## 27. Newborn screening (p. 69)

"Newborn screening began in the 1960s when Guthrie devised the blood spot test to screen for
PKU" (p. 69). "Newborn screening for PKU was begun in all states, and currently each state
offers screening for a growing number of disorders such as hemoglobinopathies, galactosemia,
hypothyroidism, and congenital adrenal hypoplasia" (p. 69).

### The two sets of criteria

**The historical criteria (p. 69):**

- "The disease occurs with a significant frequency."
- "An inexpensive and reliable method of testing exists."
- "There is effective treatment and intervention."
- "If untreated, the baby will die or be severely developmentally impaired."
- "An affected newborn may appear normal at birth."

**The ACMG minimum criteria (p. 69).** "Because the actual disorders screened by individual
states varied considerably, the American College of Medical Genetics (ACMG) was commissioned by
the Maternal Child Health Bureau of the Health Resources and Services Administration to develop
a uniform screening panel." The three:

- "It can be identified at a phase (24 to 48 hours after birth) at which it would not ordinarily
  be clinically detected."
- "A follow-up diagnostic test with appropriate sensitivity and specificity is available for it."
- "There are demonstrated benefits of early detection, timely intervention, and efficacious
  treatment of the condition being tested."

**The panel (p. 69):** "A panel of 29 core conditions and 25 secondary conditions was recommended
by ACMG in its 2006 report."

**29 core and 25 secondary.** And "With advancing technology the number of conditions screened by
individual states can be expected to change" (p. 69).

**Note the shared logic of both criteria lists: a screened disease must be silent at birth and
treatable once found.** A condition with obvious signs or no treatment fails the test (p. 69).

### Timing, and the follow-up requirement (p. 69)

**"Because many of the conditions tested for are metabolic disorders, it is important that the
first newborn screening blood sample be obtained at least 24 hours after the first protein
feeding or within the first 72 hours of life"** (p. 69).

"Infants found to be screen positive (thus at high risk) for a metabolic condition such as PKU
or galactosemia are immediately referred to a medical center for testing to confirm the
diagnosis and initiate treatment" (p. 69).

**"Of course, this relies on a coordinated follow-up program that includes well-informed primary
care providers and nurses who can locate newborns with abnormal screening results and facilitate
the necessary follow-up visits"** (p. 69).

**Screen positive is not a diagnosis.** It triggers confirmatory testing, and the whole system
fails if the family cannot be found (pp. 63, 69).

**Three timing rules coexist in this chapter and they are not identical.** The general newborn
screening rule is 24 hours after the first protein feeding or within 72 hours of life (p. 69);
the PKU rule is no later than 7 days after birth with a repeat by 2 weeks if the first was drawn
before 24 hours (p. 63); and the ACMG identification window is 24 to 48 hours after birth
(p. 69). **A question naming PKU wants the p. 63 rule.**

## 28. Screening for reproductive and epidemiologic information (p. 69)

### Carrier screening (p. 69)

"Screening for heterozygotes (carriers) can detect unaffected persons with certain variant genes
who, when they mate with an individual who carries a mutation in the same gene, are at high risk
of producing an affected offspring. These individuals are thus provided with the information they
need for making decisions about family planning" (p. 69).

"Carriers of a number of diseases can be detected by laboratory tests, but, because of the rarity
of these diseases, mass screening is not feasible except in persons or populations known to be at
risk" (p. 69).

**Who is at risk (p. 69):** "close relatives of those with an inborn error of metabolism or other
detectable disorder and certain ethnic populations known to have a high incidence of a specific
disease, such as sickle cell anemia in African Americans, Tay-Sachs disease in Ashkenazi Jews,
and thalassemia in people of Mediterranean ancestry."

### Why carrier screening is contested (p. 69)

**"This type of screening is sometimes controversial"** (p. 69). The chapter gives three distinct
problems:

| Problem | As given (p. 69) |
|---|---|
| **Incomplete panels** | "Children with CF have a disease-associated mutation in each of their CFTR genes. More than 1000 different CFTR mutations have been identified, yet carrier testing for CF typically analyzes less than 100 of the possible CFTR mutations. **Therefore after screening, a couple may feel safe to proceed with pregnancy yet still have a child with CF**" |
| **Social consequences** | "Carrier status screening has influenced marriage partner selection" |
| **Misunderstanding** | "Some carriers have made life choices based on inaccurate understanding of the implications of carrier status (e.g., thinking they would get the disease or that they were at high risk for having an affected child regardless of whether the partner is also a carrier)" |

**"These misunderstandings and their consequences underscore the importance of careful education
before decision making regarding carrier screening"** (p. 69).

**A negative carrier screen is not a guarantee.** Fewer than 100 of more than 1000 mutations are
tested (p. 69), and that is the concrete reason the chapter insists on pretest education.

**Children and carrier testing (p. 69):** "Carrier testing for children is generally not
recommended because the information is for reproductive purposes. Instead, parents are counseled
that the child should make a decision about carrier testing when she or he is old enough to make
an informed choice. **Yet newborn screening tests reveal carrier status for certain conditions
such as CF and sickle cell.**"

**Newborn screening reveals carrier status whether or not anyone wanted it to.** That tension is
stated and not resolved (p. 69), and it echoes the exome-sequencing autonomy problem (p. 67).

### Epidemiologic screening (p. 69)

"Public health officials may use screening as a method for monitoring the incidence of diseases
or malformations in a population to detect environmental or other causes that might
significantly influence the incidence of the disorders" (p. 69).

**The worked example, which closes the folic acid loop (p. 69):** "geographic and socioeconomic
variations in the incidence of NTDs eventually led to research that determined that folic acid
supplementation of 0.4 mg/day in women of childbearing age reduces NTD occurrence within the
population."

**Purpose 3 of screening produced a public health intervention.** Population data on NTDs led to
the folic acid recommendation (pp. 67, 69).

## 29. What screening means to families (pp. 69–70)

| Benefit | As given (p. 69) |
|---|---|
| Carrier screening | "facilitating genetic counseling and reproductive planning and providing useful information to other at-risk family members" |
| Newborn screening | "provides for early detection and treatment initiation, maximizing quality of life" |
| The condition on both | "realizing these benefits requires that couples and parents be given adequate information to make informed decisions about whether to pursue screening" |

**Consent and its limits (p. 69):** "For newborn screening, parent choice about testing may be
limited by state regulations. Some states allow exemptions from newborn screening in certain
situations, such as objections on religious grounds if there is a conflict with religious
practices and beliefs of an established church. **Regardless, parents need to be educated about
the purpose, benefits, risks, and limitations of newborn screening and the implications of
abnormal results.**"

**Education is required whether or not consent is.** Newborn screening is largely mandatory and
the teaching obligation does not depend on the choice (p. 69).

**How families want the information (p. 70):** "Parents have indicated that multiple exposures to
information in different formats beginning before conception are important" (the sentence begins
on p. 69 and completes on p. 70).

### The harms (p. 70)

**"The social stigma of being the carrier of a defective gene may be a side effect of screening.
In some families such knowledge is embarrassing and damaging to self-esteem"** (p. 70).

| Group | As given (p. 70) |
|---|---|
| **Teenagers** | "Teenagers may be especially vulnerable to the effects of knowing they carry a specific disease-associated gene mutation at a time when identity formation and peer approval are extremely important" |
| Cultural groups | "Cultural views regarding this knowledge can have profound effects on the members of some ethnic groups. In some cases, social status within the cultural group can be impaired" |

**Adolescence is the worst time to learn you are a carrier.** That developmental argument is the
chapter's strongest reason for deferring carrier testing to adulthood (pp. 69, 70).

### The nurse's job here (p. 70)

**"Probably the most important area for nursing practice is teaching, or the ability to convey
clear, precise, and unambiguous information to clients"** (p. 70).

| Element | As given (p. 70) |
|---|---|
| What families must understand | "why the screening is proposed, what the results mean, and how the family can interpret false-positive and false-negative results" |
| Why it matters | "Anxiety is greater when families have not received sufficient information about the screening or testing process and its significance for their health" |
| **The practical question nobody asks** | "Families also have a right to know who assumes the cost of additional testing or retesting, the family or the state" |
| The role | "The nurse is a valuable resource in ensuring that families are aware of alternatives and in helping them make the best decision" |

**Who pays for the retest is named as a right, not a courtesy** (p. 70).

## 30. Prenatal testing (pp. 70–71)

### Why a pediatric nurse needs this (p. 70)

"Pediatric nurses may encounter couples who had prenatal testing and are preparing for the
anticipated care of their unborn child. Such couples may be making primary care arrangements;
meeting professionals in the newborn intensive care unit; meeting with surgeons to learn about
the surgeries their newborn will need in the first few days, weeks, or months of life; and
talking with other parents who have children with the same condition. **Thus it is important
that pediatric nurses have a general understanding of the capabilities, limitations, and risks
of prenatal testing**" (p. 70).

### Screening versus diagnosis (p. 70)

**"Although screening options are available for all pregnant women, prenatal diagnostic testing
is reserved for pregnancies considered at increased risk for less than healthy outcomes"**
(p. 70).

**The three purposes, as given (p. 70):** "identifying congenital anomalies and genetic disorders
that may need intervention during pregnancy or soon after delivery; enabling parents to prepare
for the birth of a child who will require long-term medical or developmental interventions; and
enabling parents to make decisions about placing their child for adoption or terminating the
pregnancy."

**And the limit stated in the same paragraph (p. 70):** "However, even normal prenatal test
results do not guarantee the birth of a healthy baby."

### Box 3.6, indications for prenatal testing (p. 70)

**General risk factors:** "Maternal age of at least 35 years at time of delivery or at least 31
years if twin gestation"; "Elevated or low trisomy profile screen results."

**Specific risk factors:**

- "Previous child with a structural defect or chromosome abnormality"
- "Previous stillbirth or neonatal death"
- "Structural abnormality in mother or father"
- "Balanced translocation in mother or father"
- inherited disorders: "Cystic fibrosis, metabolic disorders, sex-linked recessive disorders"
- medical disease in the mother: "Diabetes mellitus, phenylketonuria"
- exposure to a teratogen: "Ionizing radiation, anticonvulsant medicines, lithium, isotretinoin, alcohol"
- infection: "Rubella, toxoplasmosis, cytomegalovirus"
- "Abnormal ultrasound findings"

**Ethnic risk factors:** Tay-Sachs disease in "Ashkenazi Jewish, French Canadian"; Sickle cell
anemia in "Black African, Mediterranean, Arab, Indian, Pakistani"; the thalassemias in
"Mediterranean, Southern and Southeast Asian, Chinese."

**35 at delivery, or 31 with twins.** The twin threshold is lower and is easy to miss (p. 70).

**Maternal PKU is listed as an indication for prenatal testing**, alongside diabetes (p. 70),
which is consistent with its listing as a teratogen (p. 62).

### Prenatal screening tests (p. 70)

"Noninvasive screening uses maternal blood or ultrasound to assess fetal risk for certain
congenital malformations or chromosome abnormalities" (p. 70).

| Method | As given (p. 70) |
|---|---|
| Maternal age | "Maternal age has been the longest-standing screening measure to detect women at increased risk for having a child with a chromosome abnormality" |
| Serum markers | "The most common laboratory screening method is to analyze maternal serum for abnormal levels of pregnancy-related chemical markers (such as free human chorionic gonadotropin [hCG], unconjugated estriol, and pregnancy-associated plasma protein A)" |
| Ultrasound | "Screening ultrasound can be used to measure thickness of the back of the fetal neck" |
| **Combining them** | "Screening tests that reveal two or more abnormal markers in addition to maternal age are more likely to detect an abnormal pregnancy" |
| What follows a positive | "Pregnancies that have a positive screen are recommended to have further evaluation with diagnostic ultrasound, amniocentesis, or fetal cord blood sampling" |

### Cell free DNA screening (p. 70)

"Cell free DNA screening is a first-trimester, noninvasive technique designed to detect common
chromosome disorders and is increasingly being used to screen for single-gene disorders when a
family mutation or pair of mutations is known" (p. 70).

| Element | As given (p. 70) |
|---|---|
| The biology | "During a pregnancy, about 10% of maternal circulating DNA from broken-down cells come from the placenta or fetus" |
| The analysis | "Maternal blood is analyzed for disproportionate fragments of targeted chromosomes (usually chromosomes 21, 18, and 13)" |
| **The asymmetry** | "Although detection of abnormally high fragments of targeted chromosomes is considered diagnostic, **the absence of detection does not rule out the possibility of chromosome abnormalities**" |
| Follow-up | "Follow-up diagnostic chorionic villi sampling or amniocentesis is recommended when noninvasive prenatal testing is normal" |

**A positive cell free DNA result is treated as diagnostic; a negative one is not.** That
asymmetry is unusual and is exactly what a question would test (p. 70).

**Chromosomes 21, 18, and 13, which are the three autosomal trisomies compatible with live
birth** (pp. 45, 70). The screen targets what can actually be born.

### Prenatal diagnostic procedures (pp. 70–71)

"Invasive prenatal diagnostic tests include chorionic villi sampling (CVS), amniocentesis, fetal
blood sampling, and preimplantation analysis of embryonic cells" (p. 70).

| Procedure | Timing as given | Sample and use as given |
|---|---|---|
| **CVS** | "can be performed between 10 and 12 weeks of gestation" (p. 70) | "Using either a transcervical or transabdominal approach guided by ultrasound, the clinician obtains a small biopsy of the chorionic villi" (p. 70); "Cells from the sample can be grown for chromosome and single-gene disorder studies" (the sentence begins on p. 70 and completes on p. 71) |
| **Amniocentesis** | "usually performed between 14 and 18 weeks of gestation under ultrasound guidance" (p. 71) | "Fetal skin cells that have sloughed off and are present in the amniotic fluid can be isolated and cultured for chromosome or single-gene analyses" (p. 71) |
| **Fetal blood sampling** | "can be performed after 18 weeks of gestation" (p. 71) | "blood is drawn from the umbilical vein"; "usually done for prenatal evaluation of fetal hematologic abnormalities, inborn errors of metabolism, fetal infection, and rapid chromosome analysis" (p. 71) |
| **Preimplantation genetic diagnosis** | before implantation (p. 71) | "first requires in vitro (test tube) fertilization followed by in vitro genetic testing of one or more cells extracted from the developing embryo or, less commonly, genetic testing of the polar bodies. Embryos without an identified genetic disorder are then candidates for implantation" (p. 71) |

**CVS is earliest and its limitation is contamination (p. 71):** "Contamination of the sample with
maternal cells can compromise the accuracy of test results."

**Amniocentesis can do something CVS cannot (p. 71):** "AFP can be measured in the amniotic fluid
and is **a much more reliable indicator of an NTD or abdominal wall defect than maternal serum
AFP**."

**The NTD confirmation sequence (p. 71):** "An additional test for an enzyme specific to neural
tissue, acetylcholinesterase, may be done; **if this test is positive in the presence of an
elevated AFP level, it is diagnostic of an open NTD.** A diagnostic ultrasound clarifies the
extent and location of the NTD or abdominal wall defect."

**Elevated amniotic AFP plus positive acetylcholinesterase equals an open neural tube defect.**
Two findings, one diagnosis (p. 71).

**Even preimplantation diagnosis needs confirming (p. 71):** "As with all technologies, error can
occur, so confirmatory testing with CVS or amniocentesis is recommended following
preimplantation genetic diagnosis."

**The rarely used tests (p. 71):** "Fetal biopsy is sometimes used to diagnose certain genetic
skin disorders and metabolic disorders when DNA studies are unavailable or are uninformative.
Fetal echocardiography may be performed for further diagnosis when a cardiac defect is noted on
ultrasound or if prior prenatal procedures reveal the fetus has a genetic disorder in which
cardiac malformations may occur."

**The chronologic ladder: PGD before implantation, CVS at 10 to 12 weeks, amniocentesis at 14 to
18, fetal blood sampling after 18** (pp. 70, 71). Amniocentesis is also given as "usually
performed at 14 to 20 weeks of gestation" in Box 3.1 (p. 41); see the GAP register.

## 31. Genetic evaluation and counseling (pp. 71–72)

### Who needs it (p. 71)

"The expanded recognition of genetic diseases and disorders and an increasingly well-informed
public are creating a justified demand for genetic evaluation, diagnosis, information regarding
risks to present and future generations, and access to available therapies" (p. 71).

**The problem the chapter names (p. 71):** "Unfortunately, however, persons who need expert
genetic counseling often make uninformed decisions on their own or are the victims of
well-meaning but equally uninformed relatives, acquaintances, or paraprofessionals."

### Pediatric indications for genetic consultation (p. 71)

The ACMG list, as printed. **This is one of the most directly examinable lists in the chapter.**

- "Family history of hereditary diseases, birth defects, or developmental problems"
- "Family history of sudden cardiac death or early-onset cancer"
- "Family history of mental illness"
- "Abnormal newborn screen"
- "Abnormal genetic test result ordered by a nongenetics professional who lacks the knowledge and
  experience to discuss implications of results"
- "Progressive neurologic condition"
- "Major congenital anomaly (e.g., cleft lip, congenital heart defect, limb abnormality, spina
  bifida)"
- "Pattern of major or minor anomalies suggestive of genetic disorder"
- "Congenital or early-onset hearing loss or vision loss"
- "Cognitive impairment or autism"
- "Abnormal sexual maturation or delayed puberty"
- "Abnormally tall or short stature compared with other family members"
- "Excessive bleeding or excessive clotting"
- **"Parental requests that child be evaluated by a genetics professional"**

**The last item makes a parent's request an indication in its own right** (p. 71). No other
justification is needed.

### Who asks, and why (p. 71)

| Situation | As given (p. 71) |
|---|---|
| Unaffected inquirers | "Persons may not be affected themselves but may request genetic counseling about the heritability of a trait" |
| Prospective parents | "A young couple contemplating childbearing may be concerned about a disorder in one of their families or may seek advice because they are related" |
| At-risk populations | "A couple who are both members of a population at risk for certain genetic diseases may wish to determine whether they carry an associated gene mutation" |
| Adoption | "A couple planning adoption might seek genetic evaluation and counseling regarding a prospective child" |
| **The most common** | "More often, persons who inquire about the possibility of recurrence of a disease or disorder have a child with a genetic disease or disorder or had a child who died of the disease" |
| Timing | "They might seek this advice before initiating another pregnancy or after the mother is already pregnant" |

**The clinical payoff of knowing a sibling's diagnosis (p. 71):** "If prenatal diagnostic testing
is not done, the history of a condition in an older sibling, such as galactosemia, alerts health
personnel to initiate specific and thorough testing for the condition in a newborn. In this way,
early therapy can be initiated, thus minimizing or eliminating the effects of the disease or
defect."

### Genetic services (p. 71)

"Comprehensive genetic services consist of a group of specialists, which may include clinical
geneticists, advanced practice nurses in genetics, genetic counselors, psychologists,
biochemical geneticists, cytogeneticists, molecular geneticists, nurses, social workers, and
other auxiliary personnel" (p. 71).

| Element | As given (p. 71) |
|---|---|
| Expanding role | "Although genetic centers were typically considered a resource for genetic evaluation, diagnosis, and counseling, a growing number of centers are managing the health care needs of patients with complex, rare genetic disorders" |
| Specialization | "Some centers specialize in treatment of genetic disorders (e.g., enzyme replacement therapy for lysosomal storage disorders)" |
| **The access problem** | "Unfortunately, these units are concentrated in and around large metropolitan areas. As a result, counseling is not always accessible to a large number of people who could benefit from the service" |

**What a genetic evaluation consists of (p. 71):** "a genetics professional obtains or updates the
patient's family history and pedigree, developmental history, and medical history (including
pregnancy, labor, and delivery information); performs a physical examination, including
dysmorphic features; and orders appropriate testing such as biochemical, cytogenetic, or
molecular procedures. **A genetic diagnosis may or may not be made initially, and follow-up
evaluations are common.**"

**The sentence that distinguishes genetic prognosis from all other prognosis (p. 71):** "Unlike a
medical prognosis, which predicts the outcome of a disease for an individual, **a genetic
prognosis may have implications for members of the immediate family, other relatives, and future
offspring.**"

**A genetic diagnosis is never only about the patient.** That is why confidentiality is a
corollary of screening (p. 68) and why the pedigree reaches three generations (p. 72).

### What genetic counseling is (pp. 71–72)

"Genetic counseling is a communication process that deals with the human problems associated with
the occurrence, or risk of occurrence, of a genetic disorder in a family" (the sentence begins on
p. 71 and completes on p. 72).

**The five aims, as printed (p. 72).** This process helps the individual or family:

1. "Comprehend the medical facts, including the diagnosis, the probable course of the disorder,
   and the available management"
2. "Appreciate the way heredity contributes to the disorder and the risk of recurrence in
   specified relatives"
3. "Understand the options for dealing with the risk of recurrence"
4. "Choose the course of action that seems appropriate to them in view of their risk and family
   goals and act in accordance with that decision"
5. "Make the best possible adjustment to the disorder in an affected family member and to the
   risk of recurrence of that disorder"

**Aim 4 contains the whole nondirective principle: the family chooses, in view of their own
goals** (p. 72).

### Estimating risk (p. 72)

"Risks for occurrence or recurrence of a genetic disorder are estimated from a thorough family
history and any known genetic screening information" (p. 72).

| Element | As given (p. 72) |
|---|---|
| What a family history gives | "not only provides a picture of the proband (the affected person, or index case) in relation to other family members, but also may identify others who are similarly affected or who might be presymptomatic or at risk of producing affected children" |
| What analysis gives | "Analyzing the pattern of affected family members can assist in confirming a tentative diagnosis or in determining the level of risk in multifactorial inheritance" |
| **Why the diagnosis must be exact** | "An accurate diagnosis is essential to provide specific risk figures. There are more than 10,000 known inherited disorders, many of which have similar clinical manifestations but different modes of inheritance" |

**The worked example that makes the point (p. 72):** "symptoms in the early stages of severe
X-linked muscular dystrophy appear much like those of the milder autosomal recessive and
autosomal dominant varieties, autosomal recessive neurogenic muscular atrophies, and nongenetic
poliomyelitis. **The significance of the risks related to each type of disorder is readily
apparent.**"

**Four disorders that look alike carry four completely different recurrence risks: 50%, 25%, 50%
for sons of a carrier, and zero.** Getting the diagnosis wrong makes the counseling wrong
(p. 72).

### Communicating risk (p. 72)

**The nondirective stance (p. 72):** "Genetic professionals do not attempt to make
recommendations or decisions for patients when discussing reproductive risks. Instead, they
discuss comprehensive and current information about the nature of the disorder, the extent of
risk, the probable consequences, and alternative solutions. **The goal of this communication
process is to enable patients to make their own decisions**."

**Where recommendations DO belong (p. 72):** "However, genetics professionals recognize that
recommendations do have a place when genetic testing is used to identify risk for diseases or
disorders that have available treatments that can prevent, delay, or improve symptoms." The
example is familial adenomatous polyposis: "genetics professionals recommend specific screening
measures for these children; when polyps are discovered, the professionals recommend a surgical
consultation to discuss options."

**"As with any health care encounter, shared decision making with parents and/or adolescents is
the goal"** (p. 72).

**Nondirective about reproduction, directive about treatable disease.** That is the boundary, and
it is the same distinction that permits APC testing in children while discouraging BRCA testing
(pp. 67, 72).

**How to explain a probability (p. 72):** "It is helpful to explain risks in different ways and to
use examples to aid in understanding the meaning of probabilities. Most people do not have an
adequate knowledge of genetics and human biology to fully comprehend these complex concepts.
Words and concepts that can be used include percentages, chance, odds, and likelihood."

**The worked example (p. 72):** "if a 40-year-old woman has a 1 : 112 risk of having a child with
Down syndrome, other ways to explain it include" telling her she has about a 1% chance, or that
"Out of 112 women your age having a baby, odds are that one of them will have a child with Down
syndrome." "Games of probability can also be used, such as flipping coins, baseball pools, and
lotteries."

**Nursing Alert, the single most quotable line in the chapter (p. 72):** "Families may
misunderstand probability, even when it is fully explained. The nurse should impress on them
that **each pregnancy is an independent event**. Often parents who are told that a recessive
disorder carries a 1 : 4 risk of recurrence incorrectly reason that because they already have
one affected child, the next three will be unaffected. **Chance has no memory; the risk is 1 : 4
for each and every pregnancy.**"

**Chance has no memory.** Box 3.3 says the same thing in its own wording, "in each and every
pregnancy" (p. 53), and the achondroplasia pedigree makes the identical point for dominant
disease (p. 52). **Three separate places in one chapter, which is how you know it will be
asked.**

### Family history and the pedigree (pp. 72, 74–75)

**Why it matters (p. 72):** "Family health history is an important tool to identify individuals
and families at increased risk for disease, risk factors for disease (such as obesity), and
inheritance patterns of diseases. Because of its importance, **all nurses need to be able to
elicit family history information and, when feasible, document the collected information in
pedigree format.**"

**"When eliciting a family health history, nurses should collect information about all family
members within a minimum of three generations"** (p. 72). "This process usually takes 20 to 30
minutes" (p. 74).

| Practical point | As given (p. 74) |
|---|---|
| Who to include | "When possible, it is best to include both parents in the interview to elicit information about relatives on both sides of the family" |
| What to bring | "Medical records, birth and death records, family Bibles, and photograph albums are helpful resources, and persons being interviewed should be instructed to bring such items if they are available" |
| Reliability | "The level of education and the degree of understanding vary widely among informants and influence their reliability" |
| **Reticence** | "The informants may be reticent, particularly if they view the disorder as something to be ashamed of or in some way threatening. Sometimes true relationships may be concealed, such as adoption or misattributed paternity" |

**The interviewing technique, which is genuinely skilled work (p. 74):**

| Technique | As given (p. 74) |
|---|---|
| Language about relationships | "Because the parents may not be married, they should be addressed as couples or partners and asked about other unions that may have produced a pregnancy" |
| **The birth history** | "In eliciting a birth history from the mother and father, the nurse should specifically ask about abortions, miscarriages, and stillbirths, in addition to live births" |
| Reaching everyone | "To identify all members of the family tree, it is best to ask about pregnancies, even of young teenagers" |
| **Asking a second way** | "if a person denies any cognitive impairment in the family, asking other questions, such as about learning problems, being in special education classes, and failing classes or not completing school, may uncover a family history" |

**Ask about pregnancies, not births.** And when the answer is no, ask the same question in
different words (p. 74). Those two techniques are the difference between a blank pedigree and a
useful one.

### Nursing Care Guidelines, pedigree construction (p. 74)

The nine steps, as printed:

1. "Begin diagram in the middle of a large sheet of paper."
2. **"Represent males by a square placed to the left and females by a circle placed to the
   right."**
3. "Represent the proband (index case, original patient) with an arrow (if the counselee or
   patient is different, place a C under that person's symbol)."
4. "Use a horizontal line between a square and a circle for a mating or marriage."
5. **"Suspend offspring vertically from the mating line and place in order of birth with oldest
   to the left (regardless of sex)."**
6. **"Symbolize generations by Roman numerals, with the earliest generation at the top."**
7. "Include three generations: grandparents, parents, offspring, siblings, aunts, uncles, and
   first cousins of proband."
8. "Include name of each person, their age, health problems, and date and cause of death.
   **Because of privacy concerns, include only first names of the family you are
   interviewing.**"
9. "Date the pedigree and update at subsequent visits."

**Square is male and goes on the left; circle is female and goes on the right. Oldest child on
the left. Roman numerals for generations, earliest at the top.** Those four conventions are the
whole notation (p. 74).

**Step 8's privacy instruction and step 9's dating requirement are both nursing actions, not
drafting conventions** (p. 74). A pedigree is a dated clinical document that gets updated.

**Where to start (p. 74):** "Construction of a pedigree begins with the affected child (proband,
index case, or original patient) and all of the mother's pregnancies. Next, the maternal family
history is explored in a similar manner; then information is gathered about relatives on the
father's side, as well as any children or pregnancies that may have occurred through the
father's previous unions."

**Proband first, then mother's side, then father's side** (p. 74).

### What the history must contain (pp. 74–75)

"The nurse solicits information not only about other affected family members but also about (1)
births (live birth, stillbirths, and pregnancy losses, including gestational age of pregnancy);
(2) infertility problems; (3) matings (legally sanctioned, consanguineous, multiple, unwed, and
other complex relationships); (4) health of family members, including any other genetic diseases
or disorders or birth defects; and (5) death and causes of death, including early infant deaths"
(p. 74).

**Place of birth matters (p. 74):** "Sometimes the place of birth and ethnic background are
significant. For example, the carrier frequency for Tay-Sachs disease is higher in Ashkenazi Jews
from Eastern Europe than in Jews from other geographic regions."

**Not every death is genetically relevant (p. 74):** "when a pedigree chart is being evaluated, a
sister's death in infancy from a congenital heart defect might be genetically significant,
whereas a healthy sibling's death in a car accident would not."

**And the priority (p. 75):** "Information concerning first-degree relatives is most important and
should be complete."

### The time barrier and the tool (p. 75)

"Time is recognized as one of the biggest barriers to collecting a detailed family health
history" (p. 75).

| Response | As given (p. 75) |
|---|---|
| The tool | departments within the US Department of Health and Human Services collaborated to launch "the surgeon general's My Family Health Portrait" |
| **The nurse's role** | "Nurses can play an important role in teaching families how to access and use this web-based tool and encourage them to share the collected family history with their health care providers" |
| Interoperability | "The updated version of My Family Health Portrait allows the code to be downloaded so that patients' family histories can be imported into compatible electronic medical records" |
| Language access | "The Genetic Alliance has an online booklet about how to gather family history that is available in English and other languages" |

### The other histories, and dysmorphology (p. 76)

"nurses caring for children and families with, or at risk for, a genetic disorder can further
facilitate a genetic" (p. 75), continuing "evaluation by collecting pregnancy, labor and
delivery, perinatal, medical, and developmental histories" (p. 76).

**The realistic caveat (p. 76):** "It is recognized that time limitations of the pediatric nurse
may limit the amount of assessment data that can be collected during a pediatric encounter.
**Electronic medical records are making it more practical to construct a comprehensive set of
histories, even when many health care professionals contribute only a portion of the total
history.**"

**Dysmorphology, and what nurses are and are not trained for (p. 76):** "All nurses are taught to
perform physical assessments, but they are seldom taught to recognize minor anomalies and
dysmorphology that may suggest a genetic disorder. Yet nurses are keen in recognizing delays in
development, behavior differences, and global appearances that raise concern that a newborn,
infant, child, or adolescent needs further evaluation."

**The language instruction (p. 76):** "Nurses knowledgeable in dysmorphology are able to
articulate specific concerns about a child's appearance rather than relying on the outdated and
offensive phrase" that labels a child as funny-looking.

**Describe the finding; do not label the child.** The chapter names the phrase it wants retired
and explains what replaces it: specific dysmorphology terminology (p. 76).

**The two numeric rules (p. 76):** "When a major anomaly is identified, nurses should raise
suspicion that the child could have additional congenital anomalies. **When three or more minor
anomalies are identified, nurses should suspect the possibility of an underlying syndrome.**"

**And the check on that rule (p. 76):** "it is important to consider the biologic parents' physical
appearance, development, and behavior when considering the relevance of the child's combination
of minor anomalies."

**A child's unusual feature may simply be a family feature.** Look at the parents before calling
a minor anomaly abnormal (p. 76).

## 33. Identification, referral, education, and support (pp. 76–77)

### The nurse's four responsibilities (p. 76)

"It is a nurse's responsibility to learn basic genetic principles, to be alert to situations in
which families could benefit from genetic evaluation and counseling, to know about special
services that can help manage and support affected children, and to be familiar with facilities
in their areas where these services are available" (p. 76).

### Preparing a family for a genetics visit (p. 76)

"Nurses can contact a family before genetic consultation to assess the family's needs and attempt
to reduce their anxiety. The telephone contact can also be used to obtain a family history and
explain the clinic procedures" (p. 76).

**The specific worries families have, as given (p. 76):** "Many families are concerned about such
things as whether they will be required to undress, whether blood is to be drawn, whether they
can accompany the child during the visit, or whether they will be told what to do about
reproduction."

**"Families who know what to expect are able to gain more from a genetics consultation"**
(p. 76).

**One of those four worries is about the counseling itself, not the procedure.** Families fear
being told what to do about reproduction, which is precisely what nondirective counseling does
not do (pp. 72, 76). Saying so in advance is an intervention.

### Why early identification matters (p. 76)

"Early identification of a genetic disorder allows anticipation of associated conditions and
implementation of available preventive measures and therapy to avoid potential complications and
to enhance the child's health. **It may also prevent the unexpected birth of another affected
child in the immediate or extended family**" (p. 76).

**What to hand off with the referral (p. 76):** "nurses should share with the genetics
professional the findings in the histories the nurses collected that triggered the consultation.
Nurses can also help the referral process by determining and communicating the family's initial
concerns, their state of knowledge about the reason for referral, and their attitudes and beliefs
concerning genetics."

**Reevaluation is a legitimate referral (p. 76):** "With so many recent advances in genetic
testing, it is not unusual for a child or adult with long-standing medical problems, including
cognitive impairment, to be referred for reevaluation of his or her condition as a possible
genetic disorder that might not have been diagnosable a few years earlier, such as microdeletion
disorders or single-gene mutations."

**An old undiagnosed condition is worth re-referring.** Microarray and sequencing can now name
what a karyotype could not (pp. 66, 76).

### After the visit (p. 76)

"Regardless of whether families choose to receive continued care with a genetics center, clinic,
or professional, nurses can help patients and families process and clarify the information they
receive during a genetics visit" (p. 76).

**Why they need help (p. 76):** "Misunderstanding of this information can have many causes,
including cultural differences, the disparity of knowledge between the counselor and the family,
and the heightened emotion surrounding genetic counseling. **Family members have difficulty
absorbing all the information presented during a genetics evaluation and counseling session.**"

| The nurse's tasks | As given (p. 76) |
|---|---|
| The summary letter | "genetics professionals write and send clinic summary letters to families. The nurse may need to help the family understand terminology in the letter, help them identify and articulate remaining questions or areas of clarification, and coach them through the process of accessing genetics health professionals to get remaining questions and concerns answered" |
| Repetition | "Information often needs to be repeated several times before the family understands the content and its implications" |
| Before the next visit | "nurses can help parents identify and write down the questions and concerns they need addressed before leaving the clinic" |

### Guilt, and how to address it (p. 76)

**"Nurses must assess for and address parents' feelings of guilt about carrying bad genes or
having made my child sick"** (p. 76).

| Situation | What the nurse can say, as given (p. 76) |
|---|---|
| **Cytogenetic disorder** | "the nurse may be able to absolve the parents of guilt by explaining the random nature of segregation during both gamete formation and fertilization and that those errors in cell division unique to the pregnancy in question are not likely to happen again and are not inherited" |
| **Mendelian or mitochondrial disorder** | "it is important to assess parents' understanding of recurrence risk, help them understand the chances that a subsequent pregnancy will not be affected, and ensure that they have been given information about their options for future children (preimplantation diagnosis, use of donor egg or sperm, prenatal diagnosis, or adoption)" |

**A chromosome error can be genuinely absolved; an inherited mutation cannot, and the
intervention shifts to accurate risk and options** (p. 76). Knowing which conversation you are in
is the skill.

**The false explanations families construct (p. 76):** "Families often try to reason that some
unrelated event caused the abnormality (e.g., a fall, a urinary tract infection, or one glass of
wine) before the mother was aware that she was pregnant. **These misconceptions need to be
assessed and dispelled.**"

**Emphasize the chance of an unaffected pregnancy, not only the recurrence risk** (p. 76). Both
are the same number stated from opposite ends, and the second is the one families can hold.

### The internet, before and after diagnosis (p. 76)

"After a genetics visit, and sometimes before the visit, parents often use the Internet to find
answers to their questions" (p. 76).

**The problem (p. 76):** "During the initial genetics evaluation, a diagnosis may not be
possible" (p. 76). "Diagnoses under consideration are discussed briefly with the parents. Some parents
are satisfied with the brief information and do not care to find out more until the actual
diagnosis is established. Other parents go home and seek as much information as they can about
the diagnoses under consideration. **The information they find can be terrifying and overwhelming
and inaccurate or misleading.**"

**Two nursing actions (p. 76):** "Nurses can play an important role in helping parents identify
reliable, accurate resources for information at whatever time they desire it. **It is also
important to stress that everything that is described for a genetic condition may not be relevant
to their child.**"

**Variable expressivity, used as a counseling tool.** A textbook description is the full range,
not a prediction (pp. 50, 76).

### Living with the diagnosis (p. 77)

"Once a genetics diagnosis is made or a genetic predisposition to a delayed-onset disorder is
identified, nurses need to have frequent contact with patients and families as they attempt to
incorporate recommended therapies or disease-prevention strategies into their daily lives"
(p. 77).

**The two worked examples (p. 77):** PKU "requires conscientious diet management; therefore it is
important to make certain that the family understands and follows instructions **and is able to
navigate the health care system to access the essential formula and low-phenylalanine food
products**." And VCFS, which "requires surgical intervention for the congenital malformations"
plus "early intervention services and eventually an individualized education plan in school."

**Access to the formula is part of the treatment.** Teaching the diet without securing the
products is incomplete care (p. 77).

| Ongoing task | As given (p. 77) |
|---|---|
| Assessment | "Initial and ongoing assessment of the family's coping abilities, resources, and support systems is vital to determine their need for additional assistance and support" |
| Advocacy | "As with any family who has a child with chronic health care needs, nurses must teach the family to become the child's advocate" |
| Resources | "Nurses can help families locate agencies and clinics specializing in a specific disorder or its consequences that can provide services (e.g., equipment, medication, and rehabilitation), educational programs, and parent support groups" |
| **Parent matching, with a condition** | "Referral to local and national support groups or contact with a local family that has a child with the same condition can be helpful for new parents. **Privacy and confidentiality are imperative, and both families must give permission before their contact information is given**" |
| Online groups | "Nurses can also be instrumental in helping parents evaluate disease-specific social media groups and blogs or starting a local support group when none is available" |

**Both families must consent before contact information is shared** (p. 77). Not just the family
being helped.

### Attachment (p. 77)

"Parental attachment and adjustment to the baby can be supported and facilitated by nursing
interventions. Assessing the parents' understanding of the child's disorder and providing simple
and truthful explanations can help them begin to understand their child's health issues. Guiding
the parents in recognizing their child's cues, responses, and strengths can be helpful even for
experienced parents. **A caring attitude conveys the value of their child and, by extension,
their value as parents.** The nurse can help the parents identify their strengths as a family and
identify support that is available to them" (p. 77).

### Van Riper's recommendations (p. 77)

**The framing sentence (p. 77):** "Giving birth to and raising a child with a genetic disorder do
not necessarily result in a lifetime burden. It is important for nurses to ask parents to
describe their experience raising their child with a particular genetic condition. What has been
the impact on their family? **Although parents may initially experience negative outcomes, such
as shock, emotional distress, and grief, families can adapt and thrive.**"

The five recommendations, developed for Down syndrome families and stated to be "useful for
families of children with any type of genetic disorder" (p. 77):

1. "Recognize multiple stressors, strains, and transitions in their lives (e.g., unmet family
   needs)."
2. "Discuss and implement strategies for reducing family demands (e.g., setting priorities and
   reducing the number of outside activities family members are involved in)."
3. "Identify and use individual, family, and community resources (e.g., humor, family
   flexibility, supportive extended family, respite care, local support groups, and Internet
   resources)."
4. "Expand the range and efficacy of their coping strategies (e.g., increase the use of active
   strategies such as reframing, mobilize their ability to acquire and accept help, and decrease
   the use of passive appraisal)."
5. "Encourage the use of an affirming style of family problem-solving communication (e.g., one
   that conveys support and caring and exerts a calming influence)."

**Recommendation 4 names the target directly: more active coping, less passive appraisal**
(p. 77).

### When families do not adapt (p. 77)

"Some families do struggle after learning their child has a genetic disorder. Families may feel
ashamed of a hereditary disorder and seek to blame their partner for transmitting a faulty gene
or chromosome. Intrafamilial strife, hostility, and marital or couple disharmony, sometimes to
the point of family disintegration, can occur" (p. 77).

**The nursing action (p. 77):** "Nurses should be alert for evidence of risk factors that indicate
poor adjustment (e.g., child abuse, divorce, or other maladaptive behaviors). **Referral to
psychosocial professionals for crisis intervention may be necessary.**"

**Child abuse is named as a marker of poor family adjustment to a genetic diagnosis** (p. 77).
That is a screening obligation, not a rare consideration.

## 34. Trap notes

Written against the patterns in the course rulebook. Every one is anchored to a page.

### Confusable pairs

- **Genetics vs. genomics.** *One gene vs. all of them* (pp. 41, 43). Same split for
  pharmacogenetics vs. pharmacogenomics (p. 42).
- **Congenital vs. genetic.** "A congenital disease may or may not be genetic. Likewise, a genetic
  disease may or may not be congenital, although the causative genes are present at birth"
  (p. 41). **Present at birth and inherited are different claims.**
- **Deformation vs. disruption vs. dysplasia vs. malformation.** *Pushed out of shape vs. broken
  down vs. organized wrong vs. never formed* (p. 42). **Cleft lip is a malformation**, and this
  chapter's NCLEX question 1 offers disruption as the trap.
- **Syndrome vs. association vs. sequence.** *One known cause vs. a pattern with no known cause
  vs. one defect causing the rest* (p. 45).
- **Major vs. minor anomaly.** *Serious medical, surgical, or cosmetic consequences vs. no serious
  consequences or a normal variation* (p. 43). A sacral dimple, extra nipple, or café-au-lait spot
  is minor (p. 45).
- **Euploidy vs. aneuploidy.** *A multiple of 23 vs. not a multiple of 23* (p. 45). Triploidy is
  euploid and still lethal.
- **Trisomy vs. monosomy.** *47 vs. 45*, and "The loss of a chromosome and its related complement of genes is
  overall more detrimental than the addition of a chromosome" (p. 45).
- **Mitotic vs. meiotic nondisjunction.** *After fertilization giving mosaicism vs. before
  fertilization giving whole-body aneuploidy* (p. 46).
- **Mosaic Down syndrome vs. mosaic sex chromosome aneuploidy.** The chapter says mosaicism gives
  milder disease for Klinefelter and Turner syndromes (p. 48) but explicitly warns that mosaic
  Down syndrome does "not necessarily have a better developmental outcome" (p. 48). **Do not
  generalize the milder claim to Down syndrome.**
- **The three Down syndrome configurations.** *92% free trisomy, 4% translocation, the rest
  mosaic* (pp. 46, 47, 48), with completely different recurrence implications.
- **Theoretic vs. observed translocation risk.** *33% vs. about 15% from a carrier mother and
  under 10% from a carrier father* (pp. 47, 48).
- **Reciprocal vs. robertsonian translocation.** *Any two chromosomes exchanging fragments vs.
  two acrocentric chromosomes fusing at the centromere* (p. 49). Only 13, 14, 15, 21, and 22 are
  acrocentric.
- **Klinefelter vs. XYY vs. Turner vs. triple-X** (pp. 48, 49). The full table is in §8.
- **Deletion vs. microdeletion.** *Visible on karyotype vs. "too small to be detected by standard
  cytogenetic techniques"* (p. 43). Cri du chat is the first, VCFS the second.
- **Penetrance vs. expressivity.** *Whether it shows at all vs. how much it shows* (p. 50).
- **Dominant carrier vs. recessive carrier.** In autosomal dominant disease "a carrier state does
  not exist" except for a reduced penetrant allele (p. 44); in autosomal recessive disease "A
  carrier state exists. Both males and females can be carriers" (p. 53).
- **Homozygous vs. compound heterozygous.** Both mean two broken copies; compound heterozygous
  means the two mutations differ (p. 53).
- **Autosomal dominant vs. autosomal recessive pedigrees.** *Consecutive generations vs. one
  generation* (pp. 44, 53).
- **X-linked recessive vs. X-linked dominant.** *Mostly males affected vs. both affected with
  females milder*; and an affected father gives **carrier** daughters in recessive disease
  (p. 58) but **affected** daughters in dominant disease (p. 59).
- **Sex-linked vs. X-linked.** The terms are used interchangeably and incorrectly; Y-linked
  inheritance is father-to-son (p. 57).
- **Prader-Willi vs. Angelman.** *Paternal deletion vs. maternal deletion of the same chromosome
  15 region* (p. 60). **Lose the father's copy and you get Prader-Willi.**
- **Genomic imprinting vs. uniparental disomy.** *Which parent's copy is expressed vs. both copies
  coming from one parent* (pp. 59, 60).
- **Mendelian vs. mitochondrial inheritance.** *Both parents contribute vs. mother only*, because
  "Mitochondria are not contained within the sperm head" (p. 60).
- **Oncogene vs. tumor suppressor gene vs. mismatch repair gene.** *Drives growth vs. inhibits
  growth vs. repairs errors* (p. 60).
- **Germline vs. somatic p53 mutation.** *Li-Fraumeni syndrome vs. sporadic cancers such as
  bladder cancer* (p. 60).
- **BRCA1 vs. BRCA2.** *Female breast and ovarian vs. male and female breast, prostate* (p. 60).
- **Presymptomatic vs. susceptibility testing.** *Near-100% penetrance vs. partial penetrance*
  (p. 67). APC is the first, BRCA the second.
- **Empiric vs. theoretic risk.** *Counted from observation vs. calculated from mendelian
  patterns* (p. 72).
- **PKU vs. galactosemia.** Both autosomal recessive, both triggered by milk, both on newborn
  screening. **Breastfeeding is possible in PKU with monitoring (p. 64) and contraindicated in
  galactosemia (p. 66).** Diet largely works for PKU (p. 65) and "does not significantly improve
  the outcome" in galactosemia (p. 66).
- **Screening vs. diagnostic testing.** *Populations vs. individuals* (p. 68), and screen positive
  is not a diagnosis (p. 69).
- **Karyotype vs. microarray vs. FISH.** *Large rearrangements vs. the undiagnosed child with
  anomalies or delays vs. a targeted question about a suspected condition* (p. 66).
- **CVS vs. amniocentesis.** *10 to 12 weeks, chorionic villi, maternal cell contamination risk
  vs. 14 to 18 weeks, amniotic fluid, and AFP measurement* (pp. 70, 71). **Only amniocentesis
  gives amniotic AFP and acetylcholinesterase.**

### Level and stage swaps

- **Recurrence risks by pattern.** Autosomal dominant 50% (p. 44); autosomal recessive 25%
  affected and 50% carrier (p. 53); X-linked recessive 50% of sons affected and 50% of daughters
  carriers (p. 58); X-linked dominant 100% of an affected father's daughters (p. 59);
  multifactorial usually low (<10%) (p. 61).
- **NTD recurrence: 1 in 1000 in the population, 2 or 3 in 100 after one affected child**
  (p. 61).
- **Folic acid: 0.4 mg for all women capable of becoming pregnant, 4.0 mg after an affected
  pregnancy** (p. 67), the latter starting 1 month before conception.
- **Down syndrome mortality: 20% in the first decade, 50% by 60** (p. 46), and Alzheimer disease
  in "more than 75% of those over age 60" (p. 46).
- **Fragile X repeats: about 5 to 40 normal, 59 to 200 premutation, hundreds to thousands full
  mutation** (p. 59).
- **PKU screening timing: initial specimen no later than 7 days, repeat by 2 weeks if the first
  was before 24 hours of age** (p. 63). The general newborn screening rule is different: 24 hours
  after the first protein feeding or within 72 hours of life (p. 69).
- **PKU diet: begin no later than 3 weeks of age, 20 to 30 mg of phenylalanine per kilogram per
  day** (p. 64), and monitor closely "throughout the first 12 years of age" (p. 64).
- **Prenatal test timing: PGD before implantation, CVS 10 to 12 weeks, amniocentesis 14 to 18
  weeks, fetal blood sampling after 18 weeks** (pp. 70, 71).
- **Prenatal testing age indication: 35 at delivery, or 31 with a twin gestation** (p. 70).
- **Galactosemia timeline: normal at birth, symptoms on milk, jaundice by the second week,
  cataracts by 1 to 2 months** (p. 66).
- **Anomaly counting: one major anomaly means look for more; three or more minor anomalies suggest
  a syndrome; two or more Box 3.7 findings raise concern** (pp. 72, 76).
- **Pedigree scope: a minimum of three generations, 20 to 30 minutes** (pp. 72, 74).
- **Newborn screening panel: 29 core and 25 secondary conditions** in the 2006 ACMG report
  (p. 69).
- **First-cousin risk 5% vs. general population 2% to 3%** (p. 74), and congenital anomalies
  overall in "2% to 4% of all live-born children" (p. 42).

### Priority and first-action items

- **Confirm that newborn screening was actually done**, especially for early discharge, home
  birth, NICU (p. 63), and international adoption (p. 64).
- **Document a primary care provider, current address, and phone number on the screening card**
  (p. 63). A positive screen is worthless if the family cannot be reached.
- **Heel blood, not cord blood, and after a protein feeding** (p. 63).
- **Do not layer the blood spot**; spread it uniformly with a pipette (p. 64).
- **After a positive screen, diagnostic testing must be performed promptly** (p. 63), and screen
  positive infants are "immediately referred to a medical center" (p. 69).
- **In galactosemia, stop all milk including breast milk, and monitor for hypoglycemia, liver
  failure, bleeding disorders, and E. coli sepsis** (p. 66).
- **Ask the pharmacist about lactose in every medication for a child with galactosemia** (p. 66).
- **A PKU-rescued woman must resume the diet before conception** (pp. 65, 67).
- **Both families must consent before contact information is exchanged for parent matching**
  (p. 77).
- **Screen for poor family adjustment, including child abuse and divorce, and refer** (p. 77).
- **Teach that each pregnancy is an independent event** (p. 72).

### Plausible-but-adjacent

- **The claim that a genetic disorder always shows a positive family history.** "It is therefore
  erroneous to consider all genetic disorders as having a positive family history" (p. 43).
- **The claim that a normal karyotype rules out a genetic disorder.** Single-gene disorders
  "cannot be detected by chromosome analysis" (p. 50), and microdeletions need microarray or FISH
  (pp. 43, 66).
- **The claim that XYY causes criminal behavior.** The early studies "were significantly flawed by
  selection bias" (p. 48).
- **The claim that mosaicism always means milder disease.** Not for Down syndrome (p. 48).
- **The claim that an affected parent's four children will include exactly two affected.** "50% of
  offspring do not necessarily inherit the gene mutation" (p. 52); "Chance has no memory" (p. 72).
- **The claim that a child with a recessive disease born to one carrier parent proves
  misattributed paternity.** Uniparental disomy can explain it (p. 60).
- **The claim that a negative carrier screen means the couple cannot have an affected child.**
  Fewer than 100 of more than 1000 CFTR mutations are tested (p. 69).
- **The claim that a negative cell free DNA result rules out a chromosome abnormality.** "the
  absence of detection does not rule out the possibility of chromosome abnormalities" (p. 70).
- **The claim that normal prenatal testing guarantees a healthy baby.** "even normal prenatal test
  results do not guarantee the birth of a healthy baby" (p. 70).
- **The claim that a Lyme-style titer can be drawn any time.** Not applicable here, but the
  parallel in this chapter is that **prenatal testing for most Table 3.4 disorders is
  uninformative until the family mutation is known** (p. 53).
- **The claim that carrier children should be tested so the family knows.** "Carrier testing for
  children is generally not recommended because the information is for reproductive purposes"
  (p. 69), and predisposition testing for adult-onset cancer "is discouraged in at-risk children"
  (p. 67).
- **The claim that all predisposition testing in children is wrong.** APC testing in at-risk
  children **is** done, because it changes their screening now (p. 67).
- **The claim that genetic counselors tell families what to do.** They "do not attempt to make
  recommendations or decisions for patients when discussing reproductive risks" (p. 72), though
  they do recommend screening for treatable conditions (p. 72).
- **The claim that treating PKU cures it.** "remain unable to metabolize the
  amino acid and will always exhibit phenylalaninemia" (p. 65).
- **The claim that the PKU diet is harmless.** "Total bone mineral density is considerably lower in
  children who are on a low-phenylalanine diet" (p. 65).
- **The claim that phenylalanine should be eliminated.** It "cannot be totally eliminated because
  it is an essential amino acid in tissue growth" (p. 64).
- **The claim that a child of a PKU-rescued mother is safe if the child does not have PKU.** The
  damage is done by the uterine environment (p. 65).
- **The claim that eliminating galactose fixes galactosemia.** "eliminating sources of galactose
  does not significantly improve the outcome" (p. 66).
- **The claim that cerebral palsy is caused by birth asphyxia.** "Placental abnormalities are
  increasingly being found to be the etiologic factor in neurodevelopmental disorders (such as
  cerebral palsy and cognitive impairment) that were previously attributed to asphyxia during
  delivery" (p. 62).
- **The claim that an oncogene is a foreign or viral gene.** "In reality, oncogenes are normally
  involved in cell growth and division" (p. 60).
- **The claim that a mitochondrial prenatal test predicts severity.** "it is not possible to
  predict the unborn child's phenotype based on prenatal test results" (p. 60).
- **The claim that consanguinity is a concern for any related couple.** "this is usually a concern
  only for first cousins or closer relatives" (p. 74).
- **The claim that an unusual facial feature in a child is necessarily abnormal.** Consider "the
  biologic parents' physical appearance, development, and behavior" first (p. 76).
- **The claim that everything in a textbook description will happen to this child.** "everything
  that is described for a genetic condition may not be relevant to their child" (p. 76).
- **The claim that a genetic diagnosis is a lifetime burden.** "Giving birth to and raising a child
  with a genetic disorder do not necessarily result in a lifetime burden"; "families can adapt and
  thrive" (p. 77).

## 35. The chapter's own NCLEX review questions (pp. 77–78)

Included because the answer key is printed in the source and is internally consistent with the
chapter text. The key as printed: "1. D; 2. B; 3. A, B, C, E; 4. C; 5. B, C, E."

1. **A parent of a child with cleft lip asks whether they caused the defect. Best response?**
   Key: **D.**
   - **D is correct.** It matches the chapter's own account: cleft lip results from an abnormality
     "in this developmental process" at about 5 to 7 weeks, and most malformations occur "before 12
     weeks of gestation" with causes that are often unknown (p. 42). It neither blames nor falsely
     reassures.
   - **B is wrong on the classification.** Cleft lip is given as the example of a **malformation**,
     not a disruption; disruptions "result from the breakdown of previously normal tissue" and the
     chapter's example of one is amniotic band amputation (p. 42).
   - **C is wrong because it assigns blame.** The chapter's instruction is the opposite: parents'
     guilt must be "assessed and addressed," and their invented explanations "need to be assessed
     and dispelled" (p. 76).
   - **A is wrong because it is a bare denial.** It gives the family no mechanism, and the chapter
     asks for "simple and truthful explanations" (p. 77).
2. **What the nurse can report about sex chromosome aneuploidies.** Key: **B.**
   - **B is correct on both halves.** "Klinefelter syndrome is the most common of all sex chromosome
     aneuploidies" and "Mental development is normal in most cases, with a mean full-scale IQ
     between 85 and 90" (p. 48).
   - **A is wrong because it lists the wrong karyotype.** The text's list is "Klinefelter, XYY,
     triple-X female, and Turner syndromes" (p. 48). The option substitutes **XXY**, which is
     Klinefelter itself, so the list names the same disorder twice and omits XYY.
   - **C reverses triple-X.** The text says "delayed menarche and premature menopause" (p. 49); the
     option says premature menarche and delayed menopause.
   - **D reverses Turner syndrome growth.** "There is no prepubertal growth spurt" (p. 48).
3. **What information could the nurse use to further instruct a family considering genetic
   testing? Select all that apply.** Key: **A, B, C, E.**
   - **A is purpose 1 of genetic screening**, quoted almost verbatim from p. 69.
   - **B is the four times screening can occur** (p. 69).
   - **C is purpose 2 of genetic screening** (p. 69).
   - **E is the nurse's contribution to a genetic evaluation:** "collecting pregnancy, labor and
     delivery, perinatal, medical, and developmental histories" (p. 76).
   - **D is wrong as written.** It lists "the parents' siblings, the parents, and the grandparents,"
     which omits the proband's own generation. The chapter's three generations are "grandparents,
     parents, offspring, siblings, aunts, uncles, and first cousins of proband" (p. 74), and
     construction "begins with the affected child" (p. 74). **The option leaves out the child the
     pedigree is about.**
4. **Which statement by parents of an infant with PKU indicates a further need for teaching?**
   Key: **C.**
   - **C is the incorrect statement, and therefore the answer.** The parents say they will return
     for a repeat sample by 4 weeks of age because the first was drawn before 24 hours. The
     chapter's recommendation is "obtaining a subsequent sample **by 2 weeks of age** if the initial
     specimen is collected before the newborn is 24 hours old" (p. 63).
   - **A is correct as stated.** "Because of the low phenylalanine content of breast milk, total or
     partial breastfeeding may be possible with close monitoring of phenylalanine levels" (p. 64).
   - **B is correct as stated.** "early childhood treatment with a reduced phenylalanine diet is
     highly successful at removing the possibility of cognitive impairment" (p. 65).
   - **D is correct as stated.** The diet is "restricted phenylalanine" with "frequent monitoring of
     blood phenylalanine and tyrosine levels" and is lifelong (pp. 64, 65).
5. **Which assessment findings may alert the nurse to a need for further genetic evaluation?
   Select all that apply.** Key: **B, C, E.**
   - **B, C, and E are three of Box 3.7's categories, quoted almost verbatim:** skeletal
     abnormalities including "Limb abnormalities, asymmetry, hyperextensible joints"; "Recurrent
     infection or immunodeficiency" including "Ear infection, pneumonia, poor healing of umbilicus";
     and "Development and speech delays or loss of developmental milestones" (p. 72).
   - **A and D are not in Box 3.7.** Digestive difficulties and urinary tract issues appear nowhere
     in the box's eleven categories (p. 72). **The trap is that both sound like plausible pediatric
     concerns**, which is exactly the plausible-but-adjacent tactic.
   - **Remember the footnote when answering:** "Increased concern for genetic etiology if two or
     more findings are present" (p. 72).
