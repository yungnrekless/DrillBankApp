## 1. Interviewing, setting, and confidentiality (pp. 80–81)

**The setting.** "The place where the nurse conducts the interview is almost as important as the interview itself." Keep distractions minimal, turn off the television or phone, and provide play materials for young children, because "Parents who are constantly interrupted by their children are unable to concentrate fully and tend to give brief answers to finish the interview as quickly as possible."

**Introduction.** Address parents by title ("Mr.," "Mrs.") unless they specify otherwise; using formal address or preferred names "rather than using first names or 'mother' or 'father,' conveys respect." Include the child by asking their name, preferred name, and age: directing every question to the adult "only terminates one extremely valuable source of information — the patient."

**Confidentiality has stated limits.** "If confidentiality is a concern in a particular situation, such as when talking to a parent suspected of child abuse or a teenager contemplating suicide, deal with this directly and inform the person that in such instances, confidentiality cannot be ensured."

**Telephone triage** "is more than 'just a phone call' because a child's life is a high price to pay for poorly managed or incompetent telephone assessment skills." Guidelines include screening questions and determining when to refer to emergency medical services (911) or the emergency department, "same-day appointments, appointments in 24 to 72 hours, appointments in 4 days or more, or home care." Note the finding that matters: "Assessment skills used in direct nurse-to-patient interactions are not directly transferable to the telephone."

*Box 4.1, telephone triage documentation:* date and time; background (name, age, sex, contact information; chronic illness; allergies, current medications, treatments, or recent immunizations); chief complaint; general symptoms (severity, duration, other symptoms, pain); systems review; steps taken, including "Advised to call back if symptoms worsen or fail to improve."

## 2. Communicating with parents (pp. 81–83)

- **Encouraging parents to talk.** "Whatever the parent sees as a problem should be a concern of the nurse." Broad, open-ended questions ("What is Andy eating now?") yield more than single-answer questions ("Is Andy eating what the rest of the family eats?"). "Often all that is required to keep parents talking is a nod or saying 'yes' or 'uh-huh.'"
- **Directing the focus** while allowing maximum freedom of expression "is one of the most difficult goals in effective communication." The technique is open-ended questions followed by guiding statements.
- **Listening** "is the most important component of effective communication." It is "an active process that requires concentration and attention to all aspects of the conversation — verbal, nonverbal, and abstract." **"Major blocks to listening are environmental distraction and premature judgment."**
- **Cultural awareness.** "in Western cultures, eye contact and directness are signs of paying attention. However, in many non-Western cultures, including that of Native Americans, directness (e.g., looking someone in the eye) is considered rude. Children are taught to avert their gaze and to look down when being addressed by an adult, especially one with authority."
- **Cues are often casual.** "Concerns and anxieties are often mentioned in a casual, offhand manner. Even though they are casual, they are important and deserve scrutiny."
- **Silence** "permits the interviewee to sort out thoughts and feelings and search for responses to questions. Silence can also be a cue for the interviewer to go more slowly." Break it with statements such as "Is there anything else you wish to say?" — but note that "Breaking a silence by introducing a new topic or by prolonged talking essentially terminates the interviewee's opportunity to use the silence."
- **Anticipatory guidance** should go "beyond giving general information to empowering families." The three requirements: "Base interventions on needs identified by the family, not by the professional. View the family as competent or as having the ability to be competent. Provide opportunities for the family to achieve competence."

### Box 4.2 — Blocks to communication (p. 82)

This box is two lists, and the split between them is the point: one is what the *nurse* does wrong, the other is how the *patient* signals overload.

| Communication barriers (nurse) | Signs of information overload (patient) |
|---|---|
| Socializing; giving unrestricted and sometimes unsought advice; offering premature or inappropriate reassurance; giving overready encouragement; defending a situation or opinion; using stereotyped comments or clichés; limiting expression of emotion by asking directed, closed-ended questions; interrupting and finishing the person's sentence; talking more than the interviewee; forming prejudged conclusions; deliberately changing the focus | Long periods of silence; wide eyes and fixed facial expression; constant fidgeting or attempting to move away; nervous habits (e.g., tapping, playing with hair); sudden interruptions (e.g., asking to go to the bathroom); looking around; yawning, eyes drooping; frequently looking at a watch or clock; attempting to change the topic of discussion |

The correction named: "One of the best methods for improving interviewing skills is audiotape or videotape feedback."

## 3. Working through an interpreter (p. 83)

*Nursing Care Guidelines, using an interpreter:*
- Explain to the interpreter the reason for the interview and the type of questions that will be asked; clarify whether a detailed or brief answer is required.
- Introduce the interpreter to the family and "allow some time before the interview for them to become acquainted."
- **"Communicate directly with family members when asking questions"** to reinforce interest and observe nonverbal expressions, "but do not ignore interpreter."
- "Pose questions to elicit only one answer at a time, such as 'Do you have pain?' rather than 'Do you have any pain, tiredness, or loss of appetite?'"
- "Refrain from interrupting family members and the interpreter while they are conversing."
- **"Avoid commenting to the interpreter about family members because they may understand some English."**
- Avoid medical jargon: "some medical words, such as allergy, may have no similar word in another language."
- Allow time afterward for the interpreter to share what could not be said earlier and to give impressions of nonverbal clues.
- "Arrange for family to speak with the same interpreter on subsequent visits whenever possible."

**Children as interpreters.** "the use of children in health care interpreting is strongly discouraged because they are often not mature enough to understand health care questions, answers, or messages." Children "may inadvertently commit interpretive errors, such as inaccuracies, omissions, or substitutions," "can be adversely affected by serious or sensitive information," and in some cultures using a child "is considered an insult to an adult because children are expected to show respect by not questioning their elders." Some institutions prohibit it outright. The alternative given: "a language line using a telephonic interpreter."

**Nursing Alert:** "When using translated materials, such as a health history form, be certain the informant is literate in the foreign language."

## 4. Communicating with children (pp. 83–85)

**The governing principle:** "In communication with children of all ages, the nonverbal components of the communication process convey the most significant messages." And the counterintuitive one: **"Active attempts to make friends with children before they have had an opportunity to evaluate an unfamiliar person tend to increase their anxiety."** The move is to keep talking to the parent while doing something that does not involve the child, letting the child observe from a safe position.

*Nursing Care Guidelines, communicating with children:* allow time to feel comfortable; **"Avoid sudden or rapid advances, broad smiles, extended eye contact, or other gestures that may be seen as threatening"**; talk to the parent if the child is shy; communicate through transition objects (dolls, puppets, stuffed animals) before questioning a young child directly; give older children the opportunity to talk without parents present; **"Assume a position that is at the same level as the child"**; speak in a quiet, unhurried, confident voice; be specific and use short sentences; state directions positively; **"Offer a choice only when one exists"**; be honest; allow children to express concerns and fears.

### Communication by stage of thought development

Thought "progress[es] from sensorimotor to perceptual to concrete and finally to abstract, formal operations."

| Stage | What the source says drives communication |
|---|---|
| **Infancy** (p. 84) | Infants "primarily use and understand nonverbal communication." They "become quiet when they are cuddled, rocked, or receive other forms of gentle physical contact" and "receive comfort from the sound of a soft voice even though they do not understand the words." Until stranger anxiety develops they "readily respond to any firm, gentle handling and quiet, calm speech. Loud, harsh sounds and sudden movements are frightening" |
| **Early childhood** (p. 84) | "Children younger than 5 years of age are egocentric... Therefore focus communication on them. Tell them what they can do or how they will feel. Experiences of others are of no interest to them. It is futile to use another child's experience in an attempt to gain the cooperation of small children." They "interpret words literally" and attach literal meaning to phrases such as "two-faced," "sticky fingers," or "coughing your head off"; a child told they will get "a little stick in the arm" may not envision an injection |
| **School-age** (p. 85) | They "rely less on what they see and more on what they know," "want explanations and reasons for everything but require no verification beyond that," and "are interested in the functional aspect of all procedures." They "need to know what is going to take place and why it is being done to them specifically." They have "a heightened concern about body integrity" that extends to possessions, so they "may appear to overreact to loss or threatened loss of treasured objects" |
| **Adolescence** (p. 85) | They "fluctuate between child and adult thinking and behavior" and "when tensions rise, they may seek the security of the more familiar and comfortable expectations of childhood." **"It is as much a mistake to regard an adolescent as an adult with an adult's wisdom and control as it is to assume that the teenager has the concerns and expectations of a child."** Privacy and confidentiality "are of great importance... because it is consistent with developmental maturity and autonomy" |

**Interviewing the adolescent.** Talking with the adolescent first "has the advantage of immediately identifying with the young person"; talking with the parents first "may provide insight into the family relationship." Either way, "give both parties an opportunity to be included," and if time is limited say so at the onset "to avoid appearing to 'take sides.'" Nurses must know state and federal consent and confidentiality laws for "suspected abuse, alcohol or other drug use, suicidal or homicidal ideation, contraceptive care, pregnancy, sexually transmitted infections, and sexual assault."

*Nursing Care Guidelines, communicating with adolescents* — build a foundation: spend time together; encourage expression of ideas and feelings; respect their views; tolerate differences; praise good points; respect privacy; set a good example. Communicate effectively: give undivided attention; "Listen, listen, listen"; be courteous, calm, honest, open minded; "Try not to overreact. If you do, take a break"; avoid judging or criticizing; **"Avoid the 'third degree' of continuous questioning"**; choose important issues when taking a stand.

## 5. Creative communication techniques (Box 4.3, pp. 85–87)

**Verbal**

| Technique | What it is |
|---|---|
| "I" messages | "Relate a feeling about a behavior in terms of 'I.'... Avoid use of 'you.' 'You' messages are judgmental and provoke defensiveness." Contrast: "You are being uncooperative about doing your treatments" vs. "I am concerned about how the treatments are going because I want to see you get better" |
| Third-person technique | Express a feeling as "he/she/they," which "allows children three choices: (1) to agree and, one hopes, express how they feel; (2) to disagree; or (3) to remain silent" |
| Facilitative response | Reflect feelings and content back: "You feel _________ because _________." Responses "are empathic and nonjudgmental and legitimize the person's feelings" |
| Storytelling | "Use the language of children to probe into areas of their thinking while bypassing conscious inhibitions or fears" |
| Mutual storytelling | The nurse retells the child's story with therapeutic differences — "more therapeutic approach than storytelling" |
| Bibliotherapy | Books used therapeutically; the child retells the story, reads a section, draws a picture, talks about characters, or summarizes the moral |
| Dreams | "Dreams often reveal unconscious and repressed thoughts and feelings" |
| "What if" questions | "Encourage child to explore potential situations and to consider different problem-solving options" |
| Three wishes | "If you could have any three things in the world, what would they be?" |
| Rating game | Rate an event or feeling on a scale (numbers, sad-to-happy faces) |
| Word association game | Start with neutral words, "then introduce more anxiety-producing words, such as 'illness,' 'needles,' 'hospitals,' and 'operation'" |
| Sentence completion | Partial statements the child finishes |
| Pros and cons | "five good things and five bad things" about a topic |

**Nonverbal:** writing (journals, unsent letters); **drawing** — "one of the most valuable forms of communication — both nonverbal (from looking at the drawing) and verbal (from the child's story of the picture)"; magic ("no verbal response from the child is required"); and play.

**Guidelines for evaluating drawings** — the interpretive rules, which are exactly the SATA material:
- "Use spontaneous drawings and evaluate more than one drawing whenever possible."
- "Interpret the drawings as a whole rather than focusing on specific details."
- Sex of figure drawn first "usually relates to the child's perception of his or her own sex role"; **size** "expresses importance, power, or authority"; **order drawn** "expresses priority in terms of importance"; **child's position relative to others** "expresses feelings of status or alliance"; **exclusion of a member** "may denote feeling of not belonging or desire to eliminate"; **accentuated parts** express concern (large hands may signal aggression); absent or rudimentary arms and hands "suggest timidity, passivity, or intellectual immaturity"; tiny unstable feet "may express insecurity"; hidden hands "may mean guilt feelings"; free use of paper with firm continuous strokes "express security," while small, lightly drawn, broken or wavering lines "may be signs of insecurity"; erasures, shading, or cross-hatching "expresses ambivalence, concern, or anxiety."

**Play** "is a universal language of children" and requires "minimum equipment or none at all." Peek-a-boo is "an excellent means of initiating communication with infants while maintaining a 'safe,' nonthreatening distance." Through play — needle play especially — "children reveal their perceptions of interpersonal relationships."

## 6. The pediatric health history (pp. 86–92)

**Direct beats indirect.** The format "may be (1) direct, in which the nurse asks for information via direct interview with the informant, or (2) indirect, in which the informant supplies the information by completing some type of questionnaire. **The direct method is superior**," but if time forces the indirect route, "review parents' written responses and question them regarding any unusual answers."

**Box 4.4 outline:** identifying information; chief complaint; present illness; past history (birth history; previous illnesses, injuries, surgeries; allergies; current medications; immunizations; growth and development; habits); review of systems; family medical history; psychosocial history; sexual history; family history; nutritional assessment.

**Chief complaint.** Elicit with "open-ended, neutral questions (e.g., 'What seems to be the matter?,' 'How may I help you?,' or 'Why did you come here today?'). **Avoid labeling-type questions** (e.g., 'How are you sick?' or 'What is the problem?')." If the parent names many problems, ask "which one problem or symptom prompted them to seek help now."

### Analyzing a symptom: pain (p. 89)

The five components, which map directly onto how symptom questions are written:

| Component | What the source says to do |
|---|---|
| **Type** | With young children, ask parents how they know the child is in pain. Help older children by asking "if it is sharp, throbbing, dull, or stabbing. **Record whatever words they use in quotes**" |
| **Location** | "'Stomach pains' is too general." Have the child "point with one finger to where it hurts" or "point to where mommy or daddy would put a Band-Aid." For radiation: "Does the pain stay there or move? Show me with your finger where the pain goes" |
| **Severity** | "best determined by finding out how it affects the child's usual behavior. Pain that prevents a child from playing, interacting with others, sleeping, and eating is most often severe." Use a rating scale (see Chapter 5) |
| **Duration** | Include duration, onset, and frequency, described "in terms of activity and behavior" |
| **Influencing factors** | "(1) precipitating events..., (2) relieving events..., (3) temporal events..., (4) positional events (standing, sitting, lying down), and (5) associated events (meals, stress, coughing)" |

### The past history components

- **Birth history** matters "Because prenatal influences have significant effects on a child's physical and emotional development." Approach parental acceptance of the pregnancy "through indirect questioning," since asking whether the pregnancy was planned "is a leading statement because they may respond affirmatively for fear of criticism."
- **Previous illnesses.** Parents recall serious problems, so ask specifically about "colds, earaches, and childhood diseases such as measles, rubella..., chickenpox, mumps, pertussis..., diphtheria, tuberculosis, scarlet fever, strep throat, recurrent ear infections, gastroesophageal reflux, tonsillitis, or allergic manifestations."
- **Allergies. Nursing Alert:** "Information about allergic reactions to drugs or other products is essential. Failure to document a serious reaction places the child at risk if the agent is given." The allergy history asks what was taken, the reaction, route, how soon after starting, how long ago, "Did anyone tell you it was an allergic reaction, or did you decide for yourself?", whether it was taken again, and whether providers were told.
- **Current medications:** include "vitamins, antipyretics (especially aspirin), antibiotics, antihistamines, decongestants, nutritional supplements, or herbs, essential oils, and homeopathic medications," with name, dose, route, schedule, duration, and reason. Ask parents to bring containers; a pharmacy list "will not include over-the-counter medications, which are important to know."
- **Immunizations:** record "(1) the name of the specific disease, (2) the number of injections, (3) the dosage..., (4) the date when administered, and (5) the occurrence of any reaction." "Children should be screened for contraindications and precautions before every vaccine is administered."
- **Growth and development:** birth measurements, growth chart patterns and deviations, and milestones — age of holding head steadily, sitting alone without support, walking without assistance, saying first words with meaning, achieving bladder and bowel control, present grade and school performance, whether the child has a best friend. Use specific questions, because "'sitting up' can mean many different activities," and **"A clue to misunderstanding of the requested activity may be an unusually early age of achievement."**
- **Habits (Box 4.5):** behavior patterns (nail biting, thumb sucking, pica, rituals, head banging, rocking, overt masturbation, walking on toes); activities of daily living (sleep and waking hours, naps, exercise, regularity of stools and urination, age of toilet training, bedwetting); unusual disposition and response to frustration; use or abuse of alcohol, drugs, caffeine, or tobacco.
- **Chemical use questioning.** "Many kids your age are experimenting with drugs and alcohol; have you ever had any drugs or alcohol?" gives more reliable data than "How much do you drink?" If the youngster denies use, "You mean you never tried to smoke or drink?" implies the nurse expects some activity and may prompt a truthful answer.

**Sexual history language** (pp. 90–91): "avoid asking whether the adolescent is 'sexually active' because this term is broadly defined. **'Are you having sex with anyone?' is probably the most direct and best understood question.**" Because same-sex experimentation may occur, "refer to all sexual contacts in nongender terms, such as 'partners,' rather than 'girlfriends' or 'boyfriends.'"

## 7. Family assessment (pp. 91–93)

**When a comprehensive assessment is indicated** (Nursing Care Guidelines): children receiving comprehensive well-child care; children experiencing major stressful life events (chronic illness, disability, parental divorce, death of a family member); children requiring extensive home care; children with developmental delays; **"Children with repeated accidental injuries and those with suspected child abuse"**; children with behavioral or physical problems that could be caused by family dysfunction.

During brief contacts a full assessment is not appropriate; "screening with one or two questions from each category may reflect the health of the family system."

**Structural areas:** family composition; home and community environment (dwelling, sleeping arrangements, utilities, safety features such as smoke and carbon monoxide detectors and car restraints, environmental hazards, access to health care and schools); occupation and education; cultural and religious traditions.

**Functional areas:** family interactions and roles; power, decision making, and problem solving — where "Chief concern is clarity of boundaries of power between parents and children," assessed by offering "a hypothetical conflict or problem, such as a child failing school"; communication; expression of feelings and individuality.

## 8. Nutritional assessment (pp. 92–98)

Evaluate whether the child "is malnourished, at risk for becoming malnourished, well nourished with adequate reserves, or overweight or obese." Analyze the food diary against MyPlate, and read it "in terms of the family's ethnic practices and financial resources" — encouraging more meat "is not always feasible for families on a limited budget and may conflict with food practices that use meat sparingly."

Laboratory work: "The most common laboratory studies to assess for undernutrition are hemoglobin, red blood cell indices, and serum albumin or prealbumin. For obese children, fasting serum glucose, lipids, and liver function studies may be performed."

A caution about interpretation: do not assume a dislike from an absence — "if the list includes no vegetables, inquire about this rather than assuming that the child dislikes vegetables because it is possible that none were served that day."

## 9. Approaching the physical examination (p. 98)

**Why the sequence changes for children.** The head-to-toe order is kept for *recording*, but the examination itself is reordered by developmental and chronologic age to: "Minimize stress and anxiety associated with assessment of various body parts; Foster a trusting nurse-child-parent relationship; Allow for maximum preparation of the child; Preserve the essential security of the parent-child relationship, especially with young children; Maximize the accuracy and reliability of assessment findings."

**Signs the child is ready to cooperate:** "Talking to the nurse; Making eye contact; Accepting the offered equipment; Allowing physical touching; Choosing to sit on examining table rather than parent's lap."

If those signs are absent: talk to the parent while "essentially 'ignoring' child"; make complimentary remarks; tell a funny story or play a simple magic trick; use a hand puppet as a nonthreatening "friend."

If the child refuses: assess the reason ("a child who is unduly afraid may have had a traumatic experience"); involve child and parent; **"Avoid prolonged explanations about examining procedure"**; use a firm, direct approach; perform the examination as quickly as possible; have an attendant gently restrain; limit the number of people in the room.

Other rules: "If several children in the family will be examined, begin with the most cooperative child to model desired behavior." "Examine painful areas last." "In emergency situation, examine vital functions (airway, breathing, and circulation) and injured area first." The **paper-doll technique** — tracing the child's outline on paper and demonstrating on the drawing first — is used both to prepare and to teach.

## 10. Growth measurements (pp. 98–105)

**Which chart.** "the World Health Organization growth standards be used to monitor growth for infants and children between the ages of 0 and 2 years old," because they "reflect growth patterns among children who were predominately breastfed." **"The Centers for Disease Control and Prevention growth charts... are used for children 2 years old and older."**

**Growth that is questionable:**
- "Children whose height and weight percentiles are widely disparate (e.g., height in the 10th percentile and weight in the 90th percentile...)"
- "Children who fail to follow the expected growth velocity in height and weight"
- "Children who show a sudden increase (except during normal puberty) or decrease in a previously steady growth pattern (i.e., **crossing two major percentile lines after 3 years old**)"
- "Children who are short in the absence of short parents"

The interpretive principle: "the most reliable evaluation lies in comparing growth measurements over time because they reflect change," and "same-age children (whether short, average, or tall in stature) should grow at similar rates."

**Length vs. height.** Measure **recumbent length until 24 months** (or 36 months if using the birth-to-36-months chart) using a length board, extending the body by "(1) holding the head in midline, (2) grasping the knees together gently, and (3) pushing down on the knees until the legs are fully extended." **"A tape measure should not be used to measure the length of infants and children because the methods are inaccurate and unreliable."** For height use a wall-mounted stadiometer.

**Nursing Alert:** "Normally height is less if measured in the afternoon than in the morning," so serial measurements should be taken at the same time of day.

**Weight.** Scales measure "to the nearest 10 g (0.35 oz) for infants and 100 g (0.22 lb) for children." Balance at 0 first. "When precise measurements are necessary, two nurses should take the weight independently; if there is a discrepancy, take a third reading and use the mean of the measurements in closest agreement." Weigh nude when using birth-to-2-year or birth-to-36-month charts. Safety: keep a hand slightly above the infant, or stand close to a toddler, "ready to prevent a fall."

**BMI.** `BMI = weight in kilograms ÷ (height in meters)²`, or `BMI = [weight in pounds ÷ (height in inches × height in inches)] × 703`.

**Nursing Alert, BMI-for-age and gender categories:** "underweight (<5th percentile), healthy weight (5th to <85th percentile), overweight (≥85th percentile and <95th percentile), or obese (≥95th percentile)."

**Skinfold thickness** sites: "triceps (most practical for routine clinical use), subscapular, suprailiac, abdomen, and upper thigh"; record "the average of at least two measurements of one site." **Arm circumference** midpoint is found by measuring "from the acromial process to the olecranon process; half the measured length is the midpoint."

**Head circumference.** Measure "in children up to 36 months of age and in any child whose head size is questionable," at "its greatest frontooccipital circumference, usually slightly above the eyebrows and pinna of the ears and around the occipital prominence." Use paper or nonstretchable tape "because a cloth tape can stretch and give a falsely small measurement." **"Generally, head and chest circumferences are equal at about 1 to 2 years of age. During childhood, chest circumference exceeds head size by about 5 to 7 cm (2 to 2.75 inches)."**

## 11. Physiologic measurements (pp. 105–110)

**Order for infants:** "count respirations first (before the infant is disturbed), take the pulse next, and measure temperature last." If vital signs cannot be taken without disturbing the child, "record the child's behavior (e.g., crying) along with the measurement."

*Atraumatic Care:* preschoolers "fear intrusive procedures because of their poorly defined body boundaries," so avoid rectal temperatures where possible, and **"avoid using the word 'take' when measuring vital signs because young children interpret words literally and may think that their temperature or other function will be taken away. Instead, say, 'I want to know how warm you are.'"**

### Temperature

Normal ranges as given: "For rectal temperatures in children, a value of 37° to 37.5° C (98.6° to 99.5° F) is an acceptable range... For neonates, a core body temperature between 36.5° and 37.6° C (97.7° and 99.7° F) is a desirable range." "The pulmonary artery is the closest to the hypothalamus and best reflects the core temperature."

**Table 4.3 technique by site** (verified against the printed image table `chp00004_t0020`; the printed table is **two columns** — site and technique, plus a photograph — so nothing is missing, see the GAP register):

| Site | Technique and cautions |
|---|---|
| Oral | "Place tip under tongue in right or left posterior sublingual pocket, not in front of tongue." Have the child keep the mouth closed without biting the thermometer. **"Pacifier thermometers measure intraoral or supralingual temperature and are available but lack support in the literature."** Affected by "Eating and mastication, hot or cold beverages, open-mouth breathing, and ambient temperature" |
| Axillary | "Place tip under arm in center of axilla and keep close to skin, not clothing." Lowered by poor peripheral perfusion; raised by clothing or swaddling, radiant warmer, or brown fat in a cold-stressed neonate. "Advantage: Avoids intrusive procedure and eliminates risk of rectal perforation" |
| Ear based (aural) | "Insert small infrared probe deeply into canal to allow sensor to obtain measurement from tympanic membrane." "Size of probe (most are 8 mm) may influence accuracy of result. In young children, this may be a problem because of small diameter of canal." **"Proper placement of ear is controversial related to whether the pinna should be pulled in manner similar to that used during otoscopy."** |
| Rectal | "Place well-lubricated tip at maximum **2.5 cm (1 inch) into rectum for children and 1.5 cm (0.6 inch) for infants**"; hold securely. Side-lying, supine, or prone; "cover penis because procedure may stimulate urination." "A small child may be placed prone across parent’s lap." |
| Temporal artery | Infrared probe scans across the forehead. "Temporal artery is only artery close enough to skin's surface to provide access for accurate temperature measurement" |

**The evidence summary (Translating Evidence into Practice), which is where the testable rules live:**
- **Rectal "remains the clinical gold standard,"** but is invasive and **"contraindicated for infants younger than 1 month old due to risk of rectal perforation."** Also avoid with recent rectal surgery, diarrhea, anorectal lesions, or chemotherapy.
- **Oral** is "the standard for noninvasive temperature measurement" but contraindicated in children with altered level of consciousness, receiving oxygen, mouth breathing, with mucositis, recent oral surgery or trauma, **under 5 years of age**, or unable to hold the thermometer.
- **Axillary** "is inconsistent and insensitive in infants and children over 1 month old"; usable "as a screening tool for fever in young, clinically stable infants and children."
- **Ear (aural)** "is not a precise measurement of body temperature"; tympanic thermometry "is used for screening purposes, but diagnosis of fever without a focus should not be made based on tympanic thermometry."
- **Temporal artery** "is not predictable for fever in young children"; with proper use it screens for fever under 38° C (100.4° F) in children 3 months to 4 years.
- **The bottom line as printed:** "No single site used for temperature assessment provides unequivocal estimates of core body temperature... When an accurate method for obtaining a correct reflection of core temperature is needed, the rectal temperature is recommended in younger children and the oral route in older children. **For infants younger than 1 month of age, axillary temperatures are recommended for screening.**"

**Nursing Alert:** "The belief that core temperature can be estimated by adding 1° F to the temperature taken in the axilla is incorrect. **Do not add a degree** to the finding obtained by taking a temperature by the axillary route."

### Pulse and respiration

"A satisfactory pulse can be taken radially in children older than 2 years of age. However, **in infants and young children, the apical impulse... is more reliable**." **"Count the pulse for 1 full minute in infants and young children because of possible irregularities in rhythm."** For greater accuracy measure the apical rate while the child is asleep. **"Compare radial and femoral pulses at least once during infancy to detect the presence of circulatory impairment, such as coarctation of the aorta."**

**Table 4.4, grading of pulses** (p. 107):

| Grade | Description |
|---|---|
| 0 | "Not palpable" |
| +1 | "Difficult to palpate, thready, weak, easily obliterated with pressure" |
| +2 | "Difficult to palpate, may be obliterated with pressure" |
| +3 | "Easy to palpate, not easily obliterated with pressure (normal)" |
| +4 | "Strong, bounding, not obliterated with pressure" |

**Respiration:** "in infants, observe abdominal movements because respirations are primarily diaphragmatic. Because the movements are irregular, count them for 1 full minute for accuracy."

### Blood pressure

**"Blood pressure (BP) should be measured annually in children 3 years of age through adolescence"** and in children with hypertension symptoms, in emergency departments and intensive care units, and in high-risk infants. **"Auscultation remains the recommended method,"** with automated devices preferred "for BP measurement in newborns and young infants, in whom auscultation is difficult, and in the intensive care setting."

Oscillometric readings "are generally higher (10 mm Hg higher) than measurements using auscultation," and the two "prevent the interchange of the readings by the two methods." **"An elevated BP reading obtained with an oscillometric device should be repeated using auscultation."**

**Cuff selection — the most important factor.** "the most important factor in accurately measuring BP is the use of an appropriately sized cuff (cuff size refers only to the inner inflatable bladder, not the cloth covering)." Choose "a cuff with a bladder width that is approximately **40% of the arm circumference** midway between the olecranon and the acromion," which usually gives "a cuff bladder length that covers **80% to 100% of the circumference** of the arm."

**The direction of error, which is what gets tested:** **"If the cuff size is too small, the reading on the device is falsely high. If the cuff size is too large, the reading is falsely low."**

*Nursing Alerts:* "When the correct size is not available, **use an oversized cuff rather than an undersized one**"; "Do not choose a cuff based on the name of the cuff (e.g., an 'infant' cuff may be too small for some infants)"; and "Compare blood pressure in the upper and lower extremities to detect abnormalities, such as coarctation of the aorta, in which the lower extremity pressure is less than the upper extremity pressure."

Normally, "systolic BP in the lower extremities (thigh or calf) is greater than that in the upper extremities, and systolic BP in the calf is higher than that in the thigh."

**Interpretation by percentile** (p. 109): normotensive "if the BP is below the 90th percentile"; **90th to 95th percentile = prehypertension**; **at or above the 95th percentile = possible hypertension**, requiring repeat "on at least two occasions to confirm diagnosis." "if an adolescent's BP is more than 120/80 mm Hg, consider the patient prehypertensive, even if this value is below the 90th percentile." Staging: stage 1 is "95th to 99th percentile plus 5 mm Hg" (repeat on two more occasions); stage 2 is ">99th percentile plus 5 mm Hg" (prompt referral).

**Nursing Alert:** "Published norms for blood pressure are valid only if you use the same method of measurement (auscultation and cuff size determination) in clinical practice."

**Orthostatic hypotension.** Measure "with the child first supine and then standing (**at least 2 minutes in each position**)." The diagnostic criterion: "The child with a sustained drop in systolic pressure of more than **20 mm Hg** or in diastolic pressure of more than **10 mm Hg** after standing for 2 minutes **without an increase in heart rate of more than 15 beats/min** most likely has an autonomic deficit. Nonneurogenic causes of OH have a compensatory increase in pulse of more than 15 beats/min, as well as a drop in BP." The most common cause is hypovolemia, "which may be induced by medications such as diuretics, vasodilator medications, and prolonged immobility or bed rest."

## 13. Vision and hearing screening (pp. 114, 119)

**Vision.** "The US Preventive Services Task Force (2011) recommends vision screening for the presence of amblyopia and its risk factors **for all children 3 to 5 years of age**."

- **Binocularity** develops "by the age of 3 to 4 months."
- **Strabismus** left uncorrected causes the brain to suppress the weak eye's image; **"If strabismus is not detected and corrected by 4 to 6 years old, blindness from disuse, known as amblyopia, may result."**
- **Corneal light reflex (Hirschberg) test:** shine a light into the eyes "from a distance of about 40.5 cm (16 inches)." Symmetric light in each pupil is normal; off-center light in one eye means misalignment.
- **Cover test:** cover one eye and watch the uncovered eye while the child looks at a near (33 cm) or distant (6 m) object. "If the uncovered eye does not move, it is aligned."
- **Alternate cover test:** shift occlusion back and forth and watch the just-uncovered eye. "If normal alignment is present, shifting the cover from one eye to the other will not cause the eye to move."
- **Pseudostrabismus:** epicanthal folds, "often found in Asian children," can mimic misalignment while the corneal light reflexes stay symmetric.

**Nursing Alert, red reflex:** "A brilliant, uniform red reflex is an important sign because it rules out many serious defects of the cornea, aqueous chamber, lens, and vitreous chamber. Any dark shadows or opacities are recorded because they indicate some abnormality." A white reflex "indicates possible retinoblastoma."

**Table 4.6 — eye examination guidelines, the referral criteria (p. 115).** Read from the printed image table `chp00004_t0035`. Recommended tests are the same at both ages: Snellen letters, Snellen numbers, Tumbling E, HOTV, and the picture tests (Allen figures, LEA symbols) for acuity; cross cover test at 10 feet, Random dot E stereo test at 18 inches (40 cm), and the simultaneous red reflex (**Bruckner**) test for alignment; red reflex for media clarity.

Four rules the table states in its comments column:

1. **"Tests are listed in decreasing order of cognitive difficulty; highest test that child is capable of performing should be used"** — in general Tumbling E or HOTV for 3-5 year olds, Snellen letters or numbers from 6 years.
2. **"Testing distance of 10 feet (3 m) is recommended for all visual acuity tests."**
3. **"Line of figures is preferred over single figures."**
4. The nontested eye is covered by an occluder or adhesive patch, and **"examiner must ensure that it is not possible to peek with nontested eye."**

**Two-line difference is referable on its own**, even when both eyes pass. That is the trap: a child reading 20/20 in one eye and 20/30 in the other has two passing eyes and still gets referred.

**Hearing.** The AAP "recommends pure tone audiometry testing screening at **500, 1000, 2000, and 4000 Hz**, with children **failing if they cannot hear the tones at 20 dB**." "Universal newborn hearing screening is available in all US states."

## 14. Chest and heart (pp. 122, 125)

**Breath sound classification** (p. 122): **vesicular** over the whole lung surface except the upper intrascapular area and beneath the manubrium, with inspiration "louder, longer, and higher pitched than expiration" and "a soft, swishing noise"; **bronchovesicular** over the manubrium and upper intrascapular regions "where trachea and bronchi bifurcate," with inspiration "louder and higher pitched than in vesicular breathing"; **bronchial** sounds as the third class.

**Heart sounds** (p. 125): S1 "is caused by closure of the tricuspid and mitral valves (sometimes called the atrioventricular valves)"; S2 "is the result of closure of the pulmonic and aortic valves (sometimes called semilunar valves)." "Normally the split of the two sounds in S2 is distinguishable and widens during inspiration. **Physiologic splitting is a significant normal finding.**" S3 "is normally heard in some children. S4 is rarely heard as a normal heart sound; it usually indicates the need for further cardiac evaluation."

**Nursing Alert:** "**Fixed splitting**, in which the split in S2 does not change during inspiration, **is an important diagnostic sign of atrial septal defect**."

**Table 4.9, auscultation sites:** aortic area, "Second right ICS close to sternum" (S2 louder than S1); pulmonic area, "Second left ICS close to sternum" (splitting of S2 heard best, "normally widens on inspiration"); Erb's point, "Third left ICS close to sternum" ("Frequent site of innocent murmurs"); tricuspid area, "Fourth right and left ICSs close to sternum"; mitral or apical area, "Fifth ICS, LMCL (fourth ICS and lateral to LMCL in infants)" where S1 is loudest.

The mnemonics as printed: S3 "sounds like word S1 S2 S3: 'Ken-tuc-ky'"; S4 "sounds like word S4 S1 S2: 'Ten-nes-see.'" Use "both diaphragm and bell chest pieces when auscultating heart sounds. Bell chest piece is necessary for low-pitched sounds of murmurs, S3, and S4."

*Nursing Tip:* "To distinguish between S1 and S2 heart sounds, simultaneously palpate the carotid pulse with the index and middle fingers and listen to the heart sounds; **S1 is synchronous with the carotid pulse**."

## 15. Developmental assessment (pp. 132–135)

"One of the most essential components of a complete health appraisal is assessment of developmental function." Screening identifies "children whose developmental level is below normal for their age and who therefore require further evaluation," and **"The earlier that developmental problems are identified and treated, the more likely the child can reach his or her potential."**

The AAP "does not endorse any specific developmental screening tool, but recommends **a multidomain tool that addresses fine and gross motor skills, language and communication, problem solving/adaptive behavior, and personal-social skills**."

**Ages & Stages Questionnaires:** ASQ-3 for developmental screening, ASQ:SE2 for social-emotional. Age range "1-66 months for ASQ-3, 1-72 months for ASQ:SE2." Parents or caregivers answer age-specific questions about everyday skills, and "Children whose development appears to fall significantly below results of other children their age are flagged for further evaluation."

## 16. Trap notes

### Confusable pairs

- **Empathy vs. sympathy.** *Understanding another's experience from within their frame of reference* is empathy; *having similar feelings yourself* is sympathy. The book defines them against each other on the same page, which is the shape of the item.
- **Length vs. height.** Length is recumbent, measured on a length board, **until 24 months** (or 36 on the birth-to-36-month chart); height is standing on a stadiometer. A distractor using a tape measure for an infant is wrong twice over — the book calls tape measurement "inaccurate and unreliable."
- **WHO vs. CDC growth charts.** WHO for **0 to 2 years** (breastfed reference); CDC for **2 years and older**. Distractors swap the ages.
- **Radial vs. apical pulse.** Radial is satisfactory **older than 2 years**; apical is more reliable in infants and young children. Both counted a **full minute** in infants and young children.
- **Cuff too small vs. too large.** Too small reads **falsely high**; too large reads **falsely low**. If the right size is missing, err **oversized**. This is the single most swapped pair in the chapter.
- **Physiologic vs. fixed splitting of S2.** Physiologic splitting *widens on inspiration* and is normal. Fixed splitting *does not change* with inspiration and signals **atrial septal defect**.
- **S3 vs. S4.** S3 is "normally heard in some children" (Kentucky); S4 "is rarely heard as a normal heart sound" and prompts further evaluation (Tennessee).
- **Vesicular vs. bronchovesicular.** Vesicular = most of the lung surface, soft swishing, inspiration longer. Bronchovesicular = manubrium and upper intrascapular region where the trachea and bronchi bifurcate.
- **Strabismus vs. amblyopia.** Strabismus is the *misalignment*; amblyopia is the *vision loss from disuse* that follows if it is not corrected by 4 to 6 years.
- **True strabismus vs. pseudostrabismus.** In pseudostrabismus the corneal light reflexes are still **symmetric**; epicanthal folds create the illusion. An option that diagnoses strabismus from appearance alone, without a light reflex or cover test, is the distractor.
- **Cover test vs. alternate cover test.** Cover test watches the **uncovered** eye; alternate cover test watches the eye **as the cover is removed**.
- **Prehypertension vs. hypertension.** 90th–95th percentile is prehypertension; at or above the 95th may be hypertension. In adolescents, ≥120/80 is prehypertension **even below the 90th percentile**.
- **Direct vs. indirect history format.** Direct = nurse interviews; indirect = questionnaire. "The direct method is superior."
- **Chief complaint vs. present illness.** The chief complaint is the specific reason for the visit; the present illness is the narrative from onset to now, with four components (onset, interval history, present status, reason for seeking help now).
- **Spontaneous vs. directed drawing/play.** Spontaneous = supplies plus opportunity; directed = a specific instruction or purposeful materials.

### Level, stage, and threshold swaps

- **The examination sequence is reordered by age, but always recorded head to toe.** An option saying the recording order changes is wrong.
- **Traumatic procedures last** in infants and toddlers — eyes, ears, mouth — and the **Moro reflex last of all** in the infant.
- **Equipment handling is stage-specific:** toddler inspects but demonstration "is usually ineffective"; preschooler gets a brief demonstration; school-age child gets the purpose explained; adolescent gets the findings explained.
- **Screening ages:** vision screening 3 to 5 years; BP annually from age 3; head circumference to 36 months; audiologic evaluation by 3 months.
- **Temperature route is age-gated.** Rectal is the gold standard but **contraindicated under 1 month**; oral is contraindicated **under 5 years**; axillary is the recommended **screening** route under 1 month.
- **BMI categories:** <5th underweight, 5th to <85th healthy, ≥85th to <95th overweight, ≥95th obese. A distractor placing obesity at the 85th is the classic error.
- **Crossing two major percentile lines is only a concern after 3 years old.**
- **Orthostatic criteria:** systolic drop >20 or diastolic drop >10 after 2 minutes standing, *without* a pulse rise >15 beats/min, points to an autonomic deficit; *with* a pulse rise >15, the cause is nonneurogenic.

### Plausible-but-adjacent facts

- **Do not add a degree to an axillary temperature.** The belief is named and rejected explicitly.
- **Making friends with a young child right away increases anxiety.** The correct move is to ignore the child initially and talk with the parent.
- **Broad smiles and extended eye contact are listed as potentially threatening** to a child, not as reassuring.
- **Skin color, thickness, and edema do not prevent turgor assessment, but erythema in dark skin does require palpation for warmth or edema** rather than inspection.
- **Petechiae are ≤2 mm** and "can denote some type of blood disorder, such as leukemia" — not simply bruising.
- **A single palmar crease** is associated with conditions such as Down syndrome.
- **Compare radial and femoral pulses once in infancy** and compare upper with lower extremity BP: both are coarctation screens.
- **Oscillometric readings run about 10 mm Hg higher** than auscultated ones and are not interchangeable; an elevated automated reading must be repeated by auscultation.
- **Children are not acceptable interpreters**, even when bilingual and willing.
- **An unusually early reported milestone is a clue that the question was misunderstood**, not a clue to advanced development.

### Priority and first-action items

- **Infant vital signs: respirations first, pulse next, temperature last** — least disturbing to most.
- **Emergency examination: airway, breathing, circulation and the injured area first**, sequence rules suspended.
- **Painful areas last**, always.
- **If several siblings are to be examined, start with the most cooperative one** to model behavior.
- **Elevated automated BP → repeat by auscultation.** BP over the 90th percentile → repeat twice in the same encounter and average.
