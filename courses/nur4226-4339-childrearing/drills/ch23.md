Topic: fluid physiology and maintenance requirements in children

Q: A nursing student asks why infants become dehydrated so much faster than adults. Which explanation reflects the chapter's physiology?
A) A far larger share of the infant's total body water sits in the extracellular compartment, which is the compartment lost first
B) Infants have less total body water than adults, so a given loss represents a larger fraction
C) The infant's intracellular compartment is disproportionately large and empties rapidly
D) Infants concentrate urine more efficiently, which accelerates free water loss
Answer: A
Rationale: Total body water falls from roughly 78% of body weight at birth to about 60% by the end of the first year, and the fall is almost entirely extracellular — about 45% at birth to roughly 25% at 1 year and 17% in adults — while intracellular water stays comparatively flat (Fig. 23.2, read from the artwork).
Trap: (B) inverts the proportion, since infants have *more* total body water than adults, not less; (D) reverses renal function, since immature kidneys concentrate urine *less* efficiently, which is a separate reason infants tolerate fluid loss poorly.

Q: A 15-kg child requires maintenance intravenous fluid. What is the daily volume?
A) 1250 ml
B) 1500 ml
C) 1000 ml
D) 1650 ml
Answer: A
Rationale: The chapter's maintenance formula is 100 ml/kg for the first 10 kg, then 1000 ml plus 50 ml/kg for each kilogram from 11 to 20 kg, then 1500 ml plus 20 ml/kg above 20 kg (Table 23.1, p. 737) — so 1000 ml for the first 10 kg plus 50 ml for each of the next 5 kg gives 1250 ml.
Trap: (C) stops at the first band and forgets the increment; (B) jumps to the 20-kg baseline, which applies only above 20 kg. The bands are cumulative, and that is where the arithmetic goes wrong.

Q: A child has a fever of 39 degrees Celsius. How does this affect fluid requirements?
A) Requirements increase by about 12% for each degree Celsius above normal
B) Requirements are unchanged, since fever does not alter insensible loss
C) Requirements decrease, because antidiuretic hormone rises with fever
D) Requirements increase by about 50% regardless of how high the fever is
Answer: A
Rationale: The chapter gives an added fluid requirement of 12% per 1 degree Celsius of fever, and separately gives the rise in insensible water loss as 7 ml/kg per 24 hours for each degree Fahrenheit above 99 (p. 736).
Trap: (C) invents a mechanism in the wrong direction; (D) replaces a graded adjustment with a flat one, which fails for a child whose fever is mild.

Q: A nurse is weighing a hospitalized infant with suspected fluid loss. Why does the chapter emphasize weight?
A) Weight is the most important determinant of the percentage of total body fluid lost in infants and young children
B) Weight correlates directly with serum sodium and replaces electrolyte measurement
C) Weight is the only sign that appears before a fluid deficit reaches 10%
D) Weight predicts the type of dehydration as well as its degree
Answer: A
Rationale: The chapter states that "Weight is the most important determinant of the percent of total body fluid loss in infants and younger children," while adding the practical difficulty that "often the preillness weight is unknown" (p. 743).
Trap: (C) understates the sensitivity of clinical assessment, since "Objective signs of dehydration are present at a fluid deficit of less than 5%"; (D) conflates degree with type, and type is determined by serum sodium rather than by weight.

Topic: types and severity of dehydration

Q: An infant with gastroenteritis has a serum sodium of 142 mEq/L and has lost water and electrolytes in roughly balanced proportions. Which type of dehydration is this, and what is its chief danger?
A) Isotonic, and shock is the greatest threat to life
B) Hypotonic, and neurologic injury is the greatest threat
C) Hypertonic, and seizures are the greatest threat
D) Isotonic, and hypernatremic seizures are the greatest threat
Answer: A
Rationale: Isotonic dehydration keeps plasma sodium between 130 and 150 mEq/L with deficits "in approximately balanced proportions," is "the primary form of dehydration occurring in children," and the chapter states that "Shock is the greatest threat to life in isotonic dehydration" (pp. 742–743).
Trap: (D) keys the right type with the wrong danger, borrowing the hypertonic complication; the chapter's own review question offers oliguria, shock, arrhythmia, and hypotension for this, and hypotension is a *component* of shock and a late one, which is why it arrives too late in the sequence to be the answer.

Q: A toddler has a serum sodium of 158 mEq/L after a week of high-protein nasogastric feedings. How should the nurse interpret the physical findings?
A) The physical signs understate the fluid lost, because the extracellular compartment stays proportionately larger
B) The physical signs overstate the fluid lost, because water has shifted out of the cells
C) The physical signs accurately reflect the deficit, as in any dehydration
D) Skin turgor will be the most reliable indicator of the true deficit
Answer: A
Rationale: In hypertonic dehydration water shifts from the intracellular to the extracellular compartment, so the extracellular volume is relatively maintained and signs are "less severe for the same fluid lost"; the chapter calls this type "the most dangerous" and requiring "much more specific fluid therapy" (pp. 742–743).
Trap: (B) is the mirror error and describes hypotonic dehydration, where signs are "more severe with smaller losses"; (D) picks the one sign Table 23.3 footnotes as less prominent in hypernatremia, so a child with unremarkable turgor and a sodium over 150 is worse off than he looks.

Q: A nurse is assessing a 10-month-old for dehydration. Which findings indicate severe dehydration? Select all that apply.
A) Capillary refill greater than 4 seconds with tenting of the skin
B) Absent tears with sunken eyes
C) Hyperirritability progressing to lethargy
D) Deep and rapid respirations
E) Blood pressure normal with an orthostatic change under 10 mm Hg
Answer: A, B, C, D
Rationale: Table 23.3 assigns very delayed capillary refill greater than 4 seconds with tenting, absent tears with sunken eyes, hyperirritable-to-lethargic behavior, and hyperpnea to the severe column (p. 743).
Trap: (E) belongs to the mild column, and the orthostatic change is the giveaway — a change greater than 10 mm Hg marks moderate dehydration, while severe runs from orthostatic to frank shock. Note the capillary refill gradient across the whole table: under 2 seconds mild, 2 to 4 seconds moderate, over 4 seconds severe.

Q: An infant has lost 7% of body weight with an acute illness. How should the nurse classify this?
A) Moderate dehydration, since the infant band runs 5% to 10%
B) Severe dehydration, since any loss over 6% is severe
C) Mild dehydration, since the infant band for mild extends to 10%
D) Moderate dehydration, since the band is the same at every age
Answer: A
Rationale: The chapter gives percentage-of-body-weight bands that differ by age: infants are mild under 5%, moderate 5% to 10%, and severe over 10%, while older children are mild under 3%, moderate 3% to 6%, and severe over 6% (p. 743).
Trap: (B) applies the older-child threshold to an infant, which is the swap this pair invites; the cue is that if the stem says infant, the numbers are roughly double. Note separately that the chapter calls acute loss expressed as 50 ml/kg mild and 100 ml/kg severe "a more accurate means."

Q: A nurse is assessing a child for possible dehydration and wants the findings with the best predictive value. Which three does the chapter name?
A) Abnormal capillary refill, abnormal skin turgor, and abnormal respiratory pattern
B) Sunken fontanel, absent tears, and dry mucous membranes
C) Tachycardia, hypotension, and oliguria
D) Weight loss, urine specific gravity, and serum osmolality
Answer: A
Rationale: The chapter states twice that "the clinical signs of abnormal capillary refill, abnormal skin turgor, and abnormal respiratory pattern are the most useful in predicting dehydration of 5% or more in children" (pp. 743–744).
Trap: (C) leads with hypotension, which is a late finding throughout this chapter; (D) names a laboratory panel the same Research Focus box declines to endorse, since current evidence "did not support the routine use of ultrasound or urinalysis to determine dehydration severity."

Topic: rehydration therapy and water intoxication

Q: A 3-year-old weighing 25 kg has a capillary refill of 4 seconds, is lethargic, and has voided once since yesterday. Which intravenous order should the nurse expect?
A) 500 ml of 0.9% sodium chloride over 20 minutes
B) 500 ml of 5% dextrose in water over 20 minutes
C) 500 ml of 0.45% sodium chloride over 20 minutes
D) 250 ml of 5% dextrose in 0.45% sodium chloride over 60 minutes
Answer: A
Rationale: The initial phase uses an isotonic electrolyte solution — 0.9% sodium chloride or lactated Ringer, both close to the body's serum osmolality of 285 to 300 mOsm/kg — at 20 ml/kg as a bolus over 5 to 20 minutes (p. 745); 20 ml/kg of 25 kg is 500 ml.
Trap: (B), (C), and (D) are the three wrong versions the chapter's own review question offers — dextrose-containing and hypotonic solutions. The chapter states that dextrose "is contraindicated" in early rehydration, "but especially in diabetic ketoacidosis," and that isotonic fluids are safer than hypotonic in preventing severe hyponatremia.

Q: A child in the subsequent phase of parenteral fluid therapy has a potassium of 3.2 mEq/L. What governs whether potassium is added to the fluid?
A) Potassium is withheld until kidney function is restored and assessed and circulation has improved
B) Potassium is added to the initial bolus, since the deficit is already documented
C) Potassium is withheld until the serum level falls below 3.0 mEq/L
D) Potassium is given only by rapid intravenous push to correct the level quickly
Answer: A
Rationale: The chapter states that in the subsequent phase "potassium is withheld until kidney function is restored and assessed and circulation has improved" (p. 745), and separately directs the nurse to "Assess for adequate renal output before administration" and to "administer potassium slowly" (p. 741).
Trap: (B) adds potassium to a bolus given to a child whose kidneys have not yet proven themselves, which is the classic error; (D) reverses the administration rule and would require continuous electrocardiographic monitoring even if it were correct.

Q: A 9-month-old is brought in after a great-aunt gave him several bottles of plain water. He appears well hydrated but has had a generalized seizure and shows hypertonic reflexes. Which laboratory result does the nurse expect?
A) A serum sodium of 118 mEq/L
B) A serum glucose of 40 mg/dl
C) A serum pH of 7.20
D) A serum sodium of 158 mEq/L
Answer: A
Rationale: Children ingesting excessive electrolyte-free water "develop a concurrent decrease in serum sodium accompanied by central nervous system symptoms," and because water moves into the brain faster than sodium moves out, the child may show irritability, somnolence, vomiting, or generalized seizures while appearing well hydrated (p. 745).
Trap: (D) has the sodium moving in the wrong direction, and (B) and (C) are the metabolic distractors this scenario invites; the tell is that the child looks well hydrated, which separates water intoxication from dehydration with seizures. The chapter's named causes include incorrectly mixed formula, tap water enemas, and swimming lessons.

Q: A mother of a 6-month-old asks what to offer when her baby seems thirsty between feedings. What should the nurse advise?
A) Offer no plain water routinely, and never dilute formula with extra water
B) Offer plain water freely between feedings, since it is calorie free
C) Offer diluted formula, which supplies both water and nutrition
D) Offer an oral rehydration solution as the routine between-feeding fluid
Answer: A
Rationale: The chapter's anticipatory guidance covers acceptable oral fluid intake in infants — "preferably no water" — cautions against diluting formula with extra water, and advises avoiding water enemas (pp. 746–747); incorrectly mixed formula is named as "one of the more common causes of water intoxication in infants."
Trap: (B) and (C) are both routes to the water intoxication the guidance exists to prevent; the underlying vulnerability is that an infant's thirst mechanism is not well developed, a decreased glomerular filtration rate cannot excrete a repeated water load, and antidiuretic hormone levels may not be maximally reduced.

Topic: electrolyte imbalance recognition

Q: A child receiving potassium-sparing diuretics has a potassium of 6.2 mEq/L. Which findings should the nurse expect? Select all that apply.
A) Peaked T waves with a widened QRS complex
B) Bradycardia
C) Hyperreflexia
D) Flaccid muscle weakness and twitching
E) Notched or flattened T waves with a depressed ST segment
Answer: A, B, C, D
Rationale: Hyperkalemia produces a flat P wave, peaked T waves, a widened QRS, and an increased PR interval, together with muscle weakness, flaccid paralysis, twitching, hyperreflexia, bradycardia, and the risk of ventricular fibrillation (Table 23.2, pp. 739–742).
Trap: (E) is the hypokalemia electrocardiogram — notched or flattened T waves, a decreased ST segment, and premature ventricular contractions. The paired cue for the whole comparison is that hyporeflexia and tachycardia point to hypokalemia while hyperreflexia and bradycardia point to hyperkalemia.

Q: A child develops tingling of the fingertips and carpal spasm when a blood pressure cuff is inflated. Which electrocardiographic change accompanies this imbalance?
A) A prolonged QT interval
B) A shortened QT interval
C) Peaked T waves
D) A flattened P wave
Answer: A
Rationale: The findings describe a positive Trousseau sign in hypocalcemia, whose manifestations include neuromuscular irritability, tingling of the nose, ears, fingertips and toes, tetany, laryngospasm, positive Chvostek and Trousseau signs, and a prolonged QT interval (Table 23.2, pp. 739–742).
Trap: (B) is the hypercalcemia finding, and the QT direction is the cleanest discriminator between the two — hypocalcemia prolongs it, hypercalcemia shortens it; hypercalcemia otherwise presents with constipation, muscle hypotonicity, and kidney stones.

Q: A 4-month-old exclusively fed whole cow's milk develops tetany. What does the chapter identify as the mechanism?
A) An inappropriate calcium-to-phosphorus ratio in whole milk for a newborn
B) Insufficient total calcium content in cow's milk
C) Lactose intolerance causing calcium malabsorption
D) Excess vitamin D in fortified whole milk
Answer: A
Rationale: The chapter names as a cause of hypocalcemia "Cow's milk—tetany of the newborn (inappropriate calcium/phosphorus ratio in whole milk for newborn)," and its management includes avoiding cow's milk in infants younger than 12 months (Table 23.2).
Trap: (B) misidentifies the problem as quantity rather than ratio, which is the distinction the chapter draws; the other cause it names in the same row is exchange transfusion with citrated blood.

Q: A neonate who has been breastfeeding poorly is intensely thirsty, has dry sticky mucous membranes, and is lethargic at rest but hyperirritable when aroused. What does the nurse suspect?
A) Hypernatremia from insufficient breast milk intake
B) Hyponatremia from free water intake
C) Hypokalemia from poor intake
D) Water excess from overfeeding
Answer: A
Rationale: The chapter names "Insufficient breast milk intake in neonate (dehydration hypernatremia)" as a cause, with manifestations of intense thirst, dry sticky mucous membranes, flushed skin, hoarseness, oliguria, and "lethargy at rest with hyperirritability when aroused" (Table 23.2), managed by ensuring adequate intake and lactation assistance before discharge.
Trap: (B) reverses the sodium direction for a presentation that is nearly pathognomonic; the lethargy-with-hyperirritability-on-stimulation pattern recurs in the hypertonic dehydration column of Table 23.4 and is the single most distinctive behavioral cue in this chapter.

Topic: types of shock and compensated versus hypotensive shock

Q: A child develops shock after a tension pneumothorax. Which type is this, and what defines the category?
A) Obstructive shock, defined by mechanical obstruction of blood flow to or from the heart
B) Cardiogenic shock, defined by primary pump failure
C) Distributive shock, defined by reduced peripheral vascular resistance
D) Hypovolemic shock, defined by a reduction in the size of the vascular compartment
Answer: A
Rationale: Box 23.3 defines obstructive shock as "Mechanical obstruction of blood flow to or from the heart," with tension pneumothorax, cardiac tamponade, pulmonary embolism, and left ventricular outflow tract obstruction as its causes (p. 748).
Trap: (B) is the closest miss because both present with labored breathing, crackles, and hypotension on Table 23.5; the discriminator is the word mechanical, and that phrase belongs to obstructive alone.

Q: A nurse is reviewing shock categories with a student who lists hypovolemic, cardiogenic, neurogenic, anaphylactic, and septic as the five types. How should this be corrected?
A) Neurogenic, anaphylactic, and septic shock are all subtypes of distributive shock, which is the fourth category
B) Anaphylactic shock is a subtype of hypovolemic shock because of the capillary leak
C) Septic shock is a subtype of cardiogenic shock because cardiac output falls
D) The list is correct, since the chapter names five types
Answer: A
Rationale: The chapter names four types — hypovolemic, distributive, cardiogenic, and obstructive — and lists neurogenic, anaphylactic, and septic as the three distributive subtypes (p. 748).
Trap: (B) and (C) each attach a subtype to the wrong parent using a real feature of it as the excuse, which is how this distractor set is built; note also that hypovolemic shock is the most common type in children.

Q: A 4-year-old after a motor vehicle collision has a heart rate of 158, cool mottled hands and feet, narrowing pulse pressure, and a blood pressure of 96/68. What stage of shock does this describe?
A) Compensated shock, since blood pressure is still normal
B) Hypotensive shock, since the pulse pressure is narrowing
C) Irreversible shock, since perfusion to the extremities has failed
D) No shock, since the blood pressure is within normal limits
Answer: A
Rationale: In compensated shock "cardiac output and systemic arterial BP are usually normal or increased," with early signs of apprehension, irritability, normal blood pressure, narrowing pulse pressure, thirst, pallor, and diminished urinary output; the Nursing Alert names "Unexplained mild tachycardia and a decrease in perfusion of the hands and feet" as the differentiating features (p. 749).
Trap: (D) is the answer the chapter exists to prevent, and it states the correction three separate times — "Hypotension is a late and poor prognostic sign of shock, so signs of shock must be recognized and appropriate interventions implemented before hypotension is evident."

Q: A child in shock has an established airway. Which measures belong in the emergency management? Select all that apply.
A) Administer 100% oxygen
B) Give an isotonic crystalloid bolus of 20 ml/kg over 5 to 20 minutes
C) Keep the child flat with the legs raised above the level of the heart
D) Keep the child warm and calm, and monitor and treat pain
E) Administer calcium routinely to support myocardial contractility
Answer: A, B, C, D
Rationale: The Emergency Treatment box directs establishing an airway with 100% oxygen, restoring vascular volume, inotrope and vasopressor support, keeping the child flat with legs raised above the heart, and keeping the child warm and calm while treating pain (p. 751).
Trap: (E) is explicitly ruled out — routine calcium in a pediatric cardiac arrest "is not recommended unless there is documented evidence of hypocalcemia, hyperkalemia, hypermagnesemia, or calcium channel blocker overdose." Note that oxygen is named "the first drug to be administered" in shock, and dopamine is the preferred inotrope because it also improves renal perfusion.

Q: A child in hypovolemic shock has received two fluid boluses. Which finding indicates the boluses are working?
A) A rise in blood pressure together with a fall in heart rate
B) A rise in blood pressure with an unchanged heart rate
C) A fall in heart rate with an unchanged blood pressure
D) An increase in capillary refill time from 2 to 3 seconds
Answer: A
Rationale: The chapter states that success is judged by "increase in BP and decrease in heart rate" after boluses of 20 ml/kg of isotonic crystalloid repeated as necessary (p. 751).
Trap: (D) reads a worsening number as improvement, since capillary refill lengthening from 2 to 3 seconds moves toward the moderate band; note that fresh-frozen plasma is used to correct coagulopathies rather than as volume replacement, and albumin is the usual colloid because it stays in the vascular space longer than crystalloid.

Topic: septic shock and its stages

Q: A child with a proven urinary tract infection meets two systemic inflammatory response criteria, one of which is an abnormal temperature. How should this be classified?
A) Sepsis, which is the systemic inflammatory response syndrome in the presence of suspected or proven infection
B) Severe sepsis, since organ dysfunction is implied by meeting the criteria
C) Septic shock, since infection plus systemic inflammatory response defines shock
D) Systemic inflammatory response syndrome alone, since infection does not change the classification
Answer: A
Rationale: Box 23.4 defines sepsis as "SIRS in the presence of or as a result of suspected or proven infection," severe sepsis as "Sepsis plus cardiovascular organ dysfunction or ARDS or two or more other organ dysfunctions," and septic shock as sepsis with organ dysfunction and hypotension (p. 752).
Trap: (D) ignores the step the infection adds, and the chapter says so directly: the response "can occur in response to both infectious and noninfectious (e.g., trauma, burns) causes. When caused by infection, it is called sepsis." Note that the syndrome requires at least two of four criteria, one of which must be an abnormal temperature or leukocyte count.

Q: A child with sepsis is warm and flushed with chills and fever, a normal blood pressure, and normal urinary output. How should the nurse interpret this?
A) This is the early hyperdynamic stage, in which the patient has the best chance for survival
B) This is the most dangerous stage, because vasodilation indicates circulatory collapse
C) This is not shock, because blood pressure and urinary output are normal
D) This is the cold hypodynamic stage presenting atypically
Answer: A
Rationale: The chapter describes stage 1 as "hyperdynamic, warm, or hyperdynamic-compensated," with warm flushed skin from vasodilation and increased cardiac output, normal blood pressure and urine output, and normal mentation, adding that "The patient has the best chance for survival in this stage" (p. 752).
Trap: (C) is the trap the chapter answers twice — "Children with septic shock often maintain a normal BP until they are severely ill. Shock may occur long before hypotension in children"; the counterintuitive part is that warm and flushed is the *better* stage, which (B) exploits.

Q: A child with sepsis becomes hypothermic with cold extremities, weak pulses, and anuria. What does this indicate?
A) The hypodynamic or cold stage, the most dangerous stage, with multiorgan failure common
B) Improvement, since the fever has resolved
C) The normodynamic stage, which typically lasts several days
D) An anaphylactic component superimposed on the sepsis
Answer: A
Rationale: Stage 3, the "hypodynamic, or cold" stage, presents with hypothermia, cold extremities, weak pulses, hypotension, oliguria or anuria, severe lethargy or coma, and common multiorgan failure; the chapter calls it "the most dangerous stage of shock" (p. 752).
Trap: (B) reads a falling temperature as recovery, which is the same error the burn chapter warns about when a low or normal temperature signals overwhelming sepsis; (C) misassigns the duration, since the intermediate normodynamic stage "lasts only a few hours."

Q: A nurse is caring for a child at risk for septic shock. Which early signs should prompt action?
A) Fever, tachycardia, and tachypnea
B) Hypotension and oliguria
C) Cold extremities and weak peripheral pulses
D) Altered level of consciousness and anuria
Answer: A
Rationale: The Nursing Alert states that "nurses caring for children at risk for septic shock should be alert to early signs: fever, tachycardia, and tachypnea," adding that "Subtle alterations in tissue perfusion and unexplained tachypnea and tachycardia often are early warning signs" (p. 754).
Trap: (B), (C), and (D) are all real findings in septic shock and all arrive in the later stages, which is exactly what makes them attractive and wrong here; acting on them means acting after the stage with the best survival has passed.

Topic: anaphylactic and toxic shock syndrome recognition

Q: A child develops anaphylaxis 30 seconds after an intravenous medication is started. Another child develops symptoms 90 minutes after an exposure. What does the chapter say about the difference?
A) The rapidity of the reaction is directly related to its intensity, so the sooner the onset the more severe the reaction
B) Delayed reactions are more severe, because more antigen has been absorbed
C) Onset time does not predict severity
D) A reaction beginning after 1 hour is not anaphylaxis
Answer: A
Rationale: The chapter states that symptoms usually begin "within seconds or minutes" of exposure and that "The rapidity of the reaction is directly related to its intensity—the sooner the onset, the more severe the reaction," while noting that onset "may be delayed for as long as 2 hours" (p. 754).
Trap: (D) would rule out a real and still-dangerous presentation; note the penicillin-specific windows the chapter gives, an immediate onset within 1 hour of administration or an accelerated onset 1 to 72 hours after.

Q: A nurse is teaching about the most likely triggers of anaphylaxis. Which statement is accurate?
A) Food allergy is the most common cause outside the hospital, while medication and latex allergies are more common in the hospital
B) Food allergy is the most common cause in both settings
C) Insect stings are the most common cause outside the hospital
D) Latex allergy is the most common cause in both settings
Answer: A
Rationale: The chapter states that "Food allergy is the most common cause of anaphylaxis outside the hospital, whereas medication and latex allergies are more common in the hospital" (p. 754).
Trap: (B) and (D) each generalize one setting's answer to both, and the setting is what the item turns on; mechanistically the reaction releases histamine from mast cells, causing vasodilation and increased capillary permeability, which is why anaphylaxis is classified as a distributive shock.

Q: An adolescent presents with sudden high fever, vomiting, diarrhea, profound hypotension, and a diffuse macular erythroderma. Which findings would support a probable diagnosis of toxic shock syndrome? Select all that apply.
A) A fever of 38.9 degrees Celsius or higher
B) Desquamation of the palms and soles 1 to 2 weeks after onset
C) Hypotension below the fifth percentile for age
D) Involvement of three or more organ systems
E) A positive blood culture for group A streptococcus
Answer: A, B, C, D
Rationale: Box 23.7 lists the criteria as fever of 38.9 degrees Celsius or higher, diffuse macular erythroderma, desquamation particularly of palms and soles 1 to 2 weeks after onset, hypotension, and involvement of three or more organ systems, with the diagnosis probable when four of five major criteria are met (p. 756).
Trap: (E) is excluded by the criteria themselves, which require that blood and cerebrospinal fluid cultures be negative for organisms other than *Staphylococcus aureus*; the causative toxin is toxic shock syndrome toxin-1, and about half of cases occur in menstruating women and are usually associated with tampon use.

Q: A nurse is teaching a group of adolescent girls about reducing the risk of toxic shock syndrome. Which instruction reflects the chapter?
A) Avoid superabsorbent tampons, and do not leave any tampon in place for more than 4 to 6 hours
B) Change tampons every 12 hours and use superabsorbent products overnight
C) Use tampons only overnight and sanitary napkins during the day
D) Tampon use carries no risk provided menses are regular
Answer: A
Rationale: The chapter advises that "Young girls are advised not to use superabsorbent tampons and not to leave any tampon in the body for more than 4 to 6 hours," and notes that avoiding tampons "offers the most certain preventive measure" (p. 756).
Trap: (B) doubles the dwell time and recommends the absorbency the chapter rules out; (C) inverts the alternating pattern the chapter suggests, which is to use napkins during the night, when at home during the day, and when flow is slight.

Topic: burn depth, extent, and severity classification

Q: A child has a burn involving the epidermis and varying degrees of the dermis. The area is painful, moist, red, and blistered. How should the nurse classify this burn?
A) Partial-thickness, or second degree
B) Superficial, or first degree
C) Full-thickness, or third degree
D) Fourth degree
Answer: A
Rationale: Partial-thickness burns involve the epidermis plus varying degrees of dermis and are "Painful, moist, red, and blistered," with deep dermal burns appearing mottled pink, red, and white with blisters and edema (p. 758).
Trap: (B) is excluded by the blisters, since in a superficial burn "blisters do not form" and the injury is epidermal only, healing in 3 or 4 days without scarring; (C) would be dry and leathery with thrombosed vessels visible.

Q: The parents of a child with extensive third-degree burns ask why their daughter needs so much pain medication when they were told full-thickness burns destroy the nerves. What should the nurse explain?
A) The burned areas lack sensation, but most such burns have partial-thickness areas at the periphery with intact nerve endings, and excised eschar and donor sites expose nerve fibers
B) Full-thickness burns are painless, so the medication is for anxiety rather than pain
C) The nerve endings regenerate within 24 hours, restoring full sensation
D) The pain originates entirely from the dressing changes rather than the wound
Answer: A
Rationale: The chapter states that full-thickness burns "lack sensation in the area of injury because of the destruction of nerve endings" and then immediately adds that most have partial-thickness areas at the periphery with intact nerve endings, that excised eschar and donor sites contain exposed nerve fibers, and that pain returns as fibers regenerate — concluding that "children often experience severe pain related to the size and depth of the burn" (p. 758).
Trap: (B) states the half-truth as the whole truth, and an option asserting that a child with third-degree burns needs no analgesia is wrong in this chapter.

Q: A nurse is estimating burn extent in a 1-year-old. How does the pediatric rule of nines differ from the adult chart?
A) The head and neck are 18% and each lower extremity is 14%
B) The head and neck are 9% and each lower extremity is 18%, as in adults
C) The head and neck are 14% and each lower extremity is 18%
D) The trunk and arms are proportionately larger than in the adult
Answer: A
Rationale: The chapter gives the infant's head and neck as 18% of total body surface area and each lower extremity as 14%, with the trunk and arms roughly the same proportions as the adult; the modified rule deducts 1% from the head and adds 0.5% to each leg for each year of life after age 2 (p. 757).
Trap: (B) applies the adult chart unchanged, which underestimates the burn because it under-weights the head — the Lund-Browder chart makes the same point, giving half a head as 9.5% at birth and 3.5% in an adult while half a thigh runs the other way.

Q: A 6-year-old has a 5% total body surface area partial-thickness burn of both hands. How should this be graded?
A) It is not a minor burn, because burns of the hands are excluded from the minor category regardless of extent
B) It is a minor burn, because it is under 10% of total body surface area
C) It is a major burn, because all partial-thickness burns of the hands require a burn center
D) It is a moderate burn, because the hands count double in the extent calculation
Answer: A
Rationale: Table 23.8's footnote states that minor burns "exclude any burn involving the face, hands, feet, perineum, or crossing joints; circumferential burns; electrical injuries; any injury complicated by inhalation injury or concomitant trauma; and children with psychosocial factors affecting the injury" (p. 758).
Trap: (B) applies the percentage band without reading the exclusions, which is the whole point of the footnote; the chapter separately flags hands, feet, and joints because scar formation there interferes with growth and development.

Q: A 20-month-old and an 8-year-old sustain burns of similar magnitude. What does the chapter say about their relative risk?
A) Children younger than 2 years have a significantly higher mortality rate than older children with burns of similar magnitude
B) Mortality is similar at all ages when the burn magnitude is matched
C) The older child is at higher risk because of greater body surface area
D) Mortality differences appear only above 50% total body surface area
Answer: A
Rationale: The chapter states that "Children younger than 2 years of age have a significantly higher mortality rate than older children with burns of a similar magnitude" (p. 760), attributing it to minimal protein stores, an immature immune response, a greater proportion of body water intolerant of rapid shifts, and immature renal function; in children older than 3 years mortality is similar to that of adults.
Trap: (C) reasons from size in the wrong direction; note also that very young children sustain deeper injuries "because of the thin nature of infant skin," and that cellular death can continue at the wound site for up to 48 hours, so both depth and area can expand past the first estimate.

Topic: emergency burn management and fluid resuscitation

Q: A child's clothing has caught fire. Which actions should the rescuer take at the scene? Select all that apply.
A) Place the child horizontal and roll in a blanket, taking care not to cover the head and face
B) Apply a single brief application of tepid water to large denuded areas
C) Trim around clothing that adheres to the burn rather than pulling it off
D) Remove jewelry from the burned area
E) Apply ice packs continuously to limit the depth of injury
Answer: A, B, C, D
Rationale: The chapter directs smothering the flames with the child horizontal while not covering the head and face because of inhaled toxic fumes, cooling large denuded areas with "a single application of tepid water," trimming around adherent clothing rather than pulling, and removing jewelry because of heat transfer and constriction from edema (p. 764).
Trap: (E) reverses a stated rule — "Continuous wet dressings or the application of ice promotes vasoconstriction because of cooling, resulting in impaired circulation to the burned area and increased tissue damage." Note that remaining vertical risks igniting the hair or inhaling flames, heat, or smoke.

Q: A toddler has spilled a caustic powder onto her forearm. What should the nurse do first?
A) Brush the powder off the skin, then flush with water
B) Flush immediately with copious water to dilute the agent
C) Apply a neutralizing agent appropriate to the chemical
D) Cover the area with a dry dressing and transport without intervention
Answer: A
Rationale: The chapter directs that a chemical in powder form should be brushed off and not have water added, because water spreads the caustic agent, while liquid chemical burns are flushed with copious water during transport (p. 764).
Trap: (C) is contraindicated for both forms — "The use of neutralizing agents on the skin is contraindicated"; (B) is correct for a liquid and wrong for a powder, which is the distinction the item turns on.

Q: A 10-year-old weighing 34 kg has extensive second- and third-degree burns and is receiving fluid resuscitation. Which finding indicates the resuscitation is adequate?
A) An hourly urine output of about 30 ml
B) An hourly urine output of about 30 ml/kg
C) A systolic blood pressure within normal limits for age
D) A central venous pressure above 12 mm Hg
Answer: A
Rationale: The chapter gives the target as "an hourly urinary output of 30 ml in older children and 0.5 to 1 ml/kg in children weighing less than 30 kg," restated later as 30 to 50 ml/hr in children over 30 kg (pp. 765, 770).
Trap: (C) is ruled out by the chapter's own Nursing Alert — "Blood pressure can remain normotensive even in a state of hypovolemia" — and the stated reliable indicators are capillary refill, alterations in sensorium, and urinary output. Note that the chapter's own review item prints its correct option as 30 ml/kg, which is a units error in the source; the text's figure is per hour, not per kilogram.

Q: A child is 60 hours post-burn and urine output has risen sharply. The resident asks the nurse to increase intravenous fluids to match the output. How should the nurse respond?
A) Question the order, because diuresis at 48 to 72 hours reflects interstitial fluid returning to the vascular compartment and matching it can cause circulatory overload
B) Increase the fluids as ordered, since output must always be matched to prevent hypovolemia
C) Question the order, because urine output is not a valid measure after the first 24 hours
D) Increase the fluids and add a diuretic to prevent overload
Answer: A
Rationale: The chapter states that "Approximately 48 to 72 hours after injury, interstitial fluid returns to the vascular compartment and diuresis occurs to eliminate excess fluids. During this phase, increasing intake to match urinary output can result in circulatory overload" (p. 766).
Trap: (B) applies the resuscitation-phase logic past the point where it holds, which is the error the sentence exists to prevent; the chapter separately names over-resuscitation as "fluid creep," producing pulmonary edema, acute respiratory distress syndrome, compartment syndrome, and multiple organ dysfunction.

Topic: burn wound care, grafting, and complications

Q: A child with a 30% burn is receiving daily wound care. The parents ask why prophylactic antibiotics have not been started. What should the nurse explain?
A) Prophylactic antibiotics are not recommended, because systemic antibiotics cannot reach the deepest injury where circulation is destroyed
B) Prophylactic antibiotics are withheld only until the first culture results return
C) Prophylactic antibiotics are given only to children under 2 years of age
D) Prophylactic antibiotics were given in the emergency department and do not need repeating
Answer: A
Rationale: The chapter states twice that "Prophylactic antibiotic administration is not recommended," because systemic antibiotics cannot reach the deepest injury where circulation is destroyed, and that surveillance cultures guide treatment instead (p. 765).
Trap: (B) reframes a standing rule as a temporary delay, which is the most plausible-sounding version of the error; the chapter's approach is surveillance rather than prophylaxis.

Q: A burned child becomes disoriented, and the nurse notes that the temperature has fallen to 36 degrees Celsius after several days of low-grade fever. How should this be interpreted?
A) These are signs of overwhelming burn sepsis, since disorientation is the first sign and a low or normal temperature may indicate sepsis
B) These are signs of improvement, since the fever has resolved
C) These indicate inadequate analgesia causing agitation
D) These indicate hypoglycemia from the hypermetabolic state
Answer: A
Rationale: The chapter's Nursing Alert opens by naming disorientation as the first sign of overwhelming sepsis, and states that a "Low or 'normal' temperature may indicate overwhelming sepsis" (p. 763), with fever spiking and then falling to subnormal.
Trap: (B) reads the falling temperature as recovery, and the reason it is wrong is that burned children normally run warm from hypermetabolism, so a normal temperature is itself abnormal here.

Q: A child requires permanent coverage of a full-thickness burn. Which graft type provides it?
A) An autograft, taken from the child's own skin
B) An allograft, taken from a cadaver donor
C) A xenograft, taken from porcine skin
D) Either an allograft or a xenograft, both of which are permanent
Answer: A
Rationale: The chapter distinguishes an autograft, the patient's own skin and the only permanent coverage, from an allograft or homograft from another human, and a xenograft or heterograft from another species such as pigskin, both of which are temporary (Box 23.11, p. 768).
Trap: (D) collapses the permanence distinction, which is the point of the classification; the naming cue is that auto means self, allo means another of the same species, and xeno means a foreign species.

Q: A child with a circumferential full-thickness burn of the forearm has decreasing perfusion distally. Which procedure is performed first?
A) An escharotomy, an incision through the burned tissue
B) A fasciotomy, an incision of the muscle sheath
C) An autograft to restore coverage
D) A flap using tissue with its blood supply intact
Answer: A
Rationale: The chapter directs escharotomy or fasciotomy for constricting circumferential eschar, with fasciotomy done only "if the escharotomy is not sufficient" (p. 765).
Trap: (B) is the correct second step presented as the first, which is what this pair tests; note that positioning matters for the same reason — children with burns assume flexion, which "encourages the formation of contractures and loss of function," so splinting and positioning are in extension.
