# Chapter 28 — The Child With Hematologic or Immunologic Dysfunction

Source: `courses/nur4226-4339-childrearing/sources/Ch28_Fact_Bank.md`
(Hockenberry, Wilson & Rodgers, *Wong's Nursing Care of Infants and Children*, 11th ed.,
Chapter 28, printed pp. 1020–1066.)

**Blueprint weight: ~5 questions of 202, depth `standard`.** Band is 30–45 questions; this
file carries **40** across **9 topics**, averaging 4.4 each. The count sits below the 43 used
for six-item chapters in this course and above the light-band floor, tracking the weight down
one step rather than filling the band.

**Where the weight went.** Sickle cell disease gets two topics — one for the pathophysiology
and the six named crises, one for management and prevention — because it is the longest
disorder in the chapter and because its management contains the single most inverted
intervention in the bank. Iron deficiency anemia, hemophilia, and transfusion safety follow,
since those carry the chapter's own review questions.

**The cross-chapter swap worth naming up front.** In sickle cell pain, cold is
contraindicated because it "enhances vasoconstriction and occlusion"; in hemophilia, ice is
part of the RICE mnemonic. Same intervention, opposite verdict, decided by the disease. Both
sides are drilled here, in adjacent topics, because a learner who meets only one will apply it
to the other.

**Drilled from both ends:** anemia against hypoxemia, since anemia is too little hemoglobin
rather than too little oxygen on it, and cyanosis "is typically not evident"; serum iron
against total iron-binding capacity, which move in opposite directions; hemosiderosis against
hemochromatosis, which are storage against damage; hemophilia against immune thrombocytopenia
on whether petechiae appear; hemophilia against von Willebrand disease on inheritance and
bleeding site; and immune thrombocytopenia against Henoch-Schönlein purpura, which are purpura
with a low platelet count and purpura with a normal one.

**This chapter's review-question key is internally consistent with its text**, unlike the
previous chapter's, so the five items it contains are drilled as the source keys them.

**What ~5 blueprint items could not buy.** Left undrilled: normal hematopoiesis and the
complete blood count reference values; hereditary spherocytosis; folate and vitamin B12
deficiency; disseminated intravascular coagulation; Wiskott-Aldrich syndrome and severe
combined immunodeficiency; and the detailed blood-component table. Disseminated intravascular
coagulation is the largest real loss, since it is the named hematologic complication of septic
shock in Chapter 23 and is drilled in neither place.

**One topic was deliberately narrowed to avoid a collision.** Chapter 6 already carries
`immunity terminology and vaccine types`, so the humoral-versus-cell-mediated distinction is
drilled here only where it does work this chapter needs — inside the human immunodeficiency
virus topic, where the virus suppresses cell-mediated immunity first and humoral immunity
second.

**Select-all count: 4** (1 in 10), each on a source list that genuinely sorts two ways: the
anemia signs across their three etiologic groups, the six objectives of sickle cell crisis
management against an intervention the chapter affirmatively contraindicates, the hemophilia
joint-bleed measures against the one exercise a Nursing Alert forbids, and the immune
thrombocytopenia restrictions against a drug class the chapter rules out. The count is below
this bank's usual ratio because most of this chapter's other high-yield material is a
two-column discrimination between diseases — hemophilia against thrombocytopenia, thalassemia
against iron deficiency, aplastic anemia against leukemia — and a discrimination is drilled

Topic: anemia recognition and blood transfusion safety

Q: A nurse is assessing a child with moderate iron deficiency anemia and notes pallor and fatigue but no cyanosis. How should this be interpreted?
A) Expected, because anemia is caused by decreased hemoglobin rather than inadequate oxygen saturation of existing hemoglobin
B) Unexpected, since cyanosis accompanies all significant anemias
C) Unexpected, and pulse oximetry should be obtained immediately to confirm hypoxemia
D) Expected only if the hemoglobin is above 10 g/dl
Answer: A
Rationale: The chapter states that "Anemia is caused by decreased levels of hemoglobin or RBCs, not inadequate oxygen saturation of existing hemoglobin," and that cyanosis "is typically not evident" (p. 1025).
Trap: (B) is the option the chapter's own review question plants, and it substitutes the wrong mechanism — cyanosis requires a quantity of deoxygenated hemoglobin that an anemic child may not have enough hemoglobin to produce at all.

Q: A nurse is sorting the signs of anemia by mechanism. Which findings belong to the increased-destruction group? Select all that apply.
A) Jaundice
B) Icteric sclera
C) Dark urine
D) Splenomegaly
E) Cool skin with decreased peripheral pulses
Answer: A, B, C, D
Rationale: Box 28.2 sorts the signs of anemia into three etiologic groups — decreased production, increased destruction, and increased loss — placing jaundice, icteric sclera, dark urine, and splenomegaly in the destruction column (p. 1025).
Trap: (E) belongs only to the increased-loss column, where volume depletion produces cool skin and diminished peripheral pulses; sorting each option into its group before answering is what makes an all-or-nothing item survivable here.

Q: Fifteen minutes into a transfusion, a child develops chills, flank pain, and red urine. What should the nurse do first?
A) Stop the transfusion
B) Slow the transfusion rate and reassess in 15 minutes
C) Notify the practitioner and await orders
D) Administer acetaminophen and continue the infusion
Answer: A
Rationale: The chapter gives a fixed action order for a suspected reaction: stop the transfusion, take vital signs, maintain a patent intravenous line with normal saline and new tubing, notify the practitioner, and do not restart until the child has been medically evaluated (p. 1030).
Trap: (B) is the plausible-but-wrong option, because slowing keeps incompatible blood entering the circulation; the findings described — chills, flank pain, and red or black urine — are those of a hemolytic reaction, and ABO incompatibility "remains the most common cause of death from blood transfusion, and human error is usually responsible."

Q: A nurse is starting a unit of packed red blood cells on a 4-year-old. Which action is correct?
A) Administer the first 50 ml or initial 20% of the volume slowly and stay with the child
B) Begin at the full rate and check on the child in 30 minutes
C) Prime the line with 5% dextrose in water before connecting the blood
D) Return unused blood to the unit refrigerator for later use
Answer: A
Rationale: The chapter directs administering "the first 50 ml of blood or initial 20% of volume (whichever is smaller) slowly and stay with the child," with vital signs before, 15 minutes after initiation, hourly while infusing, and on completion (p. 1030).
Trap: (C) violates the rule against infusing solutions other than normal saline in the line with red cells, and (D) violates the instruction not to store blood in a regular unit refrigerator — blood is used within 30 minutes of arrival and each unit infused within 4 hours.

Q: A child receiving a transfusion develops dyspnea, a dry cough, distended neck veins, and hypertension. Which reaction is this, and what positioning helps?
A) Circulatory overload, and the child is placed upright with the feet dependent to increase venous resistance
B) Hemolytic reaction, and the child is placed flat with legs elevated
C) Allergic reaction, and the child is placed in a side-lying position
D) Air embolism, and the child is placed in Trendelenburg position
Answer: A
Rationale: Table 28.2 lists precordial pain, dyspnea, rales, cyanosis, dry cough, distended neck veins, and hypertension as circulatory overload, directing the nurse to "Place child upright with feet in dependent position to increase venous resistance" (pp. 1027–1029).
Trap: (B) names the most feared reaction, whose signs are chills, flank pain, and red or black urine rather than congestion; note that the distinguishing feature here is hypertension with distended neck veins, which points to too much volume rather than to incompatibility.

Topic: iron deficiency anemia

Q: A 14-month-old drinks about 1.5 liters of whole milk daily, eats few solids, is above the ninetieth percentile for weight, and is pale. What should the nurse suspect?
A) Iron deficiency anemia, since "milk baby" infants are overweight yet anemic
B) Iron deficiency is unlikely, since the child is well nourished and gaining weight
C) Lactose intolerance, since milk intake is high
D) Physiologic anemia, which resolves without intervention
Answer: A
Rationale: The chapter states that although infants with iron deficiency anemia "tend to be underweight," many "are overweight because of excessive milk ingestion (known as milk baby)," because milk is a poor iron source given almost to the exclusion of solids, and because "Increased fecal loss of blood occurs in 50% of iron deficient infants fed cow's milk" (p. 1031).
Trap: (B) treats good weight gain as reassurance, which is exactly the bait; (D) confuses two entities the chapter separates explicitly — "Physiologic anemia should not be confused with iron deficiency anemia resulting from nutritional causes."

Q: Which laboratory finding confirms iron deficiency anemia?
A) A total iron-binding capacity elevated above the normal range
B) A total iron-binding capacity below the normal range
C) A markedly elevated reticulocyte count
D) Decreased blood pressure as an early sign
Answer: A
Rationale: The chapter states that "In iron deficiency anemia TIBC is elevated above the normal range," an elevation that "represents the body's compensatory mechanism to absorb more iron" (p. 1031).
Trap: (B) reverses the compensation, which is the error this pair exists to catch — serum iron falls while binding capacity rises. (C) misstates the reticulocyte count, which is "usually normal or slightly reduced," and (D) misplaces decreased blood pressure, which is a late rather than early sign.

Q: A nurse is teaching a parent to give oral iron to a toddler. Which instruction is correct?
A) Give it between meals with a citrus juice, and expect green-black stools
B) Give it with milk to reduce stomach upset
C) Give it with tea to improve absorption
D) Stop the iron as soon as the hemoglobin returns to normal
Answer: A
Rationale: The chapter directs giving iron between meals because free hydrochloric acid is greatest then, notes that ascorbic acid enhances absorption while tannins in tea bind nonheme iron, and describes green-black stool as an expected effect whose absence is a compliance clue (p. 1033).
Trap: (D) stops too early — iron therapy continues "2 to 3 months after blood values normalize" to replenish stores; (B) and (C) each name a substance that impairs absorption, and milk is already the likely cause of the deficiency.

Q: A nurse is giving anticipatory guidance to the parents of a healthy 5-month-old about preventing iron deficiency. What should be included?
A) Iron supplementation of 1 mg/kg/day by 4 to 6 months, breast milk or iron-fortified formula only for the first 12 months, and no fresh cow's milk before 12 months
B) Introduce fresh cow's milk at 6 months to increase calcium and iron intake
C) No supplementation is needed, since maternal iron stores last through the first year
D) Limit all solid foods until 12 months to avoid displacing formula
Answer: A
Rationale: The chapter gives iron supplementation of 1 mg/kg/day by 4 to 6 months in full-term infants, breast milk or iron-fortified formula only for the first 12 months, formula limited to no more than 1 L/day to encourage iron-rich solids, and states that "Infants younger than 12 months old should not be given fresh cow's milk" (p. 1032).
Trap: (C) overstates the duration of maternal stores, which "are adequate for the first 5 to 6 months in the full-term infant but for only about 2 to 3 months in premature infants or infants of multiple births"; universal screening for anemia occurs at approximately 12 months.

Topic: sickle cell disease pathophysiology and crises

Q: A nurse is teaching the parents of a child with sickle cell anemia what triggers sickling. Which conditions does the chapter name?
A) Dehydration, acidosis, hypoxia, and temperature elevation
B) Dehydration, alkalosis, hyperoxia, and hypothermia
C) Infection alone, since sickling is otherwise spontaneous
D) Physical exertion alone, since sickling is exercise induced
Answer: A
Rationale: The chapter states that "Under conditions of dehydration, acidosis, hypoxia, and temperature elevation, the relatively insoluble HgbS changes its molecular structure to form long, slender crystals" (p. 1034).
Trap: (B) reverses three of the four, and the reason the list matters is that these four conditions are the whole prevention plan in one sentence; sickling is reversible under adequate oxygenation and hydration until repeated cycles make the cells irreversibly sickled.

Q: Parents ask why their newborn with sickle cell anemia has no symptoms. What should the nurse explain?
A) The newborn is protected by fetal hemoglobin, which is 60% to 80% at birth and falls rapidly during the first year
B) Symptoms never appear before school age in this disease
C) The diagnosis is probably incorrect, since symptoms are present at birth
D) Symptoms are absent because the spleen is still functioning normally
Answer: A
Rationale: The chapter states that "The newborn with SCD is generally asymptomatic because of the protective effect of HgbF (60% to 80%), but this rapidly decreases during the first year, so the child is at risk for sickle cell-related complications" (p. 1034).
Trap: (D) names a real fact at the wrong time — the spleen is indeed functioning at birth, but it is progressively infarcted until "by the age of 5 years, the spleen has decreased in size and has been totally replaced by a fibrous mass (functional asplenia)," which is a later development rather than the reason for newborn protection.

Q: A 3-year-old with sickle cell anemia develops sudden profound anemia, rapidly enlarging spleen, and hypotension. Which crisis is this?
A) Splenic sequestration
B) Vasoocclusive crisis
C) Aplastic crisis
D) Hyperhemolytic crisis
Answer: A
Rationale: The chapter describes sequestration as "pooling of large quantities of blood, usually in the spleen... which causes a decrease in blood volume and ultimately shock," occurring "most commonly in children between 2 months and 5 years of age" and potentially resulting in "death from profound anemia and cardiovascular collapse" (p. 1037).
Trap: (C) also produces profound anemia, but through diminished red cell production "usually triggered by infection with a virus (especially the human parvovirus)" rather than through pooling and shock — the enlarging spleen and hypotension are what separate them.

Q: A child with sickle cell disease has a new pulmonary infiltrate on chest radiograph with chest pain, fever, and hypoxia. What is this, and why does it matter?
A) Acute chest syndrome, which is clinically similar to pneumonia and may cause restrictive lung disease and pulmonary hypertension if repeated
B) Vasoocclusive crisis, which is the most common and non-life-threatening type
C) Aplastic crisis, which follows parvovirus infection
D) Simple pneumonia, which requires no different management than in other children
Answer: A
Rationale: The chapter defines acute chest syndrome as "a new pulmonary infiltrate on chest x-ray of a SCD patient that may be accompanied by chest pain, fever, cough, tachypnea, wheezing, and hypoxia," clinically similar to pneumonia, with repeated episodes causing restrictive lung disease and pulmonary hypertension (p. 1037).
Trap: (D) is dangerous because exchange transfusion is specifically used in this syndrome and after stroke; note that overwhelming infection with *Streptococcus pneumoniae* and *Haemophilus influenzae* type b "is the major cause of death in children with SCD under the age of 5 years."

Q: A screening Sickledex test is positive in a 6-year-old. What does the nurse tell the family?
A) Hemoglobin electrophoresis is needed, because the screening test cannot distinguish trait from disease
B) The child has sickle cell anemia, since the test is positive
C) The child has sickle cell trait, since disease would have presented earlier
D) The test should be repeated in 6 months before further workup
Answer: A
Rationale: The chapter states that the sickle turbidity test "can be performed on blood from a finger or heel stick and yields accurate results in 3 minutes," but that "Hemoglobin electrophoresis is necessary to distinguish between those children with the trait and those with the disease" (p. 1037).
Trap: (B) and (C) each convert a screen into a diagnosis in opposite directions; note the separate rule that "Whenever sickle cells are found, diagnostic test results are usually positive for SCA, not sickle cell trait," so a smear showing sickled cells points to disease.

Topic: sickle cell disease management and prevention

Q: A child is admitted in vasoocclusive crisis. Which interventions should the nurse expect? Select all that apply.
A) Intravenous fluids started and oral fluids encouraged
B) Antibiotics to treat any existing infection
C) Analgesia for severe pain
D) Bed rest to minimize energy expenditure
E) Cold compresses applied to painful joints
Answer: A, B, C, D
Rationale: The chapter lists six objectives: bed rest, hydration through oral and intravenous therapy, electrolyte replacement, analgesia, blood replacement, and antibiotic therapy to treat any existing infection (p. 1039).
Trap: (E) is affirmatively contraindicated — "Cold compresses are not applied to the area because doing so enhances vasoconstriction and occlusion" — and it is the distractor in the chapter's own review question; the other planted wrong option there is meperidine.

Q: A child in sickle cell crisis is prescribed meperidine for pain. What should the nurse recognize?
A) Meperidine is contraindicated, because normeperidine accumulates and causes seizures
B) Meperidine is the preferred opioid in this disease
C) Meperidine is acceptable if given with an antiemetic
D) Meperidine is acceptable for doses under 24 hours
Answer: A
Rationale: The chapter states that meperidine "is not recommended" in sickle cell disease because the metabolite normeperidine accumulates and causes seizures (p. 1041).
Trap: (C) and (D) both look for a safe window that does not exist for this drug in this population; the underlying point is that severe vasoocclusive pain requires adequate opioid analgesia, just not this opioid.

Q: A nurse is asked to apply oxygen to a child in vasoocclusive crisis who has a saturation of 97%. What should the nurse understand?
A) Oxygen is of little value unless the child is hypoxic, does not reverse sickling, and with prolonged use decreases erythropoiesis
B) Oxygen should be applied to all children in crisis to prevent further sickling
C) Oxygen reverses sickling within 30 minutes in most children
D) Oxygen is contraindicated in sickle cell disease at any saturation
Answer: A
Rationale: The chapter states that "Oxygen therapy is of little therapeutic value unless the patient is hypoxic," that it "is usually not effective in reversing sickling or reducing pain because the oxygen is not able to reach the enmeshed sickled RBCs through the clogged vessels," and that prolonged therapy aggravates the anemia (pp. 1039, 1041).
Trap: (D) overcorrects into a contraindication the chapter does not state — oxygen is appropriate for a hypoxic child; what is wrong is treating it as a routine crisis intervention.

Q: The parents of an infant newly diagnosed with sickle cell anemia ask what will protect their child from infection. What should the nurse explain?
A) Penicillin prophylaxis started by 2 months of age, plus pneumococcal, *Haemophilus influenzae* type b, and meningococcal vaccines
B) Vaccines alone, since prophylactic antibiotics promote resistance
C) Penicillin prophylaxis beginning at school entry
D) No specific prophylaxis is needed until the spleen is removed
Answer: A
Rationale: The chapter states that "Penicillin prophylaxis is started by 2 months of age" and that pneumococcal, *Haemophilus influenzae* type b, and meningococcal vaccines are recommended because of functional asplenia (p. 1038).
Trap: (D) waits for a splenectomy that does not occur in this disease, since the spleen is destroyed by repeated infarction rather than removed; parents should seek care for a fever of 101.3 degrees Fahrenheit or higher, an increase in spleen size, or severe pallor, and most experts recommend stopping prophylaxis after age 5.

Q: A 9-year-old with sickle cell disease wants to join a sports team and take a family trip. What should the nurse advise?
A) Avoid contact sports if the spleen is enlarged, and avoid low-oxygen environments such as high altitudes or nonpressurized flights
B) Avoid all physical activity permanently
C) Sports and travel carry no additional risk in this disease
D) Avoid air travel entirely, including commercial pressurized flights
Answer: A
Rationale: The chapter directs avoiding contact sports if the spleen is enlarged "because rupture will cause massive internal hemorrhage," and avoiding "environments with low oxygen concentration, such as high altitudes or nonpressurized airplane flights," along with frequent rest breaks during activity (p. 1041).
Trap: (B) removes activity the chapter accommodates with rest breaks; (D) overextends the restriction, since the named hazard is the nonpressurized flight. Note also that enuresis in this disease is a complication of impaired urine concentration rather than a behavior problem, so restricting nighttime fluids is the wrong response.

Topic: beta-thalassemia and aplastic anemia

Q: A nurse suggests checking the iron level of a child with beta-thalassemia "to make sure he is not anemic." How should this be corrected?
A) These children are treated for iron overload with chelation, so the concern is too much iron rather than too little
B) Iron levels are not measurable in thalassemia
C) The suggestion is correct, since thalassemia is an iron deficiency disorder
D) Iron supplementation should be started empirically instead
Answer: A
Rationale: The chapter's trap note and its own review question turn on this reversal: children with thalassemia receive repeated transfusions and are managed with chelation for iron overload, so the direction of the problem is excess rather than deficiency (pp. 1045–1046).
Trap: (C) and (D) both act on the deficiency assumption, and (D) would actively worsen the overload; the anemia in thalassemia arises from defective globin chain synthesis rather than from a lack of available iron.

Q: A child with beta-thalassemia has excess iron storage without tissue injury. Which term applies?
A) Hemosiderosis
B) Hemochromatosis
C) Hemolysis
D) Hemarthrosis
Answer: A
Rationale: The chapter defines hemosiderosis as excess iron storage "but without associated tissue injury," and hemochromatosis as excess storage "that results in cellular damage" (p. 1046).
Trap: (B) is the same process one step further along, and the single word separating them is damage; the two are routinely used interchangeably in speech, which is why the chapter distinguishes them.

Q: A bone marrow aspirate in a child with pancytopenia shows a hypoplastic marrow. Which diagnosis does this support?
A) Aplastic anemia
B) Leukemia
C) Iron deficiency anemia
D) Immune thrombocytopenia
Answer: A
Rationale: The chapter contrasts the two directly: "In leukemia the bone marrow is hyperplastic (producing increased numbers of cells), whereas in aplastic anemia the bone marrow is hypoplastic... or aplastic" (p. 1026).
Trap: (B) produces the same peripheral pancytopenia by a different marrow mechanism, which is why marrow examination rather than the blood count settles it; (D) shows normal marrow with normal or increased immature megakaryocytes.

Q: A child is being evaluated for severe aplastic anemia. Which findings would meet the criteria the chapter names?
A) Marrow cellularity under 25% plus at least two of neutrophils under 500, platelets under 20,000, and absolute reticulocytes under 40,000
B) Marrow cellularity under 50% alone
C) A hemoglobin under 7 g/dl alone
D) Pancytopenia of any degree with a normal marrow
Answer: A
Rationale: Camitta's criteria for severe aplastic anemia require marrow cellularity below 25% plus at least two of an absolute neutrophil count below 500/mm3, platelets below 20,000/mm3, and absolute reticulocytes below 40,000/mm3, with moderate disease defined by cellularity below 50% (p. 1048).
Trap: (B) names the moderate threshold as though it were the severe one; (D) describes a picture inconsistent with the diagnosis, since a normal marrow points away from marrow failure.

Topic: hemophilia and von willebrand disease

Q: A 2-year-old boy has recurrent bleeding into the knees and ankles but no petechiae. Which disorder does this pattern suggest, and why do petechiae not appear?
A) Hemophilia, because repair of small hemorrhages depends on platelet function rather than on clotting mechanisms
B) Immune thrombocytopenia, because platelet counts are low
C) Von Willebrand disease, because mucous membrane bleeding predominates
D) Henoch-Schönlein purpura, because joints are involved
Answer: A
Rationale: The chapter states that "Petechiae are uncommon in persons with hemophilia because repair of small hemorrhages depends on platelet function, not on blood-clotting mechanisms," and names hemarthrosis "the most frequent form of internal bleeding," especially knees, elbows, and ankles (pp. 1051–1052).
Trap: (C) names the right family of disorders with the wrong bleeding site, since von Willebrand disease is characterized by mucous membrane bleeding, most commonly nosebleeds; this one sentence about petechiae is the whole hemophilia-versus-thrombocytopenia discriminator.

Q: A child has factor VIII activity of 0.5%. What bleeding pattern should the nurse expect?
A) Spontaneous bleeding without trauma
B) Bleeding only with severe trauma or surgery
C) Bleeding only with moderate trauma
D) No abnormal bleeding
Answer: A
Rationale: Table 28.6 grades severity by factor activity: below 1% is severe with "Spontaneous bleeding without trauma," 1% to 5% is moderate with bleeding after trauma, and above 5% to 40% is mild with bleeding after severe trauma or surgery (p. 1051).
Trap: (B) describes the mild band; note that 60% to 70% of children with hemophilia have the severe form, so spontaneous bleeding is the common presentation rather than the exception.

Q: A child with hemophilia has an acute hemarthrosis of the knee. Which measures are appropriate? Select all that apply.
A) Factor replacement
B) Rest, ice, compression, and elevation
C) Active range-of-motion exercises once bleeding is controlled
D) Acetaminophen for pain
E) Passive range-of-motion exercises to preserve joint mobility
Answer: A, B, C, D
Rationale: The chapter names factor replacement as primary therapy, describes the RICE mnemonic as a supportive measure, directs that "Active range-of-motion exercises are best so that the patient can gauge his or her own pain tolerance," and names acetaminophen as the aspirin substitute (pp. 1052–1054).
Trap: (E) is forbidden by a Nursing Alert — "Passive range-of-motion exercises should never be part of an exercise regimen after an acute episode because the joint capsule could easily be stretched and bleeding could recur." Note also that the supportive measures "do not take the place of factor replacement," which is the option the chapter's own review question plants.

Q: An adolescent girl has frequent nosebleeds and heavy menses, and her father has the same bleeding pattern. Which disorder does this suggest?
A) Von Willebrand disease, which is autosomal dominant and affects both males and females
B) Hemophilia A, which is X-linked recessive
C) Hemophilia B, which is X-linked recessive
D) Immune thrombocytopenia, which is acquired
Answer: A
Rationale: The chapter states that "Unlike hemophilia, vWD affects both males and females because its inheritance shows an autosomal dominant pattern," with mucous membrane bleeding, most commonly nosebleeds, as its characteristic feature (p. 1055).
Trap: (B) and (C) are both X-linked recessive, so an affected father transmitting to a daughter in this pattern does not fit; the two clues doing the work here are the sex distribution and the bleeding site.

Q: A child with mild hemophilia A is scheduled for a dental extraction. Which treatment does the chapter identify as the treatment of choice?
A) Desmopressin, if the child responds with a threefold to fourfold rise in factor VIII activity
B) Cryoprecipitate, which remains the standard for factor VIII deficiency
C) Aspirin for procedural pain
D) Fresh frozen plasma as the primary replacement
Answer: A
Rationale: The chapter names desmopressin "the treatment of choice in mild hemophilia and vWD (types I and IIA only)" if the child responds, with a threefold to fourfold rise in factor VIII activity expected, noting that patients with moderate deficiency do not benefit because the goal is to raise the level at least 30% (p. 1052).
Trap: (B) is ruled out by a Drug Alert — "Cryoprecipitate is no longer recommended for use in treating factor VIII deficiency" — because it cannot be treated to safely eliminate hepatitis or human immunodeficiency virus; (C) names a drug contraindicated in this disease.

Topic: immune thrombocytopenia and henoch-schonlein purpura

Q: A 4-year-old develops petechiae and easy bruising 2 weeks after a viral illness, with a platelet count of 18,000/mm3 and a normal bone marrow. What is the likely diagnosis?
A) Immune thrombocytopenia
B) Aplastic anemia
C) Hemophilia
D) Disseminated intravascular coagulation
Answer: A
Rationale: The chapter describes immune thrombocytopenia as an acquired disorder with thrombocytopenia and "normal bone marrow with a normal or increased number of immature megakaryocytes," usually presenting 1 to 4 weeks after a viral illness with peak incidence between ages 1 and 6, and notes that "Symptomatic bleeding does not usually occur until the platelet count is lower than 20,000/mm3" (p. 1055).
Trap: (B) shares the low platelet count but has a hypoplastic marrow and involves all three cell lines; the normal marrow in the stem is what separates a destruction problem from a production problem.

Q: A nurse is teaching the family of a child with immune thrombocytopenia whose platelet count is 38,000/mm3. Which instructions are correct? Select all that apply.
A) Restrict contact sports while the platelet count is below 50,000/mm3
B) Avoid bike riding, skateboarding, and gymnastics
C) Seek prompt medical evaluation for head or abdominal trauma
D) Use acetaminophen rather than aspirin or other nonsteroidal drugs for pain
E) Use ibuprofen for fever, since it is safer than aspirin in this disorder
Answer: A, B, C, D
Rationale: The chapter directs "restriction of contact sports while the child's platelet count is less than 50,000/mm3," lists bike riding, skateboarding, in-line skating, gymnastics, climbing, and running among restricted activities, directs prompt evaluation for head or abdominal trauma, and names acetaminophen as the substitute (p. 1056).
Trap: (E) names a nonsteroidal drug, which a Drug Alert rules out along with aspirin because both "have critical harmful effects for these children"; note that 50,000 governs activity while 20,000 governs when symptomatic bleeding begins — two numbers for two different decisions.

Q: A child with immune thrombocytopenia is actively bleeding. Which treatment is inappropriate at this moment?
A) Anti-D antibody, because the platelet count does not rise until approximately 48 hours after infusion
B) Intravenous immunoglobulin
C) Prednisone
D) Platelet transfusion for severe hemorrhage
Answer: A
Rationale: The chapter states that with anti-D antibody the platelet count rises "approximately 48 hours after infusion; therefore it is not appropriate therapy for patients who are actively bleeding" (p. 1055).
Trap: (B) and (C) are the two agents that pull hardest, because both are standard options in this disorder and neither carries the delay that disqualifies the keyed answer here; anti-D antibody otherwise has real advantages, a single dose over 5 to 10 minutes and significantly less expense than immunoglobulin, which is exactly why the timing caveat is worth holding. None of the three "are curative therapies," and the disorder is otherwise managed supportively.

Q: A 5-year-old has symmetric purpura on the buttocks and lower extremities, knee and ankle arthritis, colicky abdominal pain, and a normal platelet count. What is the likely diagnosis, and what is the most serious long-term concern?
A) Henoch-Schönlein purpura, with renal involvement occurring in up to 50% of affected children
B) Immune thrombocytopenia, with intracranial hemorrhage
C) Hemophilia, with joint destruction
D) Disseminated intravascular coagulation, with organ failure
Answer: A
Rationale: The chapter describes Henoch-Schönlein purpura as a nonthrombocytopenic purpura with arthritis, nephritis, and abdominal pain, with "a symmetric purpura that involves the buttocks and lower extremities," and states that "Renal involvement occurs in up to 50% of affected children and is potentially the most serious long-term complication" (pp. 1057–1059).
Trap: (B) is the confusable partner, and the normal platelet count is the discriminator — the purpura here comes from vasculitis rather than from too few platelets. Increased immunoglobulin A levels are a frequent finding, and children with nephritis need long-term follow-up.

Topic: neutropenia in children

Q: A 3-year-old has an absolute neutrophil count of 1200/mm3. How should this be classified?
A) Neutropenia, since the threshold for children older than 1 year is below 1500/mm3
B) Severe neutropenia, since the count is below 1500/mm3
C) Normal, since the threshold is below 1000/mm3 at all ages
D) Normal, since only counts below 500/mm3 are abnormal
Answer: A
Rationale: The chapter defines neutropenia as an absolute neutrophil count below 1000/mm3 in infants 2 weeks to 1 year and below 1500/mm3 in children older than 1 year, with severe neutropenia below 500/mm3 "with an increased risk of life-threatening infection" (p. 1057).
Trap: (B) jumps to the severe tier, which is a threefold lower number and carries a different level of concern; the count is calculated by multiplying the total white blood cell count by the percentage of neutrophils and bands in the differential.

Q: An African American child has an absolute neutrophil count 400/mm3 below the usual reference range but is well. How should the nurse interpret this?
A) This may be a normal variant, since African American counts run 200 to 600/mm3 lower
B) This confirms chronic benign neutropenia and requires treatment
C) This requires immediate bone marrow biopsy
D) This requires prophylactic antibiotics
Answer: A
Rationale: The chapter states that "African Americans have ANCs that are 200 to 600/mm3 lower than those of Caucasians," which is a normal-variant consideration rather than a disease (p. 1057).
Trap: (B), (C), and (D) each escalate a finding that may require nothing; note that in chronic benign neutropenia specifically, "most children have no infections despite the markedly reduced ANC," and therapy to increase the count "is rarely required."

Q: A child with chronic benign neutropenia is due for routine immunizations. What should the nurse advise?
A) Give the routine childhood immunizations, since these children have normal cellular immunity
B) Withhold all immunizations until the count normalizes
C) Give only inactivated vaccines
D) Withhold immunizations permanently
Answer: A
Rationale: The chapter states that children with chronic benign neutropenia "have normal cellular immunity, therefore they should receive their routine childhood immunizations" (p. 1057).
Trap: (C) applies the live-vaccine caution that belongs to true immunodeficiency; the distinction is that this disorder affects neutrophil numbers without impairing the cellular immunity that determines live vaccine safety.

Q: A nurse is teaching the family of a child with neutropenia when to seek care. What should be emphasized?
A) Seek medical attention for a fever of 101.3 degrees Fahrenheit or higher, or if skin lesions develop
B) Seek care only if the fever exceeds 103 degrees Fahrenheit
C) Seek care only if the child appears ill regardless of temperature
D) Fever in neutropenia is expected and does not require evaluation
Answer: A
Rationale: The chapter directs seeking medical attention for a fever of 101.3 degrees Fahrenheit or higher or if skin lesions develop, and notes that "Oral ulcerations and skin infections are the most common manifestation of chronic benign neutropenia" (p. 1057).
Trap: (B) raises a threshold that recurs throughout this chapter — the same 101.3 degrees appears for postsplenectomy patients, sickle cell patients on penicillin prophylaxis, and neutropenic children; families are also taught to avoid large indoor crowds and ill contacts.

Topic: pediatric hiv infection

Q: A 6-month-old born to a mother with human immunodeficiency virus has a positive antibody test. How should this be interpreted?
A) The result likely reflects maternal antibodies, which may persist up to 18 months, so polymerase chain reaction for proviral DNA is needed
B) The infant is infected, since the antibody test is definitive at any age
C) The infant is uninfected, since antibody tests are unreliable in infancy
D) The test should be repeated at 24 months before any further evaluation
Answer: A
Rationale: The chapter states that in children younger than 18 months antibody tests are positive from maternal antibodies, that "Maternal antibodies may persist in the infant for up to 18 months," and directs using "the HIV polymerase chain reaction (PCR) for detection of proviral DNA" (p. 1062).
Trap: (B) treats a maternal marker as an infant diagnosis, which would misclassify most exposed but uninfected infants; (D) delays a diagnosis that can be made now with the correct test.

Q: A nurse is explaining how the human immunodeficiency virus causes disease. Which statement is accurate?
A) It primarily infects CD4+ T cells, suppressing cell-mediated immunity and producing opportunistic infection, and also causes B-cell dysfunction
B) It primarily infects B cells, suppressing humoral immunity alone
C) It primarily infects neutrophils, producing bacterial infection
D) It destroys red cells, producing hemolytic anemia
Answer: A
Rationale: The chapter states that the virus "primarily infects a specific subset of T lymphocytes, the CD4+ T cells," rendering them dysfunctional, that suppression of cell-mediated immunity brings opportunistic infection, and that the virus also causes B-cell dysfunction suppressing humoral immunity (p. 1061).
Trap: (B) names only the second and later effect; the ordering matters clinically, because the opportunistic infections that define the illness follow from the cell-mediated arm failing first.

Q: A nurse is reviewing how pediatric human immunodeficiency virus differs from adult disease. Which statement is accurate?
A) Recurrent bacterial infections, parotitis, lymphoid interstitial pneumonitis, and early neurologic deterioration characterize children but are rare in adults
B) Kaposi sarcoma is more common in children than in adults
C) Receptive language is more impaired than expressive language in children
D) Latency is longer in perinatally infected children than in adults
Answer: A
Rationale: The chapter states that recurrent bacterial infections, parotitis, lymphoid interstitial pneumonitis, and early progressive neurologic deterioration "are characteristic of children with HIV infection but are rarely seen in affected adults" (p. 1061).
Trap: (B) reverses the finding, since Kaposi sarcoma is "found in fewer than 1% of affected children"; (C) reverses the language pattern, since expressive language is more frequently impaired; and (D) reverses the course, since "A more rapid progression of disease tend to occur in perinatally infected children."

Q: A pregnant woman with human immunodeficiency virus asks about the risk to her baby. What should the nurse tell her?
A) With combination antiretroviral therapy, elective cesarean delivery, and avoidance of breastfeeding, perinatal transmission falls to less than 1% to 2%
B) Transmission is unavoidable and occurs in all pregnancies
C) Transmission risk is unaffected by treatment during pregnancy
D) Breastfeeding reduces transmission by providing maternal antibodies
Answer: A
Rationale: The chapter states that untreated transmission rates were 12% to 30% in the United States and Europe, and that with combination antiretroviral therapy, elective cesarean delivery, and avoidance of breastfeeding, perinatal transmission fell to "less than 1% to 2%" (p. 1061).
Trap: (D) reverses the role of breastfeeding, which is a transmission route rather than a protective one here; note that "Perinatal transmission accounts for almost all HIV infections in young children in the United States," which is why this counseling matters so much.
