# Chapter 12 drills — Health Promotion of the Toddler and Family

Authored from `sources/Ch12_Fact_Bank.md` (Hockenberry, Wilson & Rodgers, 11th ed.,
chapter author Elizabeth A. Duffy, printed pp. 393–422).

Blueprint weight is **7 questions of 202 (3.5%), depth `standard`** — 45 drills
across 11 topics, at the top of the 30–45 band.

This is the second rung of the developmental ladder, written to the same shape as
Chapter 10 so the two stages compare directly: growth, motor, Erikson, Piaget,
social, parental concerns, health promotion, injury prevention. Every topic name
is toddler-qualified for the same reason Chapter 10's are infant-qualified —
chapters 13, 15, and 17 will bank the same categories for later stages, and an
unqualified `motor development` or `injury prevention` would merge five stages
into one dashboard entry.

**Where the weight went.** The fact bank names the chapter's testable core as the
behaviors that define the stage and the nursing responses to them, so **toilet
training gets the largest topic** (six questions, the chapter's signature subject
and the only one with an explicit readiness checklist), and **preoperational
thought gets the other six** — Box 12.1's characteristics explain nearly every
toddler behavior an exam will describe, and each one ships with the book's own
example.

**Drilled from both ends:** sensorimotor stage V and stage VI object permanence,
physiologic sphincter control at 18 to 24 months against complete readiness at 24
to 30 months, bowel before bladder, daytime against nighttime dryness, and the
normal-versus-abnormal tantrum criteria. Centration and transductive reasoning are
drilled against each other in adjacent items because Box 12.1 gives them
near-identical food-refusal examples, which is precisely what makes them swappable.

**Revised after the first pass.** This chapter first went in with car restraints
as its only safety topic, because the fact bank had recorded the other injury
categories as a deliberate omission. That left it drilling the epidemiology —
unintentional injury is the leading cause of death at ages 1 to 4, and **drowning
is the leading cause of accidental death in that age group** — while teaching
nothing a parent could act on about the hazard that kills most of these children.
All five categories were present in the raw pull, so the **fact bank was expanded**
(§10: drowning p. 416, burns p. 417, poisoning p. 418, aspiration and bodily harm
p. 419, page cites re-derived from the raw markers, validated and cite-swept clean)
and a `toddler drowning, burn, and poisoning prevention` topic was added here. To
hold the band, **three age-lookup items were removed** — the potbellied-abdomen
reassurance, casting at 15 months, and the girls-versus-boys toilet-training
interval — none of which is a flagged confusable pair and none of which decides an
action. The swap was free because no attempts existed yet.

**What twenty-nine printed pages could not buy at 45 questions.** Left undrilled:
Table 12.2's month-by-month milestone grid; Table 12.3's feeding milestones beyond
the spoon progression; the dietary guideline tables, vegetarian diet guidance, and
iron absorption factors; the named harmful herbs; sleep hours and bedtime-ritual
technique; flossing and the cariogenic-food discussion; temperament; and moral and
spiritual development beyond the preconventional principle. Of the injury
categories now added, **aspiration and suffocation is the one still drilled only
in passing** — its content largely repeats Chapter 10's, which is where it is
drilled in full.

`toddler gross and fine motor sequence` carries two questions rather than four,
having given up both casting and shape-fitting. It still names a real restudy
unit, and the alternative was cutting a drowning or poisoning item to protect a
topic's headcount.

Six questions are select-all, one for each list in the chapter that genuinely
sorts two ways: the tasks of toddlerhood against a later stage's task, readiness
signs against the family stressors that defer training, abnormal tantrum features
against ordinary ones, caries prevention against the practice that causes it,
car-restraint misuse against correct belt positioning, and kitchen burn hazards
against the one item stored correctly.

Topic: toddler growth parameters and sensory development

Q: A healthy 2-year-old weighs 12 kg and measures 86.6 cm. The parent asks what to expect over the next year. Which statement reflects the chapter's growth landmarks?
A) Birth weight is quadrupled by 2½ years old, and adult height is about twice the child's height at 2 years of age
B) Birth weight is tripled by 2 years old, and adult height is about three times the height at 2 years
C) Birth weight is quadrupled by 12 months, and growth during toddlerhood accelerates over infancy
D) Weight gain during toddlerhood averages 4 to 6 kg per year
Answer: A
Rationale: "The birth weight is quadrupled by [2½] years old," and "adult height is about twice the child's height at 2 years of age." Weight gain runs "1.8 to 2.7 kg (4 to 6 lb) per year" and height "7.5 cm (3 inches) per year," mainly in elongation of the legs rather than the trunk. The chapter opens by saying that "Physical growth slows considerably during toddlerhood," and the growth curve should be "steplike rather than linear (straight)."
Trap: (C) borrows infancy's tripling landmark, moves it earlier, and inverts the chapter's opening statement — growth slows sharply in toddlerhood rather than accelerating. (D) swaps pounds for kilograms in the annual gain, which would be more than double the true figure.

Q: A 15-month-old's chest circumference now slightly exceeds head circumference, and the anterior fontanel is no longer palpable. How should the nurse interpret these findings?
A) Both are expected; head circumference equals chest circumference at 1 to 2 years, and the anterior fontanel closes between 12 and 18 months
B) The closed anterior fontanel is premature and requires evaluation
C) Chest circumference should not exceed head circumference until 3 years of age
D) The anterior fontanel normally closes by 8 weeks, so this closure is late
Answer: A
Rationale: Head circumference is "equal to chest circumference by 1 to 2 years of age," with a total second-year increase of "2.5 cm (1 inch)." The anterior fontanel "closes between 12 and 18 months of age."
Trap: (D) is the posterior fontanel's timing, which is 6 to 8 weeks; attaching that figure to the anterior fontanel is the commonest error carried forward from the infant chapter, where both numbers are taught together.

Q: A 3-year-old's vision screening shows acuity of 20/40, and the nurse notes a persistent inward deviation of one eye. What are the correct interpretations?
A) The acuity is acceptable for the toddler years, but persistent strabismus should receive professional attention as early as possible to prevent amblyopia
B) Both findings are normal and require only routine rescreening
C) The acuity is abnormal, and the strabismus is an expected finding at this age
D) The acuity is acceptable, and strabismus resolves spontaneously by school age
Answer: A
Rationale: "Visual acuity of 20/40 is considered acceptable during the toddler years." Full binocular vision is developed by this stage, and "any evidence of persistent strabismus should receive professional attention as early as possible to prevent amblyopia."
Trap: (C) reverses both halves, and it pulls because 20/40 sounds impaired by adult standards while a wandering eye looks like something toddlers grow out of. The chapter accepts the first finding and refuses to wait on the second.

Topic: toddler gross and fine motor sequence

Q: A 30-month-old climbs stairs placing one foot per step and jumps down from a low step. How should the nurse document this?
A) Delayed; alternate footing on stairs is expected by 18 months
B) Expected; climbing stairs with alternate footing and jumping from a chair or step appear by the end of the second year into 30 months
C) Advanced; alternate footing is not expected until 4 years of age
D) Expected, but jumping from a step indicates poor safety awareness requiring referral
Answer: B
Rationale: Toddlers walk up and down stairs at 2 years, and by the end of the second year can "stand on one foot, walk on tiptoe, and climb stairs with alternate footing," and jump "from chair or step." They walk alone with "a wide stance for extra balance" at 12 to 13 months, try to run and fall easily at 18 months, and at 2½ years "jump using both feet, stand on one foot for a second or two, and manage a few steps on tiptoe."
Trap: (A) pulls the skill nearly a year early, and 18 months is a memorable age because it carries running; the two milestones are a full year apart in the book's sequence.

Q: A 24-month-old builds a tower of six blocks and imitates a circular stroke with a crayon. Which assessment is correct?
A) Expected for 24 months; a tower of six or seven blocks and imitation of a circular stroke and a vertical line belong to this age
B) Delayed; a tower of eight or more blocks is expected at 24 months
C) Advanced; six blocks is the 30-month milestone
D) Delayed; copying a circle is expected at 24 months
Answer: A
Rationale: Toddlers build a tower of three or four blocks at 18 months, six or seven at 24 months, and eight or more at 30 months. At 24 months they "imitate a circular stroke and a vertical line"; copying a circle and imitating a cross come at the end of toddlerhood.
Trap: (D) confuses imitating with copying, which the book separates by roughly a year. Imitation follows a demonstration; copying works from a finished model alone, and it is the harder task.

Topic: autonomy versus shame and doubt in the toddler

Q: A 2-year-old insists on putting on their own shoes, refuses all help, and shouts "me do" throughout the visit. Which developmental task does this represent?
A) Trust versus mistrust, with grasping and biting as the social modality
B) Autonomy versus shame and doubt, with holding on and letting go as the newly gained modality
C) Initiative versus guilt, with intrusion as the social modality
D) Industry versus inferiority, expressed through completing tasks
Answer: B
Rationale: "The developmental task of toddlerhood is acquiring a sense of autonomy while overcoming a sense of doubt and shame," and "the toddler has the newly gained modality of holding on and letting go," evident "in how the toddler uses the hands, mouth, eyes, and, eventually, sphincters when toilet training is begun."
Trap: (A) belongs to infancy and (C) to the preschooler, and the three run in sequence, so they are routinely attached to the wrong age. Note the prerequisite the chapter names: "If the need for basic trust has been satisfied, they are ready to give up dependence for control, independence, and autonomy."

Q: Which achievements does the chapter name among the tasks of toddlerhood? Select all that apply.
A) Differentiation of self from others, particularly the primary caregiver
B) Toleration of separation from parents
C) Ability to withstand delayed gratification
D) Development of a sense of industry through mastery of school tasks
E) Control over bodily functions
Answer: A, B, C, E
Rationale: The tasks of toddlerhood are "Differentiation of self from others, particularly the mother or primary caregiver; Toleration of separation from parents; Ability to withstand delayed gratification; Control over bodily functions; Acquisition of socially acceptable behavior; Verbal means of communication; Ability to interact with others in a less egocentric manner."
Trap: (D) is the one option from the wrong side of the sort and names the school-age task rather than the toddler's. It pulls because Erikson's stages are usually recited in that phrasing, and this is a task list rather than a stage list.

Q: A parent asks why a 2-year-old needs firm limits when "he's only little." Which reasoning does the chapter give?
A) Without appropriate limits on acceptable versus unacceptable behavior, children have no guidelines for establishing the end-points of their ability to control
B) Limits are primarily for the parent's convenience and can be deferred until the preschool years
C) Limits prevent autonomy from developing, which is what reduces negativism
D) Limits are needed because toddlers cannot yet experience shame or doubt
Answer: A
Rationale: The toddler's doubt is compounded by shame and by "a fear that they will exceed their own capacity for manipulating the environment. The latter fear is a basis for instituting limit setting and consistent discipline at this age. Without appropriate limits on what is acceptable versus unacceptable behavior, children have no guidelines for establishing the end-points of their ability to control."
Trap: (C) inverts the purpose entirely: limits are what make autonomy safe to exercise, not what suppresses it. That inversion is why parents who fear stifling a child abandon limits at exactly the age they are most needed.

Q: A parent says their 2-year-old answers "no" to everything, including things the child clearly wants, and asks how to stop the defiance. Which response is best?
A) The negativism is not an expression of being stubborn or disrespectful but a necessary assertion of control; reduce the opportunities for a negative answer by stating what will happen rather than asking
B) Confront each refusal consistently, so the child learns that refusing is unacceptable
C) Give in to the refusals, since resisting them prolongs the phase
D) The behavior indicates a failure of trust during infancy and warrants referral
Answer: A
Rationale: "The negativism is not an expression of being stubborn or disrespectful, but a necessary assertion of control." The technique is "reducing the opportunities for a 'no' answer": rather than asking whether the child wants to go to sleep now, "tell the child when it will be time to go to sleep (preferably within a specific time frame, such as 'after reading a story')." Real choices between two acceptable options work, and "if their response is negative, parents should make the choice for the child."
Trap: (C) is what many exhausted parents do, and the chapter names the cost: giving in "further threatens the child's search for acceptable methods of interacting with others." (B) turns every interaction into a contest, which is the opposite of reducing the openings for refusal.

Q: A 2-year-old admitted to the hospital begins wetting the bed and demanding a bottle, having given up both months ago. Which explanation does the chapter give?
A) Ritualism provides comfort, and without the familiar routines the child has little opportunity to exert autonomy, so dependency and regression occur
B) The child has developed a urinary tract infection and the behavior is physiologic
C) The child is deliberately manipulating the staff for attention
D) The child has permanently regressed to the trust versus mistrust stage
Answer: A
Rationale: Ritualism is "the need to maintain sameness and reliability," which "provides a sense of comfort. Toddlers can venture out with security when they know that familiar people, places, and routines still exist." The chapter draws the conclusion directly: "One can easily understand why change, such as hospitalization, represents such a threat to these children. Without the comfortable rituals, they have little opportunity to exert autonomy. Consequently, dependency and regression occur."
Trap: (C) treats regression as a strategy, which the chapter contradicts: "the loss of newly acquired achievements is frightening and threatening" to the child rather than rewarding. The management is to ignore the regression while praising existing appropriate behavior.

Topic: preoperational thought and toddler cognition
Subtopic: object permanence in sensorimotor stages V and VI

Q: A 16-month-old watches a nurse hide a toy under a blanket, then watches it moved to a second hiding place. The child searches only where it was hidden first. Which stage does this represent?
A) Stage V, tertiary circular reactions, in which the child can find hidden objects but only in the first location
B) Stage VI, in which the child searches for an object through several hiding places
C) Stage IV, in which object permanence has not yet begun
D) Preoperational thought, in which object permanence is complete
Answer: A
Rationale: Table 12.1 gives stage V, tertiary circular reactions, at 13 to 18 months: "Can find hidden objects, but only in first location," alongside "Active experimentation to achieve previously unattainable goals" and the realization that "'out of sight' is not out of reach; opens doors and drawers to find objects."
Trap: (B) is the next stage, and the age gap is only a few months, which is what makes the pair swappable. Searching through several hiding places requires awareness of object permanence "regardless of number of invisible displacements," which is stage VI at 19 to 24 months.

Q: A 22-month-old watches the same sequence and searches the first hiding place, then the second, then a third location entirely. Which stage does this represent?
A) Stage V, tertiary circular reactions
B) Stage VI, invention of new means through mental combinations, with awareness of object permanence regardless of the number of invisible displacements
C) Stage IV, coordination of secondary schemas
D) Concrete operational thought
Answer: B
Rationale: Stage VI, at 19 to 24 months, brings "Awareness of object permanence regardless of number of invisible displacements," so the child "Searches for object through several hiding places." Its other listed behaviors are domestic mimicry, referring to self by name, parallel play with awareness of ownership, and concern with a ritualistic, routinized schedule.
Trap: (A) is the mirror error of the previous item. The discriminator is not whether the child searches but whether the search survives being wrong the first time.

Subtopic: characteristics of preoperational thought

Q: A 3-year-old refuses to eat mashed potatoes because they are white, though the child accepts their taste and smell without complaint. Which characteristic of preoperational thought does this illustrate?
A) Centration, focusing on one aspect rather than considering all possible alternatives
B) Transductive reasoning, reasoning from the particular to the particular
C) Animism, attributing lifelike qualities to inanimate objects
D) Irreversibility, the inability to undo or reverse actions initiated physically
Answer: A
Rationale: Box 12.1 gives this example verbatim under centration: the "Child refuses to eat a food because of its color, even though its taste and smell are acceptable."
Trap: (B) is the neighbouring food example in the same box and names a different error. A child who "refuses to eat a food because something previously eaten did not taste good" is reasoning from one particular to another. Here nothing previously eaten is at issue; one attribute has crowded out all the rest.

Q: A 3-year-old whose bed was moved to the other side of the bedroom refuses to sleep there, and later scolds the stairs for making her fall. Which two characteristics do these behaviors illustrate?
A) Global organization and animism
B) Centration and magical thinking
C) Egocentrism and irreversibility
D) Transductive reasoning and inability to conserve
Answer: A
Rationale: Box 12.1 defines global organization as the "Belief that a change in any one part of the whole changes the entire whole," illustrated when a "Child refuses to sleep in room because location of bed is changed." Animism is "Attributing lifelike qualities to inanimate objects," illustrated when a "Child scolds stairs for making child fall down."
Trap: (B) is the commonest substitution, because centration also produces a refusal and magical thinking also assigns intent to the world. Centration is about attending to one attribute of a thing; global organization is about the entire whole being spoiled by a single change.

Q: A parent, frustrated after a spill, tells their 3-year-old "you are a bad boy." Which concern should the nurse raise?
A) Magical thinking makes the label literal; calling children bad because they did something wrong makes children feel as though they are bad
B) The child will not understand the word, so no harm is done
C) The label is effective discipline at this age, because moral reasoning is preconventional
D) The child will reverse the statement independently, since irreversibility resolves by age 3
Answer: A
Rationale: Box 12.1 gives magical thinking as "Believing that thoughts are all-powerful and can cause events," and its example includes that "Calling children 'bad' because they did something wrong makes children feel as though they are bad." The chapter warns elsewhere that negative labels about the body "are internalized and can last a lifetime."
Trap: (C) borrows a true fact and misapplies it. Moral development at this age is preconventional, so "whether an action is good or bad depends on whether it results in reward or punishment" — which is a reason to make the consequence of the act clear, not a licence to label the child.

Subtopic: why toddlers get injured

Q: A 2-year-old who received a shock from an electrical outlet is now seen poking and inspecting every other outlet in the house, while avoiding the one that shocked him. What does this tell the nurse about safety planning?
A) The child has learned the lesson, so the remaining outlets can be left uncovered
B) The child cannot transfer information from one situation to another, which leaves toddlers particularly vulnerable to injury; the hazard must be removed rather than the judgment expected
C) The child is deliberately testing the parents' limits and needs a time-out
D) Avoiding the original outlet proves that memory is fully developed at this stage
Answer: B
Rationale: After such a shock, "they will adamantly poke and inspect every other outlet... This inability to transfer information leaves toddlers particularly vulnerable to injuries. However, traces of memory are evident because they usually avoid the outlet where the shock occurred." The same principle governs containers: a toddler "does not perceive a difference between that toy receptacle and the garbage pail or laundry basket," so "the forbidden object, such as the garbage pail, should be placed out of reach."
Trap: (A) is exactly the inference the avoided outlet invites, and it is the dangerous one — the memory is specific to that outlet and generalizes to nothing. Expecting toddlers "to judge which receptacles are permissible to explore and which are not is inappropriate for this age-group."

Topic: toddler body image, separation, and social development

Q: A 2-year-old screams and struggles violently during an otoscopic examination and while an axillary temperature is taken. Which explanation is correct?
A) The procedures are painful, and analgesia should be offered before repeating them
B) Body integrity is poorly understood in young children and intrusive experiences are threatening; the procedure does not hurt but represents an intrusion into personal space
C) The behavior indicates a previous traumatic hospitalization
D) The behavior indicates delayed social development for the age
Answer: B
Rationale: "Body integrity is poorly understood in young children and... intrusive experiences are threatening. For example, toddlers forcefully resist procedures such as examining the ear or mouth and taking an axillary temperature. The procedure itself... does not hurt the child, but it represents an intrusion into the child's personal space, which elicits a strong protest."
Trap: (A) reads the protest as pain, which is the natural interpretation of the behavior and leads to the wrong interventions. Toddlers also have unclear body boundaries and may be "upset by flushing the toilet and watching the stool disappear," for the same reason.

Q: During a well-child visit a 2-year-old goes readily to the nurse, shows no distress when the parent leaves the room, and approaches every adult in the hallway. What should the nurse conclude?
A) The child is unusually well adjusted and the parent should be praised
B) Indiscriminate friendliness toward strangers and lack of anxiety during separation from parents may be reasons for concern
C) This is expected, since toddlers lose all stranger fear by 2 years
D) This indicates the child has achieved autonomy ahead of schedule
Answer: B
Rationale: "Indiscriminate friendliness toward strangers and lack of anxiety during separation from parents may be reasons for concern." Toddlers "show less fear of strangers, but only when their parents are present. When left alone with a stranger, they are fearful and acutely anxious; manifest depressive behavior... and may become restless, hyperactive, or passive, reverting to regressive behaviors."
Trap: (A) is the reading almost every observer makes, because an easy child looks like a secure one. The chapter reverses it, and the same pattern appears in Chapter 11 as disinhibited social engagement disorder.

Q: A 2½-year-old brings a worn blanket to every clinic visit and clutches it during the examination. The parent asks whether they should take it away to encourage independence. Which response is correct?
A) Transitional objects provide security when children are separated from parents, dealing with a new stress, or fatigued; such behavior is normal and there is no need to discourage it
B) The object should be removed at 2 years to prevent dependency
C) The object should be replaced with a toy that fosters creative thinking
D) Attachment to an object at this age suggests insecure attachment and warrants assessment
Answer: A
Rationale: Transitional objects "provide security for young children, especially when they are separated from parents, are dealing with a new stress, or are just fatigued... Such behavior is normal; there is no need to discourage this tendency."
Trap: (B) is the well-meant push toward independence that the chapter explicitly turns down, and the setting here — a clinic visit — is precisely the circumstance in which the object does its work.

Q: A toddler in the playroom repeatedly wanders a short distance from the parent and then returns to touch them before wandering off again. Which process does this represent, and what does it require of the parent?
A) Rapprochement, in which the child moves away and returns for reassurance; if the parent's response is inappropriate, the toddler may experience insecurity and confusion
B) Individuation, in which the child emerges from symbiotic fusion with the mother
C) Separation anxiety, requiring the parent to keep the child physically close at all times
D) Negativism, requiring the parent to reduce the opportunities for refusal
Answer: A
Rationale: Rapprochement is when "the child moves away and returns for reassurance. If the mother's response to the toddler is inappropriate, the toddler may experience insecurity and confusion." Separation is "the children's emergence from a symbiotic fusion with the mother"; individuation is "those achievements that mark children's assumption of their individual characteristics in the environment."
Trap: (B) swaps the two definitions — emerging from symbiotic fusion is separation, not individuation. (C) prescribes the infant's remedy for a toddler, where "Verbal and visual reassurance from the parent gradually replaces some of the previous need to be physically close for comfort."

Topic: toddler language and play

Q: A 2-year-old uses about 300 words in two- and three-word sentences, and a visiting grandparent understands roughly two-thirds of what the child says. How should the nurse interpret this?
A) Expected; a 2-year-old has approximately 300 words, and approximately 65% of the speech is understandable
B) Delayed; a 2-year-old should have about 900 words and be fully intelligible
C) Advanced; 300 words is the 3-year milestone
D) Concerning; intelligibility below 90% at 2 years warrants a speech referral
Answer: A
Rationale: At 1 year the toddler has "about 4" words, with "about 25% of the vocalizations are intelligible." At 2 years there are "approximately 300" words, with "approximately 65% of the speech is understandable." By 3 years the child "acquires five or six new words daily." Throughout, "the ability to understand speech is much greater than the number of words the child can say."
Trap: (D) applies an adult standard for intelligibility, which is the standard most families apply. The book's expectation at 2 years is roughly two-thirds understandable, which is exactly what this child shows.

Q: Two 2-year-olds in a waiting room sit side by side, each absorbed in a separate toy, glancing at each other occasionally but not interacting. A parent worries that the children are not socializing. Which response is accurate?
A) This is parallel play — the toddler plays alongside, not with, other children — and it is the expected progression from the solitary play of infancy
B) This is solitary play and indicates delayed social development
C) This is associative play and is advanced for the age
D) Toddlers should be engaging in cooperative play by 2 years, so this warrants assessment
Answer: A
Rationale: "The solitary play of infancy progresses to parallel play: the toddler plays alongside, not with, other children." The chapter adds that "Imitation is one of the most distinguishing characteristics of play."
Trap: (D) applies a much later stage to a 2-year-old, and it is the reflex worry because side-by-side play looks like a failure to connect. Playing alongside is the developmental achievement here, not a shortfall.

Q: Parents of a 20-month-old ask about tablet use and which toys to buy. Which guidance reflects the chapter?
A) Digital media may be introduced between 18 and 24 months but should be viewed together with parents; simple toys that foster creative thinking are preferred over passive battery-operated ones, and the most educational toy is the one that fosters adult-child interaction in supportive, unconditional play
B) Unlimited screen time is acceptable at this age provided the programming is educational
C) Screen media of every kind, including video-chatting, are prohibited until age 2
D) Battery-operated and mechanical toys are preferred because they hold attention longer
Answer: A
Rationale: Screen media are discouraged "other than video-chatting for children younger than 18 months"; children 18 to 24 months "can be introduced to digital media consisting of high-quality programming/apps but should be viewed together with parents." Over age 2, total media time should be limited to "1 hour or less" of quality programming per day. The chapter prefers "simple toys that foster creative thinking (such as blocks, dolls, and clay), rather than passive toys that the child observes," and states that "The most educational toy is the one that fosters the interaction of an adult with a child in supportive, unconditional play. Toys are never substitutes for the attention of devoted caregivers."
Trap: (C) overstates the under-18-month rule by dropping the video-chatting exception the chapter deliberately carves out, and that exception is the easiest part of the guidance to over-remember away.

Topic: toilet training readiness and technique

Q: A parent wants to begin toilet training their 19-month-old, noting that the child now stays dry for a couple of hours. Which response is accurate?
A) Voluntary sphincter control is physiologically possible between 18 and 24 months, but physiologic and psychologic readiness is not complete until 24 to 30 months
B) Sphincter control is not physiologically possible until 3 years of age
C) Readiness is complete once the child can stay dry for 2 hours, so training should begin immediately
D) Training should have begun at 12 months, which is the recommended starting age
Answer: A
Rationale: "Voluntary control of the anal and urethral sphincters is achieved sometime after the child is walking, probably between ages 18 and 24 months," but "complex psychophysiologic factors are required for readiness. The child must be able to recognize the urge to let go and hold on and be able to communicate this sensation to the parent." Physiologic and psychologic readiness "is not complete until ages 24 to 30 months."
Trap: (C) treats one readiness marker as the whole assessment. Being "dry for at least 2 hours during the day" is one of three markers, alongside awareness of the urge to void or stool and interest in or motivation to use the toilet. (D) quotes the 1920s practice the chapter cites as history, not as recommendation.

Q: A nurse assesses a 26-month-old for toilet training readiness. Which findings indicate readiness? Select all that apply.
A) Staying dry for 2 hours, with a decreased number of wet diapers and waking dry from a nap
B) Expressing willingness to please the parent
C) Impatience with soiled or wet diapers, and a desire to be changed immediately
D) The family is moving to a new home next month and a new sibling is due
E) Ability to sit on the toilet for 5 to 8 minutes without fussing or getting off
Answer: A, B, C, E
Rationale: The Nursing Care Guidelines assess readiness across four domains. Physical readiness includes the "Ability to stay dry for 2 hours; decreased number of wet diapers; waking dry from nap." Psychologic readiness includes "Expressing willingness to please parent," the "Ability to sit on toilet for 5 to 8 minutes without fussing or getting off," and "Impatience with soiled or wet diapers; desire to be changed immediately." Parental readiness requires the "Absence of family stress or change, such as a divorce, moving, new sibling, or imminent vacation."
Trap: (D) is the one option from the wrong side of the sort, and it names two of the exact stressors the parental-readiness domain says must be absent. It pulls because readiness is normally assessed on the child alone, and this chapter makes the family's circumstances part of the criteria.

Q: A parent asks which should be trained first, bowel or bladder. Which response is correct, and why?
A) Bowel training is usually accomplished first because of its greater regularity and predictability, and because the sensation for defecation is stronger and easier for children to recognize
B) Bladder training is accomplished first because urination is more frequent, giving more practice opportunities
C) Both are trained simultaneously, since the same sphincter controls each
D) Nighttime bladder control is achieved first, then daytime control
Answer: A
Rationale: "Bowel training is usually accomplished before bladder training because of its greater regularity and predictability. The sensation for defecation is stronger than that for urination and easier for children to recognize."
Trap: (B) offers a plausible mechanism — more frequent events, more practice — for the wrong order. The chapter's reasoning turns on how recognizable the sensation is, not on how often it occurs. (D) inverts the day-night sequence, which runs the other way and by months to years.

Q: A nurse teaches a parent how to conduct toilet training practice sessions. Which instructions are correct?
A) Use a freestanding potty chair so the child can plant the feet firmly on the floor, which facilitates defecation; limit practice sessions to 5 to 8 minutes; and stay with the child
B) Seat the child on the adult toilet with the feet dangling, and leave the child alone for 20 minutes to concentrate
C) Keep practice sessions to at least 20 minutes so the child has time to produce a result
D) Dress the child in overalls with back fasteners, so that accidents are less likely
Answer: A
Rationale: A freestanding potty chair "allows children a feeling of security," and "Planting the feet firmly on the floor also facilitates defecation." With a regular toilet, "Placing a small bench under the feet helps stabilize the child's position," or the child may sit facing the toilet tank for added support. "Practice sessions should be limited to 5 to 8 minutes, and a parent should stay with the child." The child should be dressed in easily removed clothing.
Trap: (D) inverts the clothing guidance for a plausible reason, and it defeats a readiness skill the chapter lists — the "Fine motor skills to remove clothing." (C) more than doubles the session limit, and long sessions are how training turns into a contest of wills.

Q: A 4-year-old girl who has been reliably dry during the day still wets the bed most nights, and her parents ask whether something is wrong. Which response is accurate?
A) Nighttime control normally takes several months to years after daytime training begins, because the sleep cycle must mature; bedwetting is normal in girls up to age 4 and in boys up to age 5, and children without nighttime dryness by age 6 are likely to require intervention
B) Nighttime dryness should follow daytime dryness within a few weeks, so evaluation is needed now
C) Bedwetting is normal in both girls and boys until age 8
D) The child should be woken every 2 hours through the night until dryness is achieved
Answer: A
Rationale: Nighttime control "normally takes several months to years after daytime training begins... because the sleep cycle needs to mature so the child can awake in time to urinate." "Bedwetting is normal in girls up to age 4 years old and in boys up to age 5 years," and children without nighttime dryness "by the age of 6 years old are likely to require intervention."
Trap: (B) treats the two as one continuous process, and it is the assumption behind most premature referrals. Daytime dryness is the training target; nighttime dryness waits on the maturation of sleep, which no amount of training accelerates.

Topic: toddler tantrums, sibling rivalry, and regression

Q: A 2-year-old drops to the floor screaming when told it is time to leave the playground. The behavior is not injurious. What is the nurse's best advice to the parent?
A) Ignore the behavior while continuing to be present, which provides a feeling of control and security to the child once the tantrum has subsided
B) Leave the area entirely, so the child has no audience
C) Give in to the request so that the episode ends quickly
D) Praise the child immediately after the tantrum stops, to reinforce the calm
Answer: A
Rationale: "During tantrums ignore the behavior, provided the behavior is not injurious to the child," but "Continue to be present to provide a feeling of control and security to the child once the tantrum has subsided." In public the child should be removed until the tantrum subsides, and time-outs work well starting at 18 months.
Trap: (B) is the commonest misreading of "ignore the behavior" — the instruction is to ignore the tantrum, not to leave the child. (D) mistimes the praise: the chapter directs praising the child for positive behavior "when he or she is not having a tantrum," which means between episodes rather than immediately after one.

Q: During a tantrum a 2-year-old holds his breath until he briefly faints, and his terrified parents ask whether this can cause brain damage. Which response is accurate?
A) Although holding one's breath may cause fainting from lack of oxygen, the accumulation of carbon dioxide will stimulate the respiratory control center, resulting in no physical harm
B) Breath-holding can cause permanent hypoxic injury and requires emergency evaluation
C) The child should be shaken or splashed with cold water to restart breathing
D) Breath-holding indicates a seizure disorder and warrants an electroencephalogram
Answer: A
Rationale: "Although holding one's breath may cause fainting from lack of oxygen, the accumulation of carbon dioxide will stimulate the respiratory control center, resulting in no physical harm." The chapter also describes the shape of a tantrum: "Anger increases rapidly and peaks near the beginning of the tantrum; components of distress, such as crying and comfort seeking, increase as anger subsides."
Trap: (B) is the parents' fear, and it is why this fact is in the chapter at all. The physiology is self-correcting, and treating it as an emergency teaches the child that breath-holding commands the whole room.

Q: A nurse evaluates a preschooler's temper tantrums. Which features are considered abnormal and may indicate a serious problem? Select all that apply.
A) Tantrums occurring past 5 years of age
B) Tantrums lasting longer than 15 minutes
C) Tantrums occurring more than five times a day
D) Tantrums lasting 5 minutes or less
E) Tantrums that occur when the child is ill, hungry, or tired
Answer: A, B, C
Rationale: "Temper tantrums that occur past 5 years of age, last longer than 15 minutes, or occur more than five times a day are considered abnormal and may indicate a serious problem." By contrast, "The majority of temper tantrums (75%) last 5 minutes or less," and tantrums commonly occur "when the child is ill, hungry, frustrated, or tired."
Trap: (D) and (E) are the two options from the normal side of the sort. (D) is the typical duration for three-quarters of all tantrums, and (E) names the ordinary triggers the chapter lists — so both describe the tantrum a parent should be reassured about rather than worked up.

Q: A mother pregnant with her second child asks how to prepare her 2-year-old. Which guidance reflects the chapter?
A) Tell the toddler that a new playmate is coming home soon, to build excitement
B) Begin talking about the baby when the toddler becomes aware of the pregnancy, stress the activities that will happen such as diapering and bathing, and emphasize which routines will stay the same
C) Move the toddler out of the crib and into a new room during the week the baby arrives
D) Reassure the mother that a well-adjusted toddler can safely be left alone with the newborn
Answer: B
Rationale: "A good time to start talking about the new baby is when toddlers become aware of the pregnancy and the changes occurring in the home." Stress the activities — "diapering, bottle-feeding or breastfeeding, bathing, and dressing" — and emphasize the routines that will stay the same, "such as reading stories or going to the park." Toddlers "do not hate or resent the infant; rather they hate the changes that this additional sibling brings," and rivalry "tends to be most pronounced in the firstborn, who experiences 'dethronement.'"
Trap: (A) is the intuitive framing and the one the chapter names as an error: "Telling them that a new playmate will come home soon sets up unrealistic expectations." (D) contradicts an explicit safety rule: "Regardless of how well adjusted and accepting toddlers or preschoolers appear, infants must be protected by supervising the interaction between siblings."

Q: A 2½-year-old who was toilet trained begins wetting again and demanding a bottle, two weeks after a new sibling is born. What is the best nursing guidance?
A) Ignore the regression while praising existing patterns of appropriate behavior, and avoid introducing new areas of learning when an additional crisis is present or expected
B) Restart toilet training immediately with a reward chart, to regain the lost skill
C) Explain that regression at this age indicates a developmental disorder
D) Remove the bottle and allow the child to cry it out
Answer: A
Rationale: Regression is "a retreat from one's present pattern of functioning to past levels of behavior," usually occurring under stress. "When regression does occur, the best approach is to ignore it while praising existing patterns of appropriate behavior." And: "it is advisable not to introduce new areas of learning when an additional crisis is present or expected, such as beginning toilet training shortly before a sibling is born or during a brief hospitalization."
Trap: (B) is the instinct to recover lost ground, and it applies pressure at exactly the moment the chapter says to remove it. Although regression "appears acceptable and comfortable for children," on closer inspection "the loss of newly acquired achievements is frightening and threatening" — so the child is not enjoying the setback either.

Topic: toddler nutrition and dental health

Q: A parent reports that their 18-month-old ate well until recently but now refuses meals, eats a large amount one day and almost nothing the next, and has become a picky eater with strong taste preferences. Growth is tracking normally. Which response is correct?
A) This is physiologic anorexia — at about 18 months the growth rate slows, decreasing the need for calories, and most toddlers manifest this with a decreased appetite
B) This indicates failure to thrive and requires caloric supplementation
C) The child should be required to finish each plate before leaving the table
D) A daily multivitamin will correct the intake problem
Answer: A
Rationale: "During the period from 12 to 18 months of age the growth rate slows, decreasing the child's need for calories, protein, and fluid." "At approximately 18 months of age most toddlers manifest this decreased nutritional need with a decreased appetite, a phenomenon known as physiologic anorexia." Toddlers "may eat large amounts one day and almost nothing the next," and usually require "three meals and two snacks per day."
Trap: (B) is the leap this child's normal growth contradicts; failure to thrive shows a deviation from established growth parameters, which is absent here. (D) overstates supplements, which do not fix "the problem of excessive sodium, saturated fats, sugars, and overall energy intake."

Q: A toddler refuses a new vegetable without tasting it, after watching a parent push the same vegetable aside. Which explanations does the chapter give?
A) Toddlers are much less likely than infants to try a new food because of its appearance or smell and may reject it because of its texture, and a parent's own refusal transfers a negative connotation before the child has had a chance to taste it
B) Toddlers reject new foods on taste alone, so repeated tasting will resolve it
C) Refusing food at this age indicates a sensory processing disorder
D) An overfilled plate encourages a hesitant toddler to eat more
Answer: A
Rationale: "Toddlers are much less likely than infants to try a new food because of its appearance or smell, not just its taste. Likewise, a toddler is likely to reject a new food because of its texture." And: "if parents refuse a particular food because of their dislike, they will transfer this negative connotation to the child before the child has had an opportunity to taste it."
Trap: (D) inverts the chapter's portion guidance: "If the plate is overfilled, they are likely to push it away, overwhelmed by its size." Mealtime at this age "is more closely associated with psychologic rather than nutritional components," including "the control of refusing food."

Q: A nurse teaches the parents of a 13-month-old about preventing early childhood caries. Which measures does the chapter recommend? Select all that apply.
A) Eliminate the bedtime bottle completely, or give the last bottle before bedtime
B) Do not use the bottle as a pacifier, and never coat pacifiers in sweet substances
C) Offer juice in a cup rather than a bottle, since sugar taken from a bottle is more readily converted to acid
D) Continue bottle-feeding until at least 24 months, to ensure adequate milk intake
E) Encourage drinking from a cup at the first birthday and wean from a bottle by 14 months of age
Answer: A, B, C, E
Rationale: Prevention includes "eliminating the bedtime bottle completely, feeding the last bottle before bedtime, not using the bottle as a pacifier, and never coating pacifiers in sweet substances." Juice in bottles is discouraged because "the sugar is more readily converted to acid," and juice "should always be offered in a cup." "Toddlers should be encouraged to drink from a cup at the first birthday and weaned from a bottle by 14 months of age." Early childhood caries "is now considered to be an infectious disease of childhood."
Trap: (D) is the one option from the wrong side of the sort, and it pulls because milk intake sounds protective. Prolonging the bottle ten months past the recommended weaning age is the single practice most associated with this disease.

Q: A 2-year-old lives in a home whose well water contains 0.8 ppm fluoride. The parent asks whether a fluoride supplement is needed. Which response is correct?
A) No supplement is needed; no supplement is given at any age once the water supply exceeds 0.6 ppm
B) 0.25 mg daily is needed, since the child is between 6 months and 3 years of age
C) 0.5 mg daily is needed, since the water is below 1.0 ppm
D) A supplement should have been started at birth and continued lifelong
Answer: A
Rationale: Table 12.5 gives dietary fluoride supplementation by age and by the fluoride content of the water supply, and the entire column for water above 0.6 ppm is zero at every age. Supplementation is also zero from birth to 6 months whatever the water contains. Where a supplement is given, the dose rises with age and falls as water fluoride rises — 0.25 mg for ages 6 months to 3 years when water is below 0.3 ppm, and 0.5 mg for ages 3 to 6 years at that same water level.
Trap: (B) applies the correct age-band dose without checking the water column, which is the error the table exists to prevent; this water is well above the level at which any supplement is indicated. (D) ignores the rule that no supplement is given before 6 months of age.

Topic: toddler car restraints and injury prevention

Q: A parent asks when their 20-month-old can turn forward-facing in the car. Which response is correct?
A) Children ride rear facing up to the age of 2 years, and until the child has outgrown the car seat manufacturer's height and weight recommendations
B) The child may turn forward now, since 12 months is the threshold
C) The child may turn forward once the legs touch the back of the vehicle seat
D) The child should remain rear facing until reaching 145 cm in height
Answer: A
Rationale: The recommendation is that "children up to the age of 2 years ride in a rear-facing safety seat until the child has outgrown the car seat manufacturer's height and weight recommendations." Many rear-facing seats accommodate "up to a maximum of 35 pounds." The child then transitions "to a forward-facing safety seat with a harness in the backseat."
Trap: (C) is the most common real-world reason parents turn a seat early, and legroom appears nowhere in the chapter as a criterion; the manufacturer's height and weight limits are what govern. (D) applies the booster-graduation height to the rear-facing stage.

Q: A 7-year-old weighing 27 kg and measuring 130 cm rides in a booster seat. The parent asks when the booster can be discontinued. Which response is correct?
A) When the child reaches 145 cm (4 feet, 9 inches) in height or is 8 to 12 years old, and can sit against the back of the seat with feet hanging down and legs bent at the knees
B) Immediately, since booster seats are only for children under 5 years
C) At the eighth birthday, regardless of height
D) Once the child reaches 36.3 kg, whatever the height
Answer: A
Rationale: Booster seats are for children "less than 145 cm (4 feet, 9 inches) tall and weighing 15.9 kg to 36.3 kg (35 to 80 lb)," generally "until approximately 7 to 8 years of age," and children remain in restraints "until they are 145 cm (4 feet, 9 inches) in height or 8 to 12 years old." "A booster seat should be used until the child is able to sit against the back of the seat with feet hanging down and legs bent at the knees."
Trap: (C) treats age alone as the criterion, which is how most children leave boosters too early; this child is 7 and still 15 cm short of the height threshold. The fit test, not the birthday, is the practical check the chapter offers.

Q: A nurse observes several families securing children in car restraints. Which practices require correction? Select all that apply.
A) Placing a rolled blanket and an aftermarket head cushion between the child and the harness straps
B) Misrouting the vehicle seat belt through the restraint
C) Placing the child's safety seat in the front passenger seat, where an air bag is active
D) Positioning the shoulder-lap belt low on the hips, snug, and off the abdominal area
E) Failing to use the tether strap
Answer: A, B, C, E
Rationale: Common misuse includes "misrouting the vehicle seat belt through the restraint; failing to use the vehicle seat belt to secure the restraint; failing to use a tether strap; failing to use the restraint harness system; and incorrectly positioning the child, especially by facing infants forward instead of rearward." It is "important not to add extra blankets, head cushions, or padding between the child and the restraint straps that did not come as original equipment because these 'add-ons' create spaces of air... and decrease support for the back, head, and neck." And: "Air bags do not take the place of child safety seats or seat belts and can be lethal to young children. The safest area of the car for children is the backseat."
Trap: (D) is the one option from the correct side of the sort. It describes exactly what the chapter directs — belts "worn low on the hips, snug, and not on the abdominal area" — and it pulls because "low on the hips" sounds like a belt that has slipped out of place.

Q: A nurse is preparing injury-prevention teaching for parents of children ages 1 to 4 years. Which statement reflects the chapter's epidemiology?
A) Unintentional injury was the leading cause of death among children ages 1 to 4 years, drowning is the leading cause of accidental death in that age group, and falls are the leading cause of nonfatal injuries among children
B) Suffocation is the leading cause of accidental death for children ages 1 to 4 years
C) Unintentional injury is the fifth leading cause of death in children ages 1 to 4 years
D) Girls die from unintentional injury at a higher rate than boys in this age group
Answer: A
Rationale: "Unintentional childhood injury was the leading cause of death among children ages 1 to 4 years in 2013, accounting for 32% of all deaths in this age-group." "Leading causes of accidental death include suffocation for children less than 1 year of age and drowning for children 1 to 4 years of age." "Falls are the leading cause of nonfatal injuries among children." Males die at a higher rate, 36%, than females, 28%.
Trap: (B) is the infant figure applied to toddlers, and the chapter sets the swap up by naming both in a single sentence. (C) is the infant ranking — accidents are the fifth leading cause of death from birth to 12 months, and the leading cause from age 1.

Topic: toddler drowning, burn, and poisoning prevention

Q: Parents of a 2-year-old say their child is safe around water because he has had swimming lessons and the backyard pool is fenced. Which response reflects the chapter?
A) Teaching swimming and water safety can be helpful but cannot be regarded as sufficient protection, and pool fencing, although critical, does not always deter fast-moving children; the standard is touch supervision, in which the adult can reach out and touch or grab the child having difficulty
B) Swimming lessons and a fenced pool together are adequate protection at this age
C) Drowning risk drops sharply after infancy, once the child can stand and walk
D) Line-of-sight supervision from inside the house is sufficient once the pool is fenced
Answer: A
Rationale: "Teaching swimming and water safety can be helpful but cannot be regarded as sufficient protection. Pool fencing, although critical, does not always deter fast-moving children." The American Academy of Pediatrics recommends "touch" supervision for small children, in which "the adult can reach out and touch or grab the child having difficulty." The chapter also notes that "many drownings in this age-group occur when a supervising adult becomes distracted."
Trap: (B) names the two measures parents rely on most, and the chapter qualifies each of them in the same breath rather than endorsing either. (C) inverts the epidemiology: "The highest rates of drowning occur in children ages 1 to 4 years," precisely because locomotion now carries the toddler to bathtubs, toilets, buckets, pools, hot tubs, and ponds.

Q: A nurse assesses the kitchen of a family with a 2-year-old. Which findings require teaching? Select all that apply.
A) Pot handles turned outward toward the front edge of the stove
B) Range control knobs mounted on the front panel within the child's reach
C) A slow cooker positioned near the counter edge with its cord hanging down
D) Matches and lighters stored in a locked upper cabinet
E) An adult drinking hot coffee while holding the child
Answer: A, B, C, E
Rationale: "Toddlers' ability to climb, stretch, and reach objects above their heads makes any hot surface a potential source of danger." Turn pot handles "toward the back of the stove"; electric pots "including cords, should be placed out of reach"; ideally range knobs "should be out of reach, not on the front panel where nimble fingers can turn them on and accidentally touch the hot burner"; and adults "should try not to smoke, cook, or drink hot liquids when children are nearby." Children pulling pots of hot liquid onto themselves, "especially oil and grease," are a major source of burns.
Trap: (D) is the one option from the correct side of the sort, and it pulls because matches appear in the chapter's burn discussion as a hazard. Storing them locked and out of reach is the instruction, not the violation: "all matches must be stored safely away from children," since flame burns "represent one of the most fatal types of burns."

Q: A parent of a 2-year-old says all household chemicals are stored on a high shelf in the laundry room, which worked well when the child was an infant. Which response is correct?
A) Unlike infants, who are confined to certain heights and unable to unlatch inventive locks, young children find access to many high-level, tight-security places; for this age group only a locked cabinet is safe
B) A high shelf remains adequate protection through the toddler years
C) Child-resistant lids make the storage location unimportant
D) Toddlers are at lower risk of poisoning than infants, because mouthing behavior has ended
Answer: A
Rationale: "The major reason for poisoning is improper storage." The chapter draws the contrast directly: "unlike infants, who are confined to certain heights and unable to unlatch inventive locks, young children manage to find access to many high-level, tight-security places. For this age-group only a locked cabinet is safe." Toddlers "are able to climb most heights, open most drawers or closets, and unscrew most lids."
Trap: (C) overtrusts the cap, and the chapter is explicit that by trial and error young children "manage to undo tops of bottles, plastic containers, aerosol cans, and jars, including those with child-resistant lids." (D) inverts the risk: "Toddlers are at the highest risk for accidental poisoning because of their innate curiosity and ability to open 'childproof' containers," and mouthing "continues to be prevalent after 1 year of age."

Q: A toddler is running through the kitchen holding a fork, and the family dog is asleep on the floor nearby. Which teaching points does the chapter give for bodily harm in this age group?
A) Teach that when walking with a pointed object such as a fork, knife, or scissors, the pointed end is held away from the face; and teach pet safety, because toddlers are often at the eye level of some dogs and may be bitten on the face
B) Toddlers have sufficient coordination by age 2 that walking with utensils is safe
C) A family's own pet poses no risk, since the child and animal are familiar with each other
D) Firearms may be stored unloaded in an unlocked drawer, provided ammunition is kept separately
Answer: A
Rationale: Toddlers "are still clumsy in many of their skills and can seriously harm themselves when walking while holding a sharp or pointed object or having food or objects such as spoons in their mouths." Teach that "the pointed end is held away from the face." On pets: "because of toddlers' height, they are often at the eye level of some dogs and may be bitten on the face," and even "the most loving pet may perceive a threat and react accordingly."
Trap: (C) is the assumption behind most facial dog bites in this age group, and the chapter rejects it in terms — toddlers "are often unable to understand that all pets are not as safe as their own," and familiarity does not remove the animal's threat response. (D) contradicts the instruction that "all firearms should be stored in a locked cabinet," alongside dangerous garden and workshop equipment.
