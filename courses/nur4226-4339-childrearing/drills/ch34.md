# Chapter 34 — The Child With Neuromuscular or Muscular Dysfunction

Source: `sources/Ch34_Fact_Bank.md` (Hockenberry, Wilson, & Rodgers, *Wong's Nursing Care of
Infants and Children*, 11th ed., ch. 34, printed pp. 1298–1347).

**Blueprint weight: 5 questions of 121, depth `standard`.** That buys **40 questions across 10
topics**, the count this course has used for every five-item chapter. This is the last chapter of
the course.

**Where the weight went.** The chapter is organized around one sentence — "The site of pathologic
disturbance determines the type of muscular dysfunction" (p. 1298) — and the allocation follows
that structure rather than treating the disorders as a list. Three questions establish the
organizing contrast (upper against lower motor neuron, the four lesion sites, the three steps of
neuromuscular transmission) because every later item depends on it. Cerebral palsy takes 8 and
spina bifida 9, matching their share of the chapter and their share of pediatric practice. Spinal
cord injury takes 8 across an acute topic and a long-term one, because the acute priorities and
the chronic ones are different restudy material and fusing them would hide that.

**What is drilled from both ends.** The mirror pairs the fact bank flags: upper against lower
motor neuron lesion, which decides whether a stem should read spastic or flaccid; ascending
Guillain-Barre against descending botulism; meningocele against myelomeningocele; 0.4 mg against
4 mg of folic acid; autonomic dysreflexia against neurogenic shock, where bradycardia is common to
both and the blood pressure separates them; spinal shock against dysreflexia in time; and
neonatal against congenital myasthenia gravis. **Three of the chapter's own five NCLEX review
items are drilled directly** — the postoperative myelomeningocele complication, the standing-table
dysreflexia priority item, and the Duchenne select-all — and a fourth, on clean intermittent
catheterization, is drilled with the same options.

**What the weight could not buy.** Left undrilled, deliberately: the EMG, nerve conduction
studies, and muscle biopsy as procedures; Box 34.1's full risk-factor grouping and the mapping of
each cerebral palsy type to its lesion; the 2005 four-dimension classification and the named
assessment instruments; selective dorsal rhizotomy and botulinum toxin dosing; manual jaw control
technique; anencephaly; prenatal detection and the MOMS trial; tethered cord syndrome; the
augmentation and diversion procedures (Mitrofanoff, vesicostomy, the antegrade continence enema)
beyond their appearance as distractors; the three phases of Guillain-Barre and its cerebrospinal
fluid picture; the three forms of tetanus; the incomplete spinal cord syndromes and the full ASIA
impairment scale; Table 34.1's thoracic and lumbar rows in detail; and the four other muscular
dystrophies in Table 34.2. Five blueprint questions do not reach that material, and naming it is
the honest record of what was cut.

**On the GAP register.** Nothing in this chapter's register is flagged "most likely to be tested."
Two source defects are respected rather than reproduced. **Box 34.3 inverts its own narrative**,
printing "Nonpersistence beyond 6 months of age" where the prose on p. 1303 reads "nonobligatory
persistence beyond 6 months of age"; the reflex question here is built on the narrative version
and on the obligatory-at-any-age rule, not on the box entry. And the chapter **cross-references
the immobilized child to Chapter 34**, the chapter the reader is already in, when that material is
Chapter 33. The dermatome worksheet in Fig. 34.9 lives in the artwork rather than the text, so no
question asks a learner to map a level to a dermatome.

**Verified counts: 40 questions, 10 topics, 6 select-alls.** Every select-all is drawn from a real
category list: the early signs of cerebral palsy, the preoperative myelomeningocele nursing rules,
the Guillain-Barre bedside and communication measures, the infant botulism management set, the
precipitating causes of autonomic dysreflexia, and Box 34.13's characteristics of Duchenne
muscular dystrophy. None was manufactured from a taxonomy — the ASIA grades and the spinal
muscular atrophy types would both have made easy select-alls, and neither is used, because
reciting a grading scale is not a discrimination a nurse makes at the bedside.

Topic: upper and lower motor neuron lesions in children
Subtopic: reading the lesion from the examination

Q: A child is assessed after a traumatic spinal cord injury, once the flaccid phase has resolved. Which findings are expected?
A) Spastic paralysis below the lesion, exaggerated tendon reflexes, a Babinski reflex, and no muscle wasting
B) Flaccid paralysis, absent reflexes, and marked atrophy
C) Flaccid paralysis with hyperreflexia
D) Normal reflexes with preserved strength
Answer: A
Rationale: Box 34.9 gives the upper motor neuron syndrome as "Spastic paralysis in muscle groups below lesion (intact reflex arcs below lesion)," "Hyperreflexia with tendon reflexes exaggerated, Babinski reflex present," "No wasting of muscle mass because of increased muscle tone," and "Flexion contractures and spasms of muscle groups below lesion level common" (p. 1329).
Trap: (B) is the lower motor neuron picture, and the chapter assigns each to its population in one sentence: "Most SCIs involve upper motor neurons; children born with spinal cord defects have primarily lower motor neuron deficits" (p. 1329). A myelomeningocele stem should read flaccid and a traumatic injury stem spastic. (C) mixes one finding from each column, which is how a half-remembered pair fails.

Q: A child is being worked up for progressive weakness, and the serum creatine kinase is markedly elevated. What does this suggest?
A) A muscle disease such as muscular dystrophy, because creatine kinase is not elevated in neurogenic disease
B) An anterior horn cell disease such as spinal muscular atrophy
C) A peripheral nerve disorder such as Guillain-Barre syndrome
D) Nothing, since creatine kinase rises with any cause of weakness
Answer: A
Rationale: Creatine kinase "is released in large amounts in some muscular diseases, such as MD. CK is not elevated in neurogenic disease" (p. 1299). In Duchenne muscular dystrophy the level is dramatic and early: "Serum CK levels are extremely high in the first 2 years of life, before the onset of clinical weakness" (p. 1341).
Trap: (B) and (C) are both real causes of weakness in this chapter and both are neurogenic, so the enzyme is precisely what sorts them from a myopathy. The chapter's own framework is anatomic: the most useful classification "defines the site of origin of the pathologic lesion: the anterior horn cells of the spinal cord, the peripheral nerves, the neuromuscular junction, and the muscles" (p. 1298).

Q: A nurse is reviewing how different disorders block neuromuscular transmission. Which pairing is correct?
A) Botulism blocks the release of acetylcholine, while myasthenia gravis blocks the receptor sites
B) Botulism blocks the receptor sites, while myasthenia gravis blocks acetylcholine release
C) Both act by inhibiting cholinesterase
D) Neither acts at the neuromuscular junction
Answer: A
Rationale: Transmission happens in three steps — release, diffusion to receptor sites, and removal by cholinesterase — and "Interference at any of these three steps will block transmission of nerve impulses and prevent muscular contraction" (p. 1299). "toxins that prevent the release of acetylcholine are those that produce the paralysis of botulism and tick paralysis," while action at receptor sites "is also blocked by the drug curare" and by the antibodies of myasthenia gravis. Botulism toxin "exerts its effect by inhibiting the release of acetylcholine at the neuromuscular junction" (p. 1325).
Trap: (B) swaps the two, and the swap matters clinically because the treatments diverge completely: botulism is treated by neutralizing the toxin with immune globulin, while myasthenia gravis is treated with cholinesterase inhibitors that leave more acetylcholine at the receptor. (C) describes the step blocked by organophosphate insecticide poisoning.

Topic: cerebral palsy classification and risk factors
Subtopic: what causes it

Q: A nursing student asks what the single most important risk factor for cerebral palsy is. Which answer is correct?
A) Preterm birth of extremely and very low birth weight infants
B) Birth asphyxia
C) Chorioamnionitis
D) Intraventricular hemorrhage
Answer: A
Rationale: "Although CP occurs in term births, preterm birth of ELBW and VLBW infants continues to be the single most important risk factor for CP" (p. 1301). The chapter also revises the older teaching: "Although the prevalent traditional hypothesis has been that CP results from perinatal problems, especially birth asphyxia, it is now believed that CP results more often from existing prenatal brain abnormalities."
Trap: (B) is the retired hypothesis and the one most people were taught first. (C) and (D) are real risk factors, which is what makes them selectable, but chorioamnionitis carries the caveat that "not all term infants exposed to chorioamnionitis develop CP," and intraventricular hemorrhage is named as a risk in low-birth-weight infants specifically, which makes it a consequence of the same prematurity the correct answer names. This is the chapter's own NCLEX question 3.

Q: A 3-month-old at risk for cerebral palsy is generally hypotonic, and a parent asks whether this rules out the spastic type. What should the nurse explain?
A) Early neurologic manifestations are usually generalized hypotonia, which may last weeks to as long as a year before spasticity appears
B) Hypotonia rules out spastic cerebral palsy, which is hypertonic from birth
C) Hypotonia at this age is always a normal finding
D) The diagnosis can be confirmed now on the basis of tone alone
Answer: A
Rationale: "Early neurologic manifestations are usually generalized hypotonia or decreased tone that lasts for a few weeks or may extend for months or even as long as a year" (p. 1301). The diagnosis takes time: "motor impairment associated with voluntary control is usually not apparent until after 2 to 4 months of age at the earliest. More often the diagnosis cannot be confirmed until the age of 1 or 2 years" (p. 1304).
Trap: (B) is the stage swap this section is built on, because spastic cerebral palsy does not start out spastic. (D) overstates what tone can settle, and the chapter cuts the other way as well: "some children who show signs consistent with CP before 2 years do not demonstrate such signs after 2 years."

Q: Which findings in an infant should raise concern for cerebral palsy? Select all that apply.
A) Preferential use of one hand at about 6 months of age
B) Persistence of primitive infantile reflexes
C) An obligatory tonic neck response
D) Rigidity and extension of the whole body when pulled to a sitting position
E) Absence of hand dominance at 4 years of age
Answer: A, B, C, D
Rationale: "An early sign is preferential unilateral hand use that may be apparent at approximately 6 months of age. Hand dominance does not normally develop until the preschool years" (p. 1301). "Persistence of primitive reflexes is one of the earliest clues to CP" (p. 1303), and Box 34.3 lists an "Obligatory tonic neck reflex at any age." "When pulled to a sitting position, the child may extend the entire body and be rigid and unbending at the hip and knee joints. This is an early sign of spasticity" (p. 1302).
Trap: (E) is the wrong option and it inverts the hand-preference rule: what concerns the examiner is dominance appearing too early, not too late, because dominance normally does not emerge until the preschool years.

Q: A school nurse hears a teacher say that a student with spastic cerebral palsy and dysarthria must be cognitively impaired. How should the nurse respond?
A) Children with cerebral palsy have a wide range of intelligence, and 50% to 60% are within normal limits; speech difficulty is often misread as cognitive impairment
B) The teacher is correct, since cerebral palsy always involves cognitive impairment
C) Only children with athetoid cerebral palsy have normal intelligence
D) Intelligence cannot be assessed in a child whose speech is unclear
Answer: A
Rationale: "Intellectual impairment is a concern, although children with CP have a wide range of intelligence, and 50% to 60% are within normal limits," and "Speech difficulties are often interpreted as a sign of cognitive impairment" (p. 1303). The chapter adds that "Many persons with CP who have severely limiting physical involvement actually have the least intellectual impairment," and that improved communication devices "have revealed that some people with quadriplegic spastic CP have normal intelligence."
Trap: (C) takes a true comparative statement — "As a group, children with athetosis and ataxia are intellectually superior to those with other types of CP" — and converts it into an exclusion the same paragraph refutes. (D) restates the assumption that created the problem.

Topic: cerebral palsy therapy and family care
Subtopic: feeding and medication

Q: A nurse is teaching a parent to feed a toddler with cerebral palsy who has difficulty sucking and swallowing. Which position should be used?
A) A more flexed sitting position, with the arms brought forward
B) Semireclining, so gravity assists the flow
C) Fully supine with the neck extended
D) Prone across the parent's lap
Answer: A
Rationale: "Children should be fed in the normal eating position. When they have difficulty sucking and swallowing, it is tempting to hold them in a semireclining posture to make use of gravity flow. However, this method does not promote active swallowing, and the neck hyperextension may even interfere with swallowing. A more flexed sitting position, with the arms brought forward to decrease the tendency toward back and neck extension, is more natural during bottle- or spoon-feeding and encourages active swallowing" (p. 1307).
Trap: (B) is named in the source as the tempting option and refused in the same sentence, and (C) deepens the same problem, since the neck hyperextension is what interferes with the swallow. The stakes are concrete: coughing and choking while eating "may predispose the child with CP to aspiration, which may not be readily apparent" (p. 1303).

Q: A child with an intrathecal baclofen pump is admitted with high fever, rebound spasticity, itching, and rapid deterioration, and sepsis is suspected. What else should the nurse consider?
A) Intrathecal baclofen withdrawal, which can mimic sepsis and is treated by reestablishing the dose
B) An expected adverse effect of baclofen that will resolve on its own
C) Botulinum toxin overdose from a recent injection
D) A seizure disorder unrelated to the pump
Answer: A
Rationale: "Abrupt withdrawal of intrathecal baclofen, especially at high doses, may result in adverse effects such as rebound spasticity, pruritus, hyperthermia, rhabdomyolysis, disseminated intravascular coagulation, multiorgan failure, and death; in some cases intrathecal baclofen withdrawal may mimic sepsis." Treatment "centers on reestablishing the medication dosage, with improvements observed within 1 to 2 hours" (p. 1306).
Trap: (B) treats a potentially fatal withdrawal as a nuisance effect. (C) reaches for the other spasticity drug in the chapter, whose adverse effects are far milder — "pain at the injection site and a temporary weakness" — and whose action peaks at 2 weeks rather than producing an acute crisis (p. 1305).

Subtopic: growth, energy, and the family

Q: A school-age child with spastic cerebral palsy is falling below the 5th percentile for weight. Which statement should guide the nurse's plan?
A) Children with spasticity expend more energy and often require more energy intake than same-age peers
B) Caloric intake should be restricted, since children with cerebral palsy gain weight easily
C) Growth below the 5th percentile is expected in this population and needs no intervention
D) Feeding difficulty is the only possible cause, so a gastrostomy should be placed
Answer: A
Rationale: "Children with spasticity expend more energy and often require more energy intake than same-age counterparts to maintain adequate growth," and "children with CP are at risk for failure to thrive, and the nurse must ensure an adequate caloric intake," with high-calorie supplements sometimes needed after 1 year of age (p. 1309). The balance is named too: monitor growth on a standardized growth chart while "avoiding overfeeding and obesity."
Trap: (C) is close to a real observation — "Growth is affected in children with spastic tetraplegia, and many children remain below the 5th percentile for age and sex" (p. 1308) — and turns an expected difficulty into a reason not to act, which is the plausible-but-adjacent move. (B) reverses the risk for this subgroup entirely. More frequent rest periods belong in the plan as well, because the effort of daily activities is itself the drain.

Q: A child with cerebral palsy is admitted for orthopedic surgery, and the mother says she is exhausted and asks whether she must stay overnight. What is the best response?
A) Respect her preference, since a hospitalization may be the first chance for a parent to defer care and the respite may be crucial
B) Insist that she room in, since parental presence always improves outcomes
C) Explain that the nursing staff cannot manage her child's care without her
D) Suggest she stay but sleep in the waiting area
Answer: A
Rationale: Rooming-in has real benefits, "but it is equally important to remember that a hospitalization may be the first time a parent can defer care to a nurse and not be the primary caregiver. This respite may be crucial to the parent's well-being. Respect the parent's preference in this regard" (p. 1310).
Trap: (B) states a principle that is true often enough to become a reflex, and this chapter names the exception explicitly, because the family of a child with cerebral palsy has been delivering continuous care at home for years. (C) is untrue and produces guilt on top of exhaustion.

Topic: neural tube defects and myelomeningocele care
Subtopic: prevention and classification

Q: A woman whose first child was born with a myelomeningocele asks about folic acid before her next pregnancy. What should the nurse advise?
A) 4 mg daily under a practitioner's supervision, beginning at least 1 month before conception and through the first trimester, and not from a multivitamin alone
B) 0.4 mg daily, the same as for all women of childbearing age
C) 4 mg daily, taken as extra multivitamin tablets
D) No supplement, since folic acid is already added to cereal grain products
Answer: A
Rationale: All women of childbearing age take "a multivitamin with 0.4 mg folic acid daily," while a woman with a previously affected pregnancy takes 4 mg daily "under a practitioner's supervision, beginning at least 1 month before they plan a pregnancy and through the first trimester because the neural tube closes about 1 month after conception" (p. 1311). And "Supplementation of 4 mg of folate should not be given solely in multivitamin preparations because of the risk for overdose of other vitamins" (p. 1317).
Trap: (B) applies the general-population dose to a woman whose recurrence risk is 3% to 4% (p. 1313), and the tenfold difference between the two numbers is the entire point of the pair. (C) has the right dose in the wrong vehicle, which is exactly what the overdose caution exists to prevent.

Q: A newborn has a saclike protrusion over the lumbar spine. Which finding would distinguish a myelomeningocele from a meningocele?
A) A neurologic deficit below the lesion
B) The presence of a visible sac
C) A location in the lumbosacral region
D) An elevated maternal alpha-fetoprotein level
Answer: A
Rationale: A meningocele "encases meninges and spinal fluid but no neural elements," while a myelomeningocele "contains meninges, spinal fluid, and nerves," and the chapter states the consequence directly: "Neurologic deficit is not associated with meningocele but occurs in varying, often serious, degrees in myelomeningocele" (pp. 1312–1313).
Trap: (B) and (C) belong to both, since each is a form of spina bifida cystica, "a visible defect with an external saclike protrusion," and the lumbar or lumbosacral area is the commonest site for either. What separates them is what is inside the sac and therefore what works below it.

Subtopic: caring for the unrepaired defect

Q: A newborn with an unrepaired myelomeningocele is admitted. Which nursing actions are correct? Select all that apply.
A) Cover the sac with a sterile, moist, nonadherent dressing using normal saline
B) Change the dressing every 2 to 4 hours and inspect the sac for leaks and irritation
C) Keep the infant prone
D) Avoid rectal temperatures
E) Apply a diaper over the defect to contain stool
Answer: A, B, C, D
Rationale: "Before surgical closure, the myelomeningocele is kept from drying by the application of a sterile, moist, nonadherent dressing. The moistening solution is usually sterile normal saline. Dressings are changed frequently (every 2 to 4 hours), and the sac is closely inspected for leaks, abrasions, irritation, and signs of infection." "Before surgery the infant remains in the prone position to minimize tension on the sac and the risk of trauma" (p. 1318). The safety rule is separate: "Avoid taking rectal temperatures in affected infants. Because bowel sphincter function is frequently affected, the thermometer can cause irritation and rectal prolapse" (p. 1313).
Trap: (E) is the wrong option and it is the instinct of anyone caring for a newborn passing stool continuously: "Diapering the infant may be contraindicated until the defect has been repaired and healing is well advanced" (p. 1318). Note that the continual passage of stool is itself "often misinterpreted as diarrhea."

Q: A neonate born 6 hours ago has a myelomeningocele that is leaking cerebrospinal fluid. When should surgical closure occur?
A) Within 24 hours
B) Within 24 to 72 hours, the usual window
C) After about a week, to allow the infant to stabilize
D) After hydrocephalus has been ruled out
Answer: A
Rationale: "Most authorities believe that early closure, within the first 24 to 72 hours, offers the most favorable outcome. Surgical closure within the first 24 hours is recommended if the sac is leaking CSF" (p. 1314), and earlier still is preferred: "Early closure, preferably in the first 12 to 18 hours, not only prevents local infection and trauma to the exposed tissues but also avoids stretching other nerve roots."
Trap: (B) is the general rule, and this infant meets the stated exception to it. (D) inverts the sequence, since hydrocephalus commonly declares itself only "after the primary closure of the opening on the back" (p. 1313). Delay also costs the kidneys: "Increased incidence of febrile UTIs, vesicoureteral reflux, and hydronephrosis have been shown when closure is delayed past 72 hours" (p. 1314).

Q: An infant has had surgical repair of a myelomeningocele. Which complication should the nurse most anticipate?
A) Hydrocephalus
B) Chiari II malformation
C) Latex allergy
D) Tethered cord syndrome
Answer: A
Rationale: "approximately 80% to 85% of children with SB develop hydrocephalus," and "Although present at birth, hydrocephalus may not be apparent until shortly thereafter, or after the primary closure of the opening on the back" (p. 1313). Postoperative care therefore includes measuring head circumference daily and examining the fontanels "for signs of tension or bulging" (p. 1318).
Trap: (B) is a real association in about 80% of these infants, but it is a coexisting malformation rather than a postoperative complication and is treated "only when the child is symptomatic" (p. 1315). (C) develops over years of repeated exposure rather than in the first postoperative week (p. 1319), and (D) is "more common during periods of rapid growth" (p. 1315). All three are true of this population, and none is what the nurse watches for now. This is the chapter's own NCLEX question 1.

Topic: neurogenic bladder bowel and latex allergy in spina bifida
Subtopic: protecting the urinary tract

Q: A 4-year-old with spina bifida is at risk for urinary system distress. What management should the nurse expect?
A) Clean intermittent catheterization, typically combined with an antispasmodic medication
B) An indwelling catheter for continuous drainage
C) DDAVP, to reduce urine output
D) The Mitrofanoff procedure as the first-line approach
Answer: A
Rationale: For a child with evidence of, or at risk for, urinary system distress, management is "clean intermittent catheterization (CIC), typically in combination with an antispasmodic medication such as oxybutynin or propantheline" (p. 1316). "CIC is not intended to prevent spontaneous voiding. Instead, it ensures routine, regular bladder evacuation, further preventing deleterious elevation of intravesical pressures."
Trap: (D) is a real option and a surgical one, reserved for "When the child cannot attain continence by conservative measures" (p. 1316), so it answers a different question than the one asked. (C) belongs to diabetes insipidus rather than to a neurogenic bladder, and (B) is not the described management and carries its own infection risk. This is the chapter's own NCLEX question 4.

Q: Two children with myelomeningocele are compared: one leaks urine almost constantly, the other stays dry between catheterizations. Which statement is accurate?
A) The child with more severe leakage is less predisposed to serious urinary tract infections, because the detrusor leak point pressure is lower
B) The drier child has the safer bladder
C) Leakage has no relationship to upper urinary tract risk
D) Both carry identical risk, determined only by the level of the lesion
Answer: A
Rationale: "When the magnitude of the pressure required to drive urine across the abnormal sphincter is greater than 40 cm H2O (the detrusor leak point pressure) and the compliance of the bladder wall is low, the risk of urinary system distress is high" (p. 1315). "When the detrusor leak point pressure is lower than 40 cm H2O, urinary leakage is more severe, although the risk of urinary system distress is lessened. Thus the child with more severe urinary incontinence is less predisposed than the 'drier' child to serious urinary tract infections" (p. 1316).
Trap: (B) is what continence intuitively suggests, and the chapter states the reverse in so many words. The pressure the bladder generates, not the dryness of the diaper, is what threatens the kidneys.

Subtopic: bowel program and latex

Q: A 7-year-old with myelomeningocele is starting a bowel program. Which recommendation is correct?
A) Diet modification, regular toilet habits, and a fiber supplement of the child's age in years plus 5 grams per day
B) A clear liquid diet until continence is achieved
C) Daily enemas as the first-line measure
D) No intervention, since fecal continence is not attainable in spina bifida
Answer: A
Rationale: "Some degree of fecal continence can be achieved in most children with myelomeningocele with diet modification, regular toilet habits, and prevention of constipation and impaction. It is frequently a lengthy process." "Dietary fiber supplements (recommended age of child in years + 5 g/day), laxatives, suppositories, or enemas aid in producing regular evacuation" (p. 1317).
Trap: (D) writes off an outcome the chapter says is usually attainable, and continence here is not cosmetic: incontinence leads "to significant, and sometimes devastating, problems with growth and developmental tasks, including establishing independence and social and intimate relationships." (C) moves an adjunct to the front of the plan.

Q: A nurse is taking a preoperative history. Which approach to latex allergy is correct?
A) Ask every patient about latex sensitivity as a routine part of the preoperative history
B) Ask only patients with spina bifida or urogenital anomalies
C) Rely on skin prick testing rather than on the history
D) Ask only if the parent raises the concern
Answer: A
Rationale: "The identification of those sensitive to latex is best accomplished through careful screening of all patients. During the health interview with the parent or child, ask all patients, not only those at risk, about sensitivity to latex. Be certain this is a routine part of all preoperative and preprocedural histories" (p. 1319).
Trap: (B) narrows the screen to the population in which the association was first described. (C) leans on a test the chapter distrusts: skin prick and provocation testing "carry the risk for allergic reaction or anaphylaxis," and "none of these tests demonstrates complete diagnostic reliability, and they should not be the sole determinant." The interview also has to reach nonmedical items such as "water toys, pacifiers, and plastic storage bags," and the cross-reacting foods banana, avocado, kiwi, and chestnut (p. 1320).

Topic: guillain-barre syndrome tetanus and botulism
Subtopic: acute flaccid paralysis

Q: A 6-year-old develops weakness that began in the legs about 10 days after a viral illness and is ascending, with depressed reflexes and flaccid paralysis. Which condition is this, and what is the treatment?
A) Guillain-Barre syndrome, treated primarily with intravenous immunoglobulin given within 2 weeks of onset
B) Botulism, treated with botulism immune globulin
C) Guillain-Barre syndrome, treated with corticosteroids alone
D) Myasthenia gravis, treated with a cholinesterase inhibitor
Answer: A
Rationale: Guillain-Barre syndrome is "an uncommon acute demyelinating polyneuropathy with a progressive, usually ascending flaccid paralysis," typically beginning "approximately 10 days after a nonspecific viral infection," with "Proximal muscle weakness progressing to paralysis usually occurs before distal weakness" and tendon reflexes "depressed or absent" (p. 1322). "IVIG is now recommended as the primary treatment of GBS when administered within 2 weeks of disease onset" (p. 1323).
Trap: (C) attaches a plausible antiinflammatory to a demyelinating disease, and the chapter refuses it: "Corticosteroids alone do not decrease the symptoms or shorten the duration of the disease" (p. 1323). (B) names the disease whose paralysis runs the other way, beginning with cranial nerve signs and descending.

Q: An adolescent with Guillain-Barre syndrome is intubated and nearly completely paralyzed. Which nursing considerations are correct? Select all that apply.
A) Cognitive function remains intact, so procedures and treatments should be explained
B) Recovery of muscle strength progresses in the reverse order of onset
C) Resuscitation equipment is kept at the bedside during the acute phase
D) The head of the bed is elevated 30 degrees to help prevent ventilator-associated pneumonia
E) Continuous sedation should be maintained, since the child cannot perceive the environment
Answer: A, B, C, D
Rationale: "Although the child may have a generalized paralysis, cognitive function remains intact; therefore it is important for nursing care to involve communication with the child or adolescent regarding procedures and treatments that may be frightening, especially if mechanical ventilation is required" (p. 1323). "The recovery of muscle strength progresses in the reverse order of onset of paralysis, with lower extremity strength being the last to recover." The bedside list is "oxygen source, appropriate-size insufflation bag and mask, endotracheal intubation and suctioning equipment, tracheotomy tray, and vasopressor drugs," and pneumonia prevention includes "elevating the head of the bed 30 degrees."
Trap: (E) is the wrong option and it rests on the exact assumption the chapter corrects: a paralyzed child in this disease is awake and aware, which is why communication is listed as nursing care rather than as a kindness.

Subtopic: toxin-mediated paralysis

Q: A child is admitted with generalized tetanus. Which set of nursing measures is correct?
A) Minimize sound, touch, and light; use standard precautions rather than isolation; and plan active immunization during recovery
B) Place the child in isolation, since tetanus is communicable
C) Keep the room brightly lit and the child stimulated, to assess mentation
D) Omit immunization after recovery, since the illness confers immunity
Answer: A
Rationale: "every effort should be made to control or eliminate stimulation from sound, light, and touch," because "The slightest noise, a gentle touch, or bright light triggers convulsive muscular contractions that last seconds to minutes" (pp. 1324–1325). "Standard precautions for the child with tetanus are recommended; isolation is not recommended," and "When the child recovers, active immunization should take place because contraction of the disease does not confer a permanent immunity" (p. 1325).
Trap: (D) is the assumption that holds for many infections and specifically does not hold here. (B) treats a toxin-mediated wound infection as a communicable disease. (C) trades a seizure trigger for information the nurse already has, since mentation is unaffected throughout: "the patient remains alert, and pain and distress are reflected in a rapid pulse, sweating, and an anxious expression."

Q: A fully immunized 10-year-old whose last booster was 3 years ago has a deep puncture wound contaminated with soil. What should the nurse anticipate?
A) A tetanus toxoid booster as soon as possible after the injury
B) Tetanus immunoglobulin, because the wound is contaminated
C) No intervention, since the child is fully immunized
D) Both tetanus immunoglobulin and toxoid, at separate sites
Answer: A
Rationale: For a more serious wound — contaminated, puncture, crush, or burn — in an immunized child, the child is "given a tetanus toxoid booster prophylactically as soon as possible after injury." "Protective levels of antibody are maintained for at least 10 years. Therefore antitoxin is not indicated for the fully immunized child" (p. 1324).
Trap: (B) and (D) both give immunoglobulin, which belongs to the unprotected or inadequately immunized child with a tetanus-prone wound, where "Concurrent administration of both TIG and tetanus toxoid at separate sites is recommended." The child's immune status, not the dirt in the wound, decides which column applies, and (C) would be correct only for a clean, minor wound.

Q: A 3-month-old presents with constipation, generalized weakness, a weak cry, poor feeding, and diminished reflexes, and infant botulism is suspected. Which statements are correct? Select all that apply.
A) Botulism immune globulin intravenously is given without waiting for laboratory confirmation
B) Antibiotics are not part of management, and aminoglycosides may worsen the condition
C) Honey should not be given to infants younger than 12 months
D) Spinal muscular atrophy type 1 and sepsis are common initial misdiagnoses
E) Equine antitoxin is the treatment of choice in infants
Answer: A, B, C, D
Rationale: "Treatment consists of immediate administration of botulism immune globulin intravenously (BIG-IV) without delaying for laboratory diagnosis." "Antibiotic therapy is not part of the management because the botulinum toxin is an intracellular molecule and antibiotics would not be effective; aminoglycosides in particular should not be administered because they may potentiate the blocking effects of the neurotoxin" (p. 1326). The Nursing Alert directs that "honey not be given to infants younger than 12 months." And "SMA type 1 and metabolic disorders are often mistaken for infant botulism … Presenting clinical signs also often mimic those of sepsis in young infants" (p. 1325).
Trap: (E) is the wrong option, excluded by age: "Trivalent equine botulinum antitoxin and bivalent antitoxin, used in adults and older children, are not administered to infants" (p. 1326). Constipation as the presenting symptom, and an acute onset in a previously well infant, are what point away from spinal muscular atrophy.

Topic: myasthenia gravis and spinal muscular atrophy
Subtopic: myasthenia gravis

Q: A 12-year-old with juvenile myasthenia gravis taking neostigmine develops lacrimation, salivation, abdominal cramps, sweating, diarrhea, and bradycardia along with increased weakness. What should the nurse suspect?
A) Overmedication, since these are signs of parasympathetic stimulation
B) A myasthenic crisis from undermedication
C) An allergic reaction to neostigmine
D) An unrelated gastrointestinal infection
Answer: A
Rationale: "The child must be observed for signs of parasympathetic stimulation from overmedication. These signs include lacrimation, salivation, abdominal cramps, sweating, diarrhea, vomiting, bradycardia, and weakness of respiratory muscles" (p. 1326).
Trap: (B) is the hard call, because weakness belongs to both states and is the symptom that prompts a family to give more drug. The cholinergic findings are the tell. In untreated disease the pattern is different again: "The signs and symptoms are more pronounced in the late afternoon and evening. Rest can help relieve the symptoms, but exercise and stress worsen them."

Q: A child with myasthenia gravis is scheduled for surgery and has a suspected respiratory infection. Which agents should be avoided?
A) Neuromuscular-blocking agents such as pancuronium or succinylcholine, and aminoglycoside antibiotics such as gentamicin
B) Acetaminophen and ibuprofen
C) Influenza vaccine
D) Cholinesterase inhibitors
Answer: A
Rationale: The Drug Alert states: "Avoid neuromuscular-blocking agents such as pancuronium or succinylcholine in patients with MG because they may induce paralysis that can last for weeks. Avoid aminoglycoside antibiotics such as gentamicin because they potentiate MG symptoms" (p. 1327).
Trap: (C) is the opposite of the recommendation, since "yearly influenza vaccination is recommended" precisely because "Respiratory viral infections are a common precipitating cause of a myasthenia crisis" (p. 1327). (D) names the treatment rather than a hazard.

Q: An infant born to a mother with myasthenia gravis has generalized weakness, hypotonia, ptosis, and a weak cry. What should the nurse tell the parents?
A) Symptoms usually disappear within 2 to 3 weeks, and the infant is not at increased risk of myasthenia gravis later in life
B) The condition persists throughout life and is relatively resistant to drug therapy
C) The infant will require a thymectomy
D) The findings confirm a familial, non-immune form of the disease
Answer: A
Rationale: Neonatal transient myasthenia gravis results from "transplacentally acquired maternal acetylcholine receptor antibodies," occurs in "approximately 10% to 20% of infants born to mothers with MG," and "Symptoms usually disappear within 2 to 3 weeks"; these infants "are not at increased risk of MG later in life," and "There is no evidence of neurologic damage" (p. 1327).
Trap: (B) and (D) describe the congenital form, "a rare familial abnormality of neuromuscular transmission that is not immunologically mediated," which persists lifelong and occurs in infants whose mother "usually does not have the disease." The mother's diagnosis in the stem is the detail that sorts the two. (C) applies a treatment that "is not effective for familial and congenital forms of MG."

Subtopic: spinal muscular atrophy

Q: A 4-month-old has inactivity, a frog-leg posture, generalized weakness, absent deep tendon reflexes, a weak cry and cough, and diaphragmatic breathing, but is alert with normal sensation. What should the nurse understand?
A) Spinal muscular atrophy type 1 is autosomal recessive, intellect is normal, and restrictive lung disease is the most serious complication
B) Intellectual impairment is expected alongside the motor findings
C) The disorder is acquired and will resolve with supportive care
D) Deep tendon reflexes are typically brisk in this condition
Answer: A
Rationale: Spinal muscular atrophy type 1 is "characterized by progressive weakness and wasting of skeletal muscles caused by degeneration of anterior horn cells. It is inherited as an autosomal recessive trait" (p. 1320). The type 1 sign set includes the frog-leg position, "Absent deep tendon reflexes," "Diaphragmatic breathing with sternal retractions," "Weak cry and cough," "Alert facies," and "Normal sensation and intellect." "Restrictive lung disease is the most serious complication of SMA" (p. 1321).
Trap: (B) is the assumption an alert but profoundly weak infant invites, and the source names normal intellect explicitly, which is why "verbal, tactile, and auditory stimulation are important aspects of developmental care" (p. 1321). (D) inverts the reflex finding, which is what places the lesion at the anterior horn cell rather than above it.

Topic: spinal cord injury levels and acute management
Subtopic: at the scene and on arrival

Q: An adolescent is thrown from a bicycle and a spinal cord injury is suspected. What is correct about the initial management?
A) Open the airway with a jaw thrust, and maintain spinal immobilization until injury has been ruled out by a thorough neurologic assessment
B) Open the airway with a head tilt–chin lift, and release immobilization once the child can move the fingers
C) Defer airway management until imaging is complete
D) Release immobilization once the initial neurologic examination is normal
Answer: A
Rationale: "The initial management of the child with a suspected SCI should begin with an assessment of the ABCs: airway, breathing, and circulation. The airway should be opened using the jaw-thrust technique to minimize damage to the cervical spine" (pp. 1331–1332). "In the emergency department, spinal immobilization should be maintained until a thorough neurologic assessment is completed and spinal cord injury is ruled out" (p. 1332).
Trap: (D) is the tempting option because a normal examination feels like an answer, and it is not one here: "25% to 50% of children with SCI will have a delay in the onset of neurologic abnormalities ranging from 30 minutes to 4 days" (p. 1330). (B) opens the airway with the maneuver that moves the cervical spine.

Q: An adolescent sustains a complete spinal cord injury at C6. What should the nurse expect regarding ventilation?
A) The child is unlikely to be ventilator dependent, since the diaphragm is innervated at C5, but respiratory reserve is reduced
B) The child will require continuous mechanical ventilation
C) Respiratory function will be entirely normal
D) A tracheostomy will be required immediately
Answer: A
Rationale: Table 34.1 places the diaphragm at C5, and the narrative states the boundary twice: "The child with a high-level cervical injury (C3 and above) requires continuous ventilatory assistance," and "Children with lesions below the C4 level are seldom ventilator dependent, but pulmonary vital capacity is significantly reduced" (p. 1334). At C5 the table records "Abdominal respiration" with "Poor respiratory reserve" (p. 1327).
Trap: (C) overshoots in the other direction, because the same sentence that spares this child the ventilator also says vital capacity is significantly reduced. (B) applies the high-cervical rule to a level well below it.

Subtopic: function by level

Q: A rehabilitation nurse is setting goals for two adolescents with complete injuries, one at C6 and one at C7. Which statement is accurate?
A) The C7 injury gains triceps function and can lift body weight, allowing almost complete independence within the limits of a wheelchair
B) Both will require an attendant for all transfers
C) The C6 injury will allow independent ambulation with braces
D) Neither will achieve functional use of the upper extremities
Answer: A
Rationale: At C7 the "Triceps and finger flexion and extensor muscle" are innervated, the child is "able to lift body weight," and the goal is "Almost complete independence within limitations of wheelchair," including rolling over in bed, sitting up in bed, and eating independently. At C6 there is wrist extension and good elbow flexion, and the child is "Able to assist in dressing and transfer" (p. 1327).
Trap: (C) borrows an ambulation goal from a far lower level, since it is T10 to L2 that achieves "Ambulation with bilateral long braces using four-point or swing-through crutch gait." (B) understates C7 and overstates the attendant requirement, which belongs at C5.

Q: A child admitted with a new thoracic spinal cord injury has flaccid paralysis and absent reflexes below the lesion. How should the nurse interpret this?
A) Spinal shock, in which flaccidity is later replaced by spinal reflex activity and increasing spasticity
B) A permanent lower motor neuron pattern that will not change
C) Autonomic dysreflexia
D) An incomplete injury, since reflexes are absent
Answer: A
Rationale: Spinal shock syndrome produces "flaccid paralysis below the level of the damage … caused by the sudden disruption of central and autonomic pathways," with "the absence of reflexes at or below the cord lesion, with flaccidity or limpness of the involved muscles, loss of sensation and motor function, and autonomic dysfunction" (p. 1330). "Most children with an SCI experience some spinal shock," and afterward "flaccid paralysis is replaced by spinal reflex activity and increasing spasticity" (p. 1331).
Trap: (C) names a complication that arrives later, once reflex activity returns, "especially when the lesions are above the midthoracic level" (p. 1331) — it cannot be present while the child is areflexic. (B) reads an acute, temporary phase as the final picture, which is the timing error this section is built on.

Topic: autonomic dysreflexia and long-term spinal cord injury care
Subtopic: recognizing and relieving dysreflexia

Q: A 14-year-old with a cervical spinal cord injury is on a standing table in physical therapy and becomes flushed and diaphoretic with a pounding headache. Blood pressure is rising and the heart rate is 50. What should the nurse do first?
A) Palpate the bladder for distention
B) Lower the child to a supine position and elevate the legs
C) Administer the ordered analgesic for the headache
D) End the therapy session and return the child to bed
Answer: A
Rationale: This is autonomic dysreflexia, whose signs are "a flushing face, sweating forehead, pupillary constriction, marked hypertension, headache, and bradycardia" (p. 1331). The stated sequence is that "vital signs, including blood pressure, are taken while the bladder is checked for distention (the usual precipitating cause). The bladder is drained slowly; if this does not relieve symptoms, any tight clothing is loosened, and the bowel is checked for the pressure of impacted feces" (p. 1335).
Trap: (B) treats this as orthostatic intolerance, which the chapter says to rule out quickly but which produces a drop in blood pressure and lightheadedness rather than a spike with headache and sweating (p. 1336). (C) relieves the symptom and abandons the search for the trigger, and "It can be a catastrophic event unless the irritation is relieved" (p. 1331). This is the chapter's own NCLEX question 2.

Q: Two children with spinal cord injury are assessed. One has hypertension with bradycardia; the other has hypotension with bradycardia and peripheral vasodilation. How should the nurse interpret these?
A) The first has autonomic dysreflexia and the second has neurogenic shock
B) Both have autonomic dysreflexia
C) The first has neurogenic shock and the second has autonomic dysreflexia
D) Bradycardia excludes both diagnoses
Answer: A
Rationale: Autonomic dysreflexia produces "marked hypertension, headache" with bradycardia (p. 1331), while "Neurogenic shock consists of hypotension, bradycardia, and vasodilation. Inotropic medications may be required to maintain adequate perfusion" (p. 1334).
Trap: (C) is the mirror error, and bradycardia is what makes it available, since it appears in both pictures. The blood pressure is the only finding that separates them, and it sends the treatment in opposite directions.

Q: Which factors can precipitate autonomic dysreflexia in a child with a spinal cord injury? Select all that apply.
A) A full bladder
B) Fecal impaction
C) Tight clothing
D) A pressure ulcer
E) A meal high in sodium
Answer: A, B, C, D
Rationale: The trigger is "visceral distention or irritation, particularly of the bowel or bladder," and "The precipitating stimulus may be merely a full bladder or rectum" (p. 1331). The fuller list adds "bowel impaction and abdominal distention, pressure ulcers, tight clothing, burns, DVT, menses, trauma, fractures, pregnancy, labor, surgery or invasive procedures, any painful stimulus, and hyperthermia" (pp. 1335–1336).
Trap: (E) is the wrong option and it borrows a cardiovascular association that does not belong here. The unifying principle is a noxious or distending stimulus below the level of the lesion that the child cannot feel, which is exactly why the assessment starts at the bladder and moves to the bowel.

Subtopic: living with the injury

Q: An adolescent with a lumbar-level spinal cord injury asks why the team wants him upright in braces part of each day when a wheelchair is easier. What is the best response?
A) Being weight bearing at least part of the time minimizes the risk of osteoporosis and hypercalcemia
B) Braces will restore normal walking
C) Wheelchair use should be avoided entirely
D) Bracing requires less energy than wheelchair mobility
Answer: A
Rationale: "It is especially vital that children with lumbar levels of injury be taught to walk functionally so that they are weight bearing at least part of the time; this minimizes the risk of osteoporosis and hypercalcemia" (p. 1331).
Trap: (D) reverses the comparison the chapter gives: "The energy required in walking with crutches and braces is two to four times greater than that required for normal walking," while "Wheelchair mobility has the advantages of requiring no more energy than normal walking" (p. 1336). That is also why (C) fails — the wheelchair is the efficient option rather than a concession, and the standing time is prescribed for the bones, not for getting anywhere.

Topic: duchenne muscular dystrophy and juvenile dermatomyositis
Subtopic: duchenne muscular dystrophy

Q: Which statements accurately describe Duchenne muscular dystrophy? Select all that apply.
A) The absence of dystrophin leads to muscle fiber degeneration
B) Onset is usually between 3 and 5 years of age
C) Affected boys have a waddling gait and lordosis and fall frequently
D) Ambulation usually becomes impossible by about 12 years of age
E) Cognitive and intellectual impairment is rare
Answer: A, B, C, D
Rationale: "The absence of dystrophin leads to a number of problems in muscle, including muscle fiber degeneration" (p. 1339). Box 34.13 gives "Early onset, usually between 3 and 5 years of age" and "Loss of independent ambulation by 9–12 years of age," and the clinical course states that "affected boys have a waddling gait and lordosis, fall frequently" and "Ambulation usually becomes impossible by 12 years of age" (p. 1340).
Trap: (E) is the wrong option and it reverses a finding the chapter states plainly: "Mild to moderate mental impairment is commonly associated with MD. The mean intelligence quotient (IQ) is approximately 20 points below normal, and frank mental deficit is present in 20% to 30% of these children" (p. 1340). This is the chapter's own NCLEX question 5.

Q: The parents of a 7-year-old with Duchenne muscular dystrophy point out his large, firm calves and ask whether extra effort and exercise could halt the disease. How should the nurse respond?
A) The enlarged calves are fatty infiltration rather than strength, and effort cannot stop the progression, although staying as active as possible delays wheelchair dependence
B) The large calves show preserved muscle, so strengthening exercises can reverse the weakness
C) Rest is the priority, and activity should be limited
D) Nothing affects the timing of wheelchair dependence
Answer: A
Rationale: "Muscles, especially in the calves, thighs, and upper arms, become enlarged from fatty infiltration and feel unusually firm or woody on palpation" (p. 1340). The misconception is named in the source: "Many parents erroneously believe that by exerting sufficient effort, the child can overcome the weakness and prevent progression of the disease process" (p. 1342). And activity still matters: "In general, children who remain as active as possible are able to avoid wheelchair confinement for a longer time" (p. 1341).
Trap: (C) is the intuitive response to a wasting disease and it accelerates the problem, since physical therapy "should begin if bed rest extends beyond a few days" and the daily goal for a well child with moderate disability is "at least 3 hours of ambulation." (D) discards the one variable the family can still influence.

Subtopic: juvenile dermatomyositis

Q: A 7-year-old girl has proximal symmetric muscle weakness, a violet discoloration of the eyelids, and raised lesions over the knuckles and knees, with elevated muscle enzymes. Which nursing priorities apply?
A) Ensuring an adequate airway because of the risk of aspiration pneumonia, and teaching daily sunscreen use with adequate calcium and vitamin D
B) Withholding all physical therapy until the rash resolves
C) Avoiding sun exposure only during disease flares
D) Anticipating distal muscle weakness as the earliest finding
Answer: A
Rationale: Juvenile dermatomyositis presents with "bilateral symmetric proximal weakness; a characteristic heliotropic rash of the eyelids; Gottron papules on the knuckles, knees, and elbows," and elevated serum enzymes (p. 1338). "A common problem in JDM is aspiration pneumonia, and measures should be taken to ensure the child has an adequate airway at all times." "All children with JDM should use a sunscreen to protect against ultraviolet A and B rays. Vitamin D and adequate dietary intake of calcium are also recommended."
Trap: (B) withholds the therapy the chapter calls essential: "Physical therapy is essential to prevent contracture deformity and to rebuild muscle strength," and "Meticulous skin care is an important nursing consideration." (D) inverts the pattern, since "Distal muscle strength and reflex responses remain unaffected" while the proximal limb and trunk muscles are the ones that weaken.
