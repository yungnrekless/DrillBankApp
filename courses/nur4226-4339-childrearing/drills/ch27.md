# Chapter 27 — The Child With Cardiovascular Dysfunction

Source: `courses/nur4226-4339-childrearing/sources/Ch27_Fact_Bank.md`
(Hockenberry, Wilson & Rodgers, *Wong's Nursing Care of Infants and Children*, 11th ed.,
Chapter 27, printed pp. 958–1016.)

**Blueprint weight: ~6 questions of 202, depth `standard`.** Band is 30–45 questions; this
file carries **44** across **10 topics**, averaging 4.4 each.

**Where the weight went.** Digoxin gets its own topic because the chapter calls it "a
potentially dangerous drug because the margin of safety between therapeutic, toxic, and
lethal doses is very narrow," and because more of its content is actionable at the bedside
than any other drug here. Heart failure, hypoxemia, and the defect classification follow,
since those three carry the chapter's own review questions and its densest confusable sets.
Kawasaki disease gets a full topic despite being late in the chapter, because it is now "a
leading cause of acquired heart disease in children in the United States."

**Drilled from both ends:** hypoxemia against hypoxia against cyanosis, which are a blood
value, a tissue state, and a visible sign; furosemide against spironolactone on the potassium
instruction; low against high serum potassium with digoxin, where only one direction is
dangerous; atrial against ventricular septal defect on whether heart failure is expected;
supraventricular against ventricular tachycardia on QRS width; and infective endocarditis
against acute rheumatic fever, which are a direct infection and an abnormal immune response
weeks later.

**The chapter's own review question 4 is not drilled as the source keys it.** Its answer key
marks three statements that appear verbatim as correct digoxin instruction as needing
correction, and passes the two the text contradicts — placing the drug in formula, and
repeating a dose after vomiting. The fact bank records the defect rather than resolving it, so
the digoxin items below are built from the text's own teaching box and the preamble says so.
If an exam reuses that item unchanged, its keyed answer is A, C, E.

**Two source gaps were left as gaps.** The chapter prints digoxin's half-life on p. 971 with
the numeral missing, supplying it only four pages later; no item turns on the incomplete
sentence. The p. 1001 sedation age threshold for echocardiography also drops a numeral, which
the fact bank recovered from the artwork as 2½ — recovered, not inferred, and not drilled.

**What ~6 blueprint items could not buy.** Left undrilled: normal cardiac structure and the
fetal-to-postnatal transition; the diagnostic procedure table; dyslipidemia and the lipid
values; bradydysrhythmias; pulmonary hypertension; cardiomyopathy beyond its three-way split
in one trap; advanced heart failure and transplantation; and the interstage home monitoring
program. Cardiomyopathy is the largest real loss, since the chapter distinguishes three types
with different treatments.

**Select-all count: 5** (1 in 8.8), each on a source list that genuinely sorts two ways: the
post-catheterization assessments against two the chapter's own item plants as wrong, the heart
failure manifestations across their three categories, the digoxin family teaching against a
statement the text contradicts, the endocarditis prophylaxis list against a defect that no
longer qualifies, and the Kawasaki criteria against a cardiac finding that is not one of the
five. The Jones criteria would have made a sixth, but counting major against minor
manifestations is what the diagnosis actually requires, so that material is drilled as a
single-answer item about whether a given presentation meets the threshold.

Topic: cardiac catheterization nursing care

Q: A child has just returned from cardiac catheterization. Which assessments should the nurse perform? Select all that apply.
A) Check pulses below the catheterization site for equality and symmetry
B) Check the dressing for bleeding or hematoma in the femoral or antecubital area
C) Assess temperature and color of the affected extremity
D) Monitor blood pressure, especially for hypotension
E) Take vital signs every 30 to 45 minutes
Answer: A, B, C, D
Rationale: The chapter lists six postprocedural assessments: pulses especially below the site, temperature and color of the affected extremity because "coolness or blanching may indicate arterial obstruction," vital signs as often as every 15 minutes, blood pressure "especially for hypotension," the dressing, and fluid intake (p. 966).
Trap: (E) understates the frequency — the text says as often as every 15 minutes, and this is one of the two distractors the chapter's own review question plants; the other is ambulating the child, when the extremity is kept straight for 4 to 6 hours after venous and 6 to 8 hours after arterial catheterization.

Q: Bleeding begins at a child's femoral catheterization site. Where should the nurse apply pressure?
A) 2.5 cm above the percutaneous skin site, to localize pressure over the vessel puncture
B) Directly over the skin puncture site
C) 2.5 cm below the skin site, along the line of venous return
D) Over the femoral pulse distal to the site
Answer: A
Rationale: The Nursing Alert states that "If bleeding occurs, direct, continuous pressure is applied 2.5 cm (1 inch) above the percutaneous skin site to localize pressure over the vessel puncture" (p. 966).
Trap: (B) is the intuitive location and misses the vessel entry point, which sits proximal to the skin entry because the catheter travels at an angle; the concern that makes this urgent is that hypotension may indicate hemorrhage from cardiac perforation or bleeding at the site.

Q: A nurse is preparing a 10-month-old for cardiac catheterization. Which finding may be a reason to cancel the procedure?
A) Severe diaper rash, if femoral access is required
B) A resting heart rate of 130 beats per minute
C) A hemoglobin of 14 g/dl
D) Crying during the preoperative assessment
Answer: A
Rationale: The chapter states that "Severe diaper rash may be a reason to cancel the procedure if femoral access is required," and directs obtaining an accurate height, which is essential for correct catheter selection, plus a history of allergic reactions because some contrast agents are iodine based (p. 965).
Trap: (B) is a normal infant rate, and (C) is a normal hemoglobin; the other preprocedural step most often missed is assessing and marking the pedal pulses before the child leaves for the catheterization room, so the postprocedural comparison is meaningful.

Q: A family is being discharged the day after cardiac catheterization. Which instruction is correct?
A) Avoid tub baths for the first 3 days, and avoid strenuous exercise for several days, but the child may attend school
B) Keep the child on bed rest at home for 1 week
C) Keep the pressure dressing in place for 5 days
D) Restrict the diet to clear liquids for 24 hours
Answer: A
Rationale: The Family-Centered Care box directs removing the pressure dressing the day after, covering with an adhesive bandage for several days, avoiding tub baths for the first 3 days, avoiding strenuous exercise for several days while allowing school attendance, and a regular diet without restrictions (p. 966).
Trap: (B) and (D) both over-restrict a child the chapter returns to normal activity quickly; families are also told to watch for redness, swelling, drainage, bleeding, fever, and coolness of the catheterized leg.

Topic: heart failure in children

Q: A nurse is assessing a 3-month-old for heart failure. Which findings indicate it? Select all that apply.
A) A sleeping heart rate above 160 beats per minute
B) A respiratory rate above 60 breaths per minute
C) Weight gain
D) Periorbital edema
E) Distended neck veins
Answer: A, B, C, D
Rationale: The chapter names tachycardia with a sleeping heart rate above 160 in infants as "One of the earliest signs of CHF," tachypnea above 60 breaths per minute, weight gain as "The earliest sign" of edema, and periorbital edema among the systemic venous congestion findings (pp. 969–970).
Trap: (E) is a real manifestation in the wrong age group — "Distended neck veins are difficult to detect in the short, fat necks of infants and are usually observed only in older children." Box 27.3 sorts every manifestation into impaired myocardial function, pulmonary congestion, or systemic venous congestion, and sorting first is what makes an all-or-nothing item survivable.

Q: A child with heart failure is receiving furosemide. Which nursing instruction is correct?
A) Encourage high-potassium foods, because furosemide causes potassium excretion and hypokalemia may precipitate digitalis toxicity
B) Avoid potassium supplements, because furosemide is potassium sparing
C) Restrict fluids to 50% of maintenance in all cases
D) Give the drug at bedtime to minimize daytime urination
Answer: A
Rationale: Table 27.3 calls furosemide the "Drug of choice in severe heart failure," notes that it causes excretion of chloride and potassium and that "hypokalemia may precipitate digitalis toxicity," and directs encouraging high-potassium foods and observing for signs of digoxin toxicity (p. 970).
Trap: (B) describes spironolactone, whose instruction is the opposite — "Do not administer potassium supplements" — and giving potassium alongside spironolactone or an angiotensin-converting enzyme inhibitor is the hyperkalemia trap this pair exists to catch.

Q: A nurse is reviewing the goals of heart failure management. Which set does the chapter state?
A) Improve cardiac function, remove accumulated fluid and sodium, decrease cardiac demands, and improve tissue oxygenation
B) Increase preload, increase afterload, increase contractility, and increase heart rate
C) Restrict all fluid, restrict all sodium, restrict all activity, and sedate the child
D) Correct the underlying defect surgically before any medical management
Answer: A
Rationale: The chapter numbers four goals: "(1) improve cardiac function (increase contractility and decrease afterload), (2) remove accumulated fluid and sodium (decrease preload and minimize fluid overload), (3) decrease cardiac demands, and (4) improve tissue oxygenation and decrease oxygen consumption" (p. 970).
Trap: (B) inverts the hemodynamic direction on afterload and preload, which is the axis the whole treatment plan turns on; note that fluid restriction "may be required in the acute stages" but infants rarely need it, because heart failure already makes feeding hard enough that they struggle to take maintenance volumes.

Q: An infant with heart failure is being fed every 2 hours around the clock, and the parents wake the baby for each feeding. What should the nurse advise?
A) This does not provide enough rest between feedings, and care should be organized to allow uninterrupted periods of sleep
B) Continue, since frequent small feedings are the goal in heart failure
C) Increase to hourly feedings to reduce the work of each feeding
D) Switch to a low-sodium formula to reduce fluid retention
Answer: A
Rationale: The chapter states that feeding every 2 hours "does not provide enough rest between feedings," and that nursing care is organized "to allow for uninterrupted periods of sleep" — this is the keyed statement in the chapter's own review question on reducing cardiac demands (p. 975).
Trap: (D) reaches for a measure the chapter uses infrequently in infants, because "infants need a normal sodium source to offset the sodium depletion of chronic diuretic therapy"; the underlying principle is that rest is itself a treatment when the goal is decreasing cardiac demand.

Q: A nurse is explaining why the body's compensation for heart failure eventually fails. Which statement is accurate?
A) Sympathetic stimulation raises heart rate, but tachycardia shortens diastole, increases myocardial oxygen consumption, and impairs coronary artery perfusion
B) Ventricular dilation increases contractile force without limit
C) Hypertrophy improves oxygenation of the heart muscle
D) Renin-angiotensin activation reduces preload over time
Answer: A
Rationale: The chapter describes sympathetic stimulation as increasing force and rate in the short term while tachycardia "shortens diastole, increases myocardial oxygen consumption, impairs coronary artery perfusion," and increased systemic vascular resistance increases afterload (pp. 968–969).
Trap: (B) omits the limit the chapter states — "Beyond a certain amount of dilation, the force of contraction decreases and the heart fails"; (C) reverses the cost of hypertrophy, where increased muscle mass impairs oxygenation of the heart muscle itself.

Topic: digoxin administration and toxicity

Q: A nurse is about to give digoxin to a 5-month-old. The apical pulse is 96 beats per minute. What should the nurse do?
A) Hold the dose and notify the practitioner, since the drug is withheld in infants and young children when the apical pulse is below 90 to 110
B) Give the dose, since 96 is above 90
C) Give the dose and recheck the pulse in 1 hour
D) Hold the dose only if the rate is below 60
Answer: A
Rationale: The chapter gives age-tiered hold parameters: below 90 to 110 beats per minute in infants and young children, below 70 in older children, and below 60 in adults, with the apical rate counted for 1 full minute (p. 974).
Trap: (D) applies the adult threshold to an infant, which is the level-swap this table exists to prevent; the apical rate is used "because a pulse deficit (radial pulse rate lower than apical) may be present with decreased cardiac output."

Q: A child receiving digoxin has a serum potassium of 3.1 mmol/L. What is the significance?
A) A fall in serum potassium enhances the effects of digoxin and increases the risk of toxicity
B) A fall in serum potassium diminishes the effects of digoxin and reduces the risk of toxicity
C) Serum potassium does not interact with digoxin
D) The finding is expected and requires no action
Answer: A
Rationale: A Nursing Alert states that "A fall in the serum potassium level enhances the effects of digoxin, increasing the risk of digoxin toxicity. Increased serum potassium levels diminish digoxin's effect. Therefore serum potassium levels (normal range, 3.5 to 5.5 mmol/L) must be carefully monitored" (p. 971).
Trap: (B) is the exact reversal and reads plausibly, which is why the chapter states both directions in one alert; the practical consequence is that a child on furosemide and digoxin together needs potassium watched closely.

Q: An infant on digoxin has vomited twice today, is refusing feeds, and has a heart rate of 88. What should the nurse suspect?
A) Digoxin toxicity, since bradycardia, anorexia, nausea, and vomiting are its most common signs in infants and children
B) Gastroenteritis, since vomiting in infants is usually gastrointestinal
C) Heart failure worsening, since anorexia accompanies it
D) A normal response to the drug that requires no action
Answer: A
Rationale: The chapter states that "The most common signs of digoxin toxicity in infants and children are bradycardia (although other dysrhythmias may occur), anorexia, nausea, and vomiting," and notes that vomiting from toxicity "is often unrelated to feedings" with the infant "usually less interested in feeding" (p. 974).
Trap: (B) is the competing explanation the chapter anticipates, which is why one episode of vomiting alone does not warrant stopping the drug; what shifts the reading here is the combination of repeated vomiting, decreased intake, and a low heart rate. The therapeutic serum range is 0.8 to 2 mcg/L.

Q: A nurse is teaching parents to give digoxin at home. Which statements indicate correct understanding? Select all that apply.
A) Give the drug at regular intervals, usually every 12 hours
B) Direct it slowly to the side and back of the mouth
C) If a dose is missed, do not give an extra dose
D) If more than two consecutive doses are missed, notify the physician
E) If the baby vomits, give a second dose to replace it
Answer: A, B, C, D
Rationale: The Family-Centered Care box directs giving the drug at regular intervals usually every 12 hours, administering it slowly directed to the side and back of the mouth, not giving an extra dose if one is missed, and notifying the physician if more than two consecutive doses have been missed (p. 975).
Trap: (E) reverses an explicit instruction — "If the child vomits, do not give a second dose." Note that the chapter's own review question keys three of the correct statements here as needing correction; these items follow the text rather than that key, and the two statements the text actually contradicts are repeating a dose after vomiting and mixing the drug into formula.

Q: A parent asks whether the digoxin can be mixed into the baby's bottle to make it easier to give. What should the nurse advise?
A) Do not mix the drug with foods or other fluids, because refusal to consume them would result in inaccurate intake
B) Mixing it into formula is acceptable if the full bottle is taken
C) Mixing it into a small amount of juice is preferred to reduce tooth decay
D) Mixing it into cereal is acceptable once solids are started
Answer: A
Rationale: The chapter directs parents not to mix the drug with foods or other fluids, "because refusal to consume them would result in inaccurate intake," and to give water afterward and brush the teeth "to prevent tooth decay from the sweetened liquid" (p. 975).
Trap: (B) is the most reasonable-sounding version of the error and still fails whenever the bottle is not finished, which is common in an infant with heart failure; the drug should also be kept in a safe place, preferably a locked cabinet.

Topic: hypoxemia, cyanosis, and hypercyanotic spells

Q: A child with severe anemia has a PaO2 of 45 mm Hg but does not appear cyanotic. How should the nurse interpret this?
A) Cyanosis requires 5 g/dl or more of deoxygenated hemoglobin, so severe anemia can mask it despite significant hypoxemia
B) The absence of cyanosis means oxygenation is adequate
C) Anemia intensifies cyanosis, so another explanation should be sought
D) Cyanosis and hypoxemia always appear together
Answer: A
Rationale: The chapter states that cyanosis "results from the presence of deoxygenated hemoglobin... at a concentration of 5 g/dl of blood or more," is usually apparent when arterial saturation is 85% or lower, and that "Children with severe anemia may not be cyanotic despite severe hypoxemia" (p. 976).
Trap: (C) reverses the relationship; the mirror case is that "Patients with polycythemia may appear cyanotic despite a near-normal PaO2," so appearance is unreliable in both directions — hypoxemia is a blood value, hypoxia is a tissue state, and cyanosis is only what you see.

Q: An infant with tetralogy of Fallot becomes acutely cyanotic and hyperpneic while crying. What should the nurse do first?
A) Place the infant in the knee-chest position
B) Administer 100% oxygen by face mask
C) Give morphine through the existing intravenous line
D) Begin intravenous fluid replacement
Answer: A
Rationale: The Applying Evidence to Practice guidelines order the response: place the infant in knee-chest position, employ a calm comforting approach, administer 100% oxygen by face mask, give morphine, begin fluid replacement if needed, and repeat morphine (p. 977).
Trap: (B) and (C) are both correct steps out of order, which is what this item tests; the knee-chest position works because it "reduces the venous return from the legs (which is desaturated) and increases systemic vascular resistance, which diverts more blood into the pulmonary artery," and morphine reduces the infundibular spasm that started the spell.

Q: A nurse is starting an intravenous infusion on a child with a right-to-left intracardiac shunt. Which precaution is essential?
A) Use filters on all intravenous lines, because air in the venous system can travel directly to the brain
B) Use only large-bore catheters to reduce turbulence
C) Warm all fluids to body temperature before infusion
D) Avoid dextrose-containing solutions
Answer: A
Rationale: A Nursing Alert states that intracardiac right-to-left shunting "allows air in the venous system to go directly to the brain, which results in an air embolism. Therefore all IV lines should have filters in place to prevent air from entering the system," with tubing and flush syringes checked for air (p. 984).
Trap: (D) is the opposite of what these children often need, since infants and patients with polycythemia may need dextrose-containing fluids to prevent hypoglycemia; the embolism risk here is mechanical rather than metabolic.

Q: A child with chronic hypoxemia has a hematocrit of 62%. What does the chapter identify as the risks?
A) Increased blood viscosity with thromboembolic risk, crowding out of platelets and clotting factors, and increased postoperative bleeding
B) Improved oxygen delivery with no associated risk
C) A reduced risk of stroke because of the higher oxygen-carrying capacity
D) Decreased risk of postoperative bleeding because of the higher cell mass
Answer: A
Rationale: The chapter states that persistent hypoxemia stimulates erythropoiesis, producing polycythemia that "increases blood viscosity" and crowds out platelets and coagulation factors, so it "increases the likelihood of postoperative bleeding," with small venous clots able to cross a right-to-left shunt and reach the brain (pp. 976–977).
Trap: (C) and (D) both read a higher red cell mass as protective; the chapter's practical countermeasure is to keep the child well hydrated, treating fever, vomiting, and diarrhea promptly and keeping an oral electrolyte solution at home.

Topic: congenital heart defect classification

Q: A nurse is classifying congenital heart defects by hemodynamics. Which pairing is correct?
A) Aortic stenosis and obstruction to blood flow out of the heart
B) Ventricular septal defect and decreased pulmonary blood flow
C) Tricuspid atresia and increased pulmonary blood flow
D) Atrioventricular canal defect and obstruction to blood flow
Answer: A
Rationale: The chapter classifies defects into four hemodynamic groups — increased pulmonary blood flow, decreased pulmonary blood flow, obstructive, and mixed — placing aortic stenosis among the obstructive defects, ventricular septal defect and atrioventricular canal among those with increased pulmonary blood flow, and tricuspid atresia among those with decreased flow (pp. 979–987).
Trap: (B) and (C) each reverse the flow direction, and these four pairings are exactly the ones the chapter's own review question tests; note that the book calls the older cyanotic-versus-acyanotic split "inadequate and misleading," so the hemodynamic axis is the testable one.

Q: A newborn has high blood pressure and bounding pulses in the arms, weak femoral pulses, and cool lower extremities. Which defect does this describe?
A) Coarctation of the aorta
B) Patent ductus arteriosus
C) Tetralogy of Fallot
D) Transposition of the great arteries
Answer: A
Rationale: The chapter describes coarctation as localized narrowing near the ductal insertion producing increased pressure proximally and decreased pressure distally, with "high blood pressure and bounding pulses in the arms, weak or absent femoral pulses, and cool lower extremities with lower blood pressure" (p. 979).
Trap: (B) is characterized instead by a machinery-like murmur; note that coarctation repair needs no cardiopulmonary bypass because the defect lies outside the heart and pericardium, and elective surgery is advised within the first 2 years to prevent later hypertension.

Q: An infant has a defect described as an incomplete fusion of the endocardial cushions, with a low atrial septal defect continuous with a high ventricular septal defect and clefts of the mitral and tricuspid valves. Which chromosomal condition is most associated with it?
A) Down syndrome
B) Turner syndrome
C) Trisomy 18
D) 22q11 deletion
Answer: A
Rationale: The chapter states that atrioventricular canal defect "is the most common cardiac defect in children with Down syndrome," and describes the anatomy exactly as the stem gives it, with moderate to severe heart failure usual (p. 979).
Trap: (D) belongs to truncus arteriosus, where "Thirty-five percent of patients have 22q11 deletions" — both are real chapter associations attached to different defects, which is what makes this set confusable.

Q: A preterm infant has a patent ductus arteriosus. Which medical treatment does the chapter name?
A) Indomethacin, a prostaglandin inhibitor
B) Prostaglandin E1 infusion
C) Digoxin and furosemide alone
D) Balloon atrial septostomy
Answer: A
Rationale: The chapter states that "Administration of indomethacin (prostaglandin inhibitor) has proved successful in closing a patent ductus in premature infants and some newborns" (p. 979).
Trap: (B) does the opposite and is used to *maintain* ductal patency in ductus-dependent lesions such as hypoplastic left heart syndrome; (D) is the Rashkind procedure used in transposition to increase mixing. Confusing the prostaglandin agonist with the inhibitor reverses the intended effect entirely.

Q: A nurse is reviewing tetralogy of Fallot with a student. Which four defects make up the classic form?
A) Ventricular septal defect, pulmonic stenosis, overriding aorta, and right ventricular hypertrophy
B) Atrial septal defect, pulmonic stenosis, overriding aorta, and left ventricular hypertrophy
C) Ventricular septal defect, aortic stenosis, patent ductus arteriosus, and right ventricular hypertrophy
D) Atrial septal defect, ventricular septal defect, patent ductus arteriosus, and pulmonic stenosis
Answer: A
Rationale: The chapter states that "The classic form includes four defects: (1) ventricular septal defect (VSD), (2) pulmonic stenosis, (3) overriding aorta, and (4) right ventricular hypertrophy" (p. 981).
Trap: (B) swaps two of the four, and both swaps are internally consistent-sounding — an atrial rather than ventricular defect, and left rather than right hypertrophy; the hypertrophy is right-sided precisely because the pulmonic stenosis obstructs right ventricular outflow.

Topic: single ventricle palliation and postoperative cardiac care

Q: A nurse is explaining the staged repair of hypoplastic left heart syndrome. Which sequence is correct?
A) Norwood in the first week, bidirectional Glenn at 3 to 8 months, Fontan at 2 to 4 years
B) Glenn in the first week, Norwood at 3 to 8 months, Fontan at 2 to 4 years
C) Fontan in the first week, Norwood at 6 months, Glenn at 2 years
D) Norwood at 6 months, Glenn at 2 years, Fontan at 5 years
Answer: A
Rationale: The chapter gives stage I Norwood in "first week of life," stage II bidirectional Glenn at "3 to 8 months of age," and stage III Fontan at "2 to 4 years of age," the last directing systemic venous return to the lungs "without a ventricular pump" (p. 987).
Trap: (B) reverses the first two stages, which cannot work because the Glenn depends on the low pulmonary vascular resistance the Norwood establishes; note also that the modified Fontan "does not restore normal anatomy or hemodynamics."

Q: An infant with a modified Blalock-Taussig shunt has an oxygen saturation of 66%. How should the nurse interpret this?
A) The shunt may be too small or narrowed, and surgical revision or an additional shunt may be needed
B) The shunt is too large, and diuretics should be started
C) This is the expected saturation after a shunt
D) The pulse oximeter is malfunctioning, since saturation cannot fall this low
Answer: A
Rationale: The chapter states that saturations below 70% indicate a shunt that is too small or narrowed, with the newborn remaining severely hypoxemic, while saturations above 85% with heart failure signs indicate a shunt that is too large (p. 984).
Trap: (B) names the management for the opposite problem; both figures mark abnormalities in opposite directions, and acute cyanosis with signs of tissue hypoxia means shunt occlusion, which is a medical emergency.

Q: A child's chest tube drainage stops abruptly 6 hours after cardiac surgery, and the pulse pressure is narrowing. What should the nurse do?
A) Report it immediately, since these findings suggest cardiac tamponade
B) Document that the chest tube is functioning and drainage has resolved
C) Milk the tube and continue routine monitoring
D) Clamp the tube to prevent air entry
Answer: A
Rationale: The chapter identifies an abrupt stop in chest tube drainage, paired with narrowing pulse pressure and rising, equalizing filling pressures, as the tamponade cue to report immediately (p. 992).
Trap: (B) is the plausible-but-wrong reading that treats the stoppage as the tube having done its job; at the other extreme, excessive drainage is quantified as more than 3 ml/kg/hr for more than 3 consecutive hours, or 5 to 10 ml/kg in any 1 hour.

Q: A child is 36 hours post cardiac surgery with a temperature of 100.2 degrees Fahrenheit. How should the nurse interpret this?
A) Likely the inflammatory response to tissue trauma, which is expected in the first 24 to 48 hours
B) Certain evidence of surgical site infection requiring cultures now
C) Evidence of a transfusion reaction
D) Normal at any point in the postoperative course
Answer: A
Rationale: The chapter states that a temperature to about 100 degrees Fahrenheit in the first 24 to 48 hours is the inflammatory response to tissue trauma, and that after that period fever "is most likely infection" (p. 990).
Trap: (D) removes the time dependence that makes the finding interpretable, which is the whole point — the same temperature means different things on day 1 and day 4.

Topic: infective endocarditis and its prophylaxis

Q: A child has an insidious onset of unexplained low-grade intermittent fever, malaise, anorexia, and a new murmur. What should the nurse suspect?
A) Infective endocarditis
B) Acute rheumatic fever
C) Kawasaki disease
D) Viral myocarditis
Answer: A
Rationale: The chapter describes the onset of infective endocarditis as "usually insidious, with unexplained low-grade, intermittent fever," plus malaise, anorexia, heart failure symptoms, arthralgias, and weight loss, with "A new murmur or a change in an existing one" frequently found (p. 994).
Trap: (B) is the confusable partner and differs on mechanism and timing — rheumatic fever is an abnormal immune response 2 to 6 weeks after streptococcal pharyngitis, diagnosed by Jones criteria and antistreptolysin O titers, while endocarditis is a direct infection diagnosed by blood culture plus echocardiography.

Q: Which children should receive antibiotic prophylaxis before dental procedures? Select all that apply.
A) A child with an artificial heart valve
B) A child with a previous diagnosis of infective endocarditis
C) A child with unrepaired cyanotic congenital heart disease including palliative shunts
D) A child with a ventricular septal defect repaired with prosthetic material 3 months ago
E) A child with a ventricular septal defect repaired without prosthetic material 4 years ago
Answer: A, B, C, D
Rationale: Box 27.9 limits prophylaxis to artificial heart valves, previous infective endocarditis, unrepaired cyanotic disease including palliative shunts and conduits, repaired disease using prosthetic material "during the first 6 months after the procedure," residual defects adjacent to prosthetic material, and transplant recipients with valvulopathy (p. 995).
Trap: (E) falls outside every category — repaired without prosthetic material and well beyond any window — and the chapter states that "Other than these high-risk patients, antibiotic prophylaxis is no longer routinely recommended"; the 2015 statement shifted emphasis to excellent oral hygiene instead.

Q: How many blood cultures does the chapter recommend when infective endocarditis is suspected, and why?
A) Three, drawn with strict sterile technique because the common causative organisms live on the skin
B) One, since a single positive culture is diagnostic
C) Two, drawn at least 24 hours apart
D) Cultures are not useful, and diagnosis rests on echocardiography alone
Answer: A
Rationale: The chapter directs several blood specimens, "three recommended," drawn with strict sterile technique because the common organisms live on the skin, and gives the Duke criteria as two major, or one major and three minor, or five minor criteria — the two major being positive blood cultures and echocardiographic evidence (pp. 994–995).
Trap: (D) discards half the major criteria; note that culture-negative endocarditis is reported in 5% to 10% of cases, which is another reason multiple specimens matter, and the most common organisms are *Streptococcus viridans* and *Staphylococcus aureus*.

Q: The parent of a child at high risk for infective endocarditis asks what to watch for at home. What should the nurse emphasize?
A) Any unexplained fever, weight loss, or change in behavior such as lethargy, malaise, or anorexia must be reported
B) Only chest pain or palpitations need to be reported
C) Symptoms will always begin abruptly and be obvious
D) Routine monitoring is unnecessary once prophylaxis is prescribed
Answer: A
Rationale: The chapter directs that "Any unexplained fever, weight loss, or change in behavior (lethargy, malaise, anorexia) must be brought to the practitioner's attention," and that children at risk "should have blood drawn for culture if they have a fever without an obvious source" (p. 995).
Trap: (C) contradicts the insidious onset the chapter describes, which is exactly why the vague symptoms in the correct option are the ones families must be taught to take seriously; note that endocarditis may follow routine bacteremia from ordinary activities such as brushing teeth.

Topic: acute rheumatic fever and rheumatic heart disease

Q: A 9-year-old had streptococcal pharyngitis 3 weeks ago and now has migratory joint pain, a new murmur, and a fever of 38.8 degrees Celsius. How many Jones criteria does this satisfy, and is the diagnosis supported?
A) Two major manifestations, carditis and polyarthritis, which satisfies the requirement for diagnosis
B) One major and one minor, which does not satisfy the requirement
C) Three minor manifestations, which satisfies the requirement in all populations
D) No criteria, since fever alone is not diagnostic
Answer: A
Rationale: The chapter states that "Patients must have two major manifestations or one major and two minor manifestations for diagnosis," listing carditis and polyarthritis among the five major manifestations; the arthritis is characteristically migratory, affecting "one joint for 1 to 2 days, then another" (pp. 996–997).
Trap: (B) miscounts the murmur, which represents carditis and is major rather than minor; the timing fits, since the disease develops after streptococcal infection "usually within 2 to 6 weeks," and the age of 5 to 15 years with a preceding sore throat is itself a diagnostic clue.

Q: Which manifestation of acute rheumatic fever leads to permanent damage?
A) Carditis
B) Polyarthritis
C) Chorea
D) Erythema marginatum
Answer: A
Rationale: The chapter states that "Carditis is the only manifestation of ARF that leads to permanent damage," and that rheumatic heart disease is "the most significant complication of ARF," occurring in more than half the cases, with the mitral valve most often affected (p. 996).
Trap: (B) is the most frequent major manifestation at 35% to 66% and leaves no permanent injury, which is what makes it a tempting choice; echocardiography is now recommended in all suspected and confirmed cases because it is more sensitive than auscultation for valve dysfunction.

Q: A child is diagnosed with acute rheumatic fever with carditis. How long should secondary prophylaxis continue?
A) 10 years or until age 21, whichever is longer
B) 5 years or until age 21, whichever is longer
C) Until age 40 and possibly indefinitely
D) Until two negative throat cultures are obtained
Answer: A
Rationale: The chapter tiers the duration by cardiac involvement: without carditis, 5 years or until age 21; with carditis, 10 years or until age 21; with established rheumatic heart disease, until age 40 and possibly indefinitely (p. 997).
Trap: (B) and (C) are the adjacent tiers, and the tier is set by the extent of cardiac involvement rather than by symptom resolution; the preferred agent is intramuscular benzathine penicillin G every 28 days "because it is most effective."

Q: A child is being evaluated for possible acute rheumatic fever and is in pain from arthritis. What does the chapter say about starting aspirin?
A) The diagnosis must be confirmed before aspirin is started, so clinical signs are not masked by the therapy
B) Aspirin should be started immediately to prevent cardiac sequelae
C) Aspirin is contraindicated in rheumatic fever at any point
D) Prednisone should be started first in all cases
Answer: A
Rationale: The chapter states that the diagnosis must be confirmed before aspirin therapy is started "so clinical signs are not masked by the therapy," and adds that "Neither salicylates nor prednisone has been shown to affect cardiac sequelae" (p. 997).
Trap: (B) assigns aspirin a protective role the chapter explicitly denies it; prednisone is reserved for pericarditis, heart failure, or aspirin failure rather than used first line.

Topic: kawasaki disease

Q: A 3-year-old has had fever for 6 days. Which findings would support a diagnosis of Kawasaki disease? Select all that apply.
A) Bilateral bulbar conjunctival injection without exudate
B) Red cracked lips and strawberry tongue
C) Erythema of the palms and soles with edema of the hands and feet
D) Unilateral cervical lymphadenopathy with a node larger than 1.5 cm
E) A loud pansystolic murmur with electrocardiographic changes
Answer: A, B, C, D
Rationale: Box 27.11 requires fever for at least 5 days plus at least four of five criteria: changes in the extremities, polymorphous exanthem, bilateral bulbar conjunctival injection without exudate, erythema and cracking of the lips with strawberry tongue, and cervical lymphadenopathy with a node greater than 1.5 cm, usually unilateral (p. 999).
Trap: (E) is not one of the five criteria at all, and cardiac findings are the distractor the chapter's own review question plants; note that the conjunctival reddening characteristically spares the limbus, clearing around the iris.

Q: A child with Kawasaki disease develops peeling of the skin beginning under the fingertips. Which phase is this?
A) Subacute
B) Acute
C) Convalescent
D) It occurs in all three phases equally
Answer: A
Rationale: The chapter places periungual desquamation in the subacute phase, which begins with resolution of fever and lasts until outward clinical signs disappear, along with thrombocytosis peaking about 3 weeks after fever onset (p. 998).
Trap: (B) is the misassignment the chapter's own review question tests, since peeling feels like part of the dramatic acute illness; the acute phase is defined by the high fever unresponsive to antibiotics and antipyretics plus the five criteria, and the convalescent phase is complete when laboratory values normalize at 6 to 8 weeks.

Q: A child with Kawasaki disease is receiving intravenous immunoglobulin. What is the single most important reason for giving it, and within what window?
A) It reduces the incidence of coronary abnormalities, and should be given within the first 10 days and optimally within the first 7
B) It controls fever, and may be given at any point in the illness
C) It prevents thrombocytosis, and should be given after the fever resolves
D) It replaces aspirin, which is no longer used in this disease
Answer: A
Rationale: The chapter states that intravenous immunoglobulin reduces duration of fever and incidence of coronary abnormalities "within the first 10 days of illness and optimally within the first 7 days," given as "A single large infusion of 2 g/kg over 8 to 12 hours" (p. 1000).
Trap: (D) is wrong because aspirin remains in use — but note the chapter's explicit negative finding that moderate- or high-dose aspirin "has not been shown to make a difference in the incidence of coronary artery sequelae." The coronary protection comes from the immunoglobulin; aspirin controls fever and inflammation, then drops to an antiplatelet dose of 3 to 5 mg/kg/day.

Q: A nurse is monitoring an infant during an intravenous immunoglobulin infusion for Kawasaki disease. What is the priority assessment?
A) Cardiac status, because of the large fluid load, watching for decreased urine output, gallop rhythm, tachycardia, and respiratory distress
B) Neurologic status, because of the risk of aseptic meningitis
C) Renal function, because of the risk of acute tubular necrosis
D) Skin integrity, because of the risk of infusion site breakdown
Answer: A
Rationale: The chapter directs monitoring cardiac status closely during the infusion "because of the large fluid load, particularly in children under 1 year," watching for heart failure signs including decreased urinary output, gallop rhythm, tachycardia, and respiratory distress (p. 1001).
Trap: (B) and (C) name plausible immunoglobulin concerns the chapter does not emphasize here; note also the specific documentation instruction — record the temperature just before aspirin administration, "because fever reflects ongoing inflammation and may indicate the need for further treatment."

Q: A child is being discharged after Kawasaki disease treated with intravenous immunoglobulin. What should the nurse teach about immunizations?
A) Measles-mumps-rubella and varicella vaccines should be delayed for 11 months after the immunoglobulin
B) All vaccines should be given on schedule, since immunoglobulin does not interfere
C) Only inactivated vaccines should be delayed, for 3 months
D) Vaccines should be delayed indefinitely while the child takes aspirin
Answer: A
Rationale: The chapter states that "The administration of measles-mumps-rubella vaccine should be delayed for 11 months after the administration of IVIG," and that varicella vaccine also should not be given for at least 11 months (p. 1001).
Trap: (D) confuses two separate instructions — aspirin is temporarily discontinued and the practitioner notified if the child is exposed to chickenpox or influenza because of the Reye syndrome association, which is about exposure rather than about immunization timing. Parents should also be told irritability is a hallmark and may persist up to 2 months.

Topic: childhood hypertension and supraventricular tachycardia

Q: A 15-year-old has repeated blood pressures of 134/84 mm Hg. How should this be classified?
A) Stage 1 hypertension, since for age 13 and older the range is 130 to 139 over 80 to 89
B) Elevated blood pressure, since the systolic is under 140
C) Stage 2 hypertension, since the diastolic exceeds 80
D) Normal, since adult thresholds do not apply to adolescents
Answer: A
Rationale: The chapter gives categorical cut points for age 13 and older: elevated blood pressure at 120 to 129 over less than 80, stage 1 at 130 to 139 over 80 to 89, and stage 2 at 140 over 90 or above (p. 1002).
Trap: (D) is wrong because the 2017 guideline specifically moved teenagers 13 and older to categorical cut points, while children 1 to 13 remain on percentile-based tiers — applying adult numbers to a 6-year-old is the error in the other direction.

Q: A 4-year-old is found to have stage 2 hypertension. What does the chapter suggest about the likely cause?
A) A secondary cause is likely, most commonly renal disease, since the younger the child and the more severe the hypertension, the more likely a secondary cause
B) Primary hypertension is most likely at any age
C) The cause is almost always endocrine in preschoolers
D) The finding is almost certainly white-coat hypertension
Answer: A
Rationale: The chapter states as a rule that "the younger the child and the more severe the hypertension, the more likely it is to be due to a secondary cause," and that when secondary, "the most common causes in young children is renal disease, followed by cardiovascular, endocrine, or some neurologic disorders" (p. 1002).
Trap: (D) names a real phenomenon — blood pressure above the 95th percentile in the office but below it outside — which would not explain repeated stage 2 readings; note also that upper and lower extremity pressures should always be assessed when hypertension is suspected, because of possible undiagnosed coarctation.

Q: An infant has a heart rate of 260 beats per minute with a narrow QRS complex, absent P waves, and abrupt onset. The infant is alert with adequate perfusion. What is the first intervention?
A) Vagal maneuvers, such as applying ice to the face
B) Synchronized cardioversion
C) Intravenous adenosine
D) Intravenous digoxin
Answer: A
Rationale: The chapter gives the treatment ladder as vagal maneuvers first — "applying ice to the face, massaging the carotid artery (on one side of the neck only), or having an older child perform a Valsalva maneuver" — then intravenous adenosine as "the first-line pharmacologic measure," then synchronized cardioversion for hemodynamic instability (p. 1011).
Trap: (C) is the correct *drug* and not the first *intervention* in a stable child, which is what this item tests; (B) is reserved for instability, and the chapter states that "Cardioversion should never be performed on a conscious patient" without sedation.

Q: A child with Wolff-Parkinson-White syndrome needs chronic management of supraventricular tachycardia. Which drug should be avoided?
A) Digoxin, because it can accelerate conduction across the accessory pathway
B) Propranolol, because it is too short-acting for children
C) Atenolol, because it is not approved in children
D) Adenosine, because it is ineffective in this syndrome
Answer: A
Rationale: The chapter states that "Digoxin should not be used in patients with WPW syndrome because it can accelerate conduction across the accessory pathway," with a beta blocker as the alternative first line — propranolol for infants and atenolol for older children (p. 1011).
Trap: (B) misstates propranolol's role, since being shorter-acting is precisely why it is chosen for infants; note that radiofrequency ablation "has become first-line therapy for some types of SVT," with success rates as high as 90%, and that a successful ablation is curative.
