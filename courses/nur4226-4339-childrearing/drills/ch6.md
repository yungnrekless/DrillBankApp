# Chapter 6 drills — Childhood Communicable and Infectious Diseases

Authored from `sources/Ch6_Fact_Bank.md` (Hockenberry, Wilson & Rodgers, 11th ed.,
printed pp. 169–195).

Blueprint weight is **7 questions of 202 (3.5%), depth `standard`** — 45 drills
across 10 topics, at the top of the 30–45 band.

The fact bank's own summary of this chapter is that it "is almost entirely made of
specifics, and that is what makes it heavy for its page count. Ages, doses,
intervals, incubation periods, periods of communicability, and precaution types
are the content, and every one of them is a potential single-best-answer item."
Twenty-seven printed pages against a 45-question ceiling means most of those
specifics go undrilled, so selection here followed one rule: **prefer the fact
that decides an action over the fact that decides a number.**

**The single most testable distinction is the precaution type**, and the chapter
gives the mechanism behind each — particle size, distance travelled, whether
special air handling is required — which makes the disease-by-disease assignments
derivable instead of memorized. Topic 1 drills the mechanism and topic 2 drills
the assignments, in that order, deliberately.

Four of the chapter's five NCLEX review questions are drilled: Standard
Precautions, Hib versus influenza, Td versus Tdap, and pneumococcal versus Hib.
The fifth (giardiasis versus enterobiasis) is drilled in topic 10.

**A note on the needle-size values.** Every vulgar fraction in this book is
typeset as an inline image, so the value never reaches the text layer and the pull
drops it silently. The three needle sizes on p. 176 were recovered by reading
those images directly, and all three are **⅝ inch**. They are printed in the
bank in brackets to mark their provenance; they are read values, not editorial
guesses, and are drilled as source text.

**What a `standard` band could not buy.** The full immunization schedule is not
drilled dose by dose — only the vaccines whose target disease or age rule carries
a discrimination. The four maternal-and-weight hepatitis B scenarios are
represented by the HBsAg-positive case alone. Vitamin A dosing in measles,
meningococcal epidemiology, H1N1, thimerosal, the Vaccine Information Statement
and compensation laws, Zika, bedbugs, and the stomatitis subtypes are each
covered by one item or none. Table 6.3's full parasite inventory is reduced to
the two comparisons the bank flags as confusable.

Topic: standard and transmission-based precautions

Q: Which precautions are designed for the care of all pediatric patients to reduce the risk of transmission of microorganisms from both recognized and unrecognized sources of infection?
A) Transmission-Based Precautions
B) Airborne Precautions
C) Standard Precautions
D) Droplet Precautions
Answer: C
Rationale: This is the chapter's own NCLEX question 1, and the stem quotes p. 169 almost verbatim: Standard Precautions "are designed for the care of all patients to reduce the risk of transmission of microorganisms from both recognized and unrecognized sources of infection."
Trap: (A) is the reflex pick because the phrase sounds comprehensive, and it is exactly backwards — Transmission-Based Precautions are "designed for patients with documented or suspected infection or colonization" with specific pathogens. The two phrases that identify Standard Precautions in a stem are **all patients** and **recognized and unrecognized**.

Q: A nurse lists the substances Standard Precautions are designed to protect against. Which are included? (Select all that apply.)
A) Blood
B) All body fluids, secretions, and excretions, whether or not they contain visible blood
C) Nonintact skin
D) Mucous membranes
E) Sweat
Answer: A, B, C, D
Rationale: Standard Precautions use "barrier protection (personal protective equipment [PPE]), such as gloves, goggles, gowns, and masks, to prevent contamination from blood; all body fluids, secretions, and excretions, except sweat, regardless of whether they contain visible blood; nonintact skin; and mucous membranes" (p. 169).
Trap: (E) is the one to reject, and it is the single exclusion in an otherwise universal list — which is precisely why it is the detail an item tests. Note also the 2007 additions to Standard Precautions: Respiratory Hygiene/Cough Etiquette and safe injection practices.

Q: A child is placed on Droplet Precautions. What is the nurse's responsibility regarding Standard Precautions?
A) Standard Precautions are replaced by Droplet Precautions for the duration
B) Transmission-Based Precautions are used in addition to Standard Precautions, and may be combined for diseases with multiple routes of transmission
C) Standard Precautions resume once the droplet order is discontinued
D) Standard Precautions apply only to the patient's roommate
Answer: B
Rationale: Two rules govern their use: "They may be combined for diseases that have multiple routes of transmission," and "They are to be used in addition to Standard Precautions" (p. 169). Box 6.1 repeats "In addition to Standard Precautions" at the head of every category.
Trap: (A) is the distractor the fact bank flags — replacing Standard Precautions with a transmission-based type. They are additive, never a substitute, which is also why varicella carries three precaution types at once rather than one.

Q: A nurse must explain why measles requires a negative-pressure room but pertussis does not. What is the mechanism?
A) Measles droplets are larger and therefore travel further
B) Airborne transmission involves droplet nuclei of 5 mm or less that remain suspended and can be dispersed widely by air currents, so special air handling and ventilation are required; droplet transmission involves particles larger than 5 mm that do not remain suspended and generally travel 3 feet or less, so special ventilation is not required
C) Measles is bacterial and pertussis is viral
D) The distinction is based on the severity of the disease, not the particle
Answer: B
Rationale: Airborne transmission is by "small-particle residue [≤5 mm] of evaporated droplets that may remain suspended in the air for long periods" which "can be dispersed widely by air currents"; droplets are "large-particle droplets (>5 mm)" that "generally travel only short distances, usually 3 feet or less" (p. 169).
Trap: (A) inverts the size relationship, which is the whole mechanism — the **smaller** particle is the one that stays airborne and needs ventilation. Note that 3 feet appears twice in this chapter: as droplet travel distance and as the cough-etiquette spacing.

Q: A nurse is asked to name the single most important practice for reducing transmission of infectious disease in a health care setting. What is it?
A) Wearing gloves for all patient contact
B) Hand hygiene, including hand washing with soap and water and the use of alcohol-based products for hand disinfection
C) Placing all children with fever in isolation
D) Routine environmental disinfection
Answer: B
Rationale: "Hand hygiene continues to be the single most important practice to reduce the transmission of infectious diseases in health care settings," and it is restated for resistant organisms: "Hand washing is the most critical infection control practice" (pp. 169–170). Approximately 2 million patients each year develop hospital-acquired infections, which "are preventable if caregivers practice meticulous hand washing, cleaning, and disposal techniques."
Trap: (A) is the visible intervention and it is not the ranked one. The fact bank's rule is worth carrying into any priority item in this chapter: hand hygiene is the default answer, and the chapter names it as most important twice.

Topic: precautions by communicable disease

Q: A child is admitted with an undiagnosed rash. Which precautions should be instituted?
A) Standard Precautions alone until the rash is identified
B) Contact, airborne, and droplet precautions plus Standard Precautions, until a diagnosis is confirmed
C) Airborne Precautions alone, since most exanthems are airborne
D) Droplet Precautions alone
Answer: B
Rationale: The Nursing Alert states: "If a child is admitted to the hospital with an undiagnosed exanthema, institute strict Transmission-Based Precautions (contact, airborne, and droplet) and Standard Precautions until a diagnosis is confirmed" (p. 187).
Trap: (C) applies the precaution for the most feared possibility instead of covering all of them, and the fact bank calls this the single most likely precautions item in the chapter because it inverts the usual logic: you cannot pick the precaution until you know the disease, so you use all three.

Q: A child with chickenpox is admitted. Which precautions are required, and when may they be discontinued?
A) Standard and Droplet, until the fever resolves
B) Standard, Airborne, and Contact, until all lesions are crusted — or for breakthrough varicella, until no new lesions are seen
C) Contact alone, until the rash fades
D) Airborne alone, until the fifth day of rash
Answer: B
Rationale: Varicella requires Standard plus Airborne plus Contact precautions "until all lesions are crusted"; for breakthrough varicella, "until no new lesions are seen" (p. 179). Scabs are not infectious, which is why crusting ends isolation.
Trap: (D) attaches the **measles** endpoint to varicella — measles requires Airborne Precautions "until fifth day of rash." The fact bank warns that a distractor gives the right precaution type with the wrong endpoint, and the "until" column is the second half of every precautions item.

Q: A school-age child is diagnosed with scarlet fever and started on antibiotics. When may precautions be discontinued?
A) Immediately, since treatment has begun
B) 24 hours after initiation of treatment
C) After two negative throat cultures
D) When the rash has fully desquamated
Answer: B
Rationale: Scarlet fever requires Standard plus Droplet precautions "until 24 hours after initiation of treatment" (p. 185).
Trap: (C) is the endpoint for **pharyngeal diphtheria**, which requires Standard plus Droplet "until two cultures are negative" (p. 179). Both are droplet-spread bacterial throat infections treated with antibiotics, so the endpoint is the only thing separating them.

Q: A nurse reviews Table 6.2 and is asked which diseases in it require Airborne Precautions. What is the answer?
A) All of the viral exanthems
B) Only varicella and measles
C) Measles, rubella, and mumps
D) Pertussis and diphtheria
Answer: B
Rationale: Of the diseases in Table 6.2, only varicella and measles require Airborne Precautions; Box 6.1 names the same two plus tuberculosis (pp. 169, 179, 182). Everything else in the table is Droplet, Contact, or Standard.
Trap: (C) groups the three classic childhood viral exanthems together, and two of them are wrong — rubella is Droplet and mumps is Droplet plus Contact. Being viral and rash-producing does not determine the route.

Q: A hospitalized child with sickle cell disease develops an aplastic crisis attributed to parvovirus B19. A well sibling at home also has fifth disease. What precautions apply to each?
A) Both require Droplet Precautions
B) The hospitalized child with aplastic crisis requires Standard plus Droplet Precautions; the well child at home requires none
C) Neither requires precautions, since fifth disease is benign
D) Both require Airborne Precautions
Answer: B
Rationale: Erythema infectiosum requires no precautions for the well child; the hospitalized immunosuppressed child or the child in aplastic crisis gets Standard plus Droplet Precautions (p. 181). Its period of communicability is "Uncertain but before onset of symptoms in children with aplastic crisis."
Trap: (A) applies one rule to both children, and the chapter deliberately splits them. The reason is timing: a well child with the classic rash is past the contagious period, while the child in aplastic crisis is not — so the same diagnosis carries different precautions depending on the presentation.

Topic: incubation and communicability periods

Q: A child was exposed to measles 12 days ago and is now well. What should the nurse tell the family about the incubation period?
A) The child is past the incubation period and cannot develop measles
B) The incubation period for measles is 10 to 20 days, so the child remains within it
C) The incubation period is 2 to 5 days, so the child is clear
D) The incubation period is 14 to 21 days, so the child is clear
Answer: B
Rationale: Measles has an incubation of 10 to 20 days, with a period of communicability "From 4 days before to 5 days after rash appears" (p. 182).
Trap: (D) is the incubation for **mumps and rubella**, both 14 to 21 days, and chickenpox overlaps at 14 to 16 days — the three are not separable by incubation alone. Only measles runs from 10 days.

Q: A parent asks when a child with measles was most contagious. What is the accurate answer?
A) Only while the rash is visible
B) From 4 days before the rash appears to 5 days after — so the child was contagious before any rash was seen
C) Beginning 24 hours after the rash appears
D) Only after the fever resolves
Answer: B
Rationale: Measles is communicable "From 4 days before to 5 days after rash appears" (p. 182). Koplik spots appear on the buccal mucosa opposite the molars 2 days before the rash, while the child is most infectious.
Trap: (A) is the intuition the fact bank names as an inversion — isolate a rash once it appears. Five diseases in this chapter are contagious before any sign shows: chickenpox 1 day before, measles 4 days before, rubella 7 days before, mumps immediately before swelling, and pertussis during the catarrhal stage. Rubella has the longest presymptomatic contagious window.

Q: A 6-year-old develops a sore throat and fever with an incubation history of about 3 days since exposure. Which two diseases in this chapter have the shortest incubation periods, and what is the nursing priority?
A) Measles and rubella; begin airborne precautions
B) Diphtheria and scarlet fever, both 2 to 5 days — and the priority is a throat culture, since a sore throat with fever may be diphtheria or streptococcal
C) Mumps and chickenpox; observe at home
D) Pertussis and polio; begin droplet precautions only
Answer: B
Rationale: Diphtheria incubates in "Usually 2-5 days, possibly longer" and scarlet fever in "2-5 days, with range of 1-7 days" (pp. 179, 185) — the two bacterial diseases in the table, and the two shortest incubations. The chapter's priority default for a sore throat with fever is a culture, because it may be diphtheria or streptococcal (p. 186).
Trap: (C) names diseases whose incubations run two to three weeks, which does not fit a 3-day history. The general pattern is worth holding: the viral exanthems in this chapter all run a week or more, so a short incubation points bacterial.

Q: A child with pertussis is admitted during the paroxysmal stage. When was the period of greatest communicability, and what is the immediate nursing priority during a paroxysm?
A) Communicability is greatest during the paroxysmal stage; the priority is to sit the child upright
B) Communicability is greatest during the catarrhal stage, before the onset of paroxysms; during a paroxysm, position the child on the side, ensure oxygenation, and suction
C) Communicability is greatest during convalescence; the priority is fluid replacement
D) Pertussis is not communicable once coughing begins
Answer: B
Rationale: Pertussis communicability is "Greatest during catarrhal stage before onset of paroxysms," and Droplet Precautions are maintained "during catarrhal stage" (p. 182). The chapter's priority default for an infant with pertussis paroxysms is to position on the side, ensure oxygenation, and suction.
Trap: (A) assumes the loudest phase is the most infectious phase, which is the reverse of the truth and is why pertussis spreads before anyone suspects it — during the catarrhal stage it looks like a cold. For scale, adolescents cough at least 21 days and up to half cough for 10 weeks.

Topic: immunity terminology and vaccine types

Q: A newborn is protected from several infections by antibodies received across the placenta. What type of immunity is this?
A) Natural active immunity
B) Natural passive immunity
C) Artificial active immunity
D) Herd immunity
Answer: B
Rationale: Active immunity is formed by the person's own immune bodies; passive immunity is "Temporary immunity obtained by transfusing immunoglobulins or antitoxins," and it can be acquired naturally from the mother to the fetus via the placenta or artificially by transfusion (p. 171).
Trap: (A) is the reflex pick because the antibody arrived by a natural route, and "natural" gets attached to the wrong axis. The two axes are independent: natural versus artificial describes how it was acquired, active versus passive describes who made the antibodies.

Q: A nurse must distinguish a vaccine, a toxoid, and an antitoxin. Which set of definitions is correct?
A) Vaccine: a modified bacterial toxin; toxoid: live or inactivated organisms; antitoxin: a conjugated polysaccharide
B) Vaccine: a suspension of live or inactivated organisms; toxoid: a modified bacterial toxin made nontoxic; antitoxin: ready-made antibodies from the serum of an immunized animal, conferring passive immunity
C) All three produce active immunity
D) Vaccine and toxoid both confer passive immunity
Answer: B
Rationale: Box 6.2 defines each of these on p. 171. An antitoxin confers **passive** immunity because the antibodies are transferred rather than formed, while vaccines and toxoids produce active immunity.
Trap: (C) is the reflex assumption that anything given to prevent disease works the same way. It matters clinically: a child receiving equine antitoxin needs a sensitivity test first with epinephrine available, a precaution that has no counterpart for a vaccine.

Q: An unvaccinated child in a highly vaccinated community is protected from a disease he was never immunized against. What concept describes this?
A) Active immunity
B) Herd immunity — the population that has been vaccinated protects those in the same population who are unvaccinated
C) Cocooning
D) Conjugate protection
Answer: B
Rationale: The definition names the unvaccinated explicitly: "the population that has been vaccinated protects those in the same population who are unvaccinated" (p. 171).
Trap: (C) is the nearest relative and is narrower and deliberate. Cocooning means "protecting infants from pertussis by vaccinating all persons who come in close contact with the infant, including the mother, grandparents, and health care workers" — a targeted ring around one infant, not a population effect, and pertussis-specific.

Q: A nurse documents that a child was "immunized" after administering a vaccine. What distinction does the chapter draw?
A) The terms are synonymous in all contexts
B) They are not synonymous: vaccination is the physical act of administering, while immunization is the process of inducing or providing immunity — the administration of a vaccine cannot automatically be equated with the development of adequate immunity
C) Immunization refers only to live vaccines
D) Vaccination refers only to inactivated vaccines
Answer: B
Rationale: "Although in this discussion the terms vaccination and immunization are used interchangeably in reference to active immunization, they are not synonymous because the administration of an immunobiologic such as a vaccine cannot automatically be equated with the development of adequate immunity" (p. 171).
Trap: (A) is what the chapter itself does in casual use and then explicitly disavows, which is what makes this testable. Giving the vaccine is an act; producing immunity is an outcome, and the two can come apart.

Topic: vaccine contraindications and precautions

Q: A nurse is deciding whether to withhold a vaccine. What is the difference between a contraindication and a precaution?
A) They are equivalent; both bar administration
B) A contraindication is a condition that increases the risk for a serious adverse reaction, and the vaccine is not given; a precaution might increase that risk or compromise the vaccine's ability to produce immunity, and the vaccine is given if the benefit outweighs the risk
C) A precaution bars administration and a contraindication permits it
D) A precaution applies only to live vaccines
Answer: B
Rationale: A contraindication is "a condition in an individual that increases the risk for a serious adverse reaction," and "one would not administer a vaccine when a contraindication is present." A precaution "might increase the risk... or that might compromise the ability of the vaccine to produce immunity," and "If conditions are such that the benefit of receiving the vaccine would outweigh the risk of an adverse event or incomplete response, a precaution would not prevent vaccine administration" (p. 177).
Trap: (A) treats both as absolute bars, and the chapter warns what that costs: "Unfounded fears and lack of knowledge regarding contraindications can needlessly prevent a child from having protection from life-threatening diseases."

Q: Which of the following are contraindications to immunization? (Select all that apply.)
A) Severe febrile illness
B) Altered immune function, for live virus vaccines
C) Recently acquired passive immunity through blood transfusion or immunoglobulin, for live virus vaccines
D) Anaphylactic reaction to a previously administered vaccine or one of its components
E) A family history of seizures
Answer: A, B, C, D
Rationale: The chapter lists severe febrile illness as the general contraindication for all immunizations, altered immunity and recent passive immunity as contraindications to live virus vaccines, and a known allergic response — specifically anaphylaxis to the vaccine or a component — as the fourth. Pregnancy is a contraindication to MMR (p. 177).
Trap: (E) is the one to reject and it appears on the chapter's explicit list of things "not considered contraindications." A distractor defers a pertussis-containing vaccine for a family seizure history; the chapter permits deferral only for a **neurologic contraindication in the child**.

Q: A parent reports that her child had contact dermatitis after neomycin-containing ointment and asks whether MMR can be given. What should the nurse do?
A) Withhold MMR permanently
B) Administer MMR — contact dermatitis in reaction to neomycin is not a contraindication; only a history of anaphylactic reaction to neomycin, gelatin, or the vaccine itself is
C) Administer half the dose and observe
D) Substitute individual measles, mumps, and rubella vaccines
Answer: B
Rationale: "Contact dermatitis in reaction to neomycin is not considered a contraindication to immunization," while "only a history of anaphylactic reaction to neomycin, gelatin, or the vaccine itself is considered a contraindication to their use" (p. 177).
Trap: (A) is the cautious-sounding answer and it withholds protection on the basis of a reaction type the chapter specifically clears. The fact bank calls this the sharpest pair in the section: anaphylaxis to neomycin is a contraindication, contact dermatitis to neomycin is not.

Q: A 4-year-old received immunoglobulin two weeks ago and is due for MMR and varicella vaccines. What should the nurse do?
A) Give both vaccines today
B) Postpone MMR and varicella for a minimum of 3 months after passive immunization with immunoglobulin or blood transfusion — except after washed red blood cells, which do not interfere with the immune response
C) Postpone for 1 month
D) Postpone for 28 days
Answer: B
Rationale: "Administration of MMR and varicella should be postponed for a minimum of 3 months after passive immunization with immunoglobulins and blood transfusions (except washed red blood cells, which do not interfere with the immune response)" (p. 177).
Trap: (C) and (D) are two other real intervals from this chapter attached to the wrong situation — **1 month** is the minimum between two live vaccines not given on the same day, and **28 days** is the pregnancy-avoidance interval after rubella vaccine. Three intervals, three situations; match the interval to the situation rather than to a general rule.

Q: A child arrives for a well visit with a runny nose and a temperature of 37.4° C, and is breastfeeding. Which of these is a reason to withhold today's vaccines?
A) The minor upper respiratory infection
B) Breastfeeding
C) Neither; minor illnesses such as the common cold are not a contraindication, and breastfeeding is not a contraindication for any vaccine
D) Both, until the child is well and weaned
Answer: C
Rationale: "The presence of minor illnesses, such as the common cold, is not a contraindication," and "Breastfeeding is not a contraindication for any vaccine" (p. 177). The general contraindication is a **severe** febrile illness, which avoids "adding the risk of adverse side effects from the vaccine to an already ill child or mistakenly identifying a symptom of the disease as having been caused by the vaccine."
Trap: (A) is the most common real-world deferral and it is one of the nine non-contraindications the chapter lists — alongside contact dermatitis to neomycin, egg sensitivity for MMR, family history of seizures, family history of a vaccine adverse event, penicillin allergy, duck allergy, and family history of sudden infant death syndrome.

Topic: pertussis diphtheria and tetanus vaccines

Q: A 4-year-old needs a vaccine containing diphtheria, tetanus, and pertussis components. Which preparation is correct?
A) Tdap
B) DTaP
C) Td
D) DT
Answer: B
Rationale: DTaP is the pediatric formulation for children 6 weeks through 6 years; Tdap is for those 11 and older; Td contains tetanus and diphtheria only; and DT is for a child under 7 who has a contraindication to the pertussis component (pp. 172–173).
Trap: (D) is the correct choice only when pertussis is contraindicated, and the stem specifies that the pertussis component is wanted. The capitalization convention carries the meaning: uppercase letters are full-strength antigens and lowercase are reduced, so DTaP and Tdap differ by which components are reduced, not by which diseases they cover.

Q: A pregnant adolescent who is not protected against pertussis asks which vaccine she should receive and when. What is correct?
A) Td, at any point in pregnancy
B) Tdap, optimally between 27 and 36 weeks of gestation, or postpartum before discharge from the hospital
C) DTaP, in the first trimester
D) No pertussis-containing vaccine is given during pregnancy
Answer: B
Rationale: This is the chapter's own NCLEX question 3, and the stem quotes p. 173 almost verbatim: the CDC and the American College of Obstetricians and Gynecologists recommend Tdap between 27 and 36 weeks of gestation or postpartum before discharge.
Trap: (A) is the whole question, and the discriminator is one lowercase letter — Td contains tetanus and diphtheria only, with **no pertussis component**, so it cannot protect against the disease the stem names.

Q: The parents of a 2-week-old ask how to protect their newborn from pertussis before the infant can be vaccinated. What strategy does the chapter describe?
A) Give the infant a dose of DTaP now
B) Cocooning — vaccinating all persons who come in close contact with the infant, including the mother, grandparents, and health care workers
C) Give the infant immunoglobulin monthly
D) Isolate the infant at home for 6 months
Answer: B
Rationale: Cocooning is "protecting infants from pertussis by vaccinating all persons who come in close contact with the infant, including the mother, grandparents, and health care workers" (p. 171).
Trap: (A) misses the reason the strategy exists — the infant is not the one vaccinated, because the vaccine cannot be given early enough to protect during the period of highest risk. Cocooning transfers the protection to the people around the infant, which is what makes it conceptually different from every other item in this chapter.

Q: A nurse reviews a child's record to determine whether he is fully vaccinated against pertussis. What constitutes a complete series?
A) Three doses of DTaP
B) Five doses of DTaP, or four doses with the fourth given on or after the fourth birthday
C) Four doses of DTaP regardless of timing
D) Two doses of Tdap
Answer: B
Rationale: A child is considered fully vaccinated for pertussis with "5 doses of DTaP, or 4 with the fourth on or after the fourth birthday" (p. 173). Diphtheria and tetanus protection lasts approximately 10 years.
Trap: (C) drops the timing condition, which is what makes the four-dose route valid — a fourth dose given before the fourth birthday does not complete the series. The count alone is not the answer.

Topic: specific vaccines and their target diseases

Q: Which childhood vaccine provides some protection against bacterial meningitis, epiglottitis, and bacterial pneumonia?
A) Hib vaccine
B) Hepatitis B vaccine
C) Varicella vaccine
D) Influenza vaccine
Answer: A
Rationale: This is the chapter's own NCLEX question 2. Hib conjugate vaccines "protect against a number of serious infections caused by H. influenzae type b, especially bacterial meningitis, epiglottitis, bacterial pneumonia, septic arthritis, and sepsis" (p. 174).
Trap: (D) exists because of the name similarity, and the chapter pre-empts it directly: "Hib is not associated with the viruses that cause influenza." Epiglottitis is the giveaway in the stem — it appears in the Hib list and nowhere else in the chapter.

Q: Which childhood vaccine provides protection against streptococcal infections such as otitis media, sinusitis, and pneumonia?
A) Rotavirus
B) Hib
C) Pneumococcal
D) MMR
Answer: C
Rationale: This is the chapter's own NCLEX question 4. Page 175 names "localized infections, such as otitis media, sinusitis, and pneumonia" among those caused by "Streptococcal pneumococci."
Trap: (B) is the strongest distractor and is the previous item's answer — both vaccines prevent otitis media and pneumonia, so the overlap does not discriminate. The tells are **epiglottitis and meningitis** for Hib and **sinusitis plus the word streptococcal** for pneumococcal. Read the organism named in the stem.

Q: An infant is born to a mother who is hepatitis B surface antigen positive. What is required, and in what time frame?
A) Hepatitis B vaccine alone, within 24 hours
B) Hepatitis B vaccine plus hepatitis B immune globulin 0.5 ml within 12 hours of birth, given at two separate sites
C) Hepatitis B immune globulin alone, at 1 month
D) Hepatitis B vaccine at 2 months with the routine series
Answer: B
Rationale: The newborn of an HBsAg-positive mother receives HepB vaccine plus HBIG 0.5 ml within 12 hours of birth, at two sites (p. 172). The stakes are in the epidemiology: perinatal infection becomes chronic in up to 90% of cases, compared with 25% to 50% for infection acquired before age 5.
Trap: (A) gives half the regimen. Passive and active immunization are both required here and do different jobs — the immune globulin provides antibodies now, the vaccine provokes the infant's own. Note the separate weight rule for the routine series: HepB is deferred below 2000 g (4.4 lb).

Q: A 4-month-old has not yet received any rotavirus vaccine. What limits the nurse's ability to start the series now, and what is the general rule for a lapsed series?
A) The series may not be started after 32 weeks of age; and a lapsed series of any vaccine must be restarted from the beginning
B) The rotavirus series may not be given after 32 weeks of age; and for a lapsed series, only the missed doses are given rather than restarting
C) There is no upper age limit for rotavirus; a lapsed series is restarted
D) Rotavirus may be given at any age, and a lapsed series continues where it left off
Answer: B
Rationale: The rotavirus vaccine is "not [given] after 32 weeks" (p. 176), and the chapter's catch-up rule is that a lapsed series is not restarted — only the missed doses are given (p. 172).
Trap: (A) pairs the correct age limit with the wrong catch-up rule, which is the fact bank's flagged inversion: "A lapsed vaccine series must be restarted" is the intuition, and the chapter says give only the missed doses. Note the related preterm rule that runs the same way: preterm infants receive the full dose at the appropriate **chronologic** age, not a delayed or reduced one.

Topic: immunization administration technique

Q: A nurse is preparing an intramuscular immunization for a 3-day-old newborn. What needle size and site are recommended?
A) 1 inch, 22 to 25 gauge, deltoid
B) ⅝ inch, 22 to 25 gauge, vastus lateralis
C) 1.5 inch, 20 gauge, dorsogluteal
D) ⅝ inch, 22 to 25 gauge, deltoid
Answer: B
Rationale: For newborns 0 to 28 days old, "recommended needle size is [⅝] inch, 22 to 25 gauge, recommended injection site: vastus lateralis" (p. 176). The value was recovered by reading the printed artwork, since the fraction is typeset as an inline image and does not reach the text layer.
Trap: (D) keeps the right needle and moves to a site with inadequate muscle mass at this age — the deltoid is an option only from 1 month "if muscle mass is adequately developed." (C) names the dorsogluteal site, which "is not recommended as an injection site for children because of the potential for sciatic nerve damage."

Q: A nurse is giving an intramuscular vaccine to a toddler. Which actions are correct? (Select all that apply.)
A) Give the vaccine deep into the muscle rather than into subcutaneous tissue
B) Do not aspirate for blood before injecting
C) Do not manually stimulate the site by rubbing or applying pressure afterward
D) Have the parent hold the child during the injection
E) Aspirate slowly for 5 seconds to confirm placement before injecting
Answer: A, B, C, D
Rationale: "Fewer reactions to immunizations are observed when the vaccine is given deep into the muscle rather than into subcutaneous tissue." Aspiration "is no longer recommended for an intramuscular injection because large blood vessels are not present at recommended injection sites and slow aspiration causes more pain than injection without aspiration." Beneficial positioning means "being held by a parent or caregiver for infants and young children, and sitting upright for older children and adolescents," and item 7 directs: "Do not manually stimulate (i.e., rubbing or applying pressure) the injection site" (p. 176).
Trap: (E) is the one to reject, and it is what many nurses were originally taught — which is exactly why the chapter states both the new rule and its two reasons. Aspiration is not merely unnecessary here; slow aspiration actively hurts more.

Q: A topical anesthetic is planned before an immunization. How long must each product remain in place?
A) EMLA for 30 minutes; LMX4 for at least 1 hour
B) EMLA for at least 1 hour; LMX4 for 30 minutes
C) Both for 30 minutes
D) Both for at least 1 hour
Answer: B
Rationale: Apply "the topical anesthetic EMLA (lidocaine-prilocaine) to the injection site and cover with an occlusive dressing for at least 1 hour or apply the topical anesthetic LMX4 (4% lidocaine) to the injection site and cover with an occlusive dressing for 30 minutes before the injection" (p. 176).
Trap: (A) swaps them, which is the only way this pair is tested. Note the reassurance that goes with EMLA use before vaccines: it "did not decrease antibody titers in immunized infants and was effective in reducing pain in 6-month-old children," so the anesthetic does not compromise the immune response.

Q: A nurse plans to use distraction during immunizations for a 4-year-old and for a 15-year-old. How should this plan be evaluated?
A) Appropriate for both, since distraction is universally effective
B) Appropriate for the 4-year-old — distraction is recommended for children less than 6 years old — but evidence does not show any benefit in using distraction during injections with adolescents
C) Appropriate for the adolescent only
D) Inappropriate for both; distraction increases anxiety
Answer: B
Rationale: "For children less than 6 years old, use distraction," and "Evidence does not show any benefit in using distraction during injections with adolescents" (p. 176).
Trap: (A) applies distraction universally, which is the fact bank's flagged inversion. What the chapter does recommend at every age is the nurse's own language: "Nurses administering the injections should remain calm and use neutral words such as here I go instead of here comes the sting."

Q: Two injections are to be given at separate sites. What does the chapter's research say about the order?
A) A single universal rule applies: always give the intramuscular injection first
B) Two separate findings — infants given DTaP-Hib before PCV had significantly lower pain scores than those given PCV first, and pain was significantly lower when the subcutaneous injection was administered before the intramuscular injection
C) Order makes no measurable difference
D) The most painful injection should always be given first
Answer: B
Rationale: The Research Focus reports both findings, from different studies addressing different comparisons (p. 178). The chapter's own framing before them: "When two or more injections are given at separate sites, the order of injections is arbitrary. Some practitioners suggest injecting the less painful one first."
Trap: (A) and (D) each collapse two findings into one universal rule. (D) also inverts the practitioners' suggestion — the **less** painful one first.

Topic: exanthem recognition and disease-specific care

Q: A 5-year-old has fever, coryza, cough, and conjunctivitis. The nurse notes small, irregular red spots with a minute bluish-white center on the buccal mucosa opposite the molars. What is the finding, and what does it predict?
A) Strawberry tongue, predicting scarlet fever
B) Koplik spots, which appear 2 days before the measles rash and are pathognomonic
C) Herpetic vesicles, predicting gingivostomatitis
D) Aphthous ulcers, requiring no follow-up
Answer: B
Rationale: The measles prodrome is "Fever and malaise, followed in 24 hours by coryza, cough, conjunctivitis, Koplik spots (small, irregular red spots with a minute, bluish-white center first seen on buccal mucosa opposite molars 2 days before rash)" (p. 182).
Trap: (D) treats an oral lesion as incidental, and the timing is what makes this urgent: Koplik spots appear while the child is most infectious and 2 days before anyone sees a rash. This is the window in which measles spreads through a waiting room.

Q: Two children present with fever and a rash. One has had 3 days of high fever that broke as the rash appeared, and looks well. The other's fever peaked as the rash appeared, and the child looks ill. What is the likely diagnosis for each?
A) Both have measles
B) The first has roseola (exanthem subitum), the second has measles
C) The first has measles, the second has roseola
D) Both have rubella
Answer: B
Rationale: The discriminator is the relationship between fever and rash: in roseola the fever breaks as the rash appears and the child looks well; in measles the fever peaks with the rash and the child looks ill (pp. 181–182).
Trap: (C) reverses the pair, and the general appearance is as diagnostic as the timing here. The precautions differ accordingly — roseola requires Standard Precautions only, while measles requires Airborne Precautions until the fifth day of rash.

Q: A nurse examines a child with scarlet fever on day 1 and again on day 4. What change in the tongue should be expected?
A) A red strawberry tongue on days 1 to 2, becoming a white strawberry tongue by days 4 to 5
B) A white strawberry tongue on days 1 to 2 — coated, with red swollen papillae — becoming a red strawberry tongue by days 4 to 5, when the coat has sloughed and the papillae are prominent
C) The tongue remains unchanged throughout
D) The tongue becomes coated only after antibiotics are started
Answer: B
Rationale: The white strawberry tongue is coated with red swollen papillae on days 1 to 2; the red strawberry tongue follows on days 4 to 5, when the coat has sloughed and the papillae are prominent (p. 185).
Trap: (A) reverses the sequence, which is the only way this pair is tested. The logic is in the word: the coating comes off, so the **white** stage is the earlier one.

Q: A child with chickenpox has a fever of 38.8° C. The parent asks about giving aspirin. What should the nurse teach?
A) Aspirin is acceptable for fever in chickenpox
B) Avoid aspirin because of the possible association with Reye syndrome
C) Aspirin is acceptable if given with food
D) Aspirin should be alternated with acetaminophen
Answer: B
Rationale: The nursing care for varicella includes "Avoid use of aspirin (possible association with Reye syndrome)" (p. 179). The chapter repeats the prohibition for Zika: "aspirin and other salicylates should not be administered to children to avoid Reye syndrome" (p. 190) — the only drug prohibition it states twice.
Trap: (D) sounds like a familiar antipyretic strategy and keeps the contraindicated drug in the plan. Note the related teaching about what antipyretics can and cannot do in these illnesses: acetaminophen lowers fever only, not itching, anorexia, pain, fussiness, or vomiting, and bed rest "should not be imposed unless specifically indicated."

Q: A pregnant staff nurse asks whether she can care for a child with fifth disease. What is the concern the chapter identifies?
A) There is no risk in pregnancy
B) Human parvovirus B19 carries a fetal death rate of 2% to 6%, greatest in the first 20 weeks
C) The risk is limited to the third trimester
D) The concern is teratogenicity rather than fetal loss
Answer: B
Rationale: The fetal death rate from parvovirus B19 is "2% to 6%, greatest in the first 20 weeks" (p. 187). Certain groups "are at risk for serious, even fatal, complications from communicable diseases, especially the viral diseases chickenpox and erythema infectiosum (fifth disease) caused by human parvovirus B19."
Trap: (C) inverts the gradient of risk — the danger is greatest early, not late, which is the opposite of the intuition that a larger fetus is more vulnerable. Compare rubella, which is likewise "benign except in pregnancy."

Topic: intestinal parasites and other infections

Q: One of the most common intestinal parasitic pathogens in the United States, acquired from a contaminated water source such as a lake or swimming pool, is:
A) Tinea capitis
B) Giardia intestinalis
C) Pediculosis capitis
D) Enterobiasis
Answer: B
Rationale: This is the chapter's own NCLEX question 5. Giardia is "the most common intestinal parasitic pathogen in the United States," and "Contaminated water, especially in lakes and streams, and swimming or wading pools frequented by diapered infants are common sources of transmission" (pp. 190, 192).
Trap: (D) is the strongest distractor because pinworms are also described as the most common of their kind — "the most common helminthic infection" — so *most common* alone does not discriminate. The lake and pool detail is the deciding clue, because pinworms spread by ingesting or inhaling eggs rather than through water. Note that a chlorinated pool is not protective: "Giardia organisms are resistant to chlorine," and a child should be excluded from swimming until 2 weeks infection-free.

Q: A parent reports that her 6-year-old has intense perianal itching, worse at night. How is the diagnosis confirmed?
A) A stool specimen collected at any time of day
B) A tape test: transparent tape, sticky side out on a tongue depressor, pressed against the perianal area in the morning as soon as the child awakens, before a bowel movement or bathing, on 3 consecutive days
C) A serum antibody titer
D) A perianal swab collected in the evening
Answer: B
Rationale: "A loop of transparent (not frosted or magic) tape, sticky side out, is placed around the end of a tongue depressor, which is then firmly pressed against the child's perianal area." Specimens "are collected in the morning as soon as the child awakens and before the child has a bowel movement or bathes," and the procedure "may need to be performed on 3 or more consecutive days" (p. 193).
Trap: (D) breaks the timing conditions, and an evening or post-bath specimen is worthless — the eggs are laid overnight and are removed by washing or defecation. Note two further specifics: the tape must be **transparent**, not frosted, because frosted tape is opaque under a microscope; and it goes sticky side **out** on the depressor but sticky side **down** on the slide.

Q: Pinworm infection is confirmed in a 6-year-old. What does treatment involve?
A) Treat the child only, with a single dose
B) Treat all household members, and repeat the dose in 2 weeks to eradicate the parasite and prevent reinfection
C) Treat the child and repeat daily for 2 weeks
D) No drug treatment; environmental measures alone are sufficient
Answer: B
Rationale: "Because pinworms are easily transmitted, all household members should be treated," and "The dose of antiparasitic medication should be repeated in 2 weeks to completely eradicate the parasite and prevent reinfection" (p. 193). Mebendazole "is not recommended for children younger than 2 years of age."
Trap: (D) relies on measures the chapter itself discounts: washing linens in hot water and vacuuming "may be recommended. However, [there is] little documentation on the effectiveness of these measures." Note that eggs survive indoors for 2 to 3 weeks, which is what sets the repeat interval.

Q: A 4-year-old has bilateral eye redness with purulent drainage and lids crusted shut on waking. Another child has watery discharge with a concurrent upper respiratory infection. How should each be classified?
A) Both are bacterial
B) The first is bacterial conjunctivitis; the second is viral conjunctivitis
C) The first is allergic; the second is bacterial
D) Both are allergic
Answer: B
Rationale: The chapter separates the three by discharge: bacterial conjunctivitis is purulent with morning crusting, viral is watery and accompanies an upper respiratory infection, and allergic presents with itching and a stringy discharge (p. 188).
Trap: (C) attaches the allergic label to the most inflamed-looking presentation. Itching, not redness or drainage volume, is what points to allergy. Note the separate priority for a neonate: conjunctivitis in a newborn requires prompt reporting and comprehensive evaluation, with timing of onset as a diagnostic clue.
