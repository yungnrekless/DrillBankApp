# Chapter 10 drills — Health Promotion of the Infant and Family

Authored from `sources/Ch10_Fact_Bank.md` (Hockenberry, Wilson & Rodgers, 11th ed.,
printed pp. 338–371).

Blueprint weight is **7 questions of 202 (3.5%), depth `standard`** — 45 drills
across 10 topics, at the top of the 30–45 band.

This is the first rung of the developmental ladder (chapters 10, 12, 13, 15, 17),
and the fact bank says how milestone chapters are tested: *what can a child of age
X do, and what does that make them able to hurt themselves with?* So the two
things drilled hardest are the **month-by-month motor, sensory, and social
milestones** and the **injury-prevention plan keyed to each accomplishment** —
the book pairs them and the exam pairs them. Topic 10 exists to hold that pairing
rather than scattering safety items through the milestone topics.

Topic names are all infant-qualified on purpose. Chapters 12, 13, 15, and 17 will
bank the same categories for the toddler, preschooler, school-age child, and
adolescent, and an unqualified `growth parameters` or `injury prevention` would
merge five stages into one dashboard entry that cannot tell you which chapter to
open.

**What is drilled from both ends.** Physiologic anemia and iron deficiency,
crawling and creeping, abdomen-to-back and back-to-abdomen rolling, separation
anxiety and stranger fear, Piaget's stage II and stage IV learning, extrusion
reflex present and faded, doll's eye normal and abnormal, expressive and
receptive language. Those are the eight distinctions a learner can genuinely
hold backwards, and a one-directional item would let that pass.

**Revised after the first pass.** This chapter first went in with only Box 10.5's
birth-to-4-month and 4-to-7-month injury bands, because the fact bank had recorded
the 8-to-12-month band and Table 10.5 as deliberate omissions. That left the
chapter teaching its own organizing principle — each new motor skill opens a new
injury category — and then stopping just before the months in which the infant
cruises, climbs, and can reach water, stairs, appliance doors, and a tablecloth.
Both sections turned out to be fully present in the raw pull, so the **fact bank
was expanded** (§11, with page cites re-derived from the raw markers, validated and
cite-swept clean) and three questions were added here. To stay at the top of the
band rather than overrun it, **three milestone-lookup items were removed** —
sound localization by age band, sitting at 7 versus 8 months, and the age at which
"dada" acquires meaning. All three were pure age lookups with no action attached,
which is what the cut order says to drop first. The swap was free because no
attempts existed yet.

**What thirty-three printed pages could not buy at 45 questions.** Left
undrilled: the full month-by-month vision and hearing bands (Boxes 10.2 and
10.3) beyond the items that decide an action; visual acuity figures; the
four-stage attachment timetable; the complete Box 10.4 feeding milestone grid;
sleep hour totals; contraindications to breastfeeding; child-care licensing
categories and the bleach dilution; and thumb-sucking malocclusion timing. The
rule followed when cutting was **prefer the fact that decides an action over the
fact that decides a number** — so the fontanel ages, the solid-food gates, the
supplement thresholds, and now the whole injury plan stayed, while the milestone
tables that only restate ages did not.

`infant language and personal-social milestones` carries two questions rather
than the usual four. That is deliberate: it still names a real restudy unit
(expressive versus receptive language, and when a milestone delay should send you
looking for hearing loss), and the alternative was cutting a safety item to
protect a topic's headcount.

Seven questions are select-all, one for each list in the chapter that genuinely
sorts two ways: reflexes that appear versus one that disappears, findings that
explain infant dehydration versus one that conserves fluid, foods withheld in
the first year versus one started early, teething signs versus illness, unsafe
sleep-environment findings versus a safe one, the 8-to-12-month hazards versus a
rule that inverts at that age, and SAFE PAD's interventions versus one the
chapter forbids. That is above the usual one-in-seven ratio, but this chapter is
unusually full of two-way lists and each of the seven is drawn from a real one.

Topic: infant growth parameters and sensory development
Subtopic: growth and fontanel landmarks

Q: A 5-month-old is at a well-child visit and the parent asks whether the weight gain is on track. Which statement reflects the expected pattern?
A) Birth weight should have at least doubled by now
B) Birth weight should have at least tripled by now
C) Birth weight should have increased by about 50%
D) Birth weight should have increased by about 33%
Answer: A
Rationale: Infants gain 680 g (1.5 lb) per month until age 5 months, "when the birth weight has at least doubled." The average 6-month weight is 7.26 kg (16 lb). Tripling is the 1-year landmark, not the 5-month one.
Trap: (B) is the reflex pick because doubling and tripling are taught together and tripling is the more memorable figure; it belongs to 12 months. (C) is the length landmark and (D) the head-circumference landmark, both also at 1 year.

Q: A 12-month-old is measured at the well-child visit. Which set of findings is expected at this age?
A) Weight doubled, birth length increased by 33%, chest circumference still well below head circumference
B) Weight tripled, birth length increased by almost 50%, chest circumference approximately equal to head circumference
C) Weight tripled, birth length increased by 33%, chest circumference exceeding head circumference by several centimeters
D) Weight doubled, birth length increased by almost 50%, chest circumference approximately equal to head circumference
Answer: B
Rationale: "By 1 year of age the infant's birth weight has tripled, to an average of 9.75 kg (21.5 lb)," "birth length has increased by almost 50%," and "The chest circumference approximately equals head circumference by the end of the first year." Head circumference increases by almost 33% over the same period, which is the figure (C) misattributes to length.
Trap: (A) carries the 5-month doubling figure forward to 12 months, which is the mirror error of the previous item. (C) keeps the correct weight and moves the chest past the head a year early; the two are approximately equal at 1 year, and the chest exceeds the head only afterward.

Q: A nurse palpates an open anterior fontanel and a closed posterior fontanel in a 4-month-old. What is the correct interpretation?
A) Both findings are expected at this age
B) The open anterior fontanel is abnormal and requires evaluation
C) The closed posterior fontanel is premature and warrants referral
D) Both fontanels should have closed by 8 weeks
Answer: A
Rationale: The posterior fontanel fuses by 6 to 8 weeks of age and the anterior fontanel closes by 12 to 18 months, the average age being 14 months. At 4 months the posterior is expected to be closed and the anterior still open.
Trap: (D) is the pick of a learner who remembers the 6-to-8-week figure but attaches it to both fontanels; only the posterior closes that early, and the anterior has most of a year left.

Subtopic: vision

Q: At a 4-month well-child visit the nurse notes that the infant's eyes do not consistently work together to fix on a single image. Why does this finding warrant prompt referral rather than watchful waiting?
A) Binocular vision should be well established by 4 months, and lack of it results in strabismus, which must be detected early to prevent permanent blindness
B) Binocular vision is not expected until 12 months, so referral mainly prevents unnecessary parental anxiety
C) It signals a need for corrective lenses, which are most effective when fitted before 6 months
D) It indicates that depth perception has failed to develop, which is irreversible after 9 months
Answer: A
Rationale: Binocularity, "the fixation of two ocular images into one cerebral picture (fusion)," "begins to develop by 6 weeks of age and should be well established by age 4 months." The chapter states the consequence directly: "Lack of binocular vision results in strabismus and must be detected early to prevent permanent blindness."
Trap: (B) is the reflex pick for anyone who groups all visual maturation into the second half of the first year; the 4-month deadline is exactly what makes this finding actionable at this visit rather than the next.

Q: A 9-month-old is being assessed and the nurse finds that the eyes lag behind when the head is rotated to one side. What does this finding indicate?
A) A normal finding that persists throughout infancy
B) An abnormal finding that may indicate a neurologic problem
C) A normal finding indicating intact binocular vision
D) An expected finding until depth perception is fully established
Answer: B
Rationale: The doll's eye reflex, in which "eyes lag behind if head is rotated to one side," is present in the newborn and disappears at 6 to 12 weeks. "The presence of this reflex at any other time in childhood is abnormal and may indicate a neurologic problem."
Trap: (A) is the pull for a learner who knows the reflex is normal and stops there. Normal in the newborn and abnormal afterward is the whole content of this item, and the same shape recurs with the primitive reflexes generally.

Topic: infant system maturation and physiologic vulnerability

Q: A 5-month-old is brought in with a third episode of otitis media since birth. Which anatomic explanation should the nurse give the parent?
A) The short, straight eustachian tube communicates closely with the ear, allowing infection to ascend from the pharynx to the middle ear
B) The eustachian tube is long and narrow in infancy, so secretions pool and cannot drain
C) The infant overproduces immunoglobulin A in the mucosal lining, and the resulting inflammation obstructs the tube
D) Airway lumens are large relative to lung size, so organisms are carried upward more readily
Answer: A
Rationale: "The short, straight eustachian tube closely communicates with the ear, allowing infection to ascend from the pharynx to the middle ear." The chapter adds that inability to produce immunoglobulin A in the mucosal lining "provides less protection."
Trap: (B) is the adult picture read backward into the infant and is the commonest inversion here; long and narrow is what the tube becomes with growth, not what it is. (C) inverts the immunoglobulin fact, which is underproduction, and (D) inverts the airway fact, which is that lumens remain small relative to lung size, "maintaining high resistance."

Q: A 4-month-old who has been exclusively breastfed has a mildly low hemoglobin. The parent asks whether they should have been giving more iron. Which explanation is correct?
A) Physiologic anemia at this age results from fetal hemoglobin shortening red blood cell survival, and its occurrence is not affected by an adequate iron supply
B) Physiologic anemia at this age is caused by inadequate iron intake and is prevented by iron supplementation
C) Physiologic anemia is caused by occult gastrointestinal blood loss and is prevented by avoiding cow's milk
D) Physiologic anemia does not occur before 12 months of age
Answer: A
Rationale: Fetal hemoglobin is present up to the first 5 months and "results in a shortened survival of red blood cells (RBCs)," producing physiologic anemia at 3 to 6 months of age. The chapter states the discriminating fact in one sentence: "The occurrence of physiologic anemia is not affected by an adequate iron supply."
Trap: (B) is the reflex answer because nearly every other anemia in pediatrics is an iron story. This one is not, and the book says so explicitly — which is why it is worth a question on its own.

Q: A 9-month-old has been drinking whole cow's milk since 7 months of age and now has iron deficiency anemia. Which statement correctly distinguishes this from physiologic anemia?
A) This anemia is unaffected by iron intake, whereas physiologic anemia responds to iron supplementation
B) This anemia is worsened by whole cow's milk, possibly through occult gastrointestinal blood loss, whereas physiologic anemia arises from fetal hemoglobin and is unaffected by iron supply
C) Both arise from depletion of maternal iron stores at 5 to 6 months and are managed identically
D) Physiologic anemia occurs at 9 to 12 months and iron deficiency anemia at 3 to 6 months
Answer: B
Rationale: Whole cow's milk "can cause iron deficiency anemia in infants, possibly as a result of occult gastrointestinal blood loss," and is "deficient in iron, zinc, and vitamin C." Physiologic anemia occurs at 3 to 6 months from shortened red cell survival and "is not affected by an adequate iron supply."
Trap: (A) is the mirror error of the previous item, attaching the iron-independence to the wrong anemia. (D) swaps the two age windows, which works on anyone who memorized both numbers without their owners.

Q: A nurse is teaching new graduates why infants dehydrate faster than adults. Which statements from the chapter support that vulnerability? Select all that apply.
A) At birth 75% of the infant's body weight is water
B) The high proportion of extracellular fluid predisposes the infant to more rapid loss of total body fluid
C) Complete maturity of the kidney does not occur until the latter half of the second year
D) Infant urine is concentrated at a high specific gravity, which conserves free water
E) Extracellular fluid falls from 40% at term to 20% in adulthood
Answer: A, B, C, E
Rationale: The chapter gives 75% body water at birth, extracellular fluid falling "from 40% at term to 20% in adulthood," the ECF proportion as the mechanism — it "predisposes the infant to more rapid loss of total body fluid and, consequently, dehydration" — and renal immaturity persisting into the latter half of the second year. Infant urine has a low specific gravity, 1.008 to 1.012.
Trap: (D) is the one option from the wrong side of the sort, and it pulls because concentrated urine sounds like a compensatory mechanism a stressed body would mount. Infant urine is dilute, which is part of why fluid is lost rather than conserved.

Topic: infant fine and gross motor sequence

Q: An 11-month-old is observed picking up a raisin neatly between the thumb and the tip of the index finger. How should the nurse document this?
A) Palmar grasp, which is expected at this age
B) Crude pincer grasp, which is expected at 8 to 9 months
C) Neat pincer grasp, which is expected at 11 months
D) Voluntary grasp, first achieved at 5 months
Answer: C
Rationale: The palmar grasp uses the whole hand; the pincer grasp uses "the thumb and index finger," appearing crude at 8 to 9 months and neat at 11 months. By 10 months the pincer grasp is "sufficiently established to enable infants to pick up a raisin."
Trap: (B) is the near-miss, and it pulls because both grasps use the same two digits; crude versus neat is the whole distinction, and neat is the 11-month one. (D) names a real 5-month milestone — being "able to voluntarily grasp an object" — which is three grasp stages earlier.

Q: A parent reports that their 5-month-old has started rolling from the abdomen to the back but not the other way. Which response is correct?
A) This is the expected sequence; rolling from back to abdomen typically follows at about 6 months
B) This is reversed; infants roll from back to abdomen first, at about 5 months
C) This is delayed; both directions should be achieved by 4 months
D) This suggests a persistent asymmetric tonic neck reflex and needs evaluation
Answer: A
Rationale: Infants roll from abdomen to back at 5 months and from back to abdomen at 6 months. The asymmetric tonic neck reflex "persists from birth to 3 months" and "prevents the infant from rolling over," so it is long gone by 5 months.
Trap: (B) is the inversion this pair reliably produces, and it pulls because rolling off the back feels like the easier direction to picture. Abdomen to back comes first.

Q: A 9-month-old moves across the floor on hands and knees with the abdomen off the floor. Which term does the chapter use for this?
A) Crawling, because the infant is propelling forward
B) Creeping, because the abdomen is off the floor and the infant is on hands and knees
C) Cruising, because the infant is moving independently across a surface
D) Crawling, because creeping requires holding onto furniture
Answer: B
Rationale: "Crawling (propelling forward with belly on floor) progresses to creeping on hands and knees (with belly off floor) by 9 months." The book uses the two terms precisely and distractors swap them.
Trap: (A) is ordinary everyday usage, which runs exactly backward from the book's: what most people call crawling is what the book calls creeping. (D) invents a furniture criterion the chapter never attaches to creeping.

Q: A 12-month-old has never pulled to a standing position. What is the nurse's correct action?
A) Reassure the parent, since independent standing is not expected until 15 months
B) Refer for further evaluation for possible developmental dysplasia of the hip
C) Recommend a walker to strengthen the legs
D) Reassure the parent, since bearing full weight on the legs is not expected until 12 months
Answer: B
Rationale: "An infant who does not pull to a standing position by 11 to 12 months of age should be further evaluated for possible developmental dysplasia of the hip." Infants stand holding furniture and pull to standing at 9 months, and walk holding furniture at 11 months.
Trap: (C) is the intuitive intervention and is wrong twice over: "walkers do not enhance walking and coordination, and they are dangerous if tipped over or placed near the top of stairs," and recommending one substitutes a device for the referral this finding requires. (D) misstates the age for full weight bearing with assistance, which is 6 to 7 months.

Q: A nurse is explaining to a parent which reflexes appear during infancy, as distinct from those present at birth that fade. Which reflexes does Box 10.1 list as appearing during infancy? Select all that apply.
A) Labyrinth righting reflex, appearing at 2 months
B) Neck righting reflex, appearing at 3 months
C) Doll's eye reflex, appearing at 6 to 12 weeks
D) Landau reflex, appearing at 6 to 8 months
E) Parachute reflex, appearing at 7 to 9 months
Answer: A, B, D, E
Rationale: Box 10.1 lists six reflexes that appear during infancy: labyrinth righting at 2 months, neck righting at 3 months, body righting at 6 months, Landau at 6 to 8 months, parachute at 7 to 9 months, and otolith righting at 7 to 12 months. Parachute and otolith righting "persist indefinitely." The doll's eye reflex belongs to the other group entirely — present in the newborn, disappearing at 6 to 12 weeks.
Trap: (C) is the one option from the wrong side of the sort, and it pulls because 6 to 12 weeks is a real age attached to a real reflex. That is the age the doll's eye reflex disappears, not the age it appears.

Topic: infant cognitive and psychosocial development theory

Q: Parents of a 2-month-old describe feeding, bathing, and dressing the infant on a strict schedule, and ask whether meeting every physical need is enough to raise a secure child. Which response reflects Erikson's phase I?
A) Providing food, warmth, and shelter is by itself adequate for developing a sense of trust
B) The crucial element is the quality of the parent-child relationship and the care received; provision of food, warmth, and shelter by itself is inadequate
C) Trust is determined by the quantity of food taken and the length of sucking at each feeding
D) Trust cannot be assessed until the toddler years, when autonomy emerges
Answer: B
Rationale: "The crucial element for the achievement of this task is the quality of both the relationship between the parent (or caregiver) and child and the care the infant receives. The provision of food, warmth, and shelter by itself is inadequate." The chapter adds that "the degree of mothering skill, the quantity of food, or the length of sucking does not determine the quality of the experience. Rather, it is the total quality of the interpersonal relationship."
Trap: (C) is the reflex pick because the oral stage makes feeding feel like the entire mechanism; the book names quantity of food and length of sucking specifically as the things that do not determine quality.

Q: A parent says they anticipate every need and never let their 5-month-old fuss for even a moment. Which concern should the nurse raise?
A) None; anticipating all needs before they are signaled is the ideal way to build trust
B) If parents always meet needs before the infant signals readiness, the infant never learns to test the ability to control the environment
C) Mistrust develops only from prolonged delay in meeting needs, so this approach is protective
D) This will cause the infant to skip the oral stage and progress early to autonomy
Answer: B
Rationale: "Mistrust can result from either too much or too little frustration. If parents always meet their children's needs before the children signal their readiness, infants will never learn to test their ability to control the environment." Prolonged delay produces mistrust from the other direction, "therefore consistency of care is essential."
Trap: (C) states the half of the rule nearly everyone holds, and that is why this item exists: mistrust comes from too much frustration and from too little, and only the prolonged-delay half is intuitive.

Q: A 3-month-old shows anticipation before feeding, becomes bored when left alone, and shows no separation anxiety unless the caregiver's routine differs from the usual. In Piaget's stage II, primary circular reactions, what is the infant learning from?
A) The type of interaction between objects or individuals rather than from the object itself
B) The object itself rather than from the interaction surrounding it
C) Symbolic representation, which allows thought about an object without experiencing it
D) Intentional coordination of two separate schemas to reach a goal
Answer: A
Rationale: Table 10.1 gives stage II, 1 to 4 months, as "Learns from type of interaction between objects or individual rather than from object itself," and lists anticipation before feeding, boredom when left alone, and "Shows no separation anxiety unless caregiver's skill differs from usual routine" among its behaviors.
Trap: (B) is the stage IV answer, and the chapter contrasts the two stages in a single sentence precisely because this is the inversion learners make. (D) describes stage IV's coordination of secondary schemas, which is five months away.

Q: An 11-month-old moves a pillow aside to reach a toy hidden behind it, then puts the toy into a container. In Piaget's stage IV, coordination of secondary schemas, what is the infant learning from?
A) The type of interaction between objects or individuals
B) The object itself, with the beginning of intellectual reasoning
C) Repetitious use of reflexes establishing a pattern of experiences
D) Deferred imitation of an action observed hours earlier
Answer: B
Rationale: In stage IV, 9 to 12 months, infants "learn from the object itself. This is in contrast to the second stage, in which infants learn from the type of interaction between objects or individuals." The stage brings "beginning of intellectual reasoning," actively searching for a hidden object, and distinguishing end products from their means so as to remove barriers.
Trap: (A) is the mirror error of the previous item. (C) is stage I, use of reflexes, and it is the answer chosen when "reflexes" is remembered as the Piaget keyword for infancy generally rather than for the first month specifically.

Topic: infant attachment, separation anxiety, and stranger fear

Q: A nurse explains why attachment to a specific caregiver cannot be complete in the first weeks of life. Which two achievements does the chapter name as its prerequisites?
A) The ability to discriminate the mother from other individuals, and the achievement of object permanence
B) The social smile, and the ability to self-soothe
C) Binocular vision, and the ability to localize sound
D) Symbolic thought, and the emergence of expressive language
Answer: A
Rationale: Attachment requires "(1) the ability to discriminate the mother from other individuals and (2) the achievement of object permanence." Object permanence develops at approximately 9 to 10 months of age, which is why the earliest weeks bring responses "indiscriminately to anyone."
Trap: (C) names two real infant milestones, which is what makes it attractive. They are sensory achievements; attachment is built on cognitive ones.

Q: A 7-month-old cries as soon as the parent walks out of the examination room and settles when the parent returns. Which phenomenon is this, and what makes it possible?
A) Separation anxiety, which develops between 4 and 8 months as object permanence makes the parent's absence knowable
B) Stranger fear, which develops between 6 and 8 months as the infant discriminates familiar from unfamiliar people
C) Reactive attachment disorder, which appears when comfort seeking repeatedly fails
D) Separation anxiety, which does not develop until 11 to 12 months
Answer: A
Rationale: Separation anxiety develops between 4 and 8 months, when "the infant progresses through the first stage of separation-individuation" and object permanence makes the absent parent still real. By 11 to 12 months infants "are able to anticipate her imminent departure by watching her behaviors, and they begin to protest before she leaves" — which is a later refinement, not the onset.
Trap: (B) is the companion phenomenon with a heavily overlapping age window, which is exactly why the two are confused. It is triggered by a stranger's presence, not by the parent's absence. (D) mistakes the anticipatory stage for the onset.

Q: The same 7-month-old cries and turns away when an unfamiliar nurse approaches, even while sitting on the parent's lap. Which phenomenon is this, and what does it require?
A) Separation anxiety, which requires object permanence
B) Stranger fear, which requires the ability to discriminate between familiar and unfamiliar people
C) Disinhibited social engagement disorder, which is marked by loss of stranger wariness
D) Spoiled child syndrome, which results from inconsistent limit setting
Answer: B
Rationale: "Between ages 6 and 8 months fear of strangers and stranger anxiety become prominent and are related to infants' ability to discriminate between familiar and unfamiliar people," shown as "clinging to the parent, crying, and turning away from strangers."
Trap: (A) is the mirror error of the previous item — the parent has not left, so nothing here is a separation. (C) inverts the behavior entirely, describing an infant who approaches unfamiliar adults with no suspicion at all.

Q: A nurse must assess a 7-month-old who is sitting on a parent's lap and watching warily. Which approach is best?
A) Stand at the bedside, smile broadly, and extend both arms to pick the infant up
B) Speak softly, meet the child at eye level, keep a safe distance, and avoid sudden intrusive gestures
C) Take the infant from the parent quickly, so the assessment is over sooner
D) Move the infant to an infant seat away from the parent to reduce clinging
Answer: B
Rationale: The chapter directs the stranger, including the nurse, to "talk softly; meet the child at eye level (to appear smaller); maintain a safe distance from the infant; and avoid sudden, intrusive gestures, such as holding the arms out and smiling broadly." It also names proximity as a factor: being "on parent's lap rather than in infant seat" is less stressful.
Trap: (A) is the reflex friendly approach, and the book names both of its components — outstretched arms and a broad smile — as the specific things to avoid. (D) removes the single factor most protective against the distress being assessed.

Q: A toddler in foster care approaches every unfamiliar adult in the waiting room without hesitation, shows no wariness of strangers, and has poor impulse control. Which pattern does this describe?
A) Reactive attachment disorder, marked by failure to seek and respond to comfort when distressed
B) Disinhibited social engagement disorder, marked by inappropriate approach to unfamiliar adults and lack of suspicion of strangers
C) Normal stranger fear for the age
D) Separation anxiety that has failed to resolve
Answer: B
Rationale: Children with disinhibited social engagement disorder may exhibit "inappropriate approach to unfamiliar adults, lack of suspicion of strangers, and poor impulse control." Reactive attachment disorder is the withdrawn, inhibited picture: "not being cuddly with parents, failing to seek and respond to comfort when distressed, minimal social and emotional reciprocity."
Trap: (A) is the reflex pick because reactive attachment disorder is by far the better known of the two names, so it gets applied to any attachment problem. It describes the opposite behavior — withdrawn rather than indiscriminate.

Topic: infant language and personal-social milestones

Q: A parent of a 12-month-old worries that the child says only four words. What should the nurse explain?
A) A 12-month-old says 3 to 5 words with meaning and may understand as many as 100, so comprehension normally runs far ahead of speech
B) A 12-month-old should have a vocabulary of at least 20 spoken words, so evaluation is warranted
C) Expressive and receptive language develop at the same rate, so understanding is also limited to about four words
D) Speech is not expected until 18 months, so no comparison can be made yet
Answer: A
Rationale: At 12 months the infant "can say 3 to 5 words with meaning and may understand as many as 100 words." Expressive language is the "ability to make thoughts, ideas, and desires known to others"; receptive language is the "ability to understand the words being spoken."
Trap: (C) is the inversion that worries parents most, because it treats the spoken word count as a measure of comprehension. Receptive language far outpaces expressive at this age, and the gap in this stem is the expected one.

Q: A 10-month-old does not comprehend "no," does not turn toward voices, and produces few sounds. Beyond a language referral, what does the chapter direct the nurse to consider?
A) Careful evaluation for potential hearing loss
B) Reassurance, since comprehension of "no" is not expected until 18 months
C) Immediate evaluation for reactive attachment disorder
D) Restriction of screen media, which is the usual cause of expressive delay at this age
Answer: A
Rationale: Infants comprehend "no" and obey simple commands with gestures at 9 to 10 months, and "delays in achieving milestones are carefully evaluated for potential hearing loss." A 10-month-old who does not turn toward voices has failed a hearing milestone as well as a language one.
Trap: (D) is attractive because the chapter really does discourage screen media, but it gives that guidance as a stimulation issue — media "does not provide infants with appropriate sensory stimulation and does not increase language skills" — not as a diagnosis to pursue. Hearing loss is the named consideration.

Topic: infant feeding readiness and introduction of solids

Q: A parent of a 2-month-old has begun spooning rice cereal and reports that the baby "pushes it right back out with the tongue." How should the nurse interpret this?
A) The infant dislikes the taste, and a different first cereal should be tried
B) The extrusion reflex is still strong, which is one reason solids are not recommended before 4 to 6 months
C) The infant has a swallowing disorder and needs referral
D) The infant is refusing food deliberately, which is itself a sign of readiness for self-feeding
Answer: B
Rationale: "The extrusion (protrusion) reflex is strong and often causes food to be pushed out of the mouth" in the early months, and "The addition of solid foods before 4 to 6 months of age is not recommended." Early introduction "before 4 months of life is correlated with obesity later in life."
Trap: (D) inverts the chapter's own point: young infants "are unable to deliberately push food away or avoid feeding," so tongue thrust at 2 months is a reflex, not a preference.

Q: A 5-month-old no longer thrusts the tongue when a spoon is offered, sits with support, turns the head away when full, and has begun tooth eruption. What do these findings indicate?
A) Readiness for the introduction of solid foods
B) That solids should still be withheld until 12 months, when table foods are introduced
C) Readiness for whole cow's milk
D) That weaning from the breast or bottle should be completed now
Answer: A
Rationale: Readiness at 4 to 6 months is marked by a gastrointestinal tract that "has matured sufficiently," tooth eruption that "facilitates biting and chewing," disappearance of the extrusion reflex, head control permitting sitting with support and turning away to refuse, and voluntary grasp allowing self-feeding.
Trap: (B) is the mirror error of the previous item, carrying the "not before 4 to 6 months" rule past its own endpoint; 12 months is when well-cooked table foods may be served, not when solids begin. (C) confuses readiness for solids with the separate 12-month gate on whole cow's milk.

Q: A nurse teaches a parent how to start solids for a 5-month-old. Which instruction is correct?
A) Begin with strained meats, and introduce a new food each day to build variety quickly
B) Begin with iron-fortified rice cereal for its high iron content and low allergenic potential, and introduce only one new food every 5 to 7 days
C) Begin with a mixed-grain cereal, and introduce one new food every 24 hours
D) Begin with fruit juice offered in a bottle, to supply vitamin C
Answer: B
Rationale: Iron-fortified infant cereal is given first "because of its high iron content (7 mg/3 tbsp of prepared dry cereal)," and rice is "usually suggested as an initial food because of its easy digestibility and low allergenic potential." "Only one solid is introduced every 5 to 7 days so that a reaction to a particular food can be distinguished." Meats typically come last, after fruits and vegetables.
Trap: (C) keeps a cereal start but collapses the spacing interval, which is the half of the rule that exists to make a reaction attributable; a daily change makes any reaction untraceable. It also starts with a grain combination, which the chapter says to avoid when introducing a new grain.

Q: A parent of a 7-month-old asks how much of a new vegetable to offer, and whether it can replace a milk feeding. Which response is correct?
A) About 1 tbsp per year of age, and solids should not be perceived as a substitute for milk until the child is older than 12 months
B) About one-quarter cup per feeding, and solids may replace one milk feeding per day from 6 months
C) As much as the infant will willingly take, and solids should replace milk gradually from 7 months
D) About 1 tbsp per month of age, and solids should have replaced milk by 9 months
Answer: A
Rationale: "1 tbsp per year of age" is an adequate portion, and "solids should not be perceived as a substitute for milk until the child is older than 12 months" — at this age they are for "taste and chewing experience." Smaller portions also avoid the "clean your plate" habit "known to contribute to overeating in later life."
Trap: (C) is the reflex answer of a parent who reads appetite as the guide, and it also inverts the chapter's point that solids do not replace milk in the first year. (D) keeps the correct unit and swaps the multiplier from years to months, which at 7 months would be seven times the recommended portion.

Q: A parent of a 9-month-old asks which finger foods are safe and when the bottle should go. Which response reflects the chapter?
A) Grapes and raw carrot sticks are appropriate finger foods, and the nighttime feeding should be the first one dropped
B) Junior and finger foods are appropriate except grapes; raw fruits with seeds, vegetables, and nuts are hazardous; and the nighttime feeding is usually the last to be discontinued
C) Only pureed foods are safe until 12 months, and weaning should then be completed abruptly
D) Hard candy and teething biscuits are safe because they dissolve, and weaning should not begin before 12 to 14 months
Answer: B
Rationale: By 8 to 9 months junior foods and finger foods are offered "(except grapes)," and "Raw fruits with seeds, vegetables, and nuts are hazardous." Weaning "should be gradual by replacing one bottle-feeding or breastfeeding at a time," and "The nighttime feeding is usually the last feeding to be discontinued."
Trap: (A) inverts the weaning order, and dropping the nighttime feeding first is the intuitive error because that is the feeding parents most want to end. (D) is wrong on both counts: hard candy is listed as a choking hazard, and teething biscuits carry their own caution "because large chunks may be broken off."

Topic: infant supplements and foods to avoid in the first year

Q: A mother who is exclusively breastfeeding her 2-week-old asks whether the baby needs any supplement. Which response is correct?
A) No supplement is needed, because human milk is the most desirable complete diet for the first 6 months
B) All infants, including those exclusively breastfed, should receive 400 international units of vitamin D daily beginning in the first few days of life
C) Vitamin D is needed only by formula-fed infants taking less than 1 L per day
D) Vitamin D should begin at 6 months, when maternal stores are depleted
Answer: B
Rationale: The recommendation is that "all infants (including those exclusively breastfed) receive a daily supplement of 400 international units (IU) of vitamin D beginning in the first few days of life" to prevent rickets, continued until the infant consumes at least 1 L/day of vitamin D-fortified formula.
Trap: (A) quotes a true sentence from the chapter — human milk is "the most desirable complete diet for the infant during the first 6 months" — and draws the wrong conclusion from it. The vitamin D recommendation sits alongside that statement, not against it, which is what makes this distractor work.

Q: A 4-month-old has been exclusively breastfed since birth. Which supplement does the chapter add at this point, and why?
A) Iron 1 mg/kg/day, because fetal iron stores are depleted at about this age
B) Iron 1 mg/kg/day, because human milk contains no iron at all
C) Fluoride 0.25 mg/day, because tooth eruption is imminent
D) No additional supplement, because iron-fortified cereal will be started at 6 months
Answer: A
Rationale: "If the infant is exclusively breastfed after 4 months (when fetal iron stores are depleted), iron supplementation (1 mg/kg/day) is recommended" until iron-containing complementary foods are introduced.
Trap: (C) attaches a real dose to the wrong nutrient and the wrong age: 0.25 mg is the fluoride figure, and fluoride "is not required for the first 6 months because of the risk of dental fluorosis."

Q: A parent asks whether their 3-month-old should be getting fluoride, since the family's well water is low in fluoride. Which response is correct?
A) Fluoride is not required for the first 6 months because of the risk of dental fluorosis; it begins at 6 months if the water fluoride content is inadequate
B) Fluoride should begin now at 0.25 mg daily, since the water is known to be low in fluoride
C) Fluoride is never supplemented in infancy; it is supplied by toothpaste from the first tooth
D) Fluoride should begin at 12 months, when the dental home is established
Answer: A
Rationale: Fluoride "is not required for the first 6 months because of the risk of dental fluorosis," and is "needed beginning at 6 months of age if the infant does not receive water with adequate fluoride content": "children 6 months to 3 years of age take 0.25 mg fluoride daily if water fluoride content is less than 0.3 ppm."
Trap: (B) is the reflex answer once low-fluoride water is mentioned — the dose is right and the timing is three months early, which is exactly the combination fluorosis comes from. (C) inverts the toothpaste guidance, which is water only at first, "which the infant will swallow."

Q: A nurse reviews the feeding plan for a 9-month-old. Which items should be avoided until after the first birthday? Select all that apply.
A) Honey, including honey placed on a pacifier
B) Whole cow's milk
C) Skim or low-fat milk
D) Iron-fortified infant cereal
E) Fruit juice offered in a bottle
Answer: A, B, C, E
Rationale: "Honey should be avoided in the first 12 months because of the risk of infant botulism," including on pacifiers. Whole cow's milk has "a high renal solute load, which makes it undesirable for infants less than 12 months of age." Skim and low-fat milk are unacceptable because "the essential fatty acids are inadequate and the solute concentration of protein and electrolytes, such as sodium, is too high." Juice is offered from a cup rather than a bottle to prevent caries.
Trap: (D) is the one option from the wrong side of the sort, and it pulls because cereal is a solid and every other item on this list is being withheld. Iron-fortified cereal is the food the chapter says to start first and continue longest, "until the child is 18 months of age."

Topic: common infant parental concerns and dental health

Q: A tired parent says they have stopped picking up their 3-month-old when she cries, because a relative warned that it will spoil her. Which response reflects the chapter's evidence?
A) The relative is correct; responding to crying in early infancy reinforces the crying
B) Research does not support the contention that picking up a crying baby leads to spoiling, and parents who were less responsive to crying had infants who cried more
C) Spoiling is unavoidable in the first year and is corrected by limit setting in the toddler years
D) Crying at 3 months is deliberate attention seeking and should be ignored for progressively longer intervals
Answer: B
Rationale: "Research on parents' response to crying during early infancy does not support the contention that picking up a crying baby leads to spoiling." Ainsworth found that the amount an infant cried during the first 3 months had no correlation with later crying, but "the degree of maternal responsiveness to crying did. Parents who were less responsive... had infants who cried more."
Trap: (D) is the belief the chapter warns about most sharply, because of where it leads: "infants cry because a need is not being met, not to intentionally irritate an adult. The fussy or irritable infant is a potential victim of traumatic brain injury or shaken baby syndrome."

Q: Parents of a 3-month-old ask whether to keep using the pacifier. Which response reflects the AAP position as the chapter gives it?
A) Discontinue it now, because pacifier use has been associated with an increased risk of otitis media
B) Use it during the first 6 months for pain management and prevention of sudden infant death syndrome, then wean from it during the second 6 months of life
C) Continue it until 2 years of age, when malocclusion first becomes a concern
D) Avoid it entirely in breastfed infants, since it shortens breastfeeding duration
Answer: B
Rationale: The AAP recommends a pacifier "during the first 6 months because of the benefit with regard to pain management during painful procedures and prevention of sudden infant death syndrome (SIDS)," and that "the child be weaned from the pacifier during the second 6 months of life."
Trap: (A) states an association the chapter also reports and is the trap precisely because it is true: the book gives both the otitis media risk and the SIDS benefit without resolving them, and the recommendation is still to use it in the first 6 months. (D) contradicts recent evidence the chapter cites, that pacifier use "did not adversely affect breastfeeding duration or exclusivity."

Q: A parent of an 8-month-old attributes a cluster of findings to teething. Which findings does the chapter accept as expected signs of teething? Select all that apply.
A) Drooling and increased finger sucking
B) Irritability and difficulty sleeping
C) Temperature of 39.4°C (103°F)
D) Mild temperature elevation
E) Decreased appetite for solid foods
Answer: A, B, D, E
Rationale: Expected signs are "drooling, increased finger sucking, or biting on hard objects," irritability, sleep difficulty, mild temperature elevation, ear rubbing, and decreased appetite for solids. The chapter draws the line explicitly: "Generally, signs of illness such as fever (>39°C [102°F]), vomiting, or diarrhea are not symptoms of teething but of illness."
Trap: (C) is the one option from the wrong side of the sort, and it is the most consequential miss in the set — attributing a high fever to teething delays evaluation of an actual illness. Note also that benzocaine ointments "if ingested may cause a rare but serious disorder called methemoglobinemia," and amber teething necklaces carry a "danger of choking and suffocation."

Q: A parent of a 9-month-old with three erupted teeth asks about dental care. Which instructions are correct?
A) Brush with a pea-size amount of fluoridated toothpaste, and schedule the first dental visit at 3 years
B) Wipe the teeth and gums with a damp cloth, use water rather than toothpaste initially, and establish a dental home by 1 year of age
C) Begin toothbrushing as soon as the first tooth erupts, since a cloth cannot remove plaque
D) Avoid all oral care until the primary dentition is complete
Answer: B
Rationale: Teeth and gums are cleaned as soon as the primary teeth erupt, "initially by wiping with a damp cloth; toothbrushing is too harsh for the tender gingiva." Water is preferred to toothpaste initially, "which the infant will swallow." An oral health examination is recommended "by 6 months of age" and an established dental home "by 1 year of age." The quick assessment rule for eruption is "Age of the child in months − 6 = Number of teeth," which fits this infant exactly.
Trap: (A) applies the 2-to-5-year toothpaste quantity to an infant — the recommendation below age 2 is "a 'smear' of fluoridated toothpaste" — and pushes the dental home two years late. Both halves match common practice rather than the recommendation, which is what makes it the popular wrong answer.

Topic: infant injury prevention by developmental accomplishment

Q: A nurse is preparing an injury-prevention class for parents of infants. Which statement reflects the chapter's epidemiology?
A) The three leading causes of accidental death in infants are suffocation, motor vehicle-related injuries, and drowning, and falls are the leading cause of nonfatal injuries
B) The three leading causes of accidental death in infants are falls, poisoning, and burns, and suffocation is the leading cause of nonfatal injuries
C) Unintentional injuries are the leading cause of death in infants from birth to 12 months
D) Drowning is the leading cause of nonfatal injuries among infants and accounts for about half of hospitalizations
Answer: A
Rationale: "The three leading cause of accidental death injury in infants were suffocation, motor vehicle-related injuries, and drowning," and "Falls are the leading cause of nonfatal injuries among infants," accounting for approximately half of hospitalizations in this age group — commonly from "cribs, highchairs, baby walkers, changing tables, and bouncers."
Trap: (B) swaps the fatal and nonfatal lists, which is the reflex error because falls feel like the most common infant injury and therefore the most lethal. (C) is the statistic for children ages 1 to 4 years; in infants birth to 12 months, accidents were the fifth leading cause of death.

Q: A parent is securing an 8-month-old in the family car. Which action requires the nurse to intervene?
A) Placing the car seat rear facing in the back seat
B) Placing the rear-facing car seat in the front passenger seat with an active air bag
C) Checking the surface heat of the car restraint before placing the infant in it
D) Securing the infant in a federally approved car seat rather than holding the infant in a lap
Answer: B
Rationale: Infants are transported "in federally approved, rear-facing car seat, preferably in back seat," and the chapter is specific about the exception: "Do not place infant or child (in car seat) in front passenger seat with an air bag unless air bag is deactivated."
Trap: (A) is the correct practice, and it gets picked because rear facing feels counterintuitive for an infant who now sits and reaches. The action needing intervention is the air bag, not the direction the seat faces. (C) is also correct practice, listed under burn prevention.

Q: A nurse inspects the sleep environment of a 3-month-old. Which findings require teaching? Select all that apply.
A) A plastic cover over the mattress, under the sheet
B) A firm mattress with no pillows
C) A pacifier tied on a string around the infant's neck
D) A bib left on the infant at bedtime
E) A drop-side crib
Answer: A, C, D, E
Rationale: Box 10.5 directs: "Do not cover mattress with plastic"; "Do not tie pacifier on a string around infant's neck"; "Remove bibs at bedtime"; and "Do not use drop-side crib." A firm mattress with no pillows is the recommended arrangement, and crib slats should be no more than 6 cm (2.375 inches) apart.
Trap: (B) is the one option from the correct side of the sort, and it pulls because "no pillows" reads like a deficiency being reported rather than a standard being met.

Q: A 5-month-old has begun pushing up on the hands and knees. Which action should the nurse advise the parent to take now?
A) Remove all crib toys strung across the crib or play yard
B) Lower the crib mattress, since this skill means the infant will soon pull to standing
C) Begin using a walker to support the new position
D) No change is needed until the infant begins to creep at about 9 months
Answer: A
Rationale: Box 10.5 directs parents to "Remove all crib toys that are strung across crib or play yard when infant begins to push up on hands or knees or is 5 months old." This infant meets both triggers at once. The chapter's organizing principle is that each new motor skill opens a new injury category.
Trap: (D) is the reflex delay, and it pulls because the hazard feels tied to moving around the crib rather than to pushing up into a strung toy; the book keys this action to pushing up, not to creeping. (C) recommends a device the chapter calls both ineffective and dangerous.

Q: An 8-month-old has begun cruising around the furniture and pulling to stand. Which additions to the home safety plan does Box 10.5 direct for this band? Select all that apply.
A) Keep the doors of ovens, dishwashers, refrigerators, and front-loading washers and dryers closed at all times
B) Fence stairways at both the top and the bottom if the infant has access to either end
C) Keep hanging tablecloths out of reach, since the infant may pull down hot liquids or heavy or sharp objects
D) Discontinue supervision at the bath now that the infant sits steadily and can right itself
E) Always supervise near any source of water, including cleaning buckets, drainage areas, toilets, and ponds
Answer: A, B, C, E
Rationale: The 8-to-12-month band pairs cruising, climbing, walking, and pulling on objects with a new set of hazards: "Keep doors of ovens, dishwashers, refrigerators, coolers, and front-loading clothes washers and dryers closed at all times"; "Fence stairways at top and bottom if infant has access to either end"; "Keep hanging tablecloths out of reach (infant may pull down hot liquids or heavy or sharp objects)"; and "Always supervise when near any source of water, such as cleaning buckets, drainage areas, toilets, ponds." The band also directs avoiding walkers "especially near stairs."
Trap: (D) is the one option from the wrong side of the sort, and it inverts the rule at exactly the age it becomes most dangerous. The instruction for this band is the opposite — "Keep one hand on infant at all times when in tub" — because newly acquired sitting is what lets an infant reach water, not what protects them in it.

Q: A parent asks how much water it takes for an infant to drown, and says their 10-month-old is safe because he has had swimming lessons and the pool is fenced. Which response reflects Table 10.5?
A) An infant may drown in as little as 2 inches of water; swimming lessons are encouraged but are not foolproof, and touch supervision — keeping the child within arm's reach at all times — is the standard
B) Swimming lessons and pool fencing together are sufficient protection at this age
C) Drowning requires enough water to submerge the head, so buckets and toilets are not a concern
D) Touch supervision means remaining in the same room as the child
Answer: A
Rationale: Table 10.5 states that "an infant may drown in as little as 2 inches of water," that "Swimming lessons are encouraged but are not foolproof for drowning," and directs using "touch supervision — keep child within arm's reach at all times while swimming." It also directs keeping 5-gallon or larger buckets "empty of water or elevated out of child's reach," and never leaving an infant unattended "in tub, sink, or pool of water."
Trap: (B) names the two measures parents most rely on, and the chapter qualifies both rather than endorsing either as sufficient. (D) dilutes touch supervision into line-of-sight supervision, which is the commonest misreading of the term; the standard is arm's reach, because this is the one injury category that "results in death within minutes."

Q: A nurse reviews Table 10.5's SAFE PAD categories with a parent. Which statements are correct? Select all that apply.
A) The water heater should be lowered to a safe temperature of 49°C (120°F)
B) If a toy fits into a toilet paper cardboard roll, it can be swallowed by a small child
C) Hot dogs should be cut lengthwise, and hard candy avoided in infants and toddlers
D) An infant left in a vehicle in hot weather above 26.4°C (80°F) is at risk of fatal hyperthermia, because infants dissipate heat poorly
E) Caustic cleaning solutions are best stored in clearly labeled soda bottles so the contents are identifiable
Answer: A, B, C, D
Rationale: SAFE PAD pairs seven injury categories with interventions. Under burns: "Lower the water heater to a safe temperature of 49°C (120°F)." Under asphyxia: "if the toy fits into a toilet paper cardboard roll, it can be swallowed by a small child," and "Cut hot dogs lengthwise; avoid hard candy in infants and toddlers." Under automobile safety: overheating and subsequent death may occur when an infant is left in a vehicle "in hot weather (>26.4°C [80°F])," because "Infants dissipate heat poorly, and an increase in body temperature may cause death in a few hours."
Trap: (E) is the one option from the wrong side of the sort, and the chapter forbids it in terms: "Avoid storing cleaning and caustic solutions in containers such as a soda bottle or jar — infants and toddlers cannot differentiate a soda from a caustic drain cleaner." A label solves an adult's problem, not a preliterate child's, which is why the rule is about the container rather than the labeling.
