# Chapter 17 drills — Health Promotion of the Adolescent and Family

Authored from `sources/Ch17_Fact_Bank.md` (Hockenberry, Wilson & Rodgers, 11th ed.,
printed pp. 523–550).

Blueprint weight is **7 questions of 202 (3.5%), depth `standard`** — 45 drills
across 10 topics, at the top of the 30–45 band and the heaviest chapter in this
batch.

This is the last rung of the developmental ladder, written to the same shape as
Chapters 10, 12, 13, and 15 so all five stages compare column for column. Topic
names are adolescent-qualified for the same reason the earlier four are qualified
by their stage.

**Where the weight went.** Three blocks the fact bank names. **Puberty** is the
densest and most testable material in the chapter — the hypothalamic-pituitary-
gonadal cascade, the sequence and timing of sexual maturation in each sex, and the
growth-velocity numbers. Nearly every fact there is a number with a name attached,
which is what an item set is built from. **Adolescent cognition and its two named
distortions** — the imaginary audience and the personal fable — because those two
constructs explain most adolescent risk behavior and generate the clearest
scenario items. And **health screening**, where confidentiality is treated as
first-class content: it is the single most likely priority-question topic in the
chapter and the answer to the chapter's own final review question.

**Drilled from both ends:** follicle-stimulating and luteinizing hormone in girls
against the same two in boys, peak height velocity before menarche in girls against
after genital development in boys, weight-peak timing in girls against boys, the
imaginary audience against the personal fable, emotional against behavioral
autonomy, and precocious puberty against delayed puberty with its four separate
age thresholds.

**What twenty-seven printed pages could not buy at 45 questions.** Left undrilled:
Table 17.1's full three-phase grid beyond the findings drilled here; the Tanner
stage-by-stage descriptions; body-composition and vital-sign figures beyond the
directional changes; the Bright Futures visit content; special groups of
adolescents; nutrition, sleep, physical activity, and acne; tattooing and piercing;
and the substance-use statistics. The GAP register flags no omission as likely to
be tested, so nothing required expanding before drilling.

Five questions are select-all, each drawn from a list that genuinely sorts two
ways: the criteria for precocious against delayed puberty, growth changes that
follow the normal sequence against one that inverts it, the four leading causes of
adolescent death against a disease-based distractor, the eight HEEADSSS domains
against a screening domain that is not one of them, and firearm-storage teaching
against a version that locks the ammunition with the gun.

Topic: the three phases of adolescence

Q: A 16-year-old shows the greatest push for emancipation, a peer-dominant orientation, and what the parents describe as the low point of their relationship with him. Which phase of adolescence does this describe?
A) Early adolescence, ages 11 to 14 years
B) Middle adolescence, ages 15 to 17 years
C) Late adolescence, ages 18 to 22 years
D) Preadolescence, ending at the thirteenth birthday
Answer: B
Rationale: The three phases are early adolescence at 11 to 14 years, middle at 15 to 17 years, and late at 18 to 22 years. Early adolescence is dominated by the physical changes of puberty and the emotional reaction to them; middle adolescence brings the shift to a peer-dominant orientation, experimentation, the greatest push for emancipation, and the low point in the parent-child relationship; late adolescence brings full maturation, stable intimate relationships, and independent management of health care.
Trap: (A) is chosen by anyone who associates conflict with the onset of adolescence. The turbulence peaks in the middle phase rather than at the beginning, when the adolescent's attention is still largely on the physical changes themselves.

Q: A nurse is teaching about self-esteem across adolescence. Which pattern does the chapter describe?
A) Self-esteem falls in early adolescence and rises in late adolescence
B) Self-esteem improves steadily throughout adolescence
C) Self-esteem is highest in early adolescence and declines thereafter
D) Self-esteem is stable across all three phases
Answer: A
Rationale: Table 17.1 states both directions explicitly: self-esteem falls in early adolescence and rises in late adolescence.
Trap: (B) is the reassuring trajectory adults expect as a teenager matures, and it misses the early dip entirely. That dip coincides with the phase in which the physical changes of puberty and the emotional reaction to them dominate, which is what makes it worth knowing.

Q: A 12-year-old has recently become preoccupied with her changing body and emotionally reactive to comments about her appearance, but still relies mainly on her family rather than peers. Which phase does this fit?
A) Early adolescence, in which the physical changes of puberty and the emotional reaction to them predominate
B) Middle adolescence, in which peer orientation predominates
C) Late adolescence, in which intimate relationships stabilize
D) School age, since peer orientation has not yet emerged
Answer: A
Rationale: Early adolescence, at 11 to 14 years, is characterized by the physical changes of puberty and the emotional reaction to them. The shift to a peer-dominant orientation belongs to middle adolescence, and stable intimate relationships with independent management of health care belong to late adolescence.
Trap: (D) reads continued family orientation as evidence the adolescent phase has not begun. The marker of early adolescence is the pubertal change and the response to it, not the point at which peers displace parents — that displacement is the *next* phase.

Topic: neuroendocrine events of puberty

Q: A nursing student asks what actually triggers puberty. Which explanation is correct?
A) The pituitary gland and gonads are capable of mature function at any age; what changes at puberty is that the hypothalamus loses its negative sensitivity, releasing gonadotropin-releasing hormone from inhibition
B) The pituitary gland matures at puberty and becomes able to secrete gonadotropins for the first time
C) The gonads mature first and then signal the hypothalamus
D) Puberty begins when the adrenal glands first produce androgens in adolescence
Answer: A
Rationale: "The pituitary gland and gonads are capable of mature function… at any age." What changes is that "the hypothalamus loses this negative sensitivity," which releases gonadotropin-releasing hormone from inhibition and begins the cascade.
Trap: (B) describes maturation of a gland that was already competent, which is the intuitive but incorrect model. The change is the removal of an inhibition rather than the acquisition of a capability, and that distinction is what the chapter states in its own sentence.

Q: A nurse is explaining the roles of follicle-stimulating hormone and luteinizing hormone in an adolescent girl. Which description is correct?
A) Follicle-stimulating hormone drives follicle growth and estrogen production, while luteinizing hormone drives ovulation, corpus luteum formation, and progesterone production
B) Luteinizing hormone drives follicle growth and estrogen, while follicle-stimulating hormone drives ovulation
C) Both hormones act only on the adrenal cortex in girls
D) Follicle-stimulating hormone produces progesterone directly
Answer: A
Rationale: In girls, follicle-stimulating hormone stimulates follicle growth and estrogen production, while luteinizing hormone triggers ovulation, corpus luteum formation, and progesterone production.
Trap: (B) swaps the two hormones, which is the commonest inversion in this material because the name "follicle-stimulating" is the only mnemonic anchoring either one. Luteinizing hormone is named for the corpus luteum it produces, which is the anchor for the other half.

Q: A nurse is explaining the same two hormones in an adolescent boy. Which description is correct?
A) Luteinizing hormone stimulates the Leydig cells to produce testosterone, and follicle-stimulating hormone, together with luteinizing hormone, drives sperm production
B) Follicle-stimulating hormone stimulates the Leydig cells to produce testosterone
C) Luteinizing hormone has no role in the male
D) Testosterone is produced by the pituitary gland in response to gonadotropin-releasing hormone
Answer: A
Rationale: In boys, luteinizing hormone stimulates the Leydig cells to produce testosterone, and follicle-stimulating hormone acting with luteinizing hormone drives sperm production.
Trap: (B) is the mirror error of the previous item, carried across sexes. The same two hormones act in both sexes with different targets, so an item can test either sex and the distractor is always the other hormone's job.

Q: A nurse is asked why girls typically stop growing earlier than boys. Which explanation does the chapter give?
A) Estrogen stimulates skeletal growth at low levels and inhibits it at high levels, so rising estrogen eventually closes the growth process
B) Estrogen inhibits skeletal growth at every concentration
C) Girls produce less growth hormone than boys throughout puberty
D) Testosterone has no effect on skeletal growth
Answer: A
Rationale: Estrogen stimulates skeletal growth at low levels and inhibits it at high levels. This dose-dependent reversal explains why girls stop growing sooner, and the chapter states it in a single sentence.
Trap: (B) takes half the relationship and generalizes it, which loses the mechanism entirely — a hormone that only inhibited growth could not also have driven the growth spurt that preceded the inhibition.

Q: A 12-year-old girl has just had her first menstrual period, and a 13-year-old boy has had a first ejaculation. Both families ask about fertility. Which response is correct?
A) An adolescent should be considered potentially fertile with a first menstrual period or a first ejaculation
B) Girls are not fertile for about a year after menarche, because ova are not yet mature enough to be released
C) Boys are not fertile until viable sperm appear months after first ejaculation
D) Fertility cannot be assumed until Tanner stage 5 is reached
Answer: A
Rationale: The chapter states the clinical rule flatly: "an adolescent should be considered potentially fertile with a first menstrual period or a first ejaculation." It also notes that at menarche "ova still do not generally mature enough to be released," and that viable sperm follow first ejaculation.
Trap: (B) and (C) both quote the chapter's own physiology correctly and draw the wrong clinical conclusion from it. The physiology is deliberately the distractor here: the counseling rule is set at the earliest possible point rather than at the average one, because an individual adolescent's timing cannot be known in advance.

Topic: adolescent sexual maturation and its variations

Q: An 11-year-old girl's mother reports that the first change she noticed was a small firm lump under one nipple. Which statement is correct?
A) The first visible change in girls is nipple and areolar change with a breast bud, called thelarche; pubarche typically follows, although many girls have underarm or pubic hair before breast development
B) Pubic hair development always precedes breast development in girls
C) A unilateral breast bud is abnormal and requires evaluation
D) Menarche is the first sign of puberty in girls
Answer: A
Rationale: The first visible change in girls is nipple and areolar change with a breast bud, or thelarche. Pubarche "typically follows," but "many have strands of underarm and/or pubic hair before breast development."
Trap: (B) is too absolute, and the chapter deliberately qualifies the sequence rather than fixing it. The usual order is thelarche then pubarche, but reversal is common enough that the book names it, so an option asserting an invariable order is wrong.

Q: A 12-year-old boy is being assessed for the earliest signs of puberty. Which finding appears first?
A) Testicular enlargement, accompanied by thinning, reddening, and increasing looseness of the scrotum
B) Voice change
C) Facial hair
D) Pubic hair
Answer: A
Rationale: The first pubescent change in boys is testicular enlargement "accompanied by thinning, reddening, and increasing looseness of the scrotum." Voice change and facial hair are mid-to-late events.
Trap: (B) and (C) are the changes families notice and comment on, which is why they feel like the beginning. They arrive well after the process has started, so an assessment waiting for them would miss the first year or more of puberty.

Q: A 13-year-old girl and her mother are worried about a clear-to-white vaginal discharge that has been present for several months without itching, odor, or dysuria. What is the nurse's best response?
A) Reassure them that physiologic leukorrhea is normal and a sign that her body is maturing
B) Obtain a culture and begin empiric treatment for candidiasis
C) Advise douching to reduce the discharge
D) Refer for gynecologic evaluation of possible infection
Answer: A
Rationale: Girls or parents "may be concerned that this vaginal discharge is a sign of infection." The nursing response is reassurance that "the discharge is normal and a sign that their body is maturing."
Trap: (B) and (D) both treat a normal maturational finding as pathology, and the stem gives the discriminators that rule infection out — no itching, odor, or dysuria. Physiologic leukorrhea is a reassurance item, not a workup item.

Q: A 14-year-old boy is distressed by tender breast tissue that developed over the past several months. Which response is correct?
A) Gynecomastia occurs in up to 70% of boys in midpuberty and usually disappears within 2 years, although it may persist in obese individuals; reassurance is appropriate
B) This finding requires endocrine referral in all cases
C) Gynecomastia in adolescent boys is rare and always pathologic
D) The tissue should be expected to persist permanently
Answer: A
Rationale: Gynecomastia affects up to 70% of boys, "disappears within 2 years," and "may persist in obese individuals."
Trap: (B) medicalizes a finding that occurs in most boys and resolves on its own, and the referral itself communicates to an already self-conscious adolescent that something is wrong. The prevalence figure is what makes reassurance defensible rather than dismissive.

Q: A nurse reviews thresholds for abnormal pubertal timing. Which statements are correct? Select all that apply.
A) Precocious puberty is the appearance of secondary sexual characteristics before 8 years of age in either sex
B) Delayed puberty in girls includes no breast development by age 13
C) Delayed puberty in girls includes no menarche by age 15
D) Delayed puberty in boys includes no testicular or scrotal change by age 14
E) Precocious puberty is defined as onset before age 10 in girls and 12 in boys
Answer: A, B, C, D
Rationale: Precocious puberty is the appearance of secondary sexual characteristics before 8 years old in both sexes. Delayed puberty in girls is no breast development by 13 or no menarche by 15; in boys it is no testicular or scrotal change by 14.
Trap: (E) is the one option from the wrong side of the sort, and it substitutes the *average* ages of pubertal onset for the *abnormal* threshold. Those average figures are real — from Chapter 15, puberty generally begins at 10 in girls and 12 in boys — which is exactly what makes them a convincing wrong answer here.

Topic: adolescent physical growth patterns

Q: A 12-year-old girl has just experienced her peak height velocity. Her mother asks how much more she will grow. Which response is accurate?
A) Peak height velocity in girls occurs at about 12 years, 6 to 12 months before menarche, and few girls grow more than 5 cm, about 2 inches, after menarche
B) Peak height velocity follows menarche by about a year in girls
C) Most girls gain another 15 cm after menarche
D) Growth in girls continues at the same rate until age 18
Answer: A
Rationale: Peak height velocity in girls occurs at about 12 years, 6 to 12 months before menarche, and "Few girls grow more than 5 cm (2 inches) after menarche." Growth in height usually ceases 2 to 2½ years after menarche.
Trap: (B) inverts the order of the two events, which matters clinically because it changes what a parent should expect. Menarche is a late marker in the growth sequence rather than an early one, so a girl who has begun menstruating has already completed most of her linear growth.

Q: A 14-year-old boy is at his peak height velocity. Where does this fall relative to his other pubertal changes?
A) Peak height velocity in boys occurs at about 14 years, after testicular and penile growth and after axillary and mature pubic hair
B) Peak height velocity in boys precedes all genital development
C) Peak height velocity in boys and girls occurs at the same age
D) Boys reach peak height velocity before menarche-equivalent maturation
Answer: A
Rationale: Peak height velocity in boys occurs at about 14 years, after testicular and penile growth and after axillary and mature pubic hair.
Trap: (B) is the mirror error of the previous item applied to boys. In girls the growth spurt comes early in the sequence and precedes menarche; in boys it comes late, after the genital changes, which is why boys often appear to mature genitally before they visibly grow.

Q: A nurse compares weight and height spurts in adolescent boys and girls. Which statement is correct?
A) Peak weight velocity occurs about 6 months after peak height velocity in girls, whereas weight and height spurts occur simultaneously for boys
B) Peak weight velocity precedes peak height velocity in girls
C) Weight and height spurts occur simultaneously in girls and sequentially in boys
D) Weight gain is not a measured component of the pubertal growth spurt
Answer: A
Rationale: "Peak weight velocity occurs about 6 months after PHV in girls. In contrast, weight and height spurts occur simultaneously for boys."
Trap: (C) reverses which sex shows the lag. The practical consequence is that a girl who has just finished her height spurt is still expected to gain weight, and mistaking that expected gain for excess is a common source of unnecessary dieting at exactly the wrong age.

Q: A 13-year-old suddenly needs longer pants but the same shirt size, and his parent wonders whether this is abnormal. Which changes reflect the expected adolescent growth sequence? Select all that apply.
A) Leg length increases before trunk length, by about 6 to 9 months
B) Shoulder and chest growth follows leg growth by about a year
C) Heart rate decreases through adolescence while systolic blood pressure increases
D) Boys gain more in red cell mass, hemoglobin, hematocrit, and serum iron than girls
E) Trunk and shoulder growth precede leg growth, so shirt size changes first
Answer: A, B, C, D
Rationale: Leg length precedes trunk growth by 6 to 9 months, and shoulders and chest by about a year. Heart rate decreases while systolic blood pressure increases through adolescence, and boys gain more in red cell mass, hemoglobin, hematocrit, and serum iron because of greater muscle mass. Girls run a higher pulse and lower systolic pressure than boys.
Trap: (E) is the one option from the wrong side of the sort and reverses the sequence in the stem. The order is shoes, then pants, then shirts — a teenager outgrowing footwear and trouser length while shirts still fit is following the normal pattern, not deviating from it.

Topic: adolescent cognition and its distortions

Q: A 16-year-old can reason abstractly about hypothetical situations in class but makes an impulsive decision about drinking at a party. Which understanding does the chapter offer?
A) Formal operational thought emerges at 11 to 14 years, but under stress, time pressure, sleep deprivation, or peer pressure adolescents abandon their rational thought processes, and most risky health decisions occur under exactly those conditions
B) An adolescent with formal operational thought will reason reliably in any setting
C) Formal operational thought does not develop until age 18
D) Risky decisions indicate the adolescent has not reached formal operations
Answer: A
Rationale: Formal operational thought emerges at 11 to 14 years, but under "stress, time pressure, sleep deprivation, or peer pressure," adolescents "abandon their rational thought processes," and most risky health decisions occur precisely under those conditions.
Trap: (B) assumes capacity equals performance, which is the assumption behind a great deal of ineffective adolescent health education. The reasoning ability is genuinely present; the conditions under which risk decisions get made are the conditions that suspend it.

Q: A 15-year-old with newly diagnosed diabetes refuses to use an insulin pump at school because "everyone will notice it." Which cognitive distortion does this illustrate?
A) The imaginary audience — the belief that everybody will notice
B) The personal fable — the belief that it cannot happen to me
C) Formal operational reasoning
D) Concrete operational thinking
Answer: A
Rationale: The imaginary audience is the belief that "everybody will notice," and the chapter's own clinical example is the teen with diabetes refusing to use an insulin pump at school. The discriminator is the theme of being watched.
Trap: (B) is the paired construct and the one most often substituted, because both involve a distorted sense of the self's importance. Being watched points to the imaginary audience; being exempt or unique points to the personal fable.

Q: A 17-year-old does not use condoms, explaining that "other people get sexually transmitted infections, but not me." Which cognitive distortion does this illustrate?
A) The imaginary audience
B) The personal fable — the belief that it cannot happen to me, or that others simply do not understand
C) Deferred imitation
D) Conservation failure
Answer: B
Rationale: The personal fable is the belief that "it can't happen to me," illustrated by the teen not using condoms because "other people get STDs, but not me," or by the statement "you just don't understand."
Trap: (A) is the mirror error of the previous item. Nothing in this stem concerns being observed; the distortion is a claimed exemption from a risk the adolescent acknowledges applies to others, which is the personal fable's signature.

Q: A nurse is told that adolescent egocentrism is uniformly harmful. Which response reflects the chapter?
A) Narcissistic egocentrism may be an important developmental mechanism leading to positive mental health in mature adults; the harmful form is specifically feeling invincible to danger, while psychological invulnerability is associated with better mastery and coping skills
B) All forms of adolescent egocentrism predict delinquency and drug use
C) Adolescent egocentrism has no relationship to later mental health
D) Psychological invulnerability and invincibility to danger are the same construct
Answer: A
Rationale: Narcissistic egocentrism "may be an important developmental mechanism that leads to positive mental health among mature adults." The harmful form is invulnerability to danger specifically: "teens who feel invincible to danger have more delinquent behaviors and drug use, whereas teens with psychological invulnerability have better mastery and coping skills."
Trap: (D) collapses the distinction the chapter draws, and it is the distinction that makes the finding useful. One form predicts risk behavior and the other predicts resilience, so treating them as one construct loses the clinical signal in both directions.

Q: A nurse is assessing an adolescent's moral reasoning. Which statement reflects the chapter?
A) Different levels of reasoning are used at different times and in different situations, and Gilligan's caring orientation is an equally valid alternative to Kohlberg's justice orientation, with both sexes capable of both
B) Adolescents reason consistently at the postconventional level
C) Gilligan's caring orientation represents a lower stage than Kohlberg's justice orientation
D) Girls reason from care and boys from justice, without overlap
Answer: A
Rationale: "Different levels of reasoning are used at different times and in different situations," so both conventional and principled reasoning coexist in adolescence. Gilligan's caring orientation is an equally valid alternative to Kohlberg's justice orientation rather than a lesser stage, and both sexes are capable of both.
Trap: (B) assigns adolescents cleanly to one level, which is tidier than the evidence supports. (D) makes the two orientations sex-specific, which the chapter denies explicitly.

Topic: adolescent autonomy and identity

Q: A 15-year-old no longer confides in her parents, has developed an intense admiration for a teacher, and invests most of her emotional energy outside the family. Which type of autonomy does this describe?
A) Emotional autonomy, which involves changes in close relationships
B) Behavioral autonomy, which is the capacity to make independent decisions and follow through
C) Value autonomy, which consolidates between ages 18 and 20
D) Cognitive autonomy
Answer: A
Rationale: Emotional autonomy involves changes in close relationships — no longer running to parents, developing crushes on other adults, and investing outside the family. Behavioral autonomy is the capacity to make independent decisions and follow through.
Trap: (B) is the paired construct, and the discriminator is what is changing: relationships point to emotional autonomy, and decisions point to behavioral autonomy. The two develop on different timelines, so an adolescent can be well advanced in one and not the other.

Q: A 19-year-old has begun articulating a personal value system that differs from her parents' in considered ways. When does the chapter say this typically consolidates?
A) Relatively late in adolescence, between the ages of 18 and 20 years
B) In early adolescence, by age 13
C) In middle adolescence, between 15 and 17
D) Not until the mid-twenties
Answer: A
Rationale: Behavioral independence expands earlier, but the personal value system consolidates "relatively late in adolescence, between the ages of 18 and 20 years."
Trap: (C) attaches value autonomy to the phase of greatest outward rebellion, which is when the adolescent most loudly asserts different values. Asserting a different position and holding a consolidated value system are different achievements, and the second arrives years after the first.

Q: Parents of a 16-year-old ask whether they should step back entirely, since "teenagers need to rebel." Which response reflects the chapter?
A) In contrast to popular stereotypes, autonomy does not always involve rebellion, and problems are often understandable reactions to excessively controlling circumstances or to absent expectations; abandonment during this phase is harmful
B) Complete withdrawal of parental involvement is the healthiest approach
C) Rebellion is a necessary stage that parents should not interfere with
D) Parents should increase control to counter the drive for autonomy
Answer: A
Rationale: "In contrast to popular stereotypes," autonomy "does not always involve rebellion," and problems are "often understandable reactions to excessively controlling circumstances" or to absent expectations. Abandonment during this phase is harmful.
Trap: (B) is the question in the stem restated as advice, and the chapter answers it directly: absent expectations produce problems just as excessive control does. (D) is the opposite error and is named as the other source of the same difficulties.

Q: A 17-year-old attends religious services far less often than she did at 12, but describes her beliefs as more personally important than ever. How should the nurse interpret this?
A) Adolescent beliefs become more oriented toward spiritual and ideological matters and less toward rituals, practice, and strict observance, with the emphasis shifting from attendance to belief; greater religiosity and spirituality are associated with protective, health-promoting behaviors
B) Declining attendance indicates loss of faith
C) Religiosity has no documented relationship to adolescent health behaviors
D) Adolescents typically increase ritual observance while abandoning belief
Answer: A
Rationale: Adolescent beliefs become "more oriented toward spiritual and ideological matters" and less toward "rituals, practice, and the strict observance," with the emphasis shifting from attendance to belief. "Greater levels of religiosity and spirituality are associated with protective, health-promoting behaviors."
Trap: (B) uses the most visible measure — attendance — as a proxy for the internal one, and the chapter separates them deliberately. The direction of travel is inward, so the observable behavior can fall while the belief it once expressed strengthens.

Topic: adolescent relationships, sexuality, and peer influence

Q: A 16-year-old tells the nurse he has had sexual contact with male partners but identifies as heterosexual and reports attraction to girls. How should the nurse understand this?
A) Sexual orientation has three dimensions — identity, attraction, and behavior — that need not agree with one another
B) Behavior is the definitive indicator, so the adolescent's stated identity should be disregarded
C) The adolescent is confused and should be counseled toward a consistent identity
D) Identity always determines both attraction and behavior
Answer: A
Rationale: Sexual orientation has three dimensions: identity, or how one defines oneself; attraction, or who one is drawn to; and behavior, or who one has sex with. The chapter's own example has all three disagreeing.
Trap: (B) infers orientation from behavior alone, which the chapter specifically rules out. The clinical consequence matters: a nurse who assumes identity from behavior will counsel the wrong risks and may damage the trust the confidentiality discussion was meant to establish.

Q: A parent worries that their 14-year-old has close friendships but no romantic relationships. Which response reflects the chapter?
A) Intimate relationships are distinct from sexual relationships and either can exist without the other; same-gender peer intimacy develops first, and opposite-gender intimacy comes relatively late
B) Absence of romantic relationships at 14 indicates delayed social development
C) Intimacy and sexual activity develop together and cannot be separated
D) Opposite-gender intimacy normally precedes same-gender friendship
Answer: A
Rationale: "Intimate relationships are distinct from sexual relationships," and one can exist without the other in either direction. Same-gender peer intimacy develops first, and opposite-gender intimacy comes "relatively late."
Trap: (C) fuses the two, which is the assumption behind both parental worry and a good deal of adolescent risk. A teenager can be intimately connected without sexual activity, and sexually active without intimacy, and the chapter treats those as independent axes.

Q: Parents ask the nurse about dating for their 13-year-old. Which guidance does the chapter give?
A) Mixed-gender group activities are positive for young teens, and a moderate degree of dating with serious relationships delayed until late adolescence may be the ideal pattern; one-on-one dating with a lot of time alone in early adolescence may lead to sexual intimacy before the teen is ready
B) Early exclusive pair dating builds relationship skills and should be encouraged
C) All dating should be prohibited until age 18
D) The amount and type of dating has no bearing on outcomes
Answer: A
Rationale: "A moderate degree of dating, with serious relationships delayed until late adolescence, may be the ideal pattern." Mixed-gender group activities are positive for young teens, while "one-on-one dating with a lot of time alone in early adolescence" may lead to sexual intimacy before a teen is ready.
Trap: (C) is the protective extreme, and the chapter's recommendation is moderation rather than prohibition — some dating is part of the ideal pattern. The variable that matters is the format and the amount of unsupervised time alone, not whether dating occurs.

Q: A 16-year-old works 30 hours a week during the school year. What does the chapter say about adolescent employment?
A) Work can build skills and provide mentors, but detrimental effects are more likely for adolescents who work more than 20 hours a week
B) Any employment during high school is detrimental
C) Employment has benefits at every level of hours worked
D) The threshold for detrimental effects is 40 hours a week
Answer: A
Rationale: Below the threshold, work can build skills and provide mentors; "detrimental effects are more likely for adolescents who work more than 20 hours a week."
Trap: (D) uses the adult full-time standard, which is the intuitive anchor and roughly double the adolescent threshold. This teen is 10 hours past the point at which the chapter expects harm rather than benefit.

Topic: adolescent mortality and health screening

Q: A nurse is preparing an adolescent health presentation. Which are the leading causes of death in this age group? Select all that apply.
A) Motor vehicle crashes
B) Other accidental injuries
C) Homicide
D) Suicide
E) Congenital anomalies and chronic illness
Answer: A, B, C, D
Rationale: Motor vehicle crashes, other accidental injuries, homicide, and suicide together account for more than 70% — approximately 71% — of adolescent deaths. Suicide is second among Caucasian adolescents, while homicide is the most likely cause among older African American adolescents.
Trap: (E) is the one option from the wrong side of the sort and names the disease-based causes that dominate in younger children. The striking feature of adolescent mortality is that none of the four leading causes is a disease, which is what makes the entire chapter's emphasis on risk behavior rather than pathology coherent.

Q: A 16-year-old asks whether what she tells the nurse will be reported to her parents. Which response reflects the chapter's guidance?
A) Assure her of confidentiality and explain its limits, which include abuse reporting and suicidality, since adolescents are unwilling to seek care for sensitive topics if their parents will know and are often unaware they can receive confidential care
B) Tell her that everything discussed will be reported to her parents
C) Promise unconditional confidentiality with no exceptions
D) Defer the question until a parent can join the discussion
Answer: A
Rationale: Adolescents are "unwilling to seek health care related to sensitive topics if their parents will know," and often "are unaware of their ability to receive confidential health care." The guideline pairs the assurance with its limits — abuse reporting and suicidality.
Trap: (C) makes a promise that cannot be kept and that will have to be broken at precisely the moment the adolescent is most at risk. The two halves of this answer are inseparable: confidentiality is what opens the conversation, and its stated limits are what keep the promise honest.

Q: A nurse plans an adolescent visit and considers whether to involve the parents. Which approach is correct?
A) Interview the adolescent alone while still engaging the parents, since efforts to exclude parents from adolescent health services are both unrealistic and unwise, balancing the adolescent's growing autonomy against the parents' diminishing control
B) Exclude parents entirely from all adolescent health encounters
C) Conduct the entire visit with the parents present
D) Allow the adolescent to decide whether parents are informed of every finding
Answer: A
Rationale: "Efforts to exclude parents from adolescent health services are both unrealistic and unwise." The correct approach balances "the adolescent's growing autonomy" against "the parents' diminishing control" — interview the teen alone, and still engage the parents.
Trap: (B) takes the confidentiality principle to an extreme the chapter names as unwise. Confidentiality governs what is disclosed from the private portion of the visit; it does not mean the parents are removed from their child's health care.

Q: A nurse begins a health interview with a 15-year-old. Which sequence is correct?
A) Begin with less sensitive issues and open-ended questions, move to directive questions only if necessary, and preface sexual questions with their purpose and the limits of confidentiality
B) Begin with the sexual history, to establish that no topic is off limits
C) Use closed, directive questions throughout to obtain precise answers
D) Avoid sexual topics unless the adolescent raises them
Answer: A
Rationale: Begin with less sensitive issues, use open-ended questions, move to directive questions only if necessary, and preface sexual questions with their purpose and the confidentiality limits.
Trap: (B) is sequenced backward and is the commonest error in adolescent interviewing. Opening with the most sensitive material before rapport exists produces guarded answers to everything that follows, which costs more information than it gains.

Q: A nurse uses the HEEADSSS framework for an adolescent psychosocial assessment. Which domains does it include? Select all that apply.
A) Home, and Education or employment
B) Eating, and Activities
C) Drugs, under which caffeine is assessed
D) Sexuality, Suicide or depression, and Safety, under which texting and guns are assessed
E) Hearing and vision screening
Answer: A, B, C, D
Rationale: HEEADSSS covers eight domains: Home, Education or employment, Eating, Activities, Drugs, Sexuality, Suicide or depression, and Safety. Caffeine is assessed under Drugs, and texting and guns under Safety.
Trap: (E) is the one option from the wrong side of the sort. It names legitimate adolescent screening, but the framework is a psychosocial one, and three of its eight domains begin with S — sexuality, suicide, and safety — which is the part of the mnemonic most often miscounted.

Topic: adolescent sexual health and contraception counseling

Q: A 17-year-old taking combined oral contraceptives says she no longer needs condoms. Which response is correct?
A) Hormonal contraception does not protect against sexually transmitted infections, so barrier protection is still needed
B) Oral contraceptives provide partial protection against sexually transmitted infections
C) Condoms are unnecessary once a reliable contraceptive is in use
D) Barrier protection is needed only with new partners
Answer: A
Rationale: Hormonal contraception does not protect against sexually transmitted infections. The real-world figures underline the gap: approximately 57% of sexually active adolescents used a condom at last intercourse, and fewer than 5% use long-acting reversible contraception.
Trap: (C) conflates the two purposes of protection, which is the single most consequential misunderstanding in adolescent contraceptive counseling. Pregnancy prevention and infection prevention are separate goals requiring separate methods, and a highly effective contraceptive can leave infection risk entirely unaddressed.

Q: A school board is considering an abstinence-only sexual education program. Which statement reflects the chapter?
A) Abstinence-only programs do not delay sexual initiation and may increase unprotected sex, although abstinence itself is still counseled as one of the responsible sexual behaviors deserving positive reinforcement
B) Abstinence-only programs reliably delay first intercourse
C) Abstinence should not be discussed with adolescents at all
D) Abstinence-only programs and comprehensive programs produce equivalent outcomes
Answer: A
Rationale: Abstinence-only programs do not delay sex and may increase unprotected sex, but abstinence itself is still counseled as one of the "responsible sexual behaviors" deserving positive reinforcement.
Trap: (C) overreads the evidence against the program as evidence against the behavior, and the chapter separates them deliberately. What the data indict is an exclusive curriculum, not the choice an individual adolescent makes.

Q: A nurse asks a 15-year-old, "Do any of your friends drink alcohol?" A colleague questions why. Which rationale is correct?
A) Questions about peers give information that typically reflects similar behaviors of the teen, so this is an interviewing technique rather than small talk
B) The question is irrelevant to the adolescent's own risk
C) The question is intended only to build rapport
D) Peer behavior has no documented relationship to adolescent behavior
Answer: A
Rationale: Asking "Do any of your friends drink?" gives "information that typically reflects similar behaviors of the teen."
Trap: (C) treats a deliberate assessment technique as a conversational warm-up. It does build rapport, but the reason it appears in the interviewing guidance is that the answer is diagnostically informative about the adolescent in the room.

Q: A nurse is counseling a sexually active 16-year-old about contraception. Which finding reflects the chapter's data on adolescent practice?
A) Approximately 57% of sexually active adolescents used a condom at last intercourse, and fewer than 5% use long-acting reversible contraception
B) More than 90% of sexually active adolescents use condoms consistently
C) Long-acting reversible contraception is the most commonly used adolescent method
D) Adolescent contraceptive use has not been measured
Answer: A
Rationale: Approximately 57% of sexually active adolescents used a condom at last intercourse, and fewer than 5% use long-acting reversible contraception.
Trap: (C) reverses the actual uptake of the most effective category. The gap between how effective long-acting reversible contraception is and how rarely adolescents use it is exactly what makes it worth raising in counseling rather than assuming.

Topic: adolescent depression, suicide risk, and injury prevention

Q: A 16-year-old is brought in for fatigue. He is well groomed, makes eye contact, and jokes with the nurse. When asked, "Have you been feeling down or blue lately?" he answers yes. How should the nurse interpret this?
A) Most adolescents who are depressed respond affirmatively to that question although they may not necessarily look depressed, so the affirmative answer should be pursued
B) His presentation rules out depression, so the answer can be discounted
C) The question is too general to yield useful information
D) Depression should be assessed only when the adolescent appears sad
Answer: A
Rationale: "Most adolescents who are depressed respond affirmatively to 'Have you been feeling down or blue lately?' although they may not necessarily 'look' depressed."
Trap: (B) weighs the appearance over the answer, and the chapter names that mismatch specifically. An adolescent who presents well and reports low mood is the typical case rather than a contradictory one.

Q: A depressed 17-year-old denies any thoughts of harming herself. A second adolescent describes a specific plan and has access to the means. How should the nurse triage each?
A) The nonsuicidal adolescent is referred for evaluation and treatment; the adolescent with a specific plan requires immediate acute intervention
B) Both require immediate acute intervention regardless of plan
C) Both can be referred for routine outpatient follow-up
D) Neither requires intervention unless a previous attempt has occurred
Answer: A
Rationale: The triage splits on the presence of a plan: a nonsuicidal depressed adolescent is referred, while an adolescent with a specific plan requires immediate acute intervention.
Trap: (D) makes a prior attempt the threshold for acting, which would leave the adolescent in this stem — plan and means in place, no prior attempt — without intervention. The plan is the trigger, and it does not require a history to be taken seriously.

Q: A nurse reviews adolescent suicide statistics. Which statement is accurate?
A) Girls both consider and attempt suicide more often — 23.4% of females versus 12.2% of males considered, and 11.6% of girls versus 5.5% of boys attempted — but feeling sad or hopeless for 2 weeks or longer was reported by 34% of boys and 22% of girls
B) Boys both consider and attempt suicide more often than girls
C) Rates of considering and attempting suicide are equal between the sexes
D) Girls report feeling sad or hopeless more often than boys
Answer: A
Rationale: Considered suicide: 23.4% of females versus 12.2% of males. Attempted: 11.6% of girls versus 5.5% of boys. But feeling sad or hopeless for 2 weeks or longer was reported by 34% of boys and 22% of girls.
Trap: (D) applies the direction of the first two figures to the third, and the third runs the other way. That reversal is the reason this cluster is worth drilling as one item: two of the three measures favor one direction and the third does not, so a learner holding a single rule will get one of them wrong.

Q: A nurse is completing a health supervision visit with a 15-year-old whose family has no history of violence. Should firearm access be assessed?
A) Yes — assess all families for the presence of a gun in the home, regardless of history
B) Only if the adolescent has a history of aggression or depression
C) Only in households known to hunt or collect firearms
D) No, since asking may offend families with no risk factors
Answer: A
Rationale: The directive is to "Assess all families for the presence of a gun in the home." Storage teaching has three parts: the gun is never loaded, it is locked up, and the ammunition is locked separately.
Trap: (B) makes the assessment conditional on the risk factors it exists to detect, which is the logic that leaves unrecognized risk unaddressed. Given that suicide is among the four leading causes of adolescent death, universal assessment is what makes the screening meaningful.

Q: A nurse teaches a family about safe firearm storage. Which instructions are correct? Select all that apply.
A) The firearm is stored unloaded
B) The firearm is locked up
C) Ammunition is locked in a separate location
D) All families should be assessed for the presence of a gun in the home
E) Storing an unloaded firearm in a locked cabinet with its ammunition is sufficient
Answer: A, B, C, D
Rationale: Storage teaching has three parts: the firearm is never loaded, it is locked up, and the ammunition is locked separately. All families are assessed for the presence of a gun in the home regardless of history.
Trap: (E) is the one option from the wrong side of the sort and satisfies two of the three requirements while dropping the third. It is the most plausible wrong answer here because storing the ammunition with the locked gun feels like reasonable practice; separating them is what prevents the firearm from being made ready in a single step.

Q: A 16-year-old needs a tetanus booster and received a tetanus-diphtheria vaccine 3 years ago. Which immunization statements are correct?
A) When used as a booster, the tetanus-diphtheria-acellular pertussis vaccine may be administered earlier than the previous 5-year interval, regardless of interval from the last tetanus-diphtheria dose; meningococcal vaccine is given at 11 to 12 years with a booster before 16, or as one dose at 13 to 18 if previously unvaccinated
B) The 5-year interval must elapse before a tetanus-diphtheria-acellular pertussis booster
C) Meningococcal vaccine is given only once, with no booster
D) Varicella vaccine at 13 years or older is a single dose
Answer: A
Rationale: When used as a booster, the tetanus-diphtheria-acellular pertussis vaccine "may be administered earlier than the previous 5-year interval… regardless of interval from the last Td dose." Meningococcal vaccine is given at 11 to 12 years with a booster before 16, or as one dose at 13 to 18 for the previously unvaccinated. Varicella at 13 years or older is two doses 4 or more weeks apart.
Trap: (B) applies the superseded interval, which is the reason the chapter states the exception explicitly — the 5-year rule is familiar enough that it gets applied where it no longer governs. (D) halves the varicella schedule for older adolescents, which is the dose count most often missed.
