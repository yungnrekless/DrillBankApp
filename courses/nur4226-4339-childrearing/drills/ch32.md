Source: `sources/Ch32_Fact_Bank.md` (Hockenberry, Wilson, & Rodgers, *Wong's Nursing Care of
Infants and Children*, 11th ed., ch. 32, printed pp. 1209–1238).

**Blueprint weight: 5 questions of 121, depth `standard`.** That buys **40 questions across 10
topics**, the same count the other five-item chapters in this course received.

**Where the weight went.** This chapter has an unusual shape: roughly the first third is one
continuous argument about wounds and how skin heals, and the remaining two-thirds is a catalog
delivered almost entirely inside seven large tables. The allocation keeps that split. Nine
questions go to the wound argument, because it generates mechanism and priority items and
because every dressing and cleansing rule in the chapter follows from it. The rest go to the
conditions a pediatric nurse actually teaches families about — infestations, contact dermatitis,
diaper rash, eczema, acne — rather than spreading thinly across the whole catalog.

**What is drilled from both ends.** The mirror pairs the fact bank flags and this file writes as
adjacent or opposed items: irritant against candidal diaper dermatitis (folds spared against
folds involved), atopic against seborrheic dermatitis, infantile against childhood eczema
distribution (extensor against flexural), scabies against pediculosis, black widow against brown
recluse (neurotoxin against necrotoxin, antivenin against antibiotics and steroids), and tinea
corporis against contact dermatitis. **The single cleanest trap in the infestation material is
drilled head-on: permethrin appears at 5% for scabies and 1% for head lice**, and the two are put
in one stem so the strengths cannot be answered by recognition alone.

**What the weight could not buy.** Left undrilled, deliberately: the descriptive lesion
vocabulary and the primary-versus-secondary lesion distinction; the six acute wound types and
the labile-permanent-stable cell classification; the ten-question nursing history; vacuum-assisted
closure and recombinant growth factors; the four systemic mycoses and their endemic regions; the

and cat bites and cat scratch disease; foreign bodies; drug reactions; all eight of Table 32.9's

material that a fortieth question could not reach without either fusing it to an unrelated topic
or standing up a one-question topic, and neither is worth doing. The psychosocial material on
living with a visible skin condition is also left out, though it is the chapter's most humane
page.

**On the GAP register.** Nothing in this chapter's register is flagged "most likely to be

Topic: wound healing phases and factors that delay it
Subtopic: the four phases in order

Q: A nurse is teaching a student about wound healing. In what order do the four phases occur?
A) Inflammation, fibroplasia, contraction, maturation
B) Fibroplasia, inflammation, contraction, maturation
C) Inflammation, contraction, fibroplasia, maturation
D) Contraction, inflammation, fibroplasia, maturation
Answer: A
Rationale: The chapter names them once, in order: "The nonspecific repair mechanism of wound healing with scar formation involves the processes of inflammation, fibroplasia, scar contraction, and scar maturation" (p. 1210). Inflammation is "The initial response at the site of injury"; fibroplasia "continues 5 days to 4 weeks"; contraction "helps bring wound edges closer together"; and with maturation "the scar becomes pale, does not tan when exposed to sunlight, will not sweat or produce hair, and may itch" (p. 1211).
Trap: (C) is the likeliest error, because contraction sounds like something the clot does early, when in fact it is "Fibroblast movement" during and after fibroplasia that draws the edges together (p. 1211). (B) puts repair before the vascular and cellular response that prepares the tissue for it.

Q: A child's wound is 10 days old, and shiny granulation tissue is visible as the nurse changes the dressing. What should the nurse anticipate?
A) The wound is in the fibroplasia phase, and granulation tissue bleeds profusely if disturbed
B) The wound is in the inflammatory phase and is at its weakest point
C) The wound is fully matured and needs no special handling
D) Granulation tissue indicates infection and should be reported
Answer: A
Rationale: "Fibroplasia (granulation or proliferation), the second phase of healing, continues 5 days to 4 weeks," and the warning attached to it is specific: "The wound is fragile at this time, and granulation tissue bleeds profusely if disturbed" (p. 1211).
Trap: (B) names a real fragility statement belonging to a different phase — the wound "is weakest" during "the first 3 to 5 days after injury" (pp. 1210–1211) — and a 10-day-old wound is past it. Two fragility warnings, two phases, and the one that answers a dressing-change question is the second. (D) reads healthy repair as pathology.

Subtopic: what helps and what delays healing

Q: Which factors delay wound healing? Select all that apply.
A) Cleansing the wound with hydrogen peroxide
B) A dry wound environment
C) Vitamin C deficiency
D) Systemic corticosteroid therapy
E) A moisture-retentive occlusive dressing
Answer: A, B, C, D
Rationale: Table 32.1 pairs each factor with its mechanism: hydrogen peroxide is "Toxic to fibroblasts; can cause subcutaneous gas formation (mimics gas-forming infection)"; a dry wound environment "Allows epithelial cells to dry out and die; impairs migration of epithelial cells across wound surface"; vitamin C deficiency "Inhibits formation of collagen fibers and capillary development"; and corticosteroids "Inhibit fibroblast proliferation" and "Depress formation of granulation tissue" (p. 1211).
Trap: (E) is the one wrong option, and it is the intervention the whole chapter argues for: "An acute full-thickness wound kept in a moist environment usually reepithelializes in 12 to 15 days, whereas the same wound kept open to the air heals in approximately 25 to 30 days" (p. 1211).

Q: A parent says she is keeping her child's abrasion uncovered so a good scab can form. What should the nurse explain?
A) A moist, covered wound resurfaces in roughly half the time of one left open to air
B) A dry scab is the best protection a wound can have against infection
C) The scab should be lifted daily so the wound underneath can be inspected
D) Covering the wound traps bacteria and causes infection
Answer: A
Rationale: "Emphasis has shifted from interventions directed at maintaining a dry environment that promotes eschar formation to those that promote a moist, crust-free environment that enhances the migration of epithelial cells across the wound and facilitates resurfacing," with 12 to 15 days moist against 25 to 30 days open to air. Eschar "interferes with healing by preventing wound contraction" (p. 1211).
Trap: (C) adds a second error to the first, since scabs "are allowed to slough off without assistance; picking or early removal may cause scarring and secondary infection" (p. 1215). (D) states the belief the moist-wound evidence overturned — moist wound healing in fact "decreases pain and inflammation" and creates the conditions for autolytic debridement (p. 1212).

Q: A contaminated laceration is deliberately left open and then sutured several days later, once granulation tissue is present. How should the nurse describe this?
A) Healing by tertiary intention, in which closure is intentionally delayed
B) Healing by secondary intention, because the wound edges could not be approximated
C) Wound dehiscence, a complication of the original repair
D) Healing by primary intention, because the wound was sutured in the end
Answer: A
Rationale: Tertiary intention "takes place when suturing is delayed after injury or the wound later breaks down and is sutured or resutured when granulation is present," and the chapter gives the reason: "Frequently, suturing of a contaminated wound is deliberately delayed to afford better removal of infection before closing" (p. 1211).
Trap: (C) reads a planned decision as a failure, which is the error this category invites — delayed closure is a choice. (B) describes wounds "in which the edges cannot be approximated, such as an avulsion or a third-degree burn"; here they could be approximated, and were not, on purpose.

Topic: wound care dressings and topical therapy in children
Subtopic: cleansing and dressings at home

Q: Parents ask what to use to clean their child's minor wound at home. What should the nurse advise?
A) Normal saline, because nothing should go into a wound that you would not put in the eye
B) Hydrogen peroxide, to bubble debris out of the wound
C) Povidone-iodine, to prevent infection
D) Rubbing alcohol, to disinfect the surface
Answer: A
Rationale: The Nursing Alert is written as a rule of thumb: "Do not put anything in a wound that you would not put in the eye. The safest solution is normal saline" (p. 1214). Parents are cautioned "to avoid povidone-iodine, alcohol, and hydrogen peroxide because these products are toxic to wounds."
Trap: (B) and (C) are the two products a family most likely already owns, and both sit in Table 32.1's delay-healing list — hydrogen peroxide is "Toxic to fibroblasts" and povidone-iodine "Toxic to white and red blood cells and fibroblasts" (p. 1211). Povidone-iodine carries an added pediatric caution, since it "may be absorbed through the skin in neonates and young children."

Q: A parent calls, alarmed that the gel under her child's hydrocolloid dressing looks like yellow pus and smells faintly fruity. The surrounding skin looks normal and the child is afebrile. What should the nurse say?
A) This is normal leakage under this type of dressing
B) Remove the dressing at once and bring the child in for a wound culture
C) Start the leftover antibiotic ointment from the child's last infection
D) Clean the wound with hydrogen peroxide before reapplying the dressing
Answer: A
Rationale: Nursing Alert: "Advise parents that the yellow gel forming under hydrocolloid dressings may look like pus and has a distinct odor (somewhat fruity) but is normal leakage" (p. 1215). The signs that would matter are different ones — increased erythema beyond the wound margin, edema, purulent exudate, odor, pain extending beyond the margin, and increased temperature (p. 1214) — and none is present.
Trap: (B) is the reasonable-sounding overreaction this alert exists to prevent; the two alerts sit one page apart and are built to be confused. (D) compounds it, since hydrogen peroxide is among the products parents are told to keep out of a wound (p. 1214).

Q: Which findings should the nurse teach parents to report as signs of wound infection? Select all that apply.
A) Redness extending beyond the wound margin
B) Purulent exudate
C) Pain extending beyond the wound margin
D) Increased temperature
E) Shiny pink granulation tissue in the wound bed
Answer: A, B, C, D
Rationale: The Nursing Alert lists the signs as "Increased erythema, especially beyond wound margin," edema, purulent exudate, odor, "Pain at the site or extending beyond the wound margin," and "Increased temperature" (p. 1214).
Trap: (E) is the wrong option and it describes healing rather than infection: granulation tissue is the highly vascular, "shiny connective tissue" of the second phase of repair (p. 1211). Note that two of the four correct options are defined by extension past the margin — redness and pain confined to the wound itself are the expected inflammatory response.

Subtopic: applying topical medication

Q: A preschooler is discharged with two topical preparations that must be applied in a specific order. Which nursing action best supports correct use at home?
A) Mark the containers 1 and 2, and give written instructions along with a demonstration
B) Tell the parents to apply whichever preparation the child tolerates better first
C) Instruct the parents to apply a thick layer of each so fewer applications are needed
D) Give verbal instructions only, to avoid overwhelming the family with paperwork
Answer: A
Rationale: The Nursing Alert is the most concrete teaching instruction in the chapter: "Provide written instructions and demonstrate to parents the correct amount of topical medication to apply (e.g., size of a pea; thin film to cover). If more than one preparation is to be applied, mark the containers 1 and 2 to help parents remember the correct order for application to the skin" (p. 1214).
Trap: (C) is the assumption most families make unprompted, and it is wrong twice over: "one thick application of topical medication is not equivalent to several thin applications" (p. 1235), and a child's skin is "more absorbent" (p. 1213), so the excess is absorbed rather than wasted. The same alert says that "more medication or increased frequency of applications is not better."

Topic: bacterial and viral skin infections in children
Subtopic: bacterial infections

Q: A 3-year-old has moist erosions on the face covered with heavy, honey-colored crusts. Which teaching is most important?
A) Keep the child from touching the lesions, because impetigo spreads easily by self-inoculation
B) Isolate the child from all contact until every lesion has healed completely
C) Scrub the crusts off with a stiff cloth at each dressing change
D) Prepare the family for permanent scarring
Answer: A
Rationale: "Impetigo contagiosa and MRSA infection can easily spread by self-inoculation; therefore caution the child against touching the involved area" (p. 1216). Table 32.3 describes the lesion exactly — exudate that "dries to form heavy, honey-colored crusts" — and labels it "Autoinoculable and contagious."
Trap: (D) inverts the prognosis, since impetigo "Tends to heal without scarring unless secondary infection" (p. 1216). (B) overreaches: the child does need separate washcloths and towels and daily clothing changes washed in hot water, but full isolation is reserved for a viral exanthem or an easily spread vector such as lice (p. 1214).

Q: An adolescent has several tender, inflamed lesions involving multiple adjacent hair follicles on the back of the neck and asks whether he should squeeze them. How should the nurse respond?
A) These lesions should never be squeezed, because squeezing risks worsening or spreading the infection
B) Squeezing will speed resolution as long as the hands are washed first
C) They can be opened at home with a sterile needle
D) They will resolve faster under a high-potency topical steroid
Answer: A
Rationale: The comments column for folliculitis, furuncle, and carbuncle states that "Lesions should never be squeezed" (p. 1216), and the nursing text gives the reasoning: squeezing "will not hasten the resolution of the infection and that there is a risk of making the lesion worse or spreading the infection. No attempt should be made to puncture the surface of the pustule with a needle or sharp instrument."
Trap: (C) is the half measure a family reaches for once told not to squeeze, and the same sentence refuses it. (D) reaches for the wrong drug class entirely: the management is cleanliness, "Local warm, moist compresses," topical or systemic antibiotics, and incision and drainage of severe lesions — and folliculitis is one of only two conditions in this chapter for which heat is indicated at all (pp. 1213, 1216).

Subtopic: viral infections

Q: A 9-year-old is diagnosed with herpes zoster following a dermatome on the trunk, and the parents ask what to expect. Which response is accurate?
A) Pain in children is usually minimal, and postherpetic pain does not occur in children
B) He should be warned to expect months of nerve pain after the rash clears
C) He poses no risk to others, since shingles is a reactivation rather than a new infection
D) The rash will cross the midline and become generalized within a few days
Answer: A
Rationale: The comments column states "Pain in children usually minimal" and "Postherpetic pain does not occur in children" (p. 1218). The virus "has affinity for posterior root ganglia, posterior horn of spinal cord, and skin," and lesions appear as "crops of vesicles usually confined to dermatome following along course of affected nerve."
Trap: (B) imports an adult complication into a pediatric case, and it is the warning most people have heard attached to shingles. (C) is right about the mechanism and wrong about the consequence: "Chickenpox may follow exposure; isolate affected child from other children in a hospital or school" (p. 1218), so a susceptible child exposed to these lesions can develop varicella.

Q: A parent asks about treating the several warts on her otherwise healthy 7-year-old's fingers. Which response reflects the chapter's guidance?
A) Warts tend to disappear spontaneously, treatment is not uniformly successful, and most destructive techniques leave scars
B) Warts should be removed promptly, because untreated warts become malignant
C) Warts should be treated at once with liquid nitrogen to protect the rest of the family
D) Warts are not contagious, so no precautions are needed at all
Answer: A
Rationale: Table 32.4 opens the management column for verruca with "Not uniformly successful," and the comments read "Tend to disappear spontaneously," "Course unpredictable," and "Most destructive techniques tend to leave scars" (p. 1218).
Trap: (D) contradicts that same comments column, which calls warts "Autoinoculable"; the argument for restraint is that the treatment costs more than the disease, not that the disease cannot spread. (C) also misses the caution that "Repeated irritation will cause to enlarge."

Topic: dermatophyte infections in children
Subtopic: ringworm of the scalp and body

Q: A 6-year-old has scaly, circumscribed patches of hair loss on the scalp, and tinea capitis is diagnosed. The parent asks whether an antifungal cream will clear it. What should the nurse explain?
A) Scalp ringworm requires an oral antifungal such as griseofulvin or terbinafine
B) An over-the-counter antifungal cream applied twice daily is sufficient
C) A topical antifungal combined with a high-potency steroid is the usual treatment
D) No treatment is needed, since the hair loss resolves on its own
Answer: A
Rationale: "Oral antifungal agents are the only effective means for treating systemic fungal infections and tinea capitus" (p. 1214), and Table 32.5 gives management as "Oral griseofulvin or terbinafine," with oral ketoconazole for difficult cases and selenium sulfide shampoo twice weekly to decrease fungal shedding (p. 1218).
Trap: (C) sounds like it addresses both the fungus and the inflammation, and the chapter forbids it twice: combining a topical antifungal with a high-potency steroid "may lead to further infection" (p. 1218), and over-the-counter hydrocortisone "may further aggravate inflammation caused by fungus or bacteria" (p. 1213). A steroid on a fungal rash is wrong on two separate pages.

Q: A child is starting griseofulvin for tinea capitis. Which instruction is most important?
A) Give the drug with high-fat foods, and continue the full course even after the scaling clears
B) Give the drug on an empty stomach for the best absorption
C) Stop the drug once the lesions are no longer visible
D) Expect the course to last about seven days
Answer: A
Rationale: Families are taught to give "the medication with high-fat foods for best absorption," and the adherence risk is named directly: "because subjective symptoms subside, children or parents may be tempted to decrease or discontinue the drug," even though "the therapy frequently continues for weeks or months" (p. 1218).
Trap: (B) applies the ordinary empty-stomach rule for oral antibiotics to a drug whose absorption depends on fat; (C) and (D) both underestimate a course measured in weeks to months, and (C) is the specific behavior the chapter predicts. For long courses, "periodic testing is required to monitor leukopenia and assess liver and renal function."

Q: A child has a round, erythematous, scaling patch on the forearm that is spreading outward with a clearing center. Which combination of actions is correct?
A) Scrape the advancing periphery for microscopic examination, and have the household pets examined
B) Scrape the clear center, where the organism is most concentrated
C) Keep the child home from school until the lesion has completely cleared
D) Apply the topical antifungal only to the visible ring, stopping when it disappears
Answer: A
Rationale: "Diagnosis is made from microscopic examination of scrapings taken from the advancing periphery of the lesion, which almost always produces a scale" (p. 1218), because the lesion "spreads peripherally and clears centrally." Tinea corporis is "Usually of animal origin from infected pets," so "all household pets should be examined for the disorder."
Trap: (C) is stricter than the source, which says "The child may return to school after the therapy is initiated" (p. 1218). (D) stops short in both space and time: the topical is "applied 2.5 cm (1 inch) beyond periphery of lesion," and "application continued 1-2 weeks after no sign of lesion."

Topic: contact dermatitis and poison ivy in children
Subtopic: reading the distribution

Q: A school-age child has a widespread, symmetric, itchy rash over most of the trunk and limbs, with no sharply demarcated borders. Which cause is most likely?
A) A generalized irritant such as bubble bath, laundry soap, body soap, or lotion
B) The nickel snap on the child's jeans
C) Mascara
D) Ultraviolet injury
Answer: A
Rationale: The chapter turns distribution into a diagnostic method: a generalized eruption is "more likely to be caused by bubble bath, laundry soap, body soap, or lotion," a rash around the eyes suggests mascara, a rash on skin covered by clothing but not by undergarments suggests wool, and a rash on uncovered skin suggests ultraviolet injury (p. 1222).
Trap: (B) names the offender families never guess, and it is a real one — "nickel found in jewelry and the snaps on sleepers and denim" (p. 1222) — but a point contact produces a localized reaction with "a sharp demarcation between inflamed and normal skin" (p. 1219). This rash has no such border, so the exposure was not a point contact.

Q: What is the major goal of treatment in contact dermatitis?
A) Preventing further exposure of the skin to the offending substance
B) Applying a high-potency topical steroid until the rash clears
C) Starting a systemic antihistamine and an oral antibiotic
D) Covering the area so the rash cannot spread to other body sites
Answer: A
Rationale: "The major goal in treatment is to prevent further exposure of the skin to the offending substance," and "Provided there is no further irritation, the skin's normal recuperative powers will often produce healing without treatment" (pp. 1219, 1222).
Trap: (B) treats the visible problem while leaving the cause in place, which is why the rash returns, and it runs against the chapter's stated stance that practitioners "aim to avoid overtreatment, particularly in young children" (p. 1212). (D) assumes a dermatitis spreads from the rash itself, which is the misconception the poison ivy section exists to refute.

Subtopic: poison ivy, oak, and sumac

Q: A child runs through poison ivy while camping. What should the nurse instruct the family to do first?
A) Flush the skin with cold running water, preferably within 15 minutes, without scrubbing
B) Wash the area vigorously with a harsh soap and a washcloth
C) Apply calamine lotion and wait to see whether a rash develops
D) Cover the area with an occlusive dressing so the oil cannot spread
Answer: A
Rationale: "When it is known that the child has made contact with the plant, the area is immediately flushed (preferably within 15 minutes) with cold running water to neutralize the urushiol not yet bonded to the skin. Once the oil has been removed from the skin, the allergen has been neutralized" (p. 1223). Clothing that touched the plant is removed with care and laundered in hot water and detergent.
Trap: (B) is the instinctive response, and the chapter names it a contraindication with a mechanism attached: harsh soap and scrubbing "removes protective skin oils and dilutes the urushiol, allowing it to spread" (p. 1223). The window matters because "The earlier the skin is cleansed, the greater the chance of removing the urushiol before it attaches to the skin."

Q: A child with weeping poison ivy lesions has been kept home so classmates will not catch it, and the parents are covering the blisters to stop the rash from spreading. How should the nurse respond?
A) The rash cannot be spread to another child, and it does not spread from blister serum or from scratching
B) The rash is contagious until every lesion has crusted over
C) Scratching spreads the rash to new sites, so the blisters must stay covered
D) The blister fluid contains urushiol and will spread the rash on the child's own skin
Answer: A
Rationale: "The rash that results from poison ivy cannot be spread to another child; only direct contact with the oil can cause the response," and "Although the lesions do not spread by contact with the blister serum or from scratching, they can become secondarily infected" (p. 1223). The lesions "dry and heal spontaneously, and itching stops by 10 to 14 days."
Trap: (C) and (D) are the two halves of the same near-universal belief, and the chapter denies both in one sentence while keeping the one real risk it leaves standing — scratching does not spread the rash, but it can infect it.

Topic: arthropod bites stings and tickborne disease in children
Subtopic: stings and venomous arachnids

Q: A child is stung by a bee and the stinger is visible in the skin. What is the first action?
A) Remove the stinger as quickly as possible
B) Apply a cool compress and observe for 10 minutes before touching the site
C) Cover the stinger with a baking soda paste to neutralize the venom
D) Give an oral antihistamine and then remove the stinger
Answer: A
Rationale: "As long as the stinger remains in the skin, the muscles push the stinger deeper, and the venom is pumped into the wound. The best approach is to remove the stinger as quickly as possible and to get away from the vicinity of other insects to prevent further injury" (p. 1225). The treatment column leads with "Carefully scrape off stinger or pull out stinger as quickly as possible" (p. 1224).
Trap: (C) and (D) are both genuine later steps — cleansing, cool compresses, a household paste of lemon juice, aspirin, or baking soda, then antihistamines — and each delays the one action that stops venom from continuing to be injected. The table does not prefer scraping to pulling; it prefers speed.

Q: A camp nurse is teaching counselors about arachnids. Which ones require immediate medical attention? Select all that apply.
A) Scorpion
B) Black widow spider
C) Brown recluse spider
D) Tarantula
E) Chigger
Answer: A, B, C
Rationale: "Only scorpions and two spiders, the brown recluse and the black widow, inject venom deadly enough to require immediate attention. Children bitten by these arachnids must receive medical attention as soon as possible" (p. 1224).
Trap: (D) is the wrong option most often selected, because a tarantula is the most alarming of the five to look at, and the chapter names it specifically in order to exclude it: "Most arthropods in the United States, including tarantulas, are relatively harmless" (p. 1224). Chiggers cause "Erythematous papules" and intense itching and nothing worse.

Q: A child is bitten by a spider bearing a fiddle-shaped mark on its head. Three days later the site shows a purple, star-shaped area. What should the nurse expect?
A) A necrotic ulcer over the next one to two weeks, managed with antibiotics and corticosteroids
B) Antivenin, which is the specific treatment for this spider's necrotoxin
C) Rapid dizziness, weakness, and abdominal pain from a neurotoxin
D) Complete resolution within 48 hours using cool compresses alone
Answer: A
Rationale: The brown recluse is "recognized by fiddle-shaped mark on head," and its venom "contains powerful necrotoxin." The course is "mild to severe pain in 2-8 hours; purple, star-shaped area in 3-4 days; necrotic ulceration in 7-14 days," treated with cool compresses, "antibiotics, corticosteroids," pain relief, and sometimes a skin graft; "Wounds heal with scar formation" (p. 1225).
Trap: (B) and (C) both belong to the black widow, which carries "a red or orange hourglass-shaped marking on underside," whose venom "has neurotoxic action," and for whom antivenin is the treatment (p. 1225). Neuro belongs to one spider and necrosis to the other, and the antivenin follows the neurotoxin.

Subtopic: ticks and Lyme disease

Q: A parent finds an attached tick on a child's scalp. What should the nurse instruct?
A) Grasp the tick with tweezers as close to the skin as possible and pull straight up with steady, even pressure
B) Cover the tick with petroleum jelly so that it backs out on its own
C) Twist the tick counterclockwise until it releases
D) Take the child to the emergency department, since only a clinician should remove a tick
Answer: A
Rationale: "Grasp tick with tweezers (forceps) as close as possible to point of attachment"; "Pull straight up with steady, even pressure"; and if bare hands must be used, "use a tissue to touch tick during removal; wash hands thoroughly with soap and water" (p. 1225). Afterward, "wash the bite area with an iodine scrub, rubbing alcohol, or plain soap and water" (p. 1229).
Trap: (B) is the best-known home method, and the chapter rejects it by name: "The application of nail polish or petroleum jelly is not recommended and does not appear to have an effect on tick withdrawal as has been hypothesized" (p. 1229). Speed is what matters, because a tick "Must attach and feed for 1-2 hours to transmit disease" (p. 1225).

Q: A 9-year-old who camped two weeks ago has an expanding bull's-eye lesion on the thigh with fever and headache. What should the nurse anticipate?
A) Oral doxycycline
B) Oral amoxicillin, since this is a school-age child
C) A Lyme serologic titer drawn at the time of the tick bite
D) Intravenous ceftriaxone for early localized disease
Answer: A
Rationale: "Children older than 8 years of age are treated with oral doxycycline," while "amoxicillin is recommended for children younger than 8 years of age" (p. 1229). The lesion described is erythema migrans, which develops "in 3 to 30 days" at the bite site as "a large circumferential ring with a raised, edematous doughnut-like border resulting in a bull's-eye appearance" (p. 1228).
Trap: (B) is the right answer for a 7-year-old and wrong here by a single year, which is the entire point of the age rule. (D) borrows a late-disease drug, since "Neurologic, cardiac, and arthritic manifestations are managed with oral or intravenous antibiotics, such as ceftriaxone, cefotaxime, or penicillin G" (p. 1229). (C) fails on timing: serologic testing at the time of a recognized bite "is not recommended because antibodies are not detectable in most persons."

Topic: scabies and pediculosis capitis in children
Subtopic: scabies

Q: A 4-year-old is diagnosed with scabies and her 6-year-old brother with head lice. Which statement about their treatments is correct?
A) Scabies is treated with permethrin 5% cream and head lice with permethrin 1% cream rinse
B) Both are treated with permethrin 1% cream rinse
C) Scabies is treated with permethrin 1% cream rinse and head lice with permethrin 5% cream
D) Both are treated with lindane, the drug of choice in young children
Answer: A
Rationale: For scabies, "The drug of choice in children and infants older than 2 months of age is permethrin 5% cream (Elimite)" (p. 1226). For head lice, "The drug of choice for infants and children is permethrin 1% cream rinse (Nix), which kills adult lice and nits" (p. 1227).
Trap: (C) is the swap this material sets up by using one drug at two strengths in two vehicles for two infestations, and it is the cleanest error available here. (D) names a drug the chapter refuses outright: "Lindane can be neurotoxic and is contraindicated by the American Academy of Pediatrics" (p. 1226).

Q: The parents of a child with scabies ask why the entire household needs treatment when no one else has a rash. What is the best explanation?
A) It takes 30 to 60 days for symptoms to appear, so close contacts may already be infested without knowing it
B) The mite survives in bedding for months, so everyone must be treated to clear the house
C) The medication prevents infestation in people who have not been exposed
D) Only those with visible burrows need treatment; the others can wait and see
Answer: A
Rationale: "Because of the length of time between infestation and physical symptoms (30 to 60 days), all persons who were in close contact with the affected child need treatment," which "may include boyfriends or girlfriends, babysitters, grandparents, and immediate family members." "The objective is to treat as thoroughly as possible the first time" (p. 1226).
Trap: (D) is the reasonable-sounding half measure that guarantees reinfestation, since the people who look clear today are the ones who will break out next month. (B) misplaces the mite, which burrows "into the stratum corneum of the epidermis (never into living tissue)" on a host to lay its eggs.

Subtopic: head lice

Q: A school nurse examining a child's scalp finds tiny whitish oval specks near the occiput that do not brush off, sitting about 4 mm from the scalp. How should this be interpreted?
A) Viable nits, since they adhere to the hair shaft and sit within 6 mm of the scalp
B) Dandruff, which is white and sits close to the scalp
C) Empty nit cases, since hatched nits remain near the scalp
D) Hair spray residue, which cannot be told from nits without a microscope
Answer: A
Rationale: "The nits, or eggs, appear as tiny whitish oval specks adhering to the hair shaft about 6 mm (0.25 inch) from the scalp," and "The adherent nature of the nits distinguishes them from dandruff, which falls off readily." "Empty nit cases, indicating hatched lice, are translucent rather than white and are located more than 6 mm from the scalp" (p. 1226). The female "lays her eggs at night at the junction of a hair shaft and close to the skin because the eggs need a warm environment."
Trap: (C) inverts both discriminators at once, since the hatched cases are the ones farther out and the translucent ones. (B) fails on the single bedside test that settles it, which is whether the specks come away.

Q: A parent finishes the permethrin 1% treatment for head lice and asks what comes next. Which instruction is correct?
A) Repeat the treatment in 7 to 10 days, because nits shed into the environment can hatch in that time
B) No repeat is needed, since permethrin kills adult lice and nits
C) Repeat the treatment daily for a week
D) Spray the house with insecticide to prevent reinfestation
Answer: A
Rationale: "Most experts advise a second treatment at 7 to 10 days to ensure a cure" (p. 1227), and the interval matches the life cycle: "nits are shed into the environment and are capable of hatching in 7 to 10 days; retreatment is required." Daily combing with a metal nit comb is a control measure after treatment.
Trap: (B) quotes the drug's own description accurately — permethrin "kills adult lice and nits" (p. 1227) — and still reaches the wrong conclusion, because "none of the pediculicides is 100% effective in killing all the eggs." (D) is refused by name: "Spraying with insecticide is not recommended because of the danger to children and animals."

Q: Which statements should the nurse include when teaching a family about head lice? Select all that apply.
A) Anyone can get head lice, regardless of age, socioeconomic level, or cleanliness
B) Lice do not jump or fly; they are transmitted on personal items
C) Lice are not carried or transmitted by pets
D) Wash bedding and clothing in water over 130 degrees Fahrenheit and dry on hot for at least 20 minutes
E) Coating the hair with mayonnaise or petroleum jelly will smother the lice
Answer: A, B, C, D
Rationale: "Nurses should emphasize that anyone can get pediculosis; it has no respect for age, gender, socioeconomic level, or cleanliness"; "Lice do not jump or fly, but they can be transmitted from one person to another on personal items"; and "Lice are not carried or transmitted by pets" (pp. 1226, 1227). The prevention box directs families to "Machine wash all washable clothing, towels, and bed linens in water greater than 130" degrees Fahrenheit "and dry them in a hot dryer for at least 20 minutes."
Trap: (E) is the wrong option and it names the remedies families have most often already tried: of petroleum jelly, oils, vinegar, butter, alcohol, and mayonnaise, "there is no evidence demonstrating the effectiveness of these remedies" (p. 1227).

Topic: diaper dermatitis in infants
Subtopic: prevention and skin care

Q: Which interventions can the nurse safely recommend for a 10-month-old with diaper dermatitis? Select all that apply.
A) Apply a zinc oxide or petrolatum ointment to protect the skin
B) Change the diaper as soon as it becomes wet
C) Expose the skin to air to dry
D) Use a hair dryer on a low setting to dry the area
E) Switch the family to cloth diapers only
Answer: A, B, C
Rationale: The Family-Centered Care box directs the family to "Apply ointment, such as zinc oxide or petrolatum, to protect skin, especially if skin is very red or has moist, open areas," and to "Expose healthy or only slightly irritated skin to air, not heat, to dry completely" (p. 1233). "Changing the diaper as soon as it becomes wet eliminates a large part of the problem," because "The most significant factor amenable to intervention is the moist environment created in the diaper area" (p. 1232).
Trap: (D) is unsafe and is named: "The use of a hair dryer or heat lamp is not recommended because these devices can cause burns" (p. 1232). (E) moves the family the wrong way, since superabsorbent disposable diapers reduce diaper dermatitis by binding water, buffering pH, and keeping urine and feces from mixing (pp. 1232–1233), and inadequately rinsed cloth diapers are themselves listed as a source of soap and detergent irritation.

Q: A 9-month-old has a beefy red rash involving the inguinal folds with scattered satellite lesions, unchanged after two weeks of barrier ointment. What does this suggest?
A) Candida albicans infection, which involves the folds and produces satellite lesions
B) Irritant diaper dermatitis, which is worst inside the skin folds
C) An allergic reaction to the barrier ointment
D) Contact dermatitis from the diaper wipes
Answer: A
Rationale: "Candida albicans infection produces perianal inflammation and a maculopapular rash with satellite lesions that may cross the inguinal fold," whereas an eruption on "the convex surfaces of buttocks, inner thighs, mons pubis, and scrotum" but "sparing the folds" is chemical irritation from urine and feces (p. 1232). Yeast "is seen in up to 90% of infants with chronic diaper dermatitis and should be considered in diaper rashes that are recalcitrant to treatment."
Trap: (B) attaches the right disease name to the wrong distribution, and distribution is the whole discriminator here: folds spared means irritant, folds involved with satellites means yeast. The two-week failure to respond is a second clue pointing the same way.

Q: A parent asks whether cornstarch can be used in the diaper area, having heard that it feeds yeast. Which response is accurate?
A) Cornstarch does not promote fungal growth, and it is preferred over talc because of inhalation safety
B) Cornstarch does promote candidal growth and should be avoided
C) Talcum powder is the safer choice for infants
D) Powders of any kind are equally safe and may be shaken directly over the infant
Answer: A
Rationale: "A common misconception about using cornstarch on skin is that it promotes the growth of C. albicans. Neither cornstarch nor talcum powder promotes the growth of fungi under conditions normally found in the diaper area. On the basis of safety in terms of inhalation injury, cornstarch is the preferred product. Talcum powder should not be used" (p. 1233).
Trap: (D) drops the technique that is the entire inhalation precaution: "first shake it into your hand and then apply it to the diaper area" (p. 1233), never over the baby, and keep the container closed and out of reach. (C) reverses the safety comparison the chapter draws.

Topic: atopic dermatitis and seborrheic dermatitis in children
Subtopic: managing atopic dermatitis

Q: A parent of a 4-month-old with atopic dermatitis says she has stopped bathing the baby because baths seem to dry the skin further. What should the nurse teach?
A) Bathing is appropriate, but an emollient must go on within 3 minutes afterward, while the skin is still damp
B) Baths should be limited to once a week for a child with eczema
C) Bubble bath should be added to soften the water
D) The skin should be allowed to air dry completely before any product is applied
Answer: A
Rationale: "A tepid bath with a mild soap (Dove or Neutrogena), no soap, or an emulsifying oil followed immediately by application of an emollient (within 3 minutes) assists in trapping moisture and preventing its loss," and the caution is exact: "excessive bathing without emollient application only dries out the skin" (p. 1234). The Nursing Alert repeats it: the emollient goes on "immediately after bathing (while the skin is still slightly moist) to prevent drying" (p. 1235).
Trap: (D) is the intuitive error and it is precisely what makes a bath harmful in this disease, because the water evaporates before anything traps it. (C) names an irritant the chapter tells families to avoid in three separate places (pp. 1212, 1222, 1234).

Q: A nurse compares a 5-month-old and a 5-year-old, both with atopic dermatitis. Which distribution should the nurse expect?
A) Face, scalp, neck, and extensor surfaces in the infant; flexural areas, wrists, and ankles in the older child
B) Flexural areas in the infant; extensor surfaces in the older child
C) The same distribution in both, since age does not change the pattern
D) Palms and soles in both, which is the hallmark of atopic dermatitis
Answer: A
Rationale: Box 32.3 gives the infantile form as "Generalized, especially face, scalp, neck, and extensor surfaces of extremities" and the childhood form as "Flexural areas (antecubital and popliteal fossae, neck), wrists, ankles, and feet" (p. 1233).
Trap: (B) is the mirror error and the cleanest level swap in this section, since the two surfaces are exact opposites and the only variable separating them is the child's age. The appearance shifts with the distribution: erythema, vesicles, weeping, and crusting in infancy, and lichenification with dry, hyperpigmented patches later.

Q: Which measures are appropriate in managing a school-age child's atopic dermatitis? Select all that apply.
A) A mildly sedating antihistamine at night, because pruritus increases at night
B) Keeping fingernails cut short, clean, and filed frequently
C) Laundering clothes in a mild detergent and running a second wash cycle with no detergent
D) Reporting honey-colored crusts or pustules with surrounding erythema
E) Applying one thick layer of the topical medication rather than several thin ones
Answer: A, B, C, D
Rationale: "Because pruritus increases at night, a mildly sedating antihistamine may be needed," while nonsedating agents "may be preferred for daytime pruritus relief" (p. 1234). "Fingernails and toenails are cut short, kept clean, and filed frequently to prevent sharp edges" (p. 1234). "Putting the clothes through a second complete wash cycle without using detergent reduces the amount of residue remaining in the fabric" (p. 1235). And lesions are examined for infection, "usually honey-colored crusts or pustules with surrounding erythema. Any signs of infection are reported to the practitioner" (p. 1235).
Trap: (E) is the wrong option and the source names it as the misconception to correct: "one thick application of topical medication is not equivalent to several thin applications and that excessive use of an agent (particularly steroids) can be hazardous" (p. 1235).

Subtopic: seborrheic dermatitis

Q: A mother brings her 6-week-old with thick, adherent, yellowish, oily scales on the scalp. She rinses the hair but avoids shampooing because she is afraid of hurting the soft spot. Which response is best?
A) Explain that the fontanel does not puncture or tear with mild pressure, and demonstrate shampooing
B) Reassure her that avoiding the scalp is appropriate until the fontanel closes
C) Tell her a medicated antiseborrheic shampoo is required
D) Advise her to comb the dry crusts off before shampooing
Answer: A
Rationale: "Cradle cap may be prevented with adequate scalp hygiene. Frequently, parents omit shampooing the infant's hair for fear of damaging the soft spots, or fontanels. The nurse should discuss how to shampoo the infant's hair and emphasize that the fontanel is similar to skin anywhere else on the body, it does not puncture or tear with mild pressure," and education "may need to include a demonstration" (p. 1235).
Trap: (C) overtreats a condition the chapter handles simply: "Shampooing should be done daily with a mild soap or commercial baby shampoo; medicated shampoos are not necessary" (p. 1235). (D) reverses the technique, since the shampoo is "allowed to remain on the scalp until the crusts soften" and only then does a fine-tooth comb or soft facial brush lift them.

Topic: acne management in adolescents
Subtopic: skin care and myths

Q: A 15-year-old with acne says he has started scrubbing his face four times a day with an abrasive antibacterial soap, having read that acne is caused by dirt. How should the nurse respond?
A) Surface dirt does not cause acne, and vigorous scrubbing with antibacterial soap damages the skin
B) Scrubbing is correct, but twice a day is enough
C) Antibacterial soap is the most effective first-line acne treatment
D) He should add an abrasive cleanser for better exfoliation
Answer: A
Rationale: "Dirt or oil on the surface of the skin does not cause acne" (p. 1236). "Gentle cleansing with a mild cleanser once or twice daily is usually sufficient. Antibacterial soaps are ineffective and may be drying when used in combination with topical acne medications," and adolescents need education about what damages the skin, "such as too vigorous scrubbing" (pp. 1236–1237).
Trap: (B) corrects the frequency while endorsing the method, leaving the damaging part in place — the problem is the scrubbing and the product, not the count. (C) elevates an ineffective product over benzoyl peroxide, which "is effective against both inflammatory and noninflammatory acne and is an effective first-line agent."

Q: An adolescent starting tretinoin asks when to apply it. Which instruction is correct?
A) Apply it at night, and use a sunscreen of at least SPF 15 during the day
B) Apply it in the morning, when oil production is greatest
C) Apply it immediately after washing the face, to aid absorption
D) Apply it at night, and skip sunscreen, which clogs the pores
Answer: A
Rationale: "The avoidance of the sun and the daily use of sunscreen must be emphasized because sun exposure can result in severe sunburn. Adolescents should be advised to apply the medication at night and to use a sunscreen with a sun protection factor (SPF) of at least 15 in the daytime" (p. 1236).
Trap: (C) takes a real detail from the same paragraph and reverses it: "The medication should not be applied for at least 20 to 30 minutes after washing to decrease the burning sensation" (p. 1236). (D) keeps the correct timing and drops the half that prevents the injury. Whatever the regimen, "improvement may not be apparent for at least 6 weeks."

Q: A 17-year-old girl is starting isotretinoin for severe cystic acne. Which statement by the adolescent indicates that teaching has been effective?
A) I have to use effective contraception during treatment and for a month after I finish
B) I can stop my contraception as soon as I take the last dose
C) The main thing to watch for is bleaching of my clothes
D) My skin should clear within about a week
Answer: A
Rationale: "The most significant side effects of this drug are the teratogenic effects. Isotretinoin is absolutely contraindicated in pregnant women," and "Sexually active young women must use an effective contraceptive method during treatment and for 1 month after treatment" (p. 1237). Treatment runs 20 weeks, and the adolescent is also monitored "for depression, depressive symptoms, and suicidal ideation" and for elevated cholesterol and triglyceride levels.
Trap: (B) drops the trailing month, which is the detail most often lost because it sounds administrative rather than teratogenic. (C) belongs to a different drug — benzoyl peroxide bleaches "sheets, bedclothes, and towels," and "The adolescent can be reassured that skin bleaching will not occur" (p. 1236) — and (D) contradicts the timeline shared by every acne drug in the chapter.
