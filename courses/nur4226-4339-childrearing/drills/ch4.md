# Chapter 4 drills — Communication, Physical, and Developmental Assessment of the Child and Family

Authored from `sources/Ch4_Fact_Bank.md` (Hockenberry, Wilson & Rodgers, 11th ed.,
printed pp. 80–136).

Blueprint weight is **7 questions of 202 (3.5%), depth `standard`** — 45 drills
across 10 topics, at the top of the 30–45 band.

The fact bank's framing is the one this set follows: **this is really two chapters
bolted together**, and the item bank tests them differently. The communication
half is tested on **age-specific technique** — what you do differently for a
toddler than for an adolescent — and on **named lists**. The assessment half is
tested on **measurement rules that have a right and a wrong answer**: cuff size,
temperature route, apical versus radial pulse, BMI cutoffs, screening ages. The
split here is roughly even, and every number drilled is one the bank lists as
worth memorizing.

The chapter's most productive shape is the **direction-of-error pair**: a rule
whose two failure modes point opposite ways and are trivially swapped. Cuff too
small reads falsely high and too large falsely low; physiologic splitting widens
on inspiration and fixed splitting does not; too small a temperature route
restriction is age-gated in two directions at once (rectal contraindicated under
1 month, oral under 5 years). Those are drilled from both ends.

**What a `standard` band could not buy.** The fact bank itself excludes the
organ-by-organ examination findings on pp. 111–132 — the funduscopic, otoscopic,
mouth and throat, abdominal, genitalia, back, extremity, and cranial-nerve
sequences — keeping only the rules that discriminate. This drill set inherits
that boundary and adds two of its own: **Table 4.10's murmur grading** is
recorded as an open GAP in the bank and is therefore not drilled at all, and the
**nutritional assessment detail** (24-hour recall, food-frequency method, the
deficiency-sign matrix) is left to a nutrition chapter. Telephone triage, the
family assessment structural and functional areas, and the Ages and Stages
questionnaire ranges are each represented by nothing or by a single trap
reference — they are named lists with no invertible pair inside them.

Topic: therapeutic communication with families
Subtopic: listening, silence, and blocks

Q: A nurse tells a mother, "I know exactly how you feel — I was terrified when my own son was admitted." How should this response be classified?
A) Empathy, because the nurse is sharing a comparable experience
B) Sympathy — having feelings or emotions similar to those of another person, rather than understanding those feelings
C) A facilitative response
D) Anticipatory guidance
Answer: B
Rationale: Sympathy is "having feelings or emotions similar to those of another person, rather than understanding those feelings," while empathy is "the capacity to understand what another person is experiencing from within that person's frame of reference; it is often described as the ability to put oneself in another's shoes" (p. 82).
Trap: (A) is the reflex pick, and the book defines the two terms against each other on the same page, which is the shape of the item. The discriminator is whose frame of reference is being used: empathy enters the parent's, while sympathy substitutes the nurse's own.

Q: A nurse asks a father about his son's eating, then interrupts to finish his sentence, offers reassurance before he has described the problem, and moves on to the next question. Which behaviors from Box 4.2 does this show? (Select all that apply.)
A) Interrupting and finishing the person's sentence
B) Offering premature or inappropriate reassurance
C) Deliberately changing the focus
D) Constant fidgeting or attempting to move away
E) Frequently looking at a watch or clock
Answer: A, B, C
Rationale: Box 4.2 is two lists, and the split between them is the point: communication barriers are what the **nurse** does wrong, and signs of information overload are how the **patient** signals it (p. 82).
Trap: (D) and (E) are the two to reject, and both are real entries from the same box — they belong to the patient-overload column, not the nurse-barrier one. An option can be a genuine item from the printed box and still be the wrong half of it. Note the correction the chapter names for the nurse side: "One of the best methods for improving interviewing skills is audiotape or videotape feedback."

Q: A mother falls silent partway through describing her son's behavior problems. What should the nurse do?
A) Introduce a new topic to relieve the discomfort
B) Allow the silence, which permits the interviewee to sort out thoughts and feelings and search for responses, and can also be a cue for the interviewer to go more slowly
C) Fill the pause by summarizing what has been discussed so far
D) End the interview, since the mother has finished
Answer: B
Rationale: Silence "permits the interviewee to sort out thoughts and feelings and search for responses to questions. Silence can also be a cue for the interviewer to go more slowly," and "Breaking a silence by introducing a new topic or by prolonged talking essentially terminates the interviewee's opportunity to use the silence" (p. 82).
Trap: (A) and (C) both fill the pause, which is the instinct the chapter names and rejects. When a silence does need breaking, the chapter's example is a question that keeps the floor with the parent: "Is there anything else you wish to say?"

Subtopic: working through an interpreter

Q: A nurse is interviewing a family through a hospital interpreter. Which actions are correct? (Select all that apply.)
A) Communicate directly with family members when asking questions, without ignoring the interpreter
B) Pose questions to elicit only one answer at a time
C) Refrain from interrupting family members and the interpreter while they are conversing
D) Arrange for the family to speak with the same interpreter on subsequent visits whenever possible
E) Ask the interpreter privately what she thinks of the family's understanding while the family is present
Answer: A, B, C, D
Rationale: The Nursing Care Guidelines direct the nurse to explain the purpose to the interpreter beforehand, introduce the interpreter and allow time for acquaintance, communicate directly with family members, ask one-answer questions ("Do you have pain?" rather than "Do you have any pain, tiredness, or loss of appetite?"), avoid interrupting, avoid medical jargon, and use the same interpreter on later visits (p. 83).
Trap: (E) is the one to reject, and the chapter gives the reason plainly: "Avoid commenting to the interpreter about family members because they may understand some English."

Q: A 12-year-old is fluent in English and offers to interpret for her non-English-speaking mother during a history. What should the nurse do?
A) Accept, since the child is willing and fluent
B) Decline; the use of children in health care interpreting is strongly discouraged, and use a professional interpreter or a telephonic language line instead
C) Accept for the history but not for the physical examination
D) Accept if the mother consents
Answer: B
Rationale: "the use of children in health care interpreting is strongly discouraged because they are often not mature enough to understand health care questions, answers, or messages." Children "may inadvertently commit interpretive errors, such as inaccuracies, omissions, or substitutions," "can be adversely affected by serious or sensitive information," and in some cultures using a child "is considered an insult to an adult because children are expected to show respect by not questioning their elders." Some institutions prohibit it outright; the alternative named is "a language line using a telephonic interpreter" (p. 83).
Trap: (D) is the most defensible-sounding wrong answer, and it misplaces the objection. The problem is not consent — it is accuracy, the burden placed on the child, and in some cultures the insult to the adult.

Topic: age-specific communication with children

Q: A nurse enters an examination room and immediately kneels in front of a shy 3-year-old, smiles broadly, makes eye contact, and asks him about his toy. What is the likely effect?
A) The child will feel welcomed and relax
B) Anxiety will increase — active attempts to make friends with children before they have had an opportunity to evaluate an unfamiliar person tend to increase their anxiety
C) The approach is correct because the nurse is at the child's level
D) The effect depends entirely on the child's temperament
Answer: B
Rationale: "Active attempts to make friends with children before they have had an opportunity to evaluate an unfamiliar person tend to increase their anxiety," and the guidelines direct the nurse to "Avoid sudden or rapid advances, broad smiles, extended eye contact, or other gestures that may be seen as threatening" (pp. 83–84). The move is to keep talking to the parent while doing something that does not involve the child.
Trap: (C) names a real guideline — "Assume a position that is at the same level as the child" — and applies it at the wrong moment. Being at eye level is right; arriving there before the child has had time to evaluate you is not. Broad smiles and extended eye contact appear on the chapter's list of potentially **threatening** gestures, not reassuring ones.

Q: A 4-year-old is told she will feel "a little stick in the arm." She becomes distressed and looks for a stick. What accounts for this?
A) She is developmentally delayed
B) Children in early childhood interpret words literally, and the chapter uses this exact example
C) She is exhibiting regression from the stress of hospitalization
D) The explanation was given too far in advance
Answer: B
Rationale: Children younger than 5 "interpret words literally" and attach literal meaning to phrases such as "two-faced," "sticky fingers," or "coughing your head off"; a child told they will get "a little stick in the arm" may not envision an injection (p. 84). The related instruction under vital signs is to "avoid using the word 'take' when measuring vital signs because young children interpret words literally and may think that their temperature or other function will be taken away."
Trap: (A) reads normal preoperational thinking as a deficit. The same egocentrism means the chapter says it is "futile to use another child's experience in an attempt to gain the cooperation of small children" — focus communication on the child in front of you.

Q: A 9-year-old is scheduled for a procedure and keeps asking why it is being done and how the equipment works. How should the nurse respond?
A) Redirect the questions, since detailed explanations increase anxiety at this age
B) Give explanations and reasons, since school-age children want them and are interested in the functional aspect of all procedures — and they need to know what is going to take place and why it is being done to them specifically
C) Answer only through a doll or puppet
D) Defer all explanations to the parent
Answer: B
Rationale: School-age children "rely less on what they see and more on what they know," "want explanations and reasons for everything but require no verification beyond that," "are interested in the functional aspect of all procedures," and "need to know what is going to take place and why it is being done to them specifically" (p. 85).
Trap: (C) is the technique for a *young* child — communicating through transition objects such as dolls, puppets, and stuffed animals before questioning directly — applied several stages too late. Note the school-age child's other feature: "a heightened concern about body integrity" that extends to possessions, so they "may appear to overreact to loss or threatened loss of treasured objects."

Q: A 15-year-old who has been managing her own diabetes for years becomes tearful and asks her mother to stay during a painful procedure. How should the nurse interpret this?
A) As regression requiring referral
B) As normal: adolescents fluctuate between child and adult thinking and behavior, and when tensions rise they may seek the security of the more familiar and comfortable expectations of childhood
C) As evidence she is not ready to self-manage her condition
D) As manipulation to avoid the procedure
Answer: B
Rationale: Adolescents "fluctuate between child and adult thinking and behavior," and "when tensions rise, they may seek the security of the more familiar and comfortable expectations of childhood." The chapter states the balance directly: "It is as much a mistake to regard an adolescent as an adult with an adult's wisdom and control as it is to assume that the teenager has the concerns and expectations of a child" (p. 85).
Trap: (C) draws a durable conclusion from a momentary shift, which is exactly the error the chapter's two-sided warning is written against. Note also that privacy and confidentiality are "of great importance" at this age "because it is consistent with developmental maturity and autonomy."

Q: A nurse is beginning a visit with a 16-year-old and his mother, and time is limited. What should the nurse do?
A) Interview the parent first in every case, since the parent is the informant
B) Interview the adolescent first in every case
C) Either order is defensible — talking with the adolescent first immediately identifies with the young person, and talking with the parents first may provide insight into the family relationship — but give both parties an opportunity to be included, and state at the onset that time is limited to avoid appearing to take sides
D) Interview them together only, so nothing is concealed
Answer: C
Rationale: Talking with the adolescent first "has the advantage of immediately identifying with the young person"; talking with the parents first "may provide insight into the family relationship." Either way, "give both parties an opportunity to be included," and if time is limited say so at the onset "to avoid appearing to 'take sides'" (p. 85).
Trap: (D) forecloses the private conversation the chapter treats as essential — older children should be given "the opportunity to talk without parents present," and nurses must know state and federal consent and confidentiality laws for suspected abuse, substance use, suicidal or homicidal ideation, contraceptive care, pregnancy, sexually transmitted infections, and sexual assault.

Topic: creative communication techniques and drawings

Q: A nurse says to an adolescent, "You are being uncooperative about doing your treatments." Which technique should replace this, and why?
A) A third-person statement, because it is less threatening
B) An I-message: "I am concerned about how the treatments are going because I want to see you get better" — because you-messages are judgmental and provoke defensiveness
C) A facilitative response
D) Mutual storytelling
Answer: B
Rationale: The technique is to "Relate a feeling about a behavior in terms of 'I.'... Avoid use of 'you.' 'You' messages are judgmental and provoke defensiveness," and the chapter gives this exact contrast (p. 85).
Trap: (A) names a different real technique for a different purpose. The third-person technique expresses a feeling as "he," "she," or "they," which "allows children three choices: (1) to agree and, one hopes, express how they feel; (2) to disagree; or (3) to remain silent" — useful for eliciting, not for correcting a behavior.

Q: A hospitalized child says she is scared the other children will laugh at her scar. The nurse replies, "You feel worried because you think the other kids will notice." Which technique is this?
A) Facilitative response
B) Bibliotherapy
C) Storytelling
D) Sentence completion
Answer: A
Rationale: A facilitative response reflects feelings and content back: "Listen carefully and reflect back to patients the feelings and content of their statements," with the formula "You feel _________ because _________." Such responses "are empathic and nonjudgmental and legitimize the person's feelings" (p. 85).
Trap: (C) is the nearest relative and works the other direction. Storytelling uses "the language of children to probe into areas of their thinking while bypassing conscious inhibitions or fears"; mutual storytelling goes further, with the nurse retelling the child's story with therapeutic differences, and the chapter calls it the "more therapeutic approach than storytelling."

Q: A nurse is evaluating a child's drawing of her family. Which principles apply? (Select all that apply.)
A) Use spontaneous drawings and evaluate more than one drawing whenever possible
B) Interpret the drawing as a whole rather than focusing on specific details
C) Size expresses importance, power, or authority
D) Exclusion of a family member may denote a feeling of not belonging or a desire to eliminate
E) A single drawing is sufficient to establish a diagnosis of family dysfunction
Answer: A, B, C, D
Rationale: The guidelines for evaluating drawings state each of the keyed items, and add that the order drawn "expresses priority in terms of importance," the child's position relative to others "expresses feelings of status or alliance," and erasures, shading, or cross-hatching "expresses ambivalence, concern, or anxiety" (p. 87).
Trap: (E) is the one to reject and it contradicts the first two principles at once — more than one drawing, and the whole rather than the detail. Drawing is called "one of the most valuable forms of communication — both nonverbal (from looking at the drawing) and verbal (from the child's story of the picture)," and the verbal half is why a single image is not read as a finding.

Topic: pediatric health history taking

Q: A clinic is short on time and considers replacing the interview with a written questionnaire the parent completes. What does the chapter say?
A) The indirect method is preferred because it is more complete
B) The direct method is superior; if the indirect route must be used, review the parents' written responses and question them regarding any unusual answers
C) The two methods are equivalent
D) The indirect method should be used for all initial visits
Answer: B
Rationale: The history format may be direct, "in which the nurse asks for information via direct interview with the informant," or indirect, "in which the informant supplies the information by completing some type of questionnaire. The direct method is superior" (p. 88).
Trap: (A) inverts the chapter's ranking. Note the related Nursing Alert if translated forms are used: "be certain the informant is literate in the foreign language" — a written form assumes a literacy the interview does not.

Q: A nurse begins a visit by asking, "How is your son sick?" How should this opening be evaluated?
A) Appropriate, because it is open ended
B) It is a labeling-type question and should be avoided; elicit the chief complaint with open-ended, neutral questions such as "What seems to be the matter?" or "Why did you come here today?"
C) Appropriate only for a well-child visit
D) It is too broad and should be replaced with a closed-ended question
Answer: B
Rationale: Elicit the chief complaint with "open-ended, neutral questions (e.g., 'What seems to be the matter?,' 'How may I help you?,' or 'Why did you come here today?'). Avoid labeling-type questions (e.g., 'How are you sick?' or 'What is the problem?')" (p. 88). If the parent names many problems, ask which one prompted them to seek help now.
Trap: (D) moves in the wrong direction. Closed-ended questions are named among the blocks to communication — "limiting expression of emotion by asking directed, closed-ended questions." The fault in the stem's question is the label it plants, not its breadth.

Q: A 7-year-old reports abdominal pain. Which approach best establishes the location?
A) Accept the description "stomach pains" and document it
B) Have the child point with one finger to where it hurts, or point to where a parent would put a bandage — and for radiation, ask the child to show with a finger where the pain goes
C) Ask the parent to indicate the location on the child's body
D) Palpate each quadrant and note where the child withdraws
Answer: B
Rationale: "'Stomach pains' is too general." Have the child "point with one finger to where it hurts" or "point to where mommy or daddy would put a Band-Aid," and for radiation: "Does the pain stay there or move? Show me with your finger where the pain goes" (p. 89). Type, location, severity, duration, and influencing factors are the five components of symptom analysis; for type, "Record whatever words they use in quotes."
Trap: (A) is the failure the chapter names by name, and it is what makes location a technique rather than a question. Note that severity is "best determined by finding out how it affects the child's usual behavior" — pain that prevents playing, interacting, sleeping, and eating is most often severe.

Q: A parent reports that her child walked without assistance at 6 months. How should the nurse interpret this?
A) As evidence of advanced motor development, to be documented as reported
B) As a clue to misunderstanding of the requested activity, prompting a more specific question
C) As a reason to refer for developmental evaluation
D) As unreliable recall that should be omitted from the record
Answer: B
Rationale: The chapter directs the use of specific questions, because "'sitting up' can mean many different activities," and "A clue to misunderstanding of the requested activity may be an unusually early age of achievement" (p. 90).
Trap: (A) is the reflex reading of an impressive milestone, and it is exactly backwards — an implausibly early age is more likely to mean the parent and nurse are describing different behaviors than that the child was precocious.

Q: A nurse is taking a sexual history from a 16-year-old. Which approach does the chapter recommend?
A) Ask whether the adolescent is "sexually active," since it is the standard clinical term
B) Ask "Are you having sex with anyone?" — probably the most direct and best understood question — and refer to all sexual contacts in nongender terms, such as "partners"
C) Ask the parent to leave and then ask about girlfriends or boyfriends by name
D) Defer sexual history to the provider
Answer: B
Rationale: "avoid asking whether the adolescent is 'sexually active' because this term is broadly defined. 'Are you having sex with anyone?' is probably the most direct and best understood question." Because same-sex experimentation may occur, "refer to all sexual contacts in nongender terms, such as 'partners,' rather than 'girlfriends' or 'boyfriends'" (pp. 90–91).
Trap: (A) is the clinical habit the chapter singles out as too vague to yield an answer. (C) gets the privacy half right and then reintroduces the gendered assumption. Compare the chemical-use phrasing on the same pages, which works the same way: "Many kids your age are experimenting with drugs and alcohol; have you ever had any drugs or alcohol?" yields more reliable data than "How much do you drink?"

Topic: age-specific approaches to physical examination

Q: A nurse examines a quiet 4-month-old in the parent's lap. Which sequence is correct?
A) Proceed strictly head to toe, beginning with the eyes and ears
B) Auscultate heart, lungs, and abdomen while the infant is quiet; then palpate and percuss the same areas; then proceed head to toe, performing traumatic procedures last and eliciting the Moro reflex last of all
C) Elicit all reflexes first while the infant is calm, then examine body systems
D) Begin with the most painful area so it is not anticipated
Answer: B
Rationale: Table 4.2 gives the infant sequence: "If quiet, auscultate heart, lungs, abdomen. Record heart and respiratory rates. Palpate and percuss same areas. Proceed in usual head-to-toe direction. Perform traumatic procedures last (eyes, ears, mouth [while crying]). Elicit reflexes as body part is examined. Elicit Moro reflex last" (p. 98).
Trap: (A) confuses the recording order with the examination order. The head-to-toe order is kept for *recording*; the examination itself is reordered by age to minimize stress, preserve the parent-child relationship, and "Maximize the accuracy and reliability of assessment findings." (D) reverses the standing rule that painful areas are examined last — except in an emergency, where airway, breathing, circulation, and the injured area come first.

Q: A nurse wants to introduce the stethoscope to a 2-year-old before auscultating. What does Table 4.2 direct?
A) Demonstrate the equipment's use on a parent, then on the child
B) Allow the toddler to inspect the equipment; demonstrating its use is usually ineffective
C) Explain the purpose of the equipment and its significance
D) Explain the findings as they are obtained
Answer: B
Rationale: The toddler column states: "Allow toddler to inspect equipment; demonstrating use of equipment is usually ineffective" (p. 98).
Trap: (A) and (C) are both correct — for older children. The Preparation column runs a developmental ladder: the toddler is *shown nothing* because demonstration does not work, the preschooler gets a *brief* demonstration, the school-age child gets the *purpose* explained ("otoscope to see eardrum, which is necessary for hearing"), and the adolescent gets the *findings* explained during the examination. Choosing the rung is the item.

Q: A nurse is deciding whether a preschooler is ready to cooperate with the examination. Which are the chapter's signs of readiness? (Select all that apply.)
A) Talking to the nurse
B) Making eye contact
C) Accepting the offered equipment
D) Choosing to sit on the examining table rather than the parent's lap
E) Remaining silent and still when approached
Answer: A, B, C, D
Rationale: The signs are "Talking to the nurse; Making eye contact; Accepting the offered equipment; Allowing physical touching; Choosing to sit on examining table rather than parent's lap" (p. 98).
Trap: (E) is the one to reject and it reads compliance as consent. Silence and stillness are not on the list; when the readiness signs are absent, the chapter's move is to talk to the parent while "essentially 'ignoring' child," make complimentary remarks, tell a funny story or a simple magic trick, or use a hand puppet as a nonthreatening friend.

Q: Three siblings are to be examined at one visit, and one is visibly anxious. What does the chapter direct?
A) Examine the anxious child first to get it over with
B) Begin with the most cooperative child to model desired behavior
C) Examine all three simultaneously to save time
D) Reschedule the anxious child for a separate visit
Answer: B
Rationale: "If several children in the family will be examined, begin with the most cooperative child to model desired behavior" (p. 98).
Trap: (A) is a defensible-sounding instinct that forfeits the modeling opportunity the chapter is pointing at — and the anxious child watching a sibling go first is the intervention.

Q: A 5-year-old refuses to be examined and begins to cry. Which actions does the chapter direct?
A) Give a prolonged, detailed explanation of the procedure until the child agrees
B) Assess the reason for the refusal, involve child and parent, avoid prolonged explanations, use a firm direct approach, perform the examination as quickly as possible, have an attendant gently restrain, and limit the number of people in the room
C) Postpone the examination indefinitely
D) Remove the parent from the room to reduce the child's audience
Answer: B
Rationale: These are the chapter's directions when a child refuses, and it notes that "a child who is unduly afraid may have had a traumatic experience" (p. 98).
Trap: (A) is the intuitive escalation and is specifically ruled out — "Avoid prolonged explanations about examining procedure." (D) inverts one of the five reasons the sequence is altered in the first place: to "Preserve the essential security of the parent-child relationship, especially with young children."

Topic: growth measurements and bmi

Q: A nurse is plotting growth for an 18-month-old and a 4-year-old. Which charts should be used?
A) CDC charts for both
B) WHO growth standards for the 18-month-old and CDC growth charts for the 4-year-old
C) WHO standards for both
D) CDC charts for the 18-month-old and WHO standards for the 4-year-old
Answer: B
Rationale: The WHO growth standards are used "to monitor growth for infants and children between the ages of 0 and 2 years old," because they "reflect growth patterns among children who were predominately breastfed," and "The Centers for Disease Control and Prevention growth charts... are used for children 2 years old and older" (pp. 98–100).
Trap: (D) swaps the two age bands, which is the only way this pair is tested. The rationale for the split is worth holding: the WHO standard is a breastfed reference, which is why it belongs to the years when breastfeeding is most common.

Q: A nurse needs to measure a 14-month-old. What is the correct method?
A) Standing height on a stadiometer, since the child can walk
B) Recumbent length on a length board, holding the head in midline, grasping the knees together gently, and pushing down until the legs are fully extended
C) A tape measure laid alongside the infant on the examining table
D) Either method, as long as it is documented
Answer: B
Rationale: Measure recumbent length until 24 months (or 36 months if using the birth-to-36-months chart) using a length board, extending the body by "(1) holding the head in midline, (2) grasping the knees together gently, and (3) pushing down on the knees until the legs are fully extended" (p. 100).
Trap: (C) is wrong twice over — wrong instrument and wrong position — and the chapter rules the instrument out explicitly: "A tape measure should not be used to measure the length of infants and children because the methods are inaccurate and unreliable." Note the related alert for older children: "Normally height is less if measured in the afternoon than in the morning," so serial measurements are taken at the same time of day.

Q: A 9-year-old's BMI-for-age plots at the 87th percentile. How is this classified?
A) Healthy weight
B) Overweight
C) Obese
D) Underweight
Answer: B
Rationale: The BMI-for-age and gender categories are "underweight (<5th percentile), healthy weight (5th to <85th percentile), overweight (≥85th percentile and <95th percentile), or obese (≥95th percentile)" (p. 104).
Trap: (C) is the classic error the fact bank names — a distractor placing obesity at the 85th percentile. The 85th opens the overweight band; obesity begins at the 95th. This is the same cut point Chapter 1 uses, so getting it wrong costs twice.

Q: Which growth patterns does the chapter identify as questionable and warranting further investigation? (Select all that apply.)
A) Height and weight percentiles that are widely disparate
B) Failure to follow the expected growth velocity in height and weight
C) A sudden increase or decrease in a previously steady pattern — crossing two major percentile lines after 3 years old
D) Short stature in the absence of short parents
E) Consistent tracking along the 10th percentile for both height and weight
Answer: A, B, C, D
Rationale: These are the four patterns the chapter lists (p. 100). The interpretive principle behind them: "the most reliable evaluation lies in comparing growth measurements over time because they reflect change," and "same-age children (whether short, average, or tall in stature) should grow at similar rates."
Trap: (E) is the one to reject, and it is chosen by anyone who reads a low percentile as a problem. A child tracking steadily along any percentile is following an expected velocity — it is the *crossing* that matters, and only after 3 years old.

Q: A nurse measures head circumference on a 15-month-old and finds it equal to the chest circumference. How should this be interpreted, and through what age is head circumference routinely measured?
A) Abnormal; head circumference should always exceed chest circumference in childhood
B) Expected — head and chest circumferences are equal at about 1 to 2 years of age — and head circumference is measured routinely up to 36 months and in any child whose head size is questionable
C) Abnormal; chest should already exceed head by 5 to 7 cm at this age
D) Expected, and head circumference is measured routinely only through 12 months
Answer: B
Rationale: "Generally, head and chest circumferences are equal at about 1 to 2 years of age. During childhood, chest circumference exceeds head size by about 5 to 7 cm (2 to 2.75 inches)." Head circumference is measured "in children up to 36 months of age and in any child whose head size is questionable," at "its greatest frontooccipital circumference" (p. 104).
Trap: (C) applies the later childhood relationship at the crossover age. Note the technique detail: use paper or nonstretchable tape "because a cloth tape can stretch and give a falsely small measurement."

Topic: temperature pulse and respiration measurement

Q: A nurse must obtain vital signs on a sleeping 5-month-old. In what order should they be taken?
A) Temperature, pulse, respirations
B) Respirations first (before the infant is disturbed), pulse next, temperature last
C) Pulse, respirations, temperature
D) Any order, provided all three are obtained
Answer: B
Rationale: For infants, "count respirations first (before the infant is disturbed), take the pulse next, and measure temperature last" (p. 105). If the vital signs cannot be taken without disturbing the child, "record the child's behavior (e.g., crying) along with the measurement."
Trap: (A) is the adult habit and it reverses the ordering principle — least disturbing to most. Note that in infants respirations are counted by observing abdominal movements, "because respirations are primarily diaphragmatic," and for a full minute "Because the movements are irregular."

Q: A 3-week-old needs an accurate temperature. Which route is recommended, and why?
A) Rectal, since it is the clinical gold standard
B) Axillary, because rectal temperature is contraindicated for infants younger than 1 month old due to risk of rectal perforation, and axillary is the recommended screening route at this age
C) Oral, since it is the standard for noninvasive measurement
D) Tympanic, since it is fastest
Answer: B
Rationale: Rectal "remains the clinical gold standard" but is "contraindicated for infants younger than 1 month old due to risk of rectal perforation," and the chapter's bottom line states: "For infants younger than 1 month of age, axillary temperatures are recommended for screening" (pp. 105–106).
Trap: (A) is true as a general statement and unsafe in this patient, which is the shape of every route question in this chapter. (C) fails on a different age gate: oral is contraindicated under 5 years of age, as well as in children with altered level of consciousness, receiving oxygen, mouth breathing, or with mucositis or recent oral surgery.

Q: An axillary temperature on a 2-year-old reads 37.2° C (99° F). A nurse suggests adding a degree to estimate the core temperature. How should this be evaluated?
A) Correct; adding 1° F to an axillary reading approximates core temperature
B) Incorrect; the belief that core temperature can be estimated by adding a degree to an axillary temperature is named and rejected — do not add a degree
C) Correct, but only for children older than 1 year
D) Incorrect; a degree should be subtracted instead
Answer: B
Rationale: The Nursing Alert states: "The belief that core temperature can be estimated by adding 1° F to the temperature taken in the axilla is incorrect. Do not add a degree to the finding obtained by taking a temperature by the axillary route" (p. 106).
Trap: (A) is a widely taught rule of thumb the chapter names specifically in order to reject it. Note also the limitation on the route itself: axillary "is inconsistent and insensitive in infants and children over 1 month old" and is usable "as a screening tool for fever in young, clinically stable infants and children."

Q: A nurse is assessing pulses on a 10-month-old. Which approach is correct?
A) Radial pulse, counted for 30 seconds and doubled
B) Apical pulse, counted for 1 full minute, because in infants and young children the apical impulse is more reliable and irregularities in rhythm are possible
C) Radial pulse, counted for 1 full minute
D) Carotid pulse, counted for 15 seconds
Answer: B
Rationale: "A satisfactory pulse can be taken radially in children older than 2 years of age. However, in infants and young children, the apical impulse... is more reliable," and "Count the pulse for 1 full minute in infants and young children because of possible irregularities in rhythm" (p. 106).
Trap: (A) fails on both counts — wrong site for the age and a shortcut interval that assumes a regular rhythm. Note the separate screening rule from this section: "Compare radial and femoral pulses at least once during infancy to detect the presence of circulatory impairment, such as coarctation of the aorta."

Q: A child's pedal pulse is easy to palpate and is not easily obliterated with pressure. How is this graded?
A) +1
B) +2
C) +3
D) +4
Answer: C
Rationale: Table 4.4 grades pulses 0 to +4, and +3 is "Easy to palpate, not easily obliterated with pressure (normal)" (p. 107).
Trap: (D) is the reflex pick for a learner who assumes a strong pulse is the top of the scale. It is not: +4 is "Strong, bounding, not obliterated with pressure," which is abnormal. The normal grade sits one step below the maximum, which is what makes this scale worth memorizing rather than reasoning through.

Topic: blood pressure measurement and interpretation

Q: A nurse selects a blood pressure cuff for a child. What determines the correct size?
A) The label on the cuff, such as "infant" or "child"
B) A bladder width approximately 40% of the arm circumference midway between the olecranon and the acromion, which usually gives a bladder length covering 80% to 100% of the arm circumference
C) A bladder width covering 80% to 100% of the arm circumference
D) The width of the child's hand
Answer: B
Rationale: "the most important factor in accurately measuring BP is the use of an appropriately sized cuff (cuff size refers only to the inner inflatable bladder, not the cloth covering)." Choose "a cuff with a bladder width that is approximately 40% of the arm circumference midway between the olecranon and the acromion," which usually gives "a cuff bladder length that covers 80% to 100% of the circumference of the arm" (p. 108).
Trap: (C) swaps the two percentages between width and length. (A) is ruled out by name: "Do not choose a cuff based on the name of the cuff (e.g., an 'infant' cuff may be too small for some infants)."

Q: The correctly sized cuff is unavailable and the nurse must choose between one that is too small and one that is too large. Which should be used, and what error will result?
A) The undersized cuff, which will read falsely low
B) The oversized cuff, accepting a falsely low reading — the chapter directs using an oversized cuff rather than an undersized one
C) The undersized cuff, accepting a falsely high reading
D) Neither; defer the measurement
Answer: B
Rationale: "If the cuff size is too small, the reading on the device is falsely high. If the cuff size is too large, the reading is falsely low," and the Nursing Alert directs: "When the correct size is not available, use an oversized cuff rather than an undersized one" (p. 108).
Trap: (A) is the mirror error and the fact bank calls this the single most swapped pair in the chapter. Note why the direction of the preference makes sense clinically: an oversized cuff underestimates, so it will not manufacture a false diagnosis of hypertension — but it can mask a real one, which is why size selection is called the most important factor.

Q: An automated oscillometric device reads a child's blood pressure above the 90th percentile. What is the next step?
A) Document the reading and refer for hypertension evaluation
B) Repeat the elevated reading using auscultation, since oscillometric readings are generally about 10 mm Hg higher and the two methods are not interchangeable
C) Repeat with the same device on the opposite arm
D) Recheck the reading in one week
Answer: B
Rationale: Oscillometric readings "are generally higher (10 mm Hg higher) than measurements using auscultation," and the two "prevent the interchange of the readings by the two methods." The directive is explicit: "An elevated BP reading obtained with an oscillometric device should be repeated using auscultation" (p. 108). Auscultation "remains the recommended method."
Trap: (A) acts on a number the chapter says is systematically high. The related Nursing Alert underlines it: "Published norms for blood pressure are valid only if you use the same method of measurement (auscultation and cuff size determination) in clinical practice."

Q: A 16-year-old's blood pressure is 124/78 mm Hg, which falls below the 90th percentile for his age, sex, and height. How should this be classified?
A) Normotensive, since it is below the 90th percentile
B) Prehypertensive — if an adolescent's blood pressure is more than 120/80 mm Hg, consider the patient prehypertensive even if the value is below the 90th percentile
C) Stage 1 hypertension
D) Stage 2 hypertension
Answer: B
Rationale: A child is normotensive "if the BP is below the 90th percentile"; the 90th to 95th percentile is prehypertension; at or above the 95th is possible hypertension, requiring repeat "on at least two occasions to confirm diagnosis." And: "if an adolescent's BP is more than 120/80 mm Hg, consider the patient prehypertensive, even if this value is below the 90th percentile" (p. 109).
Trap: (A) applies the percentile rule correctly and misses the adolescent override, which is the whole point of the item. Blood pressure is measured annually in children 3 years of age through adolescence, so this is a routine finding rather than an unusual one.

Q: A teenager reports fainting when he stands up quickly. The nurse measures blood pressure supine and again after 2 minutes standing, finding a 24 mm Hg systolic drop with a heart rate increase of 8 beats/min. How should this be interpreted?
A) A normal postural response
B) Orthostatic hypotension most likely due to an autonomic deficit, because the sustained systolic drop exceeds 20 mm Hg without an increase in heart rate of more than 15 beats/min
C) Orthostatic hypotension from a nonneurogenic cause such as hypovolemia
D) The measurement is invalid because 2 minutes is too short
Answer: B
Rationale: "The child with a sustained drop in systolic pressure of more than 20 mm Hg or in diastolic pressure of more than 10 mm Hg after standing for 2 minutes without an increase in heart rate of more than 15 beats/min most likely has an autonomic deficit. Nonneurogenic causes of OH have a compensatory increase in pulse of more than 15 beats/min, as well as a drop in BP" (p. 109). Measurement is taken "at least 2 minutes in each position."
Trap: (C) is the mirror reading of the same numbers and the more common cause overall — hypovolemia, "which may be induced by medications such as diuretics, vasodilator medications, and prolonged immobility or bed rest." What separates the two is the pulse response, not the pressure drop: a compensatory rise above 15 beats/min points away from an autonomic deficit.

Topic: vision and hearing screening

Q: A 4-year-old's corneal light reflex is symmetric, but her epicanthal folds give the appearance that one eye turns inward. How should this be documented?
A) Strabismus, requiring referral
B) Pseudostrabismus — the false impression of misalignment created by epicanthal folds, with symmetric corneal light reflexes
C) Amblyopia
D) Normal binocularity, no further testing indicated
Answer: B
Rationale: Epicanthal folds are "excess folds of skin that extend from the roof of the nose to the inner termination of the eyebrow and that partially or completely overlap the inner canthus of the eye," and may give the "false impression of misalignment" called pseudostrabismus. In the corneal light reflex test, symmetric light in each pupil is orthophoric — normal (p. 114).
Trap: (A) diagnoses from appearance alone, which is the distractor the fact bank flags for this pair. The test result, not the look of the eyes, is what settles it. (C) names the consequence rather than the finding: strabismus is the misalignment, and amblyopia is the "blindness from disuse" that follows "if it is not detected and corrected by 4 to 6 years old."

Q: A 7-year-old tests 20/20 in the right eye and 20/30 in the left. Both results are within the passing range for his age. What should the nurse do?
A) Record as normal, since both eyes pass
B) Refer — a two-line difference between the eyes is referable on its own, even within the passing range
C) Repeat the test at the next well visit
D) Refer only if the child reports symptoms
Answer: B
Rationale: Table 4.6 lists as a referral criterion at 6 years and older: "Two-line difference between eyes, even within the passing range (i.e., 10/10 and 10/15 or 20/20 and 20/30)" (p. 115).
Trap: (A) is the reflex pick and is exactly the trap the fact bank names: a child reading 20/20 in one eye and 20/30 in the other has two passing eyes and still gets referred. The interocular difference is a separate criterion from the acuity threshold, not a softer version of it.

Q: A nurse examines a 6-month-old and finds a white pupillary reflex on one side. What does this indicate?
A) A normal variant in infants
B) A possible retinoblastoma, requiring referral; a brilliant, uniform red reflex is what rules out many serious defects of the cornea, aqueous chamber, lens, and vitreous chamber
C) Cataract only
D) Pseudostrabismus
Answer: B
Rationale: The Nursing Alert states: "A brilliant, uniform red reflex is an important sign because it rules out many serious defects of the cornea, aqueous chamber, lens, and vitreous chamber. Any dark shadows or opacities are recorded because they indicate some abnormality," and a white reflex "indicates possible retinoblastoma" (p. 114). Ocular media clarity referral criteria in Table 4.6 are "White pupil, dark spots, absent reflex."
Trap: (C) names one real cause of an abnormal reflex and stops short of the finding that makes this urgent. The red reflex is a screen for several conditions at once, which is why the chapter describes what a *normal* one rules out rather than listing what an abnormal one means.

Q: A nurse is performing pure tone audiometry screening on a school-age child. Which frequencies and threshold define a failure?
A) 500, 1000, 2000, and 4000 Hz, failing if the child cannot hear the tones at 20 dB
B) 250, 500, 1000, and 2000 Hz, failing at 40 dB
C) 1000, 2000, 4000, and 8000 Hz, failing at 20 dB
D) 500, 1000, 2000, and 4000 Hz, failing at 40 dB
Answer: A
Rationale: The AAP "recommends pure tone audiometry testing screening at 500, 1000, 2000, and 4000 Hz, with children failing if they cannot hear the tones at 20 dB" (p. 119). Universal newborn hearing screening is available in all US states, and the quality indicator is children 3 months of age or younger who received an audiological evaluation.
Trap: (D) keeps the right frequencies and doubles the threshold, which would let a mild loss pass. The chapter's Research Focus explains why the threshold is set low: "failure to identify children even with mild high-frequency hearing loss may have long-term consequences."

Topic: heart and breath sounds

Q: A nurse auscultating a child's heart notes that the split in S2 does not change during inspiration. What does this suggest?
A) Physiologic splitting, a significant normal finding
B) Fixed splitting, an important diagnostic sign of atrial septal defect
C) An S3 gallop
D) A normal finding in all school-age children
Answer: B
Rationale: The Nursing Alert states: "Fixed splitting, in which the split in S2 does not change during inspiration, is an important diagnostic sign of atrial septal defect." Normally "the split of the two sounds in S2 is distinguishable and widens during inspiration. Physiologic splitting is a significant normal finding" (p. 125).
Trap: (A) is the mirror error and the pair turns on one word: physiologic splitting **widens** on inspiration, fixed splitting **does not change**. The discriminator is the respiratory variation, not the presence of a split.

Q: A nurse hears a third heart sound in a healthy 8-year-old. How should this be interpreted, and what would change the interpretation?
A) S3 always indicates heart failure and requires referral
B) S3 is normally heard in some children; S4 is rarely heard as a normal heart sound and usually indicates the need for further cardiac evaluation
C) Both S3 and S4 are normal findings in children
D) Neither S3 nor S4 is ever audible in a healthy child
Answer: B
Rationale: S3 "is normally heard in some children. S4 is rarely heard as a normal heart sound; it usually indicates the need for further cardiac evaluation" (p. 125). The chapter's mnemonics: S3 "sounds like word S1 S2 S3: 'Ken-tuc-ky'"; S4 "sounds like word S4 S1 S2: 'Ten-nes-see.'"
Trap: (A) imports the adult significance of an S3. Note the technique that goes with these sounds: use "both diaphragm and bell chest pieces when auscultating heart sounds. Bell chest piece is necessary for low-pitched sounds of murmurs, S3, and S4."

Q: A nurse auscultates over a child's manubrium and upper intrascapular region and hears inspiration that is louder and higher pitched than the sounds heard elsewhere over the lung fields. How should this be classified?
A) Vesicular breath sounds, which are abnormal in this location
B) Bronchovesicular breath sounds, which are expected over the manubrium and upper intrascapular regions where the trachea and bronchi bifurcate
C) Adventitious sounds requiring further evaluation
D) Bronchial sounds, which are normal over the entire lung surface
Answer: B
Rationale: Bronchovesicular breath sounds are "Heard over the manubrium and in the upper intrascapular regions where trachea and bronchi bifurcate. Inspiration is louder and higher pitched than in vesicular breathing." Vesicular sounds are "Heard over entire surface of the lungs, with exception of upper intrascapular area and area beneath the manubrium," where "Inspiration is louder, longer, and higher pitched than expiration. The sound is a soft, swishing noise" (p. 122).
Trap: (A) has the right sound type and the wrong location logic — vesicular sounds are normal everywhere *except* the two regions named in the stem, so the exception in one definition is the rule in the other. Chapter 26 relies on this terminology, so the classification is worth holding precisely.
