# Chapter 29 — The Child With Cancer

Source: `courses/nur4226-4339-childrearing/sources/Ch29_Fact_Bank.md`
(Hockenberry, Wilson & Rodgers, *Wong's Nursing Care of Infants and Children*, 11th ed.,
Chapter 29, printed pp. 1071–1106.)

**Blueprint weight: ~5 questions of 202, depth `standard`.** Band is 30–45 questions; this
file carries **40** across **9 topics**, averaging 4.4 each — the same count used for the other
five-item chapter in this batch, tracking the weight one step below the six-item chapters.

**Where the weight went.** Two topics go to the consequences of treatment rather than to the
tumors, because that is where the nursing actions live: infection and bleeding thresholds, and
the side effects of chemotherapy. Acute leukemia and brain tumors get the largest disease
shares, since leukemia is "the most common form of childhood cancer" and central nervous system
tumors are "the most common solid tumor in children."

**Drilled from both ends:** neuroblastoma against Wilms tumor, on whether the abdominal mass
crosses the midline; Hodgkin against non-Hodgkin lymphoma, on Reed-Sternberg cells and whether
the Ann Arbor staging applies; osteosarcoma against Ewing sarcoma, on origin and on systemic
symptoms; infratentorial against supratentorial brain tumors, which differ in presentation and
in the postoperative positioning that follows from it; and tumor lysis syndrome against
hyperleukocytosis, which are a metabolic and a rheologic emergency in the same patient
population.

**The chapter's clearest single nursing action gets its own item.** A suspected Wilms tumor is
not palpated, because manipulation "may cause dissemination of cancer cells to adjacent and
distant sites," and the chapter goes as far as directing a sign on the bed. Any plan that
includes serial abdominal palpation to track the mass is wrong.

**Four platelet numbers govern four different decisions**, and the chapter gives them
separately: 20,000 for spontaneous bleeding, 100,000 for home activity restriction, 40,000
paired with granulocytes of 500 for toothbrushing, and the transfusion reserve for active
bleeding that does not respond to local measures. Two of the four are drilled; the preamble
names all four so the set is visible.

**This chapter's review-question key is internally consistent with its text**, so its items are
drilled as the source keys them.

**What ~5 blueprint items could not buy.** Left undrilled: radiation therapy technique and its
early side-effect table; hematopoietic stem cell transplantation beyond the allogeneic-versus-
autologous distinction; biologic therapy; rhabdomyosarcoma; the childhood cancer survivor and
the late-effects table; and the procedural pain and health promotion material. The late-effects
table is the largest real loss, since it is the only place the chapter connects each treatment
modality to what it costs the survivor years later.

**Select-all count: 4** (1 in 10), each on a source list that genuinely sorts two ways: the
warning signs against a finding without the persistence qualifier every real entry carries, the
chemotherapy handling precautions against an action the chapter forbids by name, the tumor
lysis electrolytes against the one value that moves in the opposite direction, and the
neutropenia home precautions against a restriction the chapter does not impose. The ratio sits
below this bank's usual 1 in 7 because most of the rest of this chapter is either a
two-tumor discrimination or a single threshold, and both are drilled better by making the
learner choose than by having them check boxes.

Topic: cancer warning signs and diagnostic workup in children

Q: A nurse is reviewing findings in a 4-year-old that would raise concern for a malignancy. Which are among the chapter's warning signs? Select all that apply.
A) An unusual mass or swelling
B) Persistent, localized pain or limping
C) Prolonged, unexplained fever
D) Frequent headaches, often with vomiting
E) A single episode of epistaxis after a fall
Answer: A, B, C, D
Rationale: Box 29.1 lists an unusual mass or swelling, unexplained paleness and loss of energy, easy bruising, persistent localized pain or limping, prolonged unexplained fever or illness, frequent headaches often with vomiting, sudden eye or vision changes, and unexplained rapid weight loss (p. 1078).
Trap: (E) lacks the qualifier every real entry carries — the chapter states that "Cancers in children are often difficult to recognize. Therefore being alert to the persistence of possible signs and symptoms is essential," and the operative word throughout the box is persistence.

Q: A parent reports that her 5-year-old has a swollen lymph node. What additional findings would make this concerning for malignancy?
A) Fever for more than 1 week, recent weight loss, or an abnormal chest radiograph
B) Any palpable node larger than 1 cm
C) Tenderness of the node on palpation
D) A node that has been present for 3 days
Answer: A
Rationale: The chapter states that "enlarged, firm lymph nodes in a child with fever for more than 1 week, a recent history of weight loss, or an abnormal chest x-ray film may indicate a serious disease and should be evaluated further" (p. 1079).
Trap: (B) and (C) each treat a single node characteristic as sufficient, and a swollen node alone is not the cancer cue in this chapter — all three of the named qualifiers do the work, which is why they are stated together.

Q: A parent brings in a photograph showing a white glow in her toddler's pupil instead of the normal red reflex. What should the nurse recognize?
A) Leukocoria, which can be a sign of retinoblastoma
B) A normal photographic artifact requiring no follow-up
C) Cataract, which is unrelated to malignancy
D) Strabismus, which is the most common presenting sign of eye tumors
Answer: A
Rationale: The chapter describes leukocoria or white eye reflex as a yellow or whitish glow in the pupil, as opposed to the normal red pupillary reflex, noted in photographs, and states it can be a sign of retinoblastoma (pp. 1079, 1102).
Trap: (D) names the second most common sign rather than the first — strabismus results from poor fixation of the visually impaired eye — and (B) dismisses a finding whose "fleeting nature... often results in a delayed diagnosis," which is precisely why a photograph matters.

Q: A nurse is explaining childhood cancer to a parent who asks what she could have done to prevent it. What is accurate?
A) There are no generally recognized preventive measures for childhood cancer, because lifestyle risk factors drive adult rather than childhood cancer
B) Dietary changes in early childhood substantially reduce risk
C) Avoiding all environmental exposures prevents most childhood cancers
D) Childhood cancer is the leading cause of death in children ages 1 to 15
Answer: A
Rationale: The chapter states that lifestyle risk factors drive adult cancer rather than childhood cancer, and that there are no generally recognized preventive measures for childhood cancer (pp. 1072–1073).
Trap: (D) overstates a real statistic, since cancer is the leading cause of death *from disease* in children ages 1 to 15 rather than the leading cause of death overall; the distinction matters when counseling families, and so does the fact that nothing they did or failed to do caused this.

Topic: chemotherapy administration and side-effect management

Q: A nurse is preparing to handle chemotherapeutic agents. Which precautions does the chapter direct? Select all that apply.
A) Prepare the drugs in a ventilated preparation room
B) Wear gloves and protective clothing
C) Use face and eye protection and a respirator when indicated
D) Place sterile gauze at every connection point
E) Recap needles carefully before disposal
Answer: A, B, C, D
Rationale: The chapter's handling precautions name a ventilated preparation room, gloves and protective clothing, face and eye protection with a respirator when indicated, and sterile gauze at every connection point (p. 1076).
Trap: (E) reverses an explicit instruction — do not recap or break needles — and it is the kind of action that reads as careful while creating the exposure the whole list exists to prevent.

Q: A nurse notes swelling and a lack of blood return at a peripheral intravenous site during a chemotherapy infusion. What should the nurse do first?
A) Stop the infusion immediately
B) Slow the infusion and apply a warm compress
C) Flush the line with saline to confirm patency
D) Notify the practitioner before changing anything
Answer: A
Rationale: The chapter directs stopping the chemotherapy infusion immediately at the first sign of infiltration (p. 1076).
Trap: (C) pushes more vesicant into the tissue while testing a line that has already failed; note that the action differs for a suspected hypersensitivity reaction, where the nurse discontinues the drug, flushes and maintains the line with saline, and monitors vital signs — infiltration means stop, hypersensitivity means stop and keep the line open.

Q: A child is scheduled for chemotherapy known to cause nausea. When should antiemetics be given?
A) On a schedule, before chemotherapy and around the clock for at least 24 hours after
B) As needed, once nausea develops
C) Only if vomiting occurs more than three times
D) After the infusion is complete, to avoid drug interactions
Answer: A
Rationale: The chapter directs giving antiemetics on a schedule, before chemotherapy and around the clock for at least 24 hours after, rather than as needed, with the rationale of preventing anticipatory nausea (p. 1081).
Trap: (B) is standard practice for most symptoms and is specifically wrong here, because once anticipatory nausea is established it becomes conditioned and far harder to treat; note also that nausea and vomiting are usually not indications for stopping the chemotherapy itself.

Q: A child receiving vincristine has not had a bowel movement in 4 days. What should the nurse recommend?
A) A stool softener, increased activity, and a laxative such as polyethylene glycol
B) Increased dietary fiber as the primary intervention
C) A rectal suppository for prompt relief
D) Fluid restriction to firm the stool
Answer: A
Rationale: The chapter states that "Increased fiber may not be effective because the increased bulk tends to increase fecal distention and discomfort without producing the necessary mechanical stimulation," directing stool softeners, activity, and laxatives such as polyethylene glycol instead (p. 1082).
Trap: (C) is contraindicated, since rectal temperatures and suppositories are avoided in the child with cancer — an instruction that appears three separate times in this chapter; (B) is the intuitive constipation answer and the one the chapter specifically rules out for this cause.

Topic: pediatric oncologic emergencies

Q: A child beginning induction for acute lymphoblastic leukemia develops flank pain, muscle cramps, and tetany. Which laboratory pattern should the nurse expect? Select all that apply.
A) Hyperuricemia
B) Hyperphosphatemia
C) Hyperkalemia
D) Hypocalcemia
E) Hypercalcemia
Answer: A, B, C, D
Rationale: The chapter defines tumor lysis syndrome by "hyperuricemia, hypocalcemia, hyperphosphatemia, and hyperkalemia," resulting from rapid release of intracellular contents during lysis of malignant cells, typically during the initial treatment period (p. 1078).
Trap: (E) uses the pattern against the reader — three of the four values go up and calcium is the outlier that goes down, so an option offering the calcium in the same direction as the rest is the designed error.

Q: A child with tumor lysis syndrome is at risk for acute renal failure. Which intervention addresses the mechanism?
A) Aggressive intravenous fluids with allopurinol or rasburicase to reduce uric acid
B) Fluid restriction to reduce renal workload
C) Potassium supplementation to correct the electrolyte shift
D) Calcium restriction to prevent stone formation
Answer: A
Rationale: The chapter states that "The crystallization of uric acid that can occur with hyperuricemia can lead to acute renal failure," and directs frequent serum chemistries and urine pH, strict intake and output, aggressive intravenous fluids, and allopurinol, noting that hyperuricemia "is now effectively treated with recombinant urate oxidase, or rasburicase," which converts uric acid to the more soluble allantoin (p. 1078).
Trap: (C) would worsen an already elevated potassium, and (B) removes the very intervention that clears the uric acid load; risk factors for this syndrome are a high white cell count at diagnosis, large tumor burden, chemosensitivity, and a high proliferative rate.

Q: A child newly diagnosed with leukemia has a white blood cell count of 130,000/mm3 with respiratory distress and confusion. What is this, and what is the mechanism?
A) Hyperleukocytosis, in which capillary obstruction and microinfarction produce organ dysfunction
B) Tumor lysis syndrome, in which electrolyte shifts produce neurologic change
C) Superior vena cava syndrome, in which a mediastinal mass obstructs venous return
D) Disseminated intravascular coagulation, in which microthrombi consume clotting factors
Answer: A
Rationale: The chapter defines hyperleukocytosis as "a peripheral white blood cell count greater than 100,000/mm3, [which] can lead to capillary obstruction, microinfarction, and organ dysfunction," with respiratory distress, cyanosis, and neurologic changes (p. 1078).
Trap: (B) is the other early-treatment emergency in the same patient and is a metabolic rather than a rheologic problem; the distinguishing feature here is the cell count itself, and management is rapid cytoreduction with hydration, urinary alkalinization, and allopurinol.

Q: A child with Hodgkin lymphoma develops cyanosis of the face and neck, facial and upper extremity edema, and distended neck and chest veins. What is the priority?
A) Airway protection and alleviation of respiratory distress
B) Immediate lumbar puncture to assess for central nervous system involvement
C) Elevation of the lower extremities to improve venous return
D) Fluid bolus to improve perfusion
Answer: A
Rationale: The chapter describes superior vena cava syndrome from space-occupying chest lesions, "especially from Hodgkin disease and NHL," presenting with cyanosis of the face, neck, and upper chest, facial and upper extremity edema, and distended neck and chest veins, managed by "Airway protection and alleviation of respiratory distress" (p. 1078).
Trap: (D) treats the distention as hypovolemia when the problem is obstructed return; note that the second leading cause of this syndrome is thrombotic complications of implantable intravenous devices, so a child with a port belongs on this differential too.

Topic: infection and bleeding risk in the child with cancer

Q: A child receiving chemotherapy has a white blood cell count of 1000/mm3, with 7% neutrophils and 7% bands. What is the absolute neutrophil count, and what does it mean?
A) 140/mm3, which places the child at high risk for overwhelming infection
B) 1400/mm3, which is mildly reduced but not high risk
C) 70/mm3, since only segmented neutrophils are counted
D) 140/mm3, which is within normal limits during chemotherapy
Answer: A
Rationale: The chapter's guideline directs determining the total percentage of neutrophils including polys or segs and bands, then multiplying the white cell count by that percentage — so 7% plus 7% is 14%, and 0.14 times 1000 is 140/mm3; the at-risk child is the one with fever and a count below 500/mm3 (p. 1079).
Trap: (C) drops the bands, which the calculation explicitly includes; (D) normalizes a value more than threefold below the risk threshold. Note the clinical trap in the same paragraph — the usual signs of inflammation may be absent, but "fever will occur," so fever is the reliable sign precisely when redness and pus are not.

Q: A child with an absolute neutrophil count of 300/mm3 is being cared for at home. Which instructions are correct? Select all that apply.
A) Avoid crowded areas such as shopping centers
B) Report fever promptly, since it may be the only sign of infection
C) Siblings may receive live measles-mumps-rubella and varicella vaccines
D) Use acetaminophen rather than aspirin for fever or pain
E) Isolate the child from any sibling who has recently received a live vaccine
Answer: A, B, C, D
Rationale: The chapter advises cautious isolation from crowded areas when the count falls below 500/mm3, notes that fever occurs even when other signs do not, states that siblings and family members "can receive live MMR and varicella vaccine without risk to the child who is immunosuppressed," and substitutes acetaminophen for aspirin (pp. 1079–1080, 1084).
Trap: (E) over-applies the live-vaccine rule — the restriction is on the patient receiving them, not on the household, and isolating the child from a recently vaccinated sibling imposes a separation the chapter does not require.

Q: A child receiving chemotherapy has a platelet count of 18,000/mm3. Which finding should the nurse expect, and which action is appropriate?
A) Spontaneous gum or nose bleeding may occur, and pressure is applied at the site without disturbing clot formation
B) Spontaneous bleeding is not expected until the count falls below 5000/mm3
C) Prophylactic platelet transfusion should be given regardless of bleeding
D) Rectal temperatures should be taken to avoid oral mucosal trauma
Answer: A
Rationale: The chapter states that severe spontaneous internal hemorrhage "usually does not occur until the platelet count is 20,000/mm3 or less," that spontaneous gum or nose bleeding appears below that level, and that for the most common bleeds — epistaxis and gingival bleeding — "applying pressure at the site without disturbing clot formation is the general rule" (pp. 1079–1080).
Trap: (D) reverses a rule stated three times in this chapter, since the rectal area is prone to ulceration and rectal temperatures and suppositories are avoided; (C) over-treats, since platelet transfusions "are generally reserved for active bleeding episodes that do not respond to local treatment."

Q: A child with cancer has a platelet count of 85,000/mm3 and wants to ride his bicycle. What should the nurse advise?
A) Avoid activities that might cause injury or bleeding while the count is below 100,000/mm3
B) Bicycle riding is safe at any platelet count above 20,000/mm3
C) All activity should be restricted until the count exceeds 150,000/mm3
D) Activity restriction applies only to contact sports at any count
Answer: A
Rationale: The chapter directs that children with low platelet counts, usually below 100,000/mm3, "should avoid activities that might cause injury or bleeding, such as riding bicycles or skateboards, skating, climbing trees or playground equipment, and contact sports," and that once the count rises "these restrictions are not necessary" (p. 1080).
Trap: (B) applies the spontaneous-bleeding threshold to an activity decision, and the chapter deliberately keeps four platelet numbers apart for four different decisions — 20,000 for spontaneous bleeding, 100,000 for activity, 40,000 with granulocytes of 500 for toothbrushing, and transfusion reserved for refractory active bleeding.

Q: A child with cancer develops stomatitis. Which oral care measure does the chapter recommend?
A) A soft sponge toothbrush, avoiding lemon glycerin swabs and hydrogen peroxide
B) Viscous lidocaine for a young child before meals
C) Hydrogen peroxide rinses to debride the ulcerations
D) Lemon glycerin swabs to stimulate salivation
Answer: A
Rationale: The chapter directs a soft sponge toothbrush for stomatitis and states that lemon glycerin swabs and hydrogen peroxide are avoided for their drying effect and, for lemon, its acidity on eroded tissue (p. 1082).
Trap: (B) names an agent the chapter rules out in young children, because viscous lidocaine "may depress the gag reflex and increase aspiration risk" and has been associated with seizures; toothettes are the fallback when counts fall below the 500 and 40,000 thresholds.

Topic: acute leukemia in children

Q: A 4-year-old newly diagnosed with acute lymphoblastic leukemia presents with pallor, recurrent infections, and petechiae. What accounts for all three?
A) Bone marrow infiltration producing anemia, infection from neutropenia, and bleeding from decreased platelet production
B) Direct organ infiltration by leukemic cells in the skin and lungs
C) A paraneoplastic autoimmune process
D) Side effects of induction chemotherapy, which has not yet begun
Answer: A
Rationale: The chapter names three main consequences of bone marrow infiltration: "(1) anemia from decreased erythrocytes, (2) infection from neutropenia, and (3) bleeding from decreased platelet production" (p. 1086).
Trap: (D) attributes the presentation to treatment that has not started, which is a common misreading when the findings resemble chemotherapy toxicity; sorting each presenting sign into one of the three categories is what makes this material tractable.

Q: A child with acute lymphoblastic leukemia is entering the phase that begins immediately after diagnosis. What defines it?
A) Induction, lasting 4 to 5 weeks, using corticosteroids, vincristine, and L-asparaginase, aiming for less than 5% blasts in the marrow
B) Maintenance, lasting 2 to 3 years, using daily mercaptopurine and weekly methotrexate
C) Consolidation, using cyclophosphamide, cytarabine, and mercaptopurine
D) Delayed intensification, repeating the induction drugs at higher doses
Answer: A
Rationale: The chapter states that induction begins almost immediately after diagnosis and "lasts for 4 to 5 weeks," that "The principal drugs are the corticosteroids (dexamethasone or prednisone), vincristine, and L-asparaginase," and that complete remission is "the presence of less than 5% blast cells in the bone marrow and no detectable leukemia in extramedullary sites" (pp. 1086–1087).
Trap: (B) names the final phase, which runs 2 to 3 years; note that the period immediately after remission is critical, because myelosuppression leaves "the body defenseless against invading organisms... and susceptible to spontaneous hemorrhage."

Q: Why does every child with acute lymphoblastic leukemia receive central nervous system prophylaxis?
A) Leukemic cells in the central nervous system are protected by the blood-brain barrier from systemic chemotherapy
B) All children have detectable central nervous system disease at diagnosis
C) Cranial radiation is required in every case
D) The central nervous system is the most common site of the primary tumor
Answer: A
Rationale: The chapter states that because leukemic cells in the central nervous system are protected by the blood-brain barrier, "all children receive CNS prophylactic therapy," delivered as intrathecal chemotherapy plus central nervous system–directed systemic chemotherapy, with cranial radiation reserved for those at highest risk of relapse (pp. 1086–1087).
Trap: (C) generalizes a treatment the chapter restricts to the highest-risk group; note that the central nervous system and the testes are the two named "sanctuaries" for leukemic cells requiring specific therapy.

Q: A family asks why adherence matters so much during maintenance therapy. What should the nurse explain?
A) Anything less than 95% adherence increases the risk of relapse
B) Missing occasional doses has no measurable effect on outcome
C) Adherence matters only during induction
D) Doses may be doubled to make up for missed ones
Answer: A
Rationale: The chapter states that "A critical challenge during maintenance therapy is medication adherence because it has been shown that anything less than 95% adherence increases the risk of relapse," with treatment continuing for 2 to 3 years (p. 1087).
Trap: (D) proposes a correction that would risk toxicity without restoring the missed exposure; the 95% figure is unusually specific and is the reason adherence is monitored rather than assumed.

Q: A nurse is comparing acute lymphoblastic and acute myeloid leukemia. Which statement is accurate?
A) There is no established clinical staging system for acute myeloid leukemia, which is considered disseminated at diagnosis, and its treatment is more intense and shorter
B) Acute myeloid leukemia uses the same induction-through-maintenance sequence as lymphoblastic disease
C) Acute myeloid leukemia has a higher 5-year survival than lymphoblastic disease
D) Central nervous system irradiation is routine in acute myeloid leukemia
Answer: A
Rationale: The chapter states that "There is no established clinical staging system for AML; it is considered disseminated at diagnosis," that "Treatment for AML is generally more intense and of shorter duration than treatment for ALL," with maintenance not used in most regimens, and that "CNS irradiation is not usually used in AML treatment" (p. 1087).
Trap: (C) reverses the prognosis, since almost 80% of children with lymphoblastic disease live 5 years or longer while myeloid disease carries 68% 5-year survival under 15 years; note also the two distinctive myeloid skin findings, leukemia cutis in blue or purple and blue-green chloromas around the eyes.

Topic: childhood lymphomas

Q: A lymph node biopsy in an adolescent shows Reed-Sternberg cells. What does this establish?
A) Hodgkin lymphoma, since these cells are absent in the other lymphomas
B) Non-Hodgkin lymphoma, since these cells are characteristic of it
C) Either lymphoma, since the cells appear in both
D) Leukemia with nodal involvement
Answer: A
Rationale: The chapter states that "The presence of Hodgkin and Reed-Sternberg cells is considered diagnostic of Hodgkin disease because it is absent in the other lymphomas" (p. 1089).
Trap: (B) reverses the finding, and the consequence is more than nomenclature — the staging system used for Hodgkin lymphoma "is not used for NHL," so the diagnosis determines how the disease is staged as well as what it is called.

Q: A nurse is comparing the epidemiology of the childhood lymphomas. Which statement is accurate?
A) Non-Hodgkin lymphoma is more common in children overall, while Hodgkin disease shows a striking increase in ages 15 to 19
B) Hodgkin disease is more common in children than non-Hodgkin lymphoma at every age
C) Both peak in the preschool years
D) Hodgkin disease is most common before 5 years of age
Answer: A
Rationale: The chapter states that "In children NHL is more common than Hodgkin disease," and that "Hodgkin disease is extremely rare before 5 years of age, [with] a striking increase in children ages 15 to 19" (pp. 1088–1089).
Trap: (D) inverts the age distribution exactly — the rarity before 5 and the adolescent spike are what make age itself a useful discriminator when a lymphoma is suspected.

Q: An adolescent with a mediastinal lymphoma develops facial swelling and dyspnea that worsen when lying flat. What should the nurse anticipate?
A) Superior vena cava syndrome, requiring airway protection
B) Tumor lysis syndrome, requiring rasburicase
C) Spinal cord compression, requiring magnetic resonance imaging
D) Disseminated intravascular coagulation, requiring heparin
Answer: A
Rationale: The chapter names space-occupying chest lesions "especially from Hodgkin disease and NHL" as the cause of superior vena cava syndrome, presenting with cyanosis and edema of the face, neck, and upper chest with distended veins, and directs airway protection (p. 1078).
Trap: (C) is the other structural emergency in this chapter, but its initial manifestation is back pain rather than facial swelling, and magnetic resonance imaging is its gold standard; the positional worsening here points to venous obstruction.

Q: A child with a lymphoma develops back pain, extremity weakness, and loss of bladder control. What is the priority action?
A) Obtain magnetic resonance imaging, which is the gold standard for diagnosing spinal cord compression
B) Begin physical therapy for the weakness
C) Obtain a lumbar puncture to assess for malignant cells
D) Schedule a bone scan for the following week
Answer: A
Rationale: The chapter names back pain as "a common initial manifestation" of spinal cord compression, with sensation change, extremity weakness, loss of bowel and bladder function, and respiratory insufficiency, and states that "MRI is the gold standard for diagnosis," with high-dose steroids and emergent radiation or laminectomy if indicated (p. 1078).
Trap: (D) treats an emergency as routine follow-up, and the neurologic deficits here are time-critical; note that a lumbar puncture is also avoided in the presence of increased intracranial pressure elsewhere in this chapter.

Topic: childhood brain tumors

Q: A 6-year-old has headaches worst on awakening and vomiting unrelated to feeding, with ataxia and nystagmus. Which tumor location does this suggest?
A) Infratentorial, which accounts for about 60% of pediatric brain tumors
B) Supratentorial, which typically presents with seizures
C) Spinal, which presents with back pain
D) The location cannot be inferred from symptoms
Answer: A
Rationale: The chapter states that "the most common symptoms of infratentorial brain tumors are headache, especially on awakening, and vomiting that is not related to feeding," with cerebellar involvement producing nystagmus, ataxia, dysarthria, and dysmetria, and that about 60% of pediatric tumors are infratentorial (p. 1092).
Trap: (B) names the supratentorial picture, which "more commonly include seizures, personality or behavioral changes, visual disturbances, and hemiparesis"; the location matters because it determines the postoperative positioning order.

Q: A child has just returned from resection of an infratentorial brain tumor. How should the nurse position the child?
A) Flat, on either side, but not on the operative side
B) With the head elevated above the level of the heart
C) In Trendelenburg position to promote venous return
D) Prone with the head turned to the operative side
Answer: A
Rationale: The chapter gives site-specific positioning: infratentorial goes flat, on either side, not on the operative side; supratentorial goes with the head elevated above the heart (p. 1095).
Trap: (B) is correct for the other site and is the swap this pair exists to catch; (C) is contraindicated after both types, and the chapter directs notifying the practitioner before lowering the head even for impending shock.

Q: A nurse notes colorless drainage on the dressing of a child who had a craniotomy yesterday. What should the nurse do?
A) Report it immediately, since colorless drainage is cerebrospinal fluid
B) Reinforce the dressing and reassess at the end of the shift
C) Remove the dressing to inspect the wound
D) Document it as expected serous drainage
Answer: A
Rationale: The chapter directs that colorless drainage on a craniotomy dressing is cerebrospinal fluid and is reported immediately, along with sluggish, dilated, or unequal pupils (p. 1094).
Trap: (C) violates a separate instruction — reinforce a soiled craniotomy dressing rather than removing it — so an option that combines the wrong urgency with the wrong technique is doubly wrong; (D) normalizes the one drainage characteristic that signals a dural leak.

Q: A nurse is assessing a child with a brain tumor for signs of increased intracranial pressure. Which vital sign pattern is expected?
A) Decreased pulse and respirations, increased blood pressure, and decreased pulse pressure
B) Increased pulse and respirations, decreased blood pressure, and widened pulse pressure
C) Increased pulse with unchanged blood pressure
D) Decreased blood pressure with widened pulse pressure
Answer: A
Rationale: Table 29.4 lists decreased pulse and respiration, increased blood pressure, decreased pulse pressure, and hypothermia or hyperthermia among the vital sign findings, directing that pulse and respirations be monitored for 1 full minute with the pulse pressure recorded (p. 1092).
Trap: (B) is the shock pattern, which runs the opposite way on every value; the vomiting in this setting is also distinctive — progressively projectile, worse in the morning, and relieved by moving about and changing position.

Q: A child with a suspected brain tumor and signs of increased intracranial pressure is scheduled for diagnostic testing. Which test does the chapter identify as the gold standard, and which is avoided?
A) Magnetic resonance imaging is the gold standard, and lumbar puncture is avoided because of the danger of brainstem herniation
B) Computed tomography is the gold standard, and magnetic resonance imaging is avoided
C) Lumbar puncture is the gold standard, and imaging is avoided
D) Electroencephalography is the gold standard, and imaging is avoided
Answer: A
Rationale: The chapter states that "The gold standard diagnostic procedure is MRI," with computed tomography used in urgent cases when magnetic resonance imaging is unavailable, and that "In the presence of increased ICP, LP is avoided due to the danger of possible brainstem" herniation (p. 1092).
Trap: (C) proposes the one procedure the chapter specifically warns against in this setting, and the reason is mechanical rather than diagnostic — removing fluid below an obstructed compartment invites herniation.

Topic: neuroblastoma and wilms tumor

Q: A 2-year-old has a firm, nontender, irregular abdominal mass that crosses the midline. Which tumor does this suggest?
A) Neuroblastoma
B) Wilms tumor
C) Rhabdomyosarcoma
D) Hepatoblastoma
Answer: A
Rationale: The chapter states that with abdominal tumors "the most common presenting sign is a firm, nontender, irregular mass in the abdomen that crosses the midline (in contrast to Wilms tumor, which is usually confined to one side)" (p. 1096).
Trap: (B) is the confusable partner, and midline crossing is the single discriminator the chapter gives explicitly; the median age also differs, about 19 months for neuroblastoma and 44 months for unilateral Wilms tumor.

Q: A child is being evaluated for suspected neuroblastoma. Which laboratory study does the chapter name?
A) Urinary catecholamine metabolites, vanillylmandelic acid and homovanillic acid
B) Serum alpha-fetoprotein
C) Urinary protein and creatinine ratio
D) Serum renin
Answer: A
Rationale: The chapter states that because neuroblastomas excrete epinephrine and norepinephrine, "urinary excretion of catecholamine metabolites (vanillylmandelic acid [VMA] and homovanillic acid [HVA]) is measured before therapy," and these markers monitor response and detect relapse (p. 1096).
Trap: (D) belongs to Wilms tumor, where hypertension arises from excess renin secretion; each tumor has its own biochemical signature, which is another way the two abdominal masses separate.

Q: A 3-year-old is admitted with a suspected Wilms tumor. Which nursing action is essential?
A) Do not palpate the abdomen, and post a sign on the bed
B) Palpate the abdomen once each shift to track the mass size
C) Measure abdominal girth every 2 hours with deep palpation
D) Encourage the child to lie prone to reduce pressure on the mass
Answer: A
Rationale: The chapter states that "the most important" preoperative concern is "not to palpate the tumor unless absolutely necessary because manipulation of the mass may cause dissemination of cancer cells to adjacent and distant sites," and a Nursing Alert directs posting a sign on the bed reading "Do not palpate abdomen" (p. 1100).
Trap: (B) and (C) are both ordinary nursing assessments that are specifically harmful here, which is what makes this the chapter's clearest single nursing action; careful bathing and handling also matter in preventing trauma to the tumor site.

Q: A child with a Wilms tumor has a blood pressure of 132/86 mm Hg. What explains this?
A) Excess renin secretion by the tumor
B) Pain from abdominal distention
C) Fluid overload from preoperative hydration
D) Catecholamine secretion by the tumor
Answer: A
Rationale: The chapter states that "Hypertension, probably caused by secretion of excess amounts of renin by the tumor, occurs occasionally," and directs monitoring blood pressure preoperatively for this reason (pp. 1099–1100).
Trap: (D) names the neuroblastoma mechanism; note that the mass in Wilms tumor is "firm, nontender, confined to one side, and deep within the flank," and unlike the liver "it does not move with respiration," while hematuria occurs in fewer than one-fourth of children.

Q: A child is recovering from nephrectomy for Wilms tumor. Which assessment is a priority because of a specific treatment complication?
A) Gastrointestinal activity, because vincristine-induced adynamic ileus can cause intestinal obstruction
B) Neurologic status, because of the risk of intracranial hemorrhage
C) Respiratory rate, because of the risk of pulmonary edema
D) Urine output only, since the remaining kidney is the sole concern
Answer: A
Rationale: The chapter directs monitoring gastrointestinal activity — bowel movements, bowel sounds, distention, and vomiting — because of the risk of intestinal obstruction from vincristine-induced adynamic ileus, radiation-induced edema, and postsurgical adhesions, along with frequent blood pressure evaluation and observation for infection (p. 1100).
Trap: (D) narrows the assessment to the obvious organ and misses the drug effect, which is what makes this complication easy to overlook; the recovery period is otherwise described as usually rapid.

Topic: bone tumors and retinoblastoma

Q: An adolescent has localized pain in the distal femur that is relieved by holding the leg flexed. A radiograph shows a lesion in the metaphysis. Which tumor is most likely?
A) Osteosarcoma
B) Ewing sarcoma
C) Rhabdomyosarcoma
D) Osteomyelitis
Answer: A
Rationale: The chapter identifies osteosarcoma as "the most common bone tumor in adolescents and young adults," arising from bone-forming mesenchyme in the metaphyseal region, most often the distal femur, and notes that bone tumor pain "is often relieved by a flexed position, which relaxes the muscles overlying the stretched periosteum" (p. 1097).
Trap: (B) arises "in the marrow spaces of the bone rather than from osseous tissue," in the shaft rather than the metaphysis; note the shared trap that this pain is often attributed to trauma or dismissed as growing pains.

Q: A child with a bone tumor has fever and systemic symptoms in addition to localized pain. Which tumor does this favor, and how does treatment differ?
A) Ewing sarcoma, which more often has systemic symptoms and is treated with radiation
B) Osteosarcoma, which more often has systemic symptoms and is treated with radiation
C) Ewing sarcoma, which is treated surgically without radiation
D) Osteosarcoma, which is treated with radiation rather than surgery
Answer: A
Rationale: The chapter states that "Systemic symptoms (such as fever) and other clinical symptoms (such as spinal cord compression and respiratory distress) are more frequent in patients with Ewing sarcoma," which is treated with radiation, unlike osteosarcoma (pp. 1097–1099).
Trap: (D) assigns radiation to the wrong tumor, which matters because the treatment difference is one of the two clean discriminators between them; the other is the site of origin, marrow space against osseous tissue.

Q: A 2-year-old's parents notice a whitish glow in one pupil in photographs, and the child has developed strabismus. What is the likely diagnosis and its usual prognosis?
A) Retinoblastoma, with an overall 10-year survival near 90%
B) Congenital cataract, which does not affect survival
C) Retinoblastoma, with an overall survival below 50%
D) Optic glioma, which presents with proptosis
Answer: A
Rationale: The chapter names leukocoria as the first sign of retinoblastoma and strabismus as "The next most common sign... resulting from poor fixation of the visually impaired eye," and states that "The overall 10-year survival rate is nearly 90% for unilateral and bilateral tumors" (pp. 1102–1103).
Trap: (C) understates a prognosis that is among the best in this chapter; note that blindness is usually a late sign, and that retinoblastoma, like neuroblastoma, may spontaneously regress.

Q: Parents of a child with retinoblastoma ask whether their other children are at risk. What should the nurse know about the hereditary pattern?
A) About 25% are bilateral and hereditary and 15% are unilateral and hereditary, with a germline RB1 mutation present in the hereditary form
B) All cases are hereditary and all siblings will be affected
C) No cases are hereditary, so siblings carry no increased risk
D) Only bilateral cases can be hereditary, so unilateral disease carries no family risk
Answer: A
Rationale: The chapter states that "60% are unilateral and nonhereditary (also called sporadic), 25% are bilateral and hereditary, and 15% are unilateral and hereditary," with a germline RB1 mutation in the hereditary form, and that children with hereditary disease tend to be diagnosed at a younger age (p. 1102).
Trap: (D) is the intuitive assumption that bilaterality equals heritability, and the 15% unilateral-hereditary group is exactly what it misses; blood and tumor samples can be tested for the mutation, which is how that group is identified.
