# Chapter 2 drills — Social, Cultural, Religious, and Family Influences on Child Health Promotion

Authored from `sources/Ch2_Fact_Bank.md` (Hockenberry, Wilson & Rodgers, 11th ed.,
printed pp. 15–38).

Blueprint weight is **6 questions of 202 (3.0%), depth `standard`** — 45 drills
across 10 topics, at the top of the 30–45 band because the chapter is 24 printed
pages carrying far more enumerable content than its weight can buy.

The fact bank says outright that this chapter has two halves tested very
differently: pp. 15–28 are family structure, parenting, and discipline —
concrete, enumerable, full of named categories that map onto answer options — and
pp. 28–38 are sociocultural context, discursive and yielding fewer testable
specifics per page. This drill set follows that asymmetry. Roughly two-thirds of
the questions come from the first half.

The other structural fact worth naming: **this chapter thinks in threes.** Three
family theories, three parenting styles, three types of consequences, three
divorce phases, three custody arrangements. Every triad is a ready-made item
whose distractor is an invented fourth member, and the chapter's own NCLEX
question 3 is built exactly that way — so several items here key on knowing the
set is closed.

**What a `standard` band could not buy, and why.** These were read and cut:

- **Schools, peer cultures, and community** (pp. 29–30), including school
  connectedness and its four contributors. Discursive, and no confusable pair.
- **Social and mass media** (pp. 30–31), Box 2.6 and Table 2.2, and the phone and
  screen-time numbers. The 2-hour screen limit for ages 2 and older is drilled in
  Chapter 1's media item from the AAP guidance, so the loss is partial.
- **Family strengths (Box 2.3), family roles and role learning, and family size
  and configuration** (pp. 19–20), apart from the small-family inversion.
- **Parental education and poverty screening** (p. 33) and the detail of
  **immigration and refugee health** (pp. 33–34), kept only as far as the
  disparities framing carries them.
- **Box 2.4's three divorce phases** (p. 24) and the **foster-parent training
  hours** (27 initial, 12 yearly, p. 28). Both are isolated facts that connect to
  nothing else in the chapter; the divorce items that survived are the ones that
  change what a nurse says to a family.

Every confusable pair the bank flags is drilled from both ends, since `standard`
has room for it: consanguineous and affinal, traditional nuclear and nuclear,
stepsiblings and half-siblings, positive and negative feedback, discipline and
limit setting, the three consequence types, the three parenting styles, the three
custody types, race and ethnicity, religion and spirituality, cultural competence
and cultural humility, disparities and inequities.

Topic: family definitions and structure types

Q: A nursing student asks the instructor for the textbook's definition of family. What is the accurate response?
A) A family is a married couple and their dependent children
B) There is no universal definition of family; a family is what an individual considers it to be
C) A family is any group sharing a household and pooling income
D) The Census Bureau's four categories are the accepted definition
Answer: B
Rationale: The chapter opens by refusing a single definition: "The term family has been defined in many different ways according to the individual's own frame of reference, values, or discipline. There is no universal definition of family; a family is what an individual considers it to be" (p. 15).
Trap: (D) is the reflex pick for a learner who has just memorized the four Census definitions. Those four are a classification the Census Bureau uses, not the chapter's definition of the term, and the chapter names numerous other family types outside them.

Q: A child lives with his mother, his mother's unmarried partner, and a sibling who is the partner's biologic child. No other relatives live in the home. Which Census Bureau category does this household fit?
A) Traditional nuclear family
B) Nuclear family
C) Extended family
D) Blended family only, since the parents are unmarried
Answer: B
Rationale: The nuclear family is "composed of two parents and their children. The parent-child relationship may be biologic, step, adoptive, or foster. Sibling ties may be biologic, step, half, or adoptive. The parents are not necessarily married. No other relatives or nonrelatives are present in the household" (p. 18).
Trap: (A) is the pick that misses two requirements at once. The traditional nuclear family requires the couple to be **married** and all children to be **biologic**; the plain nuclear family requires neither. A distractor that makes marriage the only difference gets half the discrimination.

Q: Two children in a household share the same mother but have different fathers. How are they related?
A) Stepsiblings, because one father is not the biologic parent of both
B) Half-siblings, because they share one biologic parent
C) Stepsiblings, because the household is blended
D) Full siblings, because they share a residence and a mother
Answer: B
Rationale: "Stepsiblings do not share a common biologic parent; the biologic parent of one child is the stepparent of the other. Half-siblings share only one biologic parent" (p. 18).
Trap: (A) and (C) are the mirror error of the pair. Stepsiblings share **no** biologic parent; half-siblings share **one**. That single count is the whole distinction, and either presence of a stepparent in the home tempts the wrong label.

Q: A nurse admits a child whose grandmother brings him in, states that she has reared him since infancy, and answers all history questions while the child's young mother stands quietly beside her. What should the nurse recognize about this extended family?
A) The mother is the legal decision-maker and must be addressed directly for all teaching
B) The older relative may hold the authority and make decisions in consultation with the young parents, so identifying the actual decision-maker is part of the assessment
C) The grandmother's involvement suggests the mother is neglectful and warrants a referral
D) Extended family arrangements delay care and should be discouraged
Answer: B
Rationale: "Within the extended family, grandparents often find themselves rearing their grandchildren. Young parents are often considered too young or too inexperienced to make decisions independently. Often, the older relative holds the authority and makes decisions in consultation with the young parents" (p. 18). Sharing a residence also "assists with the management of scarce resources and provides child care for working families."
Trap: (C) reads a culturally patterned arrangement as a red flag. The chapter presents extended-family authority descriptively and connects it to its later instruction to ask families what the nurse should know about them rather than to apply assumptions.

Topic: family theories and developmental stages
Subtopic: family systems and stress theory

Q: A nurse notes that a father's job loss has changed the mother's work hours, the children's school arrangements, and the family's mealtimes. Which family systems theory assumption does this illustrate?
A) Circular causality — a change in any one part of a family system affects all other parts
B) Direct causality — each change produces a single predictable effect
C) Morphostasis — the family resists all change
D) Boundary maintenance — the family closes itself to the environment
Answer: A
Rationale: Table 2.1 states the assumption as "A change in any one part of a family system affects all other parts of the family system (circular causality)" (p. 15). General systems theory "expanded scientific thought from a simplistic view of direct cause and effect."
Trap: (B) is the chapter's own NCLEX question 2 distractor, and it is a one-word inversion of the printed line — direct for circular. That question's other distractors work the same way: react for initiate, primarily for as-well-as.

Q: A family in crisis begins praising a child's small successes to encourage a change in his behavior, while a grandparent insists the household routine stay exactly as it always has been. Which two processes are at work?
A) Positive feedback in the praise, since praise is by definition positive feedback, and negative feedback in the criticism
B) Positive feedback in the effort to initiate change, and negative feedback in the effort to resist it
C) Morphogenesis in the grandparent's stance and morphostasis in the parents'
D) Neither; feedback applies only to communication, not to behavior change
Answer: B
Rationale: "Positive feedback initiates change; negative feedback resists change" (p. 16). Feedback processes "help identify strengths and needs and determine how well goals are accomplished."
Trap: (A) is the reflex pick and the chapter's flagged inversion: positive and negative here mean change-initiating and change-resisting, **not** praise and criticism. (C) reverses the other pair — morphogenesis is change, morphostasis is no change, and "a balance between morphogenesis (change) and morphostasis (no change) is necessary."

Q: A couple describes the birth of their first child as joyful and also exhausting and destabilizing. Which family stress theory assumption applies?
A) Only negative events function as stressors for a family system
B) Stress is an inevitable part of family life, and any event, even if positive, can be stressful for the family
C) Stressors affect individual members but not the family as a unit
D) Normative transitions are excluded from stress theory, which addresses situational stressors only
Answer: B
Rationale: Table 2.1's first family stress theory assumption is that "Stress is an inevitable part of family life, and any event, even if positive, can be stressful for family," and the family "encounters both normative expected stressors and unexpected situational stressors over the life cycle." Transition to parenthood is listed first among the theory's applications (p. 15).
Trap: (A) restricts stressors to negative events, which is the intuitive reading and the one the assumption is written to exclude. (D) drops half the framework — both normative and situational stressors are named.

Subtopic: developmental theory and Duvall's stages

Q: A remarried couple has a 14-year-old from one partner's first marriage and a 2-year-old together. A student tries to place them in Duvall's stages and cannot. What explains the difficulty?
A) Duvall's stages apply only to families with more than two children
B) The stage marker is the age of the oldest child, which makes the model hard to apply to stepfamilies and single-parent families
C) The stage marker is the age of the youngest child, and the model has no stage for toddlers
D) Duvall's model was replaced by family systems theory and no longer applies
Answer: B
Rationale: "This theory is based on the predictable changes in the family's structure, function, and roles, with the age of the oldest child as the marker for stage transition" (p. 17), and Table 2.1 lists as a limitation that "Use of age of oldest child and marital duration as marker of stage transition sometimes problematic (e.g., in stepfamilies, single-parent families)."
Trap: (C) inverts the marker, which is the most likely single item from this section — the chapter states "oldest" twice, once in the narrative and once in the table's limitations.

Q: Which statements accurately describe Duvall's Developmental Stages of the Family? (Select all that apply.)
A) They are stages families progress through in adulthood
B) They designate how parenting progresses as a child develops
C) They describe the journey a couple will take as their children mature
D) They are stages an individual progresses through in moral and spiritual development
E) They designate appropriate discipline related to developmental stages
Answer: A, B, C
Rationale: These are the keyed options in the chapter's own NCLEX question 1 (p. 38). Stages I through VIII run from marriage to aging families, entirely in adulthood; stages II through VI are named by the children's stage; and the couple thread runs throughout — stage I is "Reestablish couple identity" and stage VII is "Reinvest in couple identity" (p. 17).
Trap: (D) and (E) are the two to reject, and both fail the same way — they treat the stages as a schedule for the *child*. Duvall described "eight developmental tasks of the family," the cycle begins before children arrive and continues after they leave, and no stage in Box 2.1 concerns discipline.

Topic: parenting styles

Q: A father states that his household rules are to be followed without discussion, and that when his daughter asks why, he tells her "because I say so." Which parenting style does this describe?
A) Authoritative
B) Authoritarian
C) Permissive
D) Neglectful
Answer: B
Rationale: Authoritarian parents "try to control their children's behavior and attitudes through unquestioned mandates" and establish standards of conduct "that they expect to be followed rigidly and unquestioningly." The chapter gives the message as "Do it because I say so" (pp. 20–21).
Trap: (A) is the reflex pick because the two words differ by four letters and both involve firm rules. The authoritative parent's control is firm but explained; the authoritarian parent's is firm and not open to question.

Q: A mother says she explains her reasoning for household rules and asks her son's opinion before deciding, but she rarely enforces the rules and does not want to upset the household routine. Which style does this describe, and what distinguishes it?
A) Authoritative, because she explains her reasoning and consults the child
B) Permissive, because explaining reasons does not distinguish the styles — control does, and she exerts little or none
C) Authoritarian, because she sets the rules unilaterally
D) It cannot be classified without knowing whether she uses corporal punishment
Answer: B
Rationale: Permissive parents "exert little or no control over their children's actions," employ "lax, inconsistent discipline; do not set sensible limits," and "rarely punish the children" — and yet, if rules exist, "the parents explain the underlying reason, elicit the children's opinions, and consult them in decision-making processes" (p. 21).
Trap: (A) is the trap the fact bank flags explicitly: **permissive parents do explain reasons**, and so do authoritative parents. Explaining is not the discriminator. The authoritative parent explains and then enforces "firm and consistent" control; the permissive parent explains and does not enforce.

Q: A nurse describes a child who is self-reliant, assertive, inquisitive, content, and highly interactive with peers, and who behaves well because of an internal sense of right and wrong rather than fear of being caught. Which parenting style does the chapter associate with this outcome?
A) Permissive
B) Authoritarian
C) Authoritative
D) No style is associated with a predictable outcome
Answer: C
Rationale: Authoritative parenting produces "children with high self-esteem who are self-reliant, assertive, inquisitive, content, and highly interactive with other children," and fosters "inner-directedness, a conscience that regulates behavior based on feelings of guilt or shame for wrongdoing, not on fear of being caught or punished" (p. 21).
Trap: (B) can produce children who are "courteous, loyal, honest, and dependable, but docile" — and only conditionally, when "close supervision and affection accompany parental authority." Without that affection the same style "may be associated with both defiant and antisocial behaviors," so a distractor stating the authoritarian outcome unconditionally is wrong.

Q: A nurse recognizes that a family's parenting style differs from her own upbringing and from what she considers ideal. What does the chapter direct her to do?
A) Teach the parents that authoritative parenting produces the best outcomes
B) Give differing styles equal respect, since childrearing is a culturally bound phenomenon
C) Document the style as a risk factor and refer to social work
D) Withhold parenting guidance until the family adopts an authoritative approach
Answer: B
Rationale: "There are differing philosophies in regard to parenting. Childrearing is a culturally bound phenomenon, and children are socialized to behave in ways that are important to their family," and "When working with individual families, nurses should give these differing styles equal respect" (p. 21).
Trap: (A) is the reflex pick because the chapter really does describe the authoritative outcomes most favorably, and a learner converts that into a teaching mandate. The chapter does not rank the styles for the nurse. The teachable content is specific practices — limits, consistency, timing — not the label on a parent's style.

Topic: limit setting and types of discipline

Q: A parent asks the nurse to explain the difference between limit setting and discipline. Which response is accurate?
A) They are two words for the same childrearing activity
B) Limit setting establishes the rules or guidelines for behavior; discipline, in its narrow sense, is the action taken to enforce the rules after noncompliance
C) Limit setting is used with young children and discipline with older children
D) Discipline establishes the rules and limit setting enforces them
Answer: B
Rationale: Discipline "means to teach or refers to a set of rules governing conduct. In a narrower sense, it refers to the action taken to enforce the rules after noncompliance." Limit setting "refers to establishing the rules or guidelines for behavior" (p. 21). The relationship between them: "The clearer the limits that are set and the more consistently they are enforced, the less need there is for disciplinary action."
Trap: (D) is the mirror error — a straight swap of the two definitions. (A) is the more common one in practice, and the chapter separates the terms precisely because they are used interchangeably.

Q: A mother says her 3-year-old constantly tests the household rules and asks how to stop this. What should the nurse teach?
A) Limit testing is misbehavior and should be extinguished by ignoring it
B) Children want and need limits, and testing them is how children learn the extent to which they can manipulate their environment and gain reassurance that others will protect them
C) Limit testing indicates the limits are too strict and should be relaxed
D) Limit testing at this age suggests a developmental concern warranting referral
Answer: B
Rationale: "Children want and need limits. Unrestricted freedom is a threat to their security and safety. By testing the limits imposed on them, children learn the extent to which they can manipulate their environment and gain reassurance from knowing that others are there to protect them from potential harm." The chapter lists "Test their limits of control" as the **first** function limits and discipline serve (p. 21).
Trap: (A) treats limit-testing as a behavior problem when the chapter names it as a function limits are there to serve. This is the inversion the fact bank flags.

Q: Which principles are among the nine guidelines for implementing discipline? (Select all that apply.)
A) Consistency — implement disciplinary action exactly as agreed on and for each infraction
B) Timing — initiate discipline as soon as the child misbehaves
C) Behavior orientation — always disapprove of the behavior, not the child
D) Termination — after the discipline is administered, consider the child as having a clean slate and avoid bringing up the incident or lecturing
E) Escalation — increase the severity of the consequence with each repetition of the behavior
Answer: A, B, C, D
Rationale: The Family-Centered Care box on implementing discipline lists nine principles: consistency, timing, commitment, unity, flexibility, planning, behavior orientation, privacy, and termination (p. 21).
Trap: (E) is the one to reject, and it is the mechanism the chapter names as the third flaw of *corporal punishment* — children "become accustomed to spanking, requiring more severe corporal punishment over time" (p. 23). Escalation is a failure mode in this chapter, never a principle.

Q: A mother tells her misbehaving son in the grocery store, "Wait until your father gets home." How should the nurse evaluate this?
A) Appropriate, because it avoids embarrassing the child in public
B) Problematic on two counts: delaying punishment weakens its intent, and the phrase conveys negative messages about the other parent
C) Appropriate, because it gives the mother time to calm down before disciplining
D) Problematic only because it delays the consequence
Answer: B
Rationale: "Delaying punishment weakens its intent, and practices such as telling the child, Wait until your father comes home are not only ineffectual but also convey negative messages about the other parent" (p. 21). It also violates the Unity principle, which exists "to prevent confusion and alliances between child and one parent."
Trap: (A) is close to a real exception and misapplies it. When delay is unavoidable — to avoid embarrassment, for instance — the Timing principle requires the parent to "verbally disapprove of the behavior and state that disciplinary action will be implemented." Announced deferral is permitted; silent deferral to the other parent is not. (D) catches only half the fault.

Q: A parent reports that since she began ignoring her son's tantrums, the tantrums have become louder and more frequent, and she is ready to abandon the plan. What should the nurse tell her?
A) The plan has failed and a different strategy should be selected
B) This is a response burst — the undesired behavior increases after ignoring is initiated because the child is testing whether the parents are serious — and the plan should continue
C) The tantrums indicate an underlying disorder and warrant evaluation
D) She should ignore the tantrums only when they are mild
Answer: B
Rationale: Response burst is "a phenomenon that occurs when the undesired behavior increases after ignoring is initiated because the child is testing the parents to see if they are serious about the plan" (p. 22). Being aware of it is one of the four requirements for ignoring to work. The chapter warns that when parents "give in and resort to previous patterns of discipline... the behavior is actually reinforced because the child learns that persistence gains parental attention."
Trap: (A) is the intuitive and the harmful reading, and it is the specific error the chapter is written to prevent. Abandoning the plan mid-burst teaches the child that persistence works — the outcome worse than never having started.

Q: A parent lists the types of consequences as natural, logical, unrelated, and transforming. Which statement requires correction?
A) Natural consequences are those that occur without any intervention
B) Logical consequences are those directly related to the rule
C) Transforming consequences allow the child to come to the conclusion on his or her own
D) Unrelated consequences are those imposed deliberately
Answer: C
Rationale: "The strategy of consequences involves allowing children to experience the results of their misbehavior. It includes three types" — natural, logical, and unrelated (p. 22). There is no transforming type. This is the chapter's own NCLEX question 3, keyed to the invented fourth category.
Trap: (A), (B), and (D) are all verbatim-correct definitions, so a learner searching for a factual error finds none and guesses. Knowing the set is closed at three is sufficient to answer. Note also that time-out is an **unrelated** consequence, stated twice, and that natural consequences are preferred but "effective if they are meaningful to children" — the chapter's own messy-room example shows a natural consequence failing where a logical one works.

Topic: time-out and corporal punishment

Q: A parent asks how long her 4-year-old should sit in time-out. What should the nurse advise?
A) Four minutes, following the rule of 1 minute per year of age
B) Ten minutes, a standard duration for preschoolers
C) The duration should be scaled to how serious the misbehavior was
D) Until the child apologizes
Answer: A
Rationale: "A rule for the length of time-out is 1 minute per year of age; use a kitchen timer with an audible bell to record the time rather than a watch" (p. 23).
Trap: (C) is the reflex pick because scaling punishment to severity feels like fairness, and the chapter's Flexibility principle does mention severity — but that principle governs which *strategy* is chosen, not the length of a time-out. The duration rule is fixed to age.

Q: The same parent reports that her son screams for the first several minutes of every time-out, and asks whether that time counts. What is the correct guidance?
A) The time counts from the moment the child is sent, regardless of behavior
B) The time-out period begins after the child quiets down
C) The time-out should be ended early if the child becomes upset
D) An additional minute should be added for each outburst
Answer: B
Rationale: The published procedure states: "If they cry, refuse, or display any disruptive behavior, the time-out period will begin after they quiet down," and "When they are quiet for the duration of the time, they can then leave the room" (p. 23).
Trap: (A) is the mechanic most often gotten wrong, and it is what makes time-out fail for exactly this child — a 4-year-old who screams for five minutes serves no quiet time at all. Note also that the audible kitchen timer is specified rather than a watch, so the endpoint is signalled objectively rather than by the parent.

Q: A parent asks where time-out should take place and how many warnings to give. Which response reflects the chapter's procedure?
A) Send the child to the bedroom immediately, with no warning
B) Give one warning; if the child does not obey, use a safe, convenient, unstimulating area where the child can be monitored, such as a bathroom, hallway, or laundry room
C) Give three warnings before any consequence, then use the child's bedroom
D) Any location will work as long as the parent remains present for the entire time
Answer: B
Rationale: The Family-Centered Care box specifies one warning before time-out, and an area "that is safe, convenient, and unstimulating, but where the child can be monitored, such as the bathroom, hallway, or laundry room" (p. 23). Time-out must also "be planned in advance," and one of its named advantages is that "the parent does not need to be present for all of the time-out."
Trap: (A) and (C) both miss the single warning, which is part of the published procedure — not zero, not repeated. The bedroom is a poor choice by the chapter's own standard because it is stimulating; time-out is described as "a refinement of the common practice of sending the child to his or her room," not a synonym for it.

Q: A father says spanking is the only thing that works, and the nurse observes that his son does stop the behavior immediately when spanked. Which response reflects the chapter's argument?
A) Spanking does not actually change behavior, so another approach is needed
B) Spanking causes a dramatic short-term decrease in the behavior, but it teaches that violence is acceptable, may physically harm the child if it results from parental rage, and requires more severe punishment over time as the child becomes accustomed to it
C) Spanking is acceptable if it is administered calmly and never leaves a mark
D) Spanking is appropriate for safety-related behaviors only
Answer: B
Rationale: "Based on the principles of aversive therapy, inflicting pain through spanking causes a dramatic short-term decrease in the behavior," and the chapter numbers three serious flaws: it teaches that violence is acceptable, it may physically harm the child if it results from parental rage, and "children become accustomed to spanking, requiring more severe corporal punishment over time" (p. 23).
Trap: (A) is the reflex counterargument and is **not** this chapter's position — it concedes the short-term effect explicitly, and that concession is what makes the flaws the answer. The escalation flaw is the one distractors omit, and it is the mechanism by which spanking becomes physical abuse.

Topic: divorce process and children's responses

Q: A nurse is asked what most determines how a child fares after a divorce. What does the chapter identify?
A) The child's age at the time of the divorce
B) The child's sex
C) Family characteristics, including the quality of the parent-child relationship and the level of ongoing family conflict, which are more crucial than child characteristics such as age or sex
D) Whether the divorce was contested
Answer: C
Rationale: "Family characteristics are more crucial to the child's well-being than specific child characteristics, such as age or sex," and "High levels of ongoing family conflict are related to problems of social development, emotional stability, and cognitive skills for the child" (p. 25).
Trap: (A) is the superseded belief the chapter names directly: "Previously, researchers believed that divorce had a greater impact on younger children, but recent observations indicate that divorce constitutes a major disruption for children of all ages." For scale, all children "suffer stress second only to the stress produced by the death of a parent."

Q: A 4-year-old whose parents are divorcing has begun sucking her thumb again, has lost bladder control at night, and tells the nurse it is her fault her father left. How should the nurse interpret this?
A) These are expected responses for a preschool child, including regressive behaviors and self-blame
B) The self-blame indicates the parents have told the child something inappropriate
C) Regression at this age indicates a developmental disorder
D) These responses are expected in school-age children but are premature at 4 years
Answer: A
Rationale: Box 2.5 lists for early preschool children (2 to 3 years) "Blame themselves for the divorce," "Fear of abandonment," and "Regressive behaviors (e.g., thumb sucking, loss of elimination control)," and for later preschool children (3 to 5 years) "Fear of abandonment" and "Blame themselves for the divorce; decreased self-esteem" (p. 25).
Trap: (B) reads a normal developmental response as evidence of parental error. Self-blame at this age comes from magical thinking, and the fact bank notes it is the same mechanism Chapter 19 describes for a child's understanding of death.

Q: A 7-year-old and an 11-year-old whose parents are divorcing are compared. Which difference does Box 2.5 describe?
A) The 7-year-old can express anger at the parents; the 11-year-old cannot
B) The 7-year-old has difficulty expressing anger at the parents and holds an intense desire for their reconciliation; the 11-year-old is able to express anger and may feel divided loyalties and a desire for revenge
C) Both age groups are described identically in the box
D) Only the 11-year-old shows a decline in school performance
Answer: B
Rationale: The middle school-age row (6 to 8 years) lists "Difficulty expressing anger at parents" and "Intense desire for reconciliation of parents"; the later school-age row (9 to 12 years) lists "Ability to express feelings of anger," "Divided loyalties," and "Desire for revenge; may wish to punish the parent they hold responsible" (p. 25).
Trap: (A) is the mirror error of the real flip, which the fact bank calls the cleanest age discrimination in the box. (D) is wrong because decline in school performance appears in **both** rows — it does not discriminate.

Q: Divorcing parents ask the nurse how and when to tell their three children. Which guidance reflects the chapter's protocol?
A) Tell each child separately, at the child's own developmental level, and avoid a group discussion
B) Tell the children together with both parents and all siblings present, followed by individual discussions with each child, during a period of calm rather than after an argument
C) Wait until the separation is complete so the children have concrete information
D) Tell only the school-age children; the preschooler will not understand
Answer: B
Rationale: "If possible, the initial disclosure should include both parents and siblings, followed by individual discussions with each child," with sufficient time set aside, "during a period of calm, not after an argument" (p. 26). The content "should include the reason for the divorce, if age appropriate, and reassurance that the divorce is not the fault of the children."
Trap: (A) and (C) each capture half a real recommendation and drop the other half. The order is group **then** individual, not one or the other. (C) is the reluctance the chapter names and rejects: without preparation, children experience "vague, uneasy feelings that are more difficult to cope with than being told the truth."

Topic: custody arrangements and coparenting

Q: A court awards custody of two sons to the father and two daughters to the mother. Which custody arrangement is this?
A) Joint physical custody
B) Joint legal custody
C) Divided, or split, custody
D) Binuclear custody
Answer: C
Rationale: "Divided custody, or split custody, means that each parent is awarded custody of one or more of the children, thereby separating siblings. For example, sons might live with the father and daughters with the mother" (p. 26).
Trap: (A) pulls because "divided" sounds like divided time. The name is misleading in exactly that way: divided custody divides the **children**, not the schedule, and separating siblings is its defining consequence.

Q: A child lives with her mother, and both parents remain her legal guardians and participate in childrearing decisions. Which custody arrangement is this?
A) Joint legal custody
B) Joint physical custody
C) Divided custody
D) Sole custody
Answer: A
Rationale: In joint legal custody "the children reside with one parent, but both parents are the children's legal guardians, and both participate in childrearing" (p. 27).
Trap: (B) is the mirror error of the next item's arrangement and the swap the fact bank flags. The discriminating word is in the name: **physical** custody is about where the child physically lives, and in joint physical custody "the parents alternate the physical care and control of the children on an agreed-on basis."

Q: Parents who live a few blocks apart alternate their son's residence week by week and share parenting responsibilities legally. For which families does the chapter say this arrangement works well?
A) Families in which one parent has substantially greater financial resources
B) Families who live close to each other and whose occupations permit an active role in the care and rearing of the children
C) Families in which the child is old enough to choose the schedule
D) Families in which the parents have no continuing conflict of any kind
Answer: B
Rationale: Of joint physical custody the chapter says "This custody arrangement works well for families who live close to each other and whose occupations permit an active role in the care and rearing of the children" (p. 27).
Trap: (D) overstates the requirement into something no divorcing family could meet. What coparenting actually requires is that parents be "highly committed to provide normal parenting and to separate their marital conflicts from their parenting roles" — separating the conflict from the parenting, not eliminating it.

Topic: single parenting and alternative caregiving arrangements
Subtopic: single-parent families

Q: A single mother tells the nurse that what wears on her most is not being a single parent but being poor. How does the chapter frame this?
A) Her perception is unusual and should be explored for depression
B) The chapter states that the stigma of poverty may be more keenly felt than the discrimination associated with being a single parent
C) The two stigmas are described as equivalent
D) The chapter does not address stigma in single-parent families
Answer: B
Rationale: "In fact, the stigma of poverty may be more keenly felt than the discrimination associated with being a single parent" (p. 27). For scale, "In 2015, 35% of single-parent families had incomes below the poverty line," and 35% of children under 18 lived in single-parent families.
Trap: (A) treats a claim the chapter makes explicitly as an individual distortion. The chapter's ranking is the point, and a distractor identifies single parenthood itself as the primary source of felt discrimination.

Q: A community program is being designed for single-parent families. Which supports does the chapter name as needed? (Select all that apply.)
A) Health care services that are open on evenings and weekends
B) High-quality child care
C) Respite child care to relieve parental exhaustion and prevent burnout
D) Parent enhancement centers for advancing education and job skills, recreation, and parenting education
E) Mandatory parenting classes as a condition of receiving public assistance
Answer: A, B, C, D
Rationale: The chapter names these four supports, and adds that "Single parents need social contacts separate from their children for their own emotional growth and that of their children" (p. 27). Parents Without Partners, Inc. is the named organization.
Trap: (E) is the one to reject. It converts a support into a condition, which reverses the intent of the whole list. Note that evening and weekend health care hours are named **first**, and the same systems-level factor recurs on p. 34 as "extended evening hours."

Subtopic: child care and working mothers

Q: A mother returning to work worries that her employment will harm her preschooler. What does the chapter say?
A) Maternal employment is associated with poorer preschool health outcomes and should be delayed if possible
B) Maternal employment may have variable effects, and the persistent concern for all working parents is the quality of child care
C) Maternal employment has no effect on children at any age
D) Effects depend entirely on the number of hours worked
Answer: B
Rationale: "Working mothers have become the norm in the United States," "Maternal employment may have variable effects on preschool children's health," and "The quality of child care is a persistent concern for all working parents" (p. 28).
Trap: (A) frames maternal employment itself as the risk factor, which is the inversion the fact bank flags. The chapter shifts the question from whether the mother works to the quality of the care, and then supplies eight concrete quality criteria.

Q: A nurse helps a family evaluate child care options. Which are among the eight determinants of child care quality the chapter names? (Select all that apply.)
A) Health and safety requirements
B) Responsive and warm interaction between staff and children
C) Limited group size and adequate staff-to-child ratios
D) Developmentally appropriate activities and trained staff
E) A published academic curriculum with measured learning outcomes
Answer: A, B, C, D
Rationale: The eight determinants are health and safety requirements, responsive and warm interaction between staff and children, developmentally appropriate activities, trained staff, limited group size, age-appropriate caregivers, adequate staff-to-child ratios, and adequate indoor and outdoor space (p. 28).
Trap: (E) is the one to reject. It sounds like a marker of a high-quality program and is not on the chapter's list, which is built on safety, interaction, developmental fit, and ratios rather than academic output.

Subtopic: kinship care, foster care, and adoption

Q: A grandmother assumes care of her grandchildren through a formal kinship placement. What does Census Bureau data say about kinship caregivers as a group?
A) They are more likely to be poor, single, older, less educated, and unemployed than families in which at least one parent is present
B) They are demographically similar to two-parent households
C) They are typically better resourced than foster families
D) The chapter provides no demographic data on kinship caregivers
Answer: A
Rationale: "According to US Census Bureau data, kinship caregivers are more likely to be poor, single, older, less educated, and unemployed than families in which at least one parent is present" (p. 28). Kinship care is "the placement of a child in the care of a grandparent or other relative."
Trap: (B) is the comfortable assumption, and missing this costs the discharge plan. Kinship placement preserves family continuity **and** places the child in a household with fewer resources — both halves matter for planning, and the chapter states the demographics without editorializing.

Q: Adoptive parents of a 9-month-old ask what affects their chances of forming a secure attachment. Which two factors does the chapter name?
A) The child's age at adoption only
B) The amount of time the infant spent with previous caregivers, and the number of caregivers the infant had before adoption
C) The adoptive parents' age and the presence of other children in the home
D) Whether the adoption is open or closed
Answer: B
Rationale: "The sooner infants enter their adoptive home, the better the chances of parent-infant attachment. However, the more caregivers the infant had before adoption, the greater the risk for attachment problems." The infant "must break the bond with the previous caregiver and form a new bond with the adoptive parents," and difficulty depends on "the amount of time he or she has spent with caregivers early in life, as well as the number of caregivers" (p. 23).
Trap: (A) names only the first factor and is the incomplete answer the fact bank warns about. Time and count are independent — an infant placed early who passed through several caregivers still carries elevated risk.

Topic: race ethnicity and health inequities

Q: A nurse is completing an intake form and must distinguish race from ethnicity. Which statement is accurate?
A) Race groups people by outward physical appearance; ethnicity groups individuals who consider themselves, or are considered by others, to share common characteristics from which they develop distinctive cultural behavior
B) Race is self-identified and ethnicity is assigned by observation
C) The two terms are interchangeable in current usage
D) Race is biological and ethnicity is genetic
Answer: A
Rationale: Race is "a term that groups together people by their outward, physical appearance" (p. 31); ethnicity is "a classification aimed at grouping individuals who consider themselves, or are considered by others, to share common characteristics that differentiate them from the other collectivities in a society, and from which they develop their distinctive cultural behavior" (pp. 31–32). Both "are socially constructed terms."
Trap: (B) is the mirror error and inverts the discriminating clause. Self-identification appears in the **ethnicity** definition; race in this chapter carries no such clause. (D) contradicts the chapter's statement that both are socially constructed.

Q: A student argues that the racial gap in infant mortality is fully explained by differences in education and income. Which finding from the chapter addresses this?
A) The gap disappears once maternal education is controlled for
B) Even when controlling for maternal levels of education, the infant mortality rate for college-educated African American women is 2.5 times higher than for Hispanic and Caucasian women of similar education level
C) The chapter does not report education-adjusted figures
D) The gap is explained entirely by access to prenatal care
Answer: B
Rationale: The chapter reports this figure precisely to show that "Racism remains an important social determinant of health" (p. 32). It also reports that "from birth forward, African American and Native American children have a higher mortality rate than Caucasian children in general."
Trap: (A) is the claim the statistic was chosen to refute. The whole point of the 2.5-fold figure is that it **survives** controlling for education, which is what makes racism itself the determinant rather than socioeconomic status alone.

Q: A nurse distinguishes health disparities from health inequities. What is added in the definition of inequities?
A) Nothing; the terms are synonymous
B) Inequities are differences that are avoidable **and unfair** — socially produced and systemic in unequal distribution across populations
C) Inequities are unavoidable differences arising from biology
D) Inequities apply only to adults; disparities apply to children
Answer: B
Rationale: Child health disparities are "discrepancies that are based in avoidable differences," while health inequities are "differences in health states between populations that are socially produced and systemic in unequal distribution across populations that are avoidable and unfair" (p. 32).
Trap: (A) is the reflex pick because the definitions share the word *avoidable*. The added word is **unfair**, and it is what makes inequities a policy target rather than a statistical observation. (C) inverts the shared term — both definitions turn on the difference being avoidable.

Q: A clinic director notes that the families with the greatest need for early childhood services are the least likely to be enrolled. How does the chapter frame this?
A) As evidence that those families are less motivated to seek care
B) As inequitable access that maintains or perpetuates health inequities, because the children most in need of services are most likely to not receive them
C) As a random distribution that will correct itself over time
D) As a problem confined to urban populations
Answer: B
Rationale: "Inequitable access to services maintains or perpetuates health inequities in early childhood because the children most in need of services are most likely to not receive them." Health also "follows a social gradient — better health outcomes with increased socioeconomic status" (p. 32).
Trap: (D) localizes a problem the chapter explicitly does not: "Poverty affects children and their families across all geographic areas, including urban, suburban, and rural." (A) relocates a structural mechanism into family motivation, which is the framing the chapter's three-part policy response is written against.

Topic: spiritual care and cultural humility

Q: A nurse documents a family's religion and considers the spiritual assessment complete. What distinction does the chapter draw?
A) Religion and spirituality are synonyms, so the documentation is sufficient
B) Religion is a specific set of beliefs enacted through a practice, while spirituality is a unique awareness, belief, practice, and experience starting in childhood and rooted over time — and spiritual assessment is called for by the American Nurses Association, the International Council of Nurses, and The Joint Commission
C) Spirituality applies only to adults, so a child needs no spiritual assessment
D) Spiritual assessment is optional unless the family requests it
Answer: B
Rationale: "The terms, however, are often conflated. Religion and spirituality, although frequently grouped together, denote different entities" (p. 34). Nearly 80% of people report that religion is fairly important to them, and "many regulatory bodies that guide practice call for assessment of and intervention for spiritual concerns."
Trap: (D) treats a regulatory requirement as a courtesy. Three bodies are named by the chapter, and spiritual assessment "is necessary to provide family-centered care that is aligned with spiritual and religious needs."

Q: A family explains a religious belief the nurse does not share and does not personally agree with. What is the appropriate response?
A) Agree with the family so they are not offended
B) Listen for understanding rather than agreement or disagreement
C) Explain the nurse's own religious practices to establish common ground
D) Defer the conversation entirely to the chaplain
Answer: B
Rationale: Box 2.7's eighth guideline is "Listen for understanding rather than agreement or disagreement" (p. 35). Its seventh asks the nurse to "Develop awareness of your own spiritual perspective" — awareness, not disclosure. Shared belief is not required; presence is still the intervention.
Trap: (A) is the distractor in the chapter's own NCLEX question 5 and it is chosen because agreeing feels respectful. (C) fails Box 2.7 item 7 in the same way — the chapter asks the nurse to know her own perspective, not to volunteer it.

Q: Which actions integrate spiritual care into pediatric nursing practice as Box 2.7 directs? (Select all that apply.)
A) Become knowledgeable about the religious worldviews of cultural groups found in the patients you care for
B) Allow children and families to teach you about the specifics of their religious beliefs
C) Consider the child's development when talking about spiritual concerns
D) Contact the institution's chaplaincy department for families with symptoms of spiritual distress or who ask for specific religious rituals
E) Reassure a child facing a life-threatening illness that everything will be all right
Answer: A, B, C, D
Rationale: These are four of the eight guidelines in Box 2.7 (p. 35). The chapter also names "active listening that is compassionate and keeps the child's spiritual needs at the center, **without false reassurances**" among its key practices.
Trap: (E) is the one to reject, and it is the kindest-sounding option on the list — the chapter rules out false reassurance by name. Note that (A) and (B) are both keyed and are meant to be held together: background knowledge first, then let the family supply the specifics.

Q: A nurse prepares to care for a family from a background unfamiliar to her by reading a summary of that group's typical foods, health beliefs, and rituals, and plans her teaching from it. How does the chapter evaluate this approach?
A) Appropriate; it demonstrates cultural competence
B) This is the cultural-competence posture the chapter criticizes — lumping a group together and applying an assumed set of beliefs can perpetuate stereotypes and does not illuminate what matters for this family
C) Appropriate as long as the nurse also documents the family's preferences
D) Inappropriate only if the summary is out of date
Answer: B
Rationale: The chapter judges cultural competence "too limiting for this discussion because it can perpetuate stereotypes, is often limited to race/ethnicity differences, and treats those different from the white provider as other," and gives the concrete failure: "it is not helpful to demonstrate competence by lumping a group of people together and applying an assumed set of beliefs onto them, such as Latinas/Latinos have strong family support" (p. 37). This is the keyed-wrong option in the chapter's own NCLEX question 4.
Trap: (A) is chosen because the action looks like diligent preparation. The discriminator is what the knowledge is **for**: background knowledge is endorsed by Box 2.7, but knowledge used as a substitute for asking the family is not.

Q: A nurse describes her approach as a lifelong process of self-reflection in which she examines her own beliefs, biases, and position, treats the family as the expert on their own experience, and attends to power differentials in the encounter. Which concept is this?
A) Cultural competence
B) Cultural humility
C) Ethnocentrism
D) Family-centered care
Answer: B
Rationale: Cultural humility is "the process of self-reflection to promote honest and trustworthy relationships" — "knowing one's self better in order to know others" — and is "best defined not as a discrete end point but as a commitment and active engagement in a lifelong process." It "reminds us of power differentials that influence health care encounters," holds that "people are often the experts in their own experiences," and "cannot be collapsed into a class or education offering" (p. 37).
Trap: (A) is the mirror error of the previous item, and the pair is the cleanest confusable set in the second half of the chapter. Competence is a skill set and an endpoint built on knowledge about groups; humility is an ongoing process that locates expertise in the patient. Note also that culture here "is not only race and ethnicity, but also social class, age, sexual orientation, sexuality, gender, ability, and professional discipline," so a distractor limiting culture to race, ethnicity, and religion is wrong on scope as well.
