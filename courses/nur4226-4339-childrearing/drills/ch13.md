# Chapter 13 drills — Health Promotion of the Preschooler and Family

Authored from `sources/Ch13_Fact_Bank.md` (Hockenberry, Wilson & Rodgers, 11th ed.,
chapter author Rebecca A. Monroe, printed pp. 423–439).

Blueprint weight is **6 questions of 202 (3.0%), depth `standard`** — 43 drills
across 11 topics.

Forty-three rather than 45 on purpose. This is the first chapter in the course
carrying six blueprint items rather than seven, and the `standard` band runs 30 to
45, so the count steps down with the weight instead of pinning every standard
chapter to the ceiling. Chapters 10, 11, and 12 are seven-item chapters and each
earned 45.

*Giftedness was added after the first pass.* The chapter went in at 40 with
giftedness cut on the reasoning that it is a self-contained section connecting to
nothing else in the developmental ladder. That reasoning was wrong: the fact bank
flags the IQ-130-but-test-scores-under-identify distinction as a trap note, and at
`standard` depth every flagged pair gets mirrored. Three questions were added
before any attempts existed, so nothing was orphaned.

This is the third rung of the developmental ladder, written to the same shape as
Chapters 10 and 12 so the three stages compare column for column. Topic names are
preschooler-qualified for the same reason the earlier chapters' are infant- and
toddler-qualified.

**Where the weight went.** The fact bank names the chapter's testable core as the
**thought processes** — initiative versus guilt, the two preoperational sub-stages,
magical thinking, animism, and the fear of annihilation. These are not background
theory here; they are the direct explanation for why a preschooler believes a wish
killed a parent, why a bandage matters after an injection, and why a toilet can be
frightening. Every one is drilled through a vignette rather than a definition,
because that is how the exam wraps them. The second block of weight went to **the
parental concerns the nurse is asked to counsel on** — imaginary playmates, school
readiness, sex education, aggression, stuttering, and fears — each of which has a
specific correct response in the text.

**Drilled from both ends:** magical thinking against animism (a thought causes
the event versus an object is alive), preconceptual against intuitive thought,
readiness testing against developmental screening, slow-to-warm-up against
high-activity-level temperament, and the three drawing verbs — copy, imitate,
trace — which move the same shapes across two years.

**What seventeen printed pages could not buy at 43 questions.** Left undrilled:
Table 13.1's full three-column grid beyond the findings drilled here; spiritual
development; Kohlberg's two named stages beyond the principle that punishment
defines morality; sex typing and gender identification mechanisms; Box 13.1's
full list of gifted characteristics; Box 13.2's age-by-age sources of stress; the
bilingualism guidance; the media and mutual-play sections; specific caloric,
protein, fiber, calcium, and fat figures; sleep hours; and the
anticipatory-guidance box. Of these, Box 13.2's stress sources and the
bilingualism rule — a language disability manifests in **both** languages — are
the two most likely to be tested and would be the first to expand if a miss is
logged.

Five questions are select-all, one for each list in the chapter that genuinely
sorts two ways: fear-management approaches against forced exposure, the two halves
of the imaginary-companion answer against discouraging it, the five normal-versus-
problematic discriminators against the behavior itself, the three stuttering
referral triggers against normal variation, and the 5-2-1-0 digits against their
swapped units.

Topic: preschooler growth and gross motor refinement

Q: A parent asks what to expect of their 4-year-old's growth over the coming year. Which statement reflects the chapter?
A) The rate of physical growth slows and stabilizes; weight increases about 2 to 3 kg (4.5 to 6.5 lb) per year and height 6.5 to 9 cm (2.5 to 3.5 inches) per year, with the legs rather than the trunk increasing in length
B) Growth accelerates during the preschool years, with weight gain of about 5 kg per year
C) The trunk rather than the legs accounts for most of the height gain in this period
D) Weight doubles between ages 3 and 5 years
Answer: A
Rationale: "The rate of physical growth slows and stabilizes during the preschool years," with weight gain of "approximately 2 to 3 kg (4.5 to 6.5 lb) per year" and "a yearly increase of 6.5 to 9 cm (2.5 to 3.5 inches)." "The legs of a preschooler, rather than the trunk, increase in length." Average weights are 14.5 kg at 3 years, 16.5 kg at 4 years, and 18.5 kg at 5 years.
Trap: (B) inverts the chapter's opening statement, and it pulls because preschoolers visibly lengthen and lose the toddler's squat, potbellied look for a build that is "slender but sturdy, graceful, agile, and posturally erect." That change is in proportion, not in rate. (D) would require a gain roughly three times the stated annual figure.

Q: A 4-year-old at a well-child visit skips and hops proficiently on one foot and catches a ball reliably. Which assessment is correct?
A) Expected for 4 years; skipping on alternate feet and jumping rope belong to 5 years
B) Delayed; skipping on alternate feet is expected at 4 years
C) Advanced; hopping on one foot is the 5-year milestone
D) Expected, but catching a ball reliably indicates unusually advanced coordination for the age
Answer: A
Rationale: At 3 years the child "rides a tricycle, walks on tiptoe, balances on one foot for a few seconds, and broad jumps." At 4 years the child "skips and hops proficiently on one foot and catches a ball reliably." At 5 years the child "skips on alternate feet, jumps rope, and begins to skate and swim." Motor development in this period "consists of increases in strength and refinement of previously learned skills."
Trap: (B) moves the 5-year skill back a year, and it pulls because the word "skips" appears in both descriptions. The discriminator is one foot at 4 years versus alternate feet at 5.

Q: Parents of a 5-year-old ask about enrolling the child in a formalized, competitive training program for a sport. Which response reflects the chapter?
A) Muscle development and bone growth are still far from mature and overexertion can injure delicate tissues; the most important aspect of organized play at this age is that the activity be developmentally appropriate and occur in a nonthreatening, fun, and safe environment, with readiness determined individually
B) Formalized training should begin as early as possible to maximize athletic potential
C) Organized sports should be avoided entirely until school age, since preschoolers cannot follow rules
D) Physical activity should be limited to quiet indoor play, because preschoolers are prone to falls
Answer: A
Rationale: "Muscle development and bone growth are still far from mature. Excessive activity and overexertion can injure delicate tissues." Whether young children should begin formalized training "is controversial," and "readiness must be determined individually." "The most important aspect of organized play for preschoolers is that the activity is developmentally appropriate and occurs in a nonthreatening, fun, and safe environment." Children at this age "benefit from free play and exposure to a variety of physical activities."
Trap: (D) inverts the injury picture for this age: "Because of improved gross and fine motor skills, coordination, and balance, preschoolers are less prone to falls than toddlers," and motor activity levels "continue to be high" and are therefore encouraged.

Q: A 5-year-old's examination reveals that a permanent tooth is erupting, the child writes consistently with the right hand, and the child cannot yet manage the small print in a workbook. How should the nurse interpret these findings?
A) All are expected at 5 years; eruption of permanent dentition may begin, handedness is established with about 90% right-handed, and the child is not ready for concentrated close work because of slight farsightedness and still-unrefined eye-hand coordination
B) Permanent dentition should not begin until 7 years, so dental referral is needed
C) Difficulty with small print indicates a visual defect requiring correction
D) Established handedness at 5 years is premature and should not be reinforced
Answer: A
Rationale: Table 13.1 gives for age 5 years: "eruption of permanent dentition may begin"; "handedness is established (about 90% are right-handed)"; and the child is "not ready for concentrated close work or small print because of slight farsightedness and still unrefined eye-hand coordination."
Trap: (C) reads a normal developmental limitation as pathology, and it is the reading that sends children for unnecessary vision workups. The farsightedness named here is the expected state at this age, not a defect.

Topic: preschooler drawing and fine motor progression

Q: A 3-year-old is handed a crayon and shown a model. Which performance is expected?
A) Copies a circle and imitates a cross and vertical and horizontal lines, but cannot draw a complete stick figure
B) Copies a square and traces a cross and diamond
C) Copies a diamond and triangle and adds seven to nine parts to a stick figure
D) Can only scribble, and cannot name what has been drawn
Answer: A
Rationale: At 3 years the child "copies a circle and imitates a cross and vertical and horizontal lines," holds the writing instrument "with the fingers rather than the fist," scribbles but "can name what has been drawn," and cannot draw a complete stick figure. At 4 to 5 years the child "can trace a cross and copy a square"; the triangle and diamond are "usually the last geometric figures to be mastered," at 5 to 6 years.
Trap: (B) is the 4-year-old's performance and (C) the 5-year-old's. The distractors swap the verb rather than the shape, and the verb is the whole discriminator: copying works from a finished model, imitating follows a demonstration, and tracing follows an outline — with the same shape moving through all three across two years.

Q: A 4-year-old draws a stick figure with a head, a body, and legs — three parts in all. How should the nurse document this?
A) Expected; adding three parts to a stick figure is the 4-year milestone, and seven to nine parts belongs to 5 years
B) Delayed; seven to nine parts is expected at 4 years
C) Advanced; a 4-year-old cannot draw a stick figure at all
D) Concerning; the absence of facial features indicates a perceptual problem
Answer: A
Rationale: Table 13.1 gives "adds three parts to stick figure" at 4 years and "adds seven to nine parts to stick figure" at 5 years. At 3 years the child "cannot draw stick figure but may make circle with facial features."
Trap: (B) applies the 5-year count to a 4-year-old. These are the only two stick-figure numbers in the chapter, which is exactly why they get swapped more often than any other pair in it.

Q: A nurse uses a child's drawings during assessment. Which statements reflect the chapter's guidance on children's art?
A) Children progress through placement, shape, design, and pictorial stages; two united diagrams make a combine and three or more an aggregate; and because drawings before age 6 are strikingly similar universally while later drawings are shaped by culture and environment, nurses need to consider children's backgrounds when interpreting drawings
B) Drawings are a precise and validated measure of intelligence and personality
C) The pictorial stage, in which designs are recognizable as familiar objects, appears at 3 years
D) A combine is three or more united diagrams and an aggregate is two
Answer: A
Rationale: Children "advance through four distinguishable stages": placement at 15 months, shape at 3 years, design immediately after, and pictorial at 4 to 5 years, "in which their designs are recognizable as familiar objects." "When two diagrams are united, the resulting design is called a combine"; "Three or more united diagrams produce an aggregate." Drawings before age 6 years "are strikingly similar universally," after which "cultural and environmental influences... shape much of what children draw," so nurses "need to consider children's backgrounds when interpreting drawings."
Trap: (B) overstates evidence the chapter deliberately qualifies: drawing is used to assess intelligence, personality development, and psychosocial adjustment, but "the precise value of using drawing to measure such concepts is still a nebulous science." (D) reverses combine and aggregate.

Topic: initiative versus guilt in the preschooler

Q: A 4-year-old energetically tries to pour milk for the whole family, spills it across the table, and then becomes tearful and says "I'm bad." Which psychosocial conflict does this illustrate?
A) Autonomy versus shame and doubt
B) Initiative versus guilt
C) Industry versus inferiority
D) Trust versus mistrust
Answer: B
Rationale: "The chief psychosocial task of this period is acquiring a sense of initiative." Children this age "play, work, and live to the fullest and feel a real sense of accomplishment and satisfaction in their activities," and the conflict "arises when children overstep the limits of their ability and inquiry and experience guilt for not having behaved appropriately."
Trap: (A) is the toddler's conflict, and it pulls because shame and guilt feel like one emotion. The discriminator sits in the stem: a child who attempted something beyond their ability and then feels bad about it is showing guilt, which is the preschooler's half of the pair.

Q: A 5-year-old whose father died suddenly tells the nurse, "I got mad at Daddy last week and wished he would go away. Then he died." What is the essential nursing response?
A) Clarify for the child that wishes cannot make events occur
B) Reassure the child that everyone has angry thoughts, without addressing the wish itself
C) Tell the child the thought was wrong and should not be repeated
D) Redirect the conversation, since discussing the death will increase the child's distress
Answer: A
Rationale: "A particularly stressful thought is wishing one's parent dead." The contest normally resolves when the child "strongly identifies with the same-sex parent and peers during the school years," but if that parent dies before identification is complete, "the preschooler can be overwhelmed with guilt for having wished and therefore 'caused' the death." The chapter states the intervention directly: "Clarifying for children that wishes cannot make events occur is essential in helping them overcome their guilt and anxiety."
Trap: (B) offers general reassurance and leaves the child's actual belief — that the wish caused the death — completely untouched, which is what magical thinking requires the nurse to name explicitly. (C) confirms the guilt rather than relieving it.

Q: A parent asks why verbal warnings now work with their 4-year-old when they did nothing for their 2-year-old. Which explanation does the chapter give?
A) Verbal enforcement of limits is much more effective in this age group than with toddlers; the preschooler still needs close supervision but is much more aware of danger and can listen and obey in most instances
B) Preschoolers understand why a behavior is unacceptable, so explanation alone is now sufficient
C) Preschoolers have completed superego development and no longer need parental limits
D) The change reflects the end of negativism rather than any change in comprehension
Answer: A
Rationale: "Verbal enforcement of limits is much more effective in this age-group than with toddlers." The preschooler "still needs close supervision but is much more aware of danger and can listen and obey in most instances." Development of the superego, or conscience, "starts toward the end of the toddler years and is a major task for preschoolers."
Trap: (B) overstates comprehension. Children this age "are generally unable to understand why something is acceptable or unacceptable" and are "aware of appropriate behavior primarily through punishment or reward," relying "almost completely on parental principles for developing their own moral judgment."

Q: A 4-year-old boy announces that he is going to marry his mother and has been unusually hostile toward his father. His parents are alarmed. Which response is accurate?
A) This reflects the oedipal stage, and the conflict normally resolves through identification with the same-sex parent; Table 13.1 records that the 4-year-old identifies strongly with the opposite-sex parent while the 5-year-old identifies strongly with the same-sex parent
B) This indicates a disturbance in gender identification and warrants referral
C) This is expected at 2 years and is delayed at 4
D) Girls show the same pattern but fear castration rather than experiencing penis envy
Answer: A
Rationale: Freud "described this goal in psychosexual terms and labeled the period the oedipal, or phallic, stage," in which the boy "wishes that his father were dead so he could marry his mother (Oedipus complex)." The resolution is "identification with the same-sex parent." Table 13.1 records that the 4-year-old "identifies strongly with parent of opposite sex" while the 5-year-old "strongly identifies with parent of same sex, especially boys with their fathers."
Trap: (D) reverses the girls' side of the pair. Girls "do not fear castration; rather they experience penis envy"; it is the boy whose guilt about his feelings toward his father "makes him fear the same punishment of mutilation, resulting in the castration complex."

Topic: preoperational thought in the preschooler

Q: A 3-year-old and a 4-year-old are both described as preoperational. Which statement places each more precisely?
A) The 3-year-old is in the preconceptual phase, ages 2 to 4 years, and the 4-year-old is in the phase of intuitive thought, ages 4 to 7 years
B) The 3-year-old is in the phase of intuitive thought and the 4-year-old in the preconceptual phase
C) The 3-year-old is sensorimotor and the 4-year-old preoperational
D) The 4-year-old has entered concrete operational thought
Answer: A
Rationale: The preoperational phase "covers the age span from 2 to 7 years and is divided into two stages: the preconceptual phase, ages 2 to 4 years, and the phase of intuitive thought, ages 4 to 7 years." Table 13.1 tags the 3-year-old as "in preconceptual phase" and the 4-year-old as "in phase of intuitive thought." The main transition across the two is "the shift from totally egocentric thought to social awareness and the ability to consider other viewpoints."
Trap: (D) jumps a whole period; concrete operational thought belongs to the school-age child. Note that calling a 3-year-old preoperational would not be wrong — the sub-stage is the finer discriminator, and it is what the table supplies.

Q: A 4-year-old whose newborn brother died of sudden infant death syndrome tells the nurse he had wished the baby would "go back." Which characteristic of preoperational thought does this illustrate?
A) Magical thinking — because of egocentrism and transductive reasoning, preschoolers believe that thoughts are all powerful
B) Animism — ascribing lifelike qualities to inanimate objects
C) Inability to conserve — judging everything according to one dimension
D) Egocentric speech — assuming everyone thinks as they do
Answer: A
Rationale: "Because of their egocentrism and transductive reasoning, they believe that thoughts are all powerful," which leaves preschoolers "feeling guilty and responsible for bad thoughts that may coincide with the occurrence of a wished event." The chapter's own example is a child who wishes a new sibling dead: if the sibling dies, "young children think their wish caused the death."
Trap: (B) is the adjacent characteristic and the one this pair is most often swapped with. Both arise from egocentrism, and the object of the stem tells you which: a thought or a wish points to magical thinking, a thing points to animism.

Q: A 4-year-old refuses to use the toilet after watching a television commercial in which the toilet bowl turns into a monster. Which characteristic does this illustrate?
A) Magical thinking — the belief that thoughts are all powerful
B) Animism — ascribing lifelike qualities to inanimate objects
C) Transductive reasoning — reasoning from one particular to another
D) Literal belief in the power of words
Answer: B
Rationale: Animism, "ascribing lifelike qualities to inanimate objects," is what "helps explain why children fear objects." The chapter gives this exact example: a child "may refuse to use the toilet after watching a television commercial in which the toilet bowl is portrayed as turning into a monster."
Trap: (A) is the mirror error of the previous item. Nothing here turns on the child's own thoughts causing an event; the fear is of the object itself, which is what makes it animism.

Q: A hospitalized 4-year-old repeatedly asks when her mother is coming. Which response is most appropriate?
A) "Your mother will visit you after you finish your lunch."
B) "Your mother will be here at two o'clock."
C) "Your mother will be here tomorrow afternoon."
D) "Your mother will come back in about three hours."
Answer: A
Rationale: Preschoolers interpret time "according to their own frame of reference" — "A long time means until Christmas." The chapter directs explaining time by event and gives this sentence verbatim as its example, advising against "yesterday," "tomorrow," "next week," or "Tuesday." Associating time "with usual expected daily occurrences" helps children learn temporal relationships "while increasing their trust in others' predictions."
Trap: (B) and (D) both use clock time, which a preschooler cannot convert into waiting. (C) uses a calendar word the chapter names specifically as one to avoid; it sounds concrete to an adult and carries no meaning for the child.

Q: A 5-year-old overhears a nurse describe another child as "a bad patient." Later the 5-year-old says she must be a bad girl too. Which principle applies, and what is the correct practice?
A) Preschoolers believe in the power of words and accept their meaning literally, so the act should be labeled rather than the child, as in "That was a bad thing to do"
B) Preschoolers understand figurative language by 5 years, so the comment carries no risk
C) The child should be told she misunderstood, and the matter dropped
D) Preschoolers cannot generalize a comment made about another child to themselves
Answer: A
Rationale: "Preschoolers believe in the power of words and accept their meaning literally" — calling a child "bad" means, in the child's mind, that they are bad. The chapter's nursing translation is to label the act rather than the child: "That was a bad thing to do."
Trap: (D) underestimates precisely what this age does most. A child in the preconceptual stage is "able to imagine an event without actually experiencing it," which is why "Seeing someone hurt is sufficient for realizing what the hurt must be like," and why a preschooler watching another child get an injection "may become upset, almost as if he or she received the injection."

Topic: preschooler body image, fears, and the fear of annihilation

Q: A 4-year-old who has just received an intramuscular injection becomes distressed and insists on a bandage, though there is no further bleeding. What is the nurse's best action?
A) Apply the bandage, because preschoolers have poorly defined body boundaries and fear that all their blood and insides can leak out if the skin is broken
B) Explain that the bleeding has stopped, so a bandage is unnecessary
C) Apply the bandage, but explain that it is only for decoration
D) Distract the child, since the request reflects attention seeking
Answer: A
Rationale: Preschoolers "have poorly defined body boundaries and little knowledge of their internal anatomy. Intrusive experiences are frightening, especially those that disrupt the integrity of the skin (e.g., injections and surgery). They fear that all their blood and 'insides' can leak out if the skin is 'broken.' Therefore preschoolers may believe it is critical to use bandages after an injury."
Trap: (B) is the factually correct and developmentally useless answer, and it is the one an adult reaches for. The child's concern is not bleeding but body integrity, and no explanation of hemostasis touches it.

Q: A 4-year-old screams and clings to the side of the bathtub when the drain is opened. Which explanation is correct, and what is a useful intervention?
A) Fear of annihilation, arising from poorly defined body boundaries and the inability to understand concepts of size, so the child cannot grasp that the body is too large to disappear down the drain; letting the child bathe a doll allows the child to observe that large objects cannot go down the drain
B) Ordinary stranger fear, which resolves without intervention
C) A sensory processing difficulty requiring referral
D) Deliberate bath-time resistance that should be ignored
Answer: A
Rationale: Fear of annihilation is "peculiar to this age." Because of "poorly defined body boundaries and improved cognitive abilities," children develop "concerns related to loss of body parts, such as their body going down the drain," and because they "cannot understand concepts of size, they cannot understand that their body is too large to disappear down the drain." The chapter's own remedy is "letting the child bathe a doll so that the child can observe that large objects cannot go down the drain," which reconstructs the experience "without involving the child directly as the victim."
Trap: (B) names a fear the preschooler has largely outgrown — preschoolers "have overcome much of their anxiety associated with strangers and the fear of separation of earlier years." This age brings the greatest number and variety of fears, but they are different fears.

Q: A 5-year-old is terrified of dogs. Which approaches does the chapter support? Select all that apply.
A) Gradually introducing the child to the experience by watching other children play with a dog
B) Actively involving the child in finding practical methods to deal with the frightening experience
C) Having others demonstrate fearlessness, allowing the child to progress at their own rate
D) Placing the child's hand on a calm dog, so the child learns there is nothing to fear
E) Keeping a dim night-light in the bedroom, for the child's separate fear of the dark
Answer: A, B, C, E
Rationale: "The best way to help children overcome their fears is by actively involving them in finding practical methods to deal with frightening experiences." Desensitization means "Exposing children to the feared object in a safe situation"; children afraid of dogs "should never be forced to approach or touch one, but they may be gradually introduced to the experience by watching other children play with the animal." Modeling — "having others demonstrate fearlessness" — "can be effective if children are allowed to progress at their own rate." A dim night-light is the chapter's own example for fear of the dark.
Trap: (D) is the one option from the wrong side of the sort, and it is forced exposure rather than desensitization. It pulls because the contact looks like the logical endpoint of a graded approach; the chapter says such children "should never be forced to approach or touch one."

Q: Parents of a 4-year-old are exasperated by the child's many fears and ask whether something is wrong. Which response reflects the chapter?
A) The greatest number and variety of real and imagined fears are present during the preschool years and usually by 5 or 6 years children relinquish many of them; parents should also know that when they demonstrate their own fears, those concerns are communicated to the child and tend to be long-lasting and difficult to dispel
B) Fears at this age indicate an anxiety disorder and warrant referral now
C) Fears peak in the school-age years, so this is early and unusual
D) Parents' own fears have no bearing on a preschooler's fears
Answer: A
Rationale: "The greatest number and variety of real and imagined fears are present during the preschool years," typically of "the dark, being left alone (especially at bedtime), animals (particularly large dogs and snakes), ghosts, sexual matters (castration), and objects or persons associated with pain." "Usually by 5 or 6 years of age, children relinquish many of their fears." And: "When parents demonstrate their fears, these concerns are communicated to the children. Such fears tend to be long-lasting and difficult to dispel."
Trap: (B) medicalizes a normative peak. Professional help is indicated only "when children experience severe fears that disrupt family life," which this stem does not describe.

Topic: preschooler language, play, and imaginary companions

Q: A 4-year-old speaks in four- and five-word sentences, asks constant questions, and has a vocabulary a parent estimates at about 1500 words. How should the nurse interpret this?
A) Expected; vocabulary is 1500 words or more at 4 years, sentences run four or five words, and questioning is at its peak
B) Delayed; a 4-year-old should have about 2100 words
C) Advanced; 1500 words is the 5-year figure
D) Concerning; constant questioning at this age indicates anxiety
Answer: A
Rationale: Table 13.1 gives vocabulary as about 900 words at 3 years, "1500 words or more" at 4 years, and about 2100 words at 5 years; the narrative gives the span as "from 300 words at age 2 years to more than 2100 words at the end of age 5 years." Sentences run three or four words at 3 years, four or five at 4 years, and six to eight at 5 years. At 4 years "questioning is at peak," and children "usually repeat the question until they receive an answer."
Trap: (B) applies the 5-year count to a 4-year-old, which is the commonest swap in a chapter whose vocabulary figures are its most swappable numbers. (D) pathologizes the single most characteristic behavior of the age.

Q: Four preschoolers in a playroom are all building with blocks in the same area, talking to one another and borrowing pieces, but with no assigned roles, rules, or shared goal. Which type of play is this?
A) Parallel play
B) Associative play — group play in similar or identical activities but without rigid organization or rules
C) Cooperative play, with assigned roles and a shared goal
D) Solitary play
Answer: B
Rationale: The preschooler's play is associative, defined as "group play in similar or identical activities but without rigid organization or rules." Table 13.1 still describes the 3-year-old's play as "parallel and associative"; by 4 and 5 years it is simply associative. Play "should provide for physical, social, and mental development."
Trap: (C) is the next stage, and it pulls because the children are plainly interacting. The absence of rules, organization, and assigned roles is the cue; a stem describing organized, goal-directed team play has moved to the school-age child.

Q: A preschool teacher plans a holiday art session for a class of 3-year-olds. Which planning rule does the chapter give?
A) One simple project per year of age — a 3-year-old usually has the patience to decorate three eggs but will become bored and restless with more
B) As many projects as the session allows, since preschoolers have high activity levels
C) One project per session, regardless of age
D) Projects should be avoided at 3 years, since fine motor skills are insufficient
Answer: A
Rationale: "A helpful rule in planning creative activities is one simple project per year of age. For example, a 3-year-old child usually has the patience to decorate three eggs but will become bored and restless with more."
Trap: (B) confuses activity level with attention span. Preschool motor activity is high, but this rule is about how many discrete projects a child can see through, which scales with age rather than with energy.

Q: Parents of a 4-year-old with an imaginary companion ask for guidance. Which responses reflect the chapter? Select all that apply.
A) The child's fantasy is a sign of health that helps differentiate between make-believe and reality
B) Imaginary companions usually appear between ages 2½ and 3 years and last until the child enters school
C) Parents may acknowledge the companion by name and agree to simple requests, such as setting an extra place at the table
D) Parents should discourage the companion, since it delays the child's grasp of reality
E) If the child blames the companion for a mess, parents should state clearly that the child is the only person they see and is therefore responsible for cleaning up
Answer: A, B, C, E
Rationale: Parents "should be reassured that the child's fantasy is a sign of health that helps differentiate between make-believe and reality." Imaginary companions "usually appear between ages 2½ and 3 years and, for the most part, last until the child enters school." Parents may acknowledge the companion by name and "even agree to simple requests such as setting an extra place at the table," but "should not allow the child to use the playmate to avoid punishment or responsibility"; if the child blames the companion, "the parents need to clearly state that the child is the only person they see, and therefore the child is responsible for cleaning up."
Trap: (D) is the one option from the wrong side of the sort. The correct guidance has two halves — acknowledge the companion, and refuse to let it carry the blame — and the common wrong answers supply only one: either bare reassurance with no limit, or the instruction here to discourage the companion altogether.

Topic: school readiness and preparing the preschooler for school

Q: Parents ask the nurse whether their 5-year-old is ready for kindergarten. Which response reflects the chapter?
A) There are no absolute indicators for school readiness; it is influenced by social, emotional, and physical development, health status, ability and desire to learn, life experiences, family environment, and parental support — and the child's social maturation, especially attention span, is as important as academic readiness
B) A specific readiness test score is the definitive indicator
C) Chronologic age alone determines readiness
D) Academic skills such as letter recognition are the priority indicator
Answer: A
Rationale: Readiness is influenced by "a child's social, emotional, and physical development; health status; ability and desire to learn; life experiences; family environment; and parental support. There are no absolute indicators for school readiness." The chapter's own priority statement is that "The child's social maturation, especially attention span, is as important as academic readiness."
Trap: (D) is the metric parents usually apply, and the chapter deliberately balances it against social maturation. (B) contradicts "there are no absolute indicators" outright, and it pulls because a test result feels like the objective answer a nurse ought to be able to give.

Q: A school offers both readiness testing and developmental screening. How should the nurse explain the difference?
A) Readiness testing focuses on evaluation of skill acquisition, while developmental screening focuses on the potential to learn and identifies children who may benefit from further diagnostic testing
B) Readiness testing focuses on the potential to learn, while developmental screening evaluates skill acquisition
C) The two terms describe the same process
D) Developmental screening is diagnostic, and readiness testing is a screening tool
Answer: A
Rationale: Readiness testing "focuses on evaluation of skill acquisition"; developmental screening "focuses on the potential to learn," addresses "cognitive (especially language), social, and physical milestones," and identifies "children who may benefit from further diagnostic testing." Use "varies by state and local district," and "there is controversy over the most beneficial form of evaluation."
Trap: (B) is the straight inversion of the pair, and it is the only thing separating them. The cue words carry the whole distinction: skill belongs to readiness testing, potential belongs to developmental screening.

Q: A parent asks how to handle their 4-year-old's first day of preschool. Which guidance reflects the chapter?
A) Present the idea as exciting and pleasurable beforehand; behave confidently on the day; introduce the child to the teacher and facility; and if remaining for part of the first day, be available to the child but inconspicuous
B) Leave without saying goodbye, to avoid triggering tears
C) Stay with the child throughout the entire first week, remaining actively involved in the activities
D) Begin with a full-day schedule, so the child adjusts to the routine immediately
Answer: A
Rationale: Before school, parents "should present the idea as exciting and pleasurable," talking about painting, blocks, swings, and outdoor equipment so children can "fantasize about the forthcoming event in a positive manner." On the first day "parents should behave confidently. Such behavior requires parents to have resolved their own feelings regarding the experience." Parents should introduce the child to the teacher and facility, and "If parents stay, they should be available to the child but inconspicuous."
Trap: (D) contradicts the chapter's own concession that a full-day routine "is often too overwhelming for a child and could be shortened to a morning or afternoon session if possible." (C) keeps the parent present but drops the word carrying the instruction — inconspicuous — and staying actively involved works against the separation the day exists to accomplish.

Q: A slow-to-warm-up 4-year-old and a high-activity-level 4-year-old are both starting preschool. What does the chapter recommend for each?
A) The slow-to-warm-up child needs gradual introduction to new situations and may benefit from the parent's presence until settled; the high-activity-level child adjusts better to environments allowing freedom of movement rather than a structured or regimented classroom
B) Both children should be placed in the most highly structured classroom available
C) Temperament changes markedly during the preschool years, so early observations should not guide placement
D) The high-activity-level child needs gradual introduction, and the slow-to-warm-up child needs freedom of movement
Answer: A
Rationale: Temperamental characteristics "tend to remain stable," and the preschool-specific concern is "the effect of temperament on adjustment in group situations, especially school." "Slow-to-warm-up children need gradual introduction to new situations and may benefit from the parent's presence until they have settled in." "High-activity-level children tend to adjust better to environments that allow freedom of movement, rather than a structured or regimented classroom." The Behavioral Style Questionnaire "helps identify temperamental characteristics in children from 3 to 7 years old."
Trap: (D) swaps the two recommendations, which is easy to do because both amount to accommodating the child; the accommodations are opposite in kind. (C) contradicts the stability the chapter asserts, and that stability is why these observations are worth making at all.

Topic: preschooler sex education and sexual curiosity

Q: A 4-year-old asks her mother, "Where did I come from?" What should the nurse advise the mother to do first?
A) Find out what the child already knows and thinks, before answering
B) Give a complete, anatomically correct explanation of conception and birth
C) Tell the child she is too young to know about that
D) Offer a simplified stork story, to avoid frightening the child
Answer: A
Rationale: The first rule is to find out what children know and think, so the answer corrects the child's own theory and avoids an "unasked for" answer. The chapter's illustration is 4-year-old Lauren, who after a full sex-education explanation said, "I don't know about all that! All I know is Mary came from New York, and I want to know where I came from." A child "who asks one question is looking for one answer."
Trap: (B) is the well-prepared parent's answer, and it is second in priority rather than first; given too much information, "the child will simply become bored or end the conversation with an irrelevant question." (C) and (D) are the two responses the chapter says teach children "to keep such questions to themselves," after which magical thinking supplies "any fantastic and often terrifying explanation."

Q: A parent worries that honest answers about reproduction will harm a 4-year-old. Which response reflects the chapter?
A) In general, knowledge is not harmful when delivered in a developmentally appropriate manner; children process information at their own pace, correct anatomic words become important for explaining other concepts later, and what matters is that parents are approachable
B) Detailed information at this age causes lasting anxiety and should be deferred to school age
C) Children should be given as much information as possible at once, so the subject need not be revisited
D) Nurses should avoid genital terminology during physical examinations, to prevent embarrassment
Answer: A
Rationale: "In general, knowledge is not harmful when delivered in a developmentally appropriate manner." Children "will process information at their own pace. What matters is that parents are approachable and do not dismiss their child's inquiries." Correct anatomic words "become important for explaining other concepts later on," and the child "will forget or misunderstand much of the correct information, but the correct information can be restated until the child absorbs and comprehends the facts."
Trap: (D) closes an opening the chapter specifically assigns to nurses, who "have the opportunity to contribute to early sex education by conveying accurate information regarding genital terms during physical examinations." (C) ignores scope control — a child asking one question is looking for one answer.

Q: Parents discover their 4-year-old and a playmate undressed and examining each other during "doctor play." Which parental response does the chapter recommend?
A) Neither condone nor condemn the behavior; tell the children that if they have questions they should ask the parents, then encourage them to engage in some other activity
B) Punish both children, so the behavior is not repeated
C) Allow the exploration to continue without restriction, since curiosity is normal
D) Explain the reproductive function of the genitalia in detail
Answer: A
Rationale: Parents should "neither condone nor condemn the behavior, but... tell the children that, if they have questions, they should ask the parents; the parents should then encourage the children to engage in some other activity." This "in no way condemns the act but stresses alternative methods by which to seek solutions and answers." The chapter also notes that these children are often "still unaware of the reproductive function of the genitalia" and are "curious about the eliminative function of the anatomy."
Trap: (C) is the permissive extreme, and the chapter rejects it with a reason: "Allowing children unrestricted permissiveness only intensifies their anxiety and concern because exploring and searching usually yield little evidence to satisfy their curiosity." (B) is the opposite extreme; the recommended response sits between them and carries a redirection.

Q: A parent reports that their 4-year-old touches his genitals while watching television. Which nursing response is appropriate?
A) This occurs at any age for a variety of reasons and, if not excessive, is normal and healthy; management is parent education and reassurance, redirection to other activities, and teaching that it is a private act
B) This always indicates sexual abuse and requires immediate reporting
C) The child should be told the behavior is dirty, so that it is extinguished
D) The behavior requires no discussion of any kind with the child
Answer: A
Rationale: Masturbation, "self-stimulation of the genitalia," "occurs at any age for a variety of reasons and, if not excessive, is normal and healthy." For preschoolers "it is part of sexual curiosity and exploration." Management is "parent education and reassurance, redirection of the child to other activities, and discussion with the child regarding appropriate boundaries," and parents "should emphasize that masturbation is a private act, thus teaching children socially acceptable behavior."
Trap: (B) applies the exception to the rule: it is *excessive* masturbation that "may be associated with emotional or behavioral problems and physical or sexual abuse," and this stem carries no such qualifier. Where parents are concerned, the chapter directs assessing the circumstances, since the behavior "can be an expression of anxiety, boredom, or stress."

Topic: preschooler aggression and speech problems

Q: A 5-year-old is described by his parents as well behaved at home, but he is a persistent discipline problem at school and a bully among playmates. Which mechanism does the chapter identify?
A) Frustration — the continual thwarting of self-satisfaction by parental disapproval, humiliation, punishment, and insults, which children displace on peers and other authority figures, especially if they fear their parents
B) Modeling — imitating the behavior of significant others
C) Reinforcement — a reward that shapes the behavior
D) Normal preschool assertiveness, requiring no intervention
Answer: A
Rationale: Frustration is "the continual thwarting of self-satisfaction by parental disapproval, humiliation, punishment, and insults." Children "displace their anger on others, particularly peers and other authority figures, especially if they fear their parents" — and the chapter names this child specifically as the one "who is well behaved at home but a discipline problem at school or a bully among playmates."
Trap: (B) and (C) are the two neighbouring mechanisms and both are plausible on their face. The discriminator here is the split between settings: compliance at home with aggression everywhere else is displacement, which is frustration's signature.

Q: Parents of an aggressive 4-year-old say they spank him after every incident and otherwise pay him little attention. Which response reflects the chapter?
A) If a child is spanked for an aggressive act, aggression is being used to teach a lesson against aggression; negative attention can itself be reinforcing, and time-out and solitary play are the effective disciplinary measures in the preschool years
B) Spanking is the most effective disciplinary measure for aggression at this age
C) Ignoring the aggression entirely is the recommended approach
D) Aggression at 4 years always requires professional treatment
Answer: A
Rationale: "If a child is spanked for an aggressive act, aggression is being used to 'teach' a lesson against aggression." A reward may be negative and still reinforcing "because it brings attention" — children ignored until they hit a sibling "learn that this act attracts attention." "The use of time-out and solitary play are effective disciplinary measures in the preschool years," and "minimizing anger and frustration can lead to fewer opportunities for acting out."
Trap: (C) is the other reinforcement error the chapter names: parents who permit aggression "by not interfering communicate silent, implicit approval." Neither punishing nor ignoring is the answer; naming an alternative measure is. The chapter warns separately that "inconsistency in disciplinary practices may also foster behavior problems in children."

Q: A nurse evaluates whether a preschooler's aggressive behavior is problematic. According to the chapter, the difference between normal and problematic behavior lies in which features? Select all that apply.
A) Quantity, meaning the number of occurrences
B) Severity, meaning interference with social or cognitive functioning
C) Duration, with a threshold of at least 4 weeks
D) The specific behavior itself, independent of how often or how long it occurs
E) Onset, meaning when the behavior started
Answer: A, B, C, E
Rationale: "Generally, the difference between normal and problematic behavior is not the behavior itself but its" quantity, "number of occurrences"; severity, "interference with social or cognitive functioning"; distribution, "different manifestations"; onset, "when the behavior started"; and duration, "at least 4 weeks." "Extreme aggression requires professional treatment and is often difficult to change."
Trap: (D) is the one option from the wrong side of the sort, and it states exactly what the chapter denies in the same sentence that introduces the list. It pulls because an act such as hitting sounds inherently pathologic, and the chapter's whole point is that the same act is normal or problematic depending on these five dimensions.

Q: A 3-year-old has been repeating the initial sounds of words for the past few weeks. Which findings would warrant referral for speech evaluation? Select all that apply.
A) The stuttering persists for 3 to 6 months
B) There is a family history of stuttering
C) There is evidence of struggling or distress related to the stuttering
D) The child is 3 years old, which falls outside the normal range for developmental stuttering
E) The parents report that the child stutters more when excited
Answer: A, B, C
Rationale: Refer for speech evaluation if stuttering "persists for 3 to 6 months," if "there is a family history of stuttering," or if "there is evidence of struggling or distress related to stuttering." Developmental stuttering "is common during language development in children ages 2 to 5 years" and "usually resolves during childhood," affecting boys more frequently than girls. The most critical period for speech development is 2 to 4 years, when "children are using their rapidly growing vocabulary faster than they can produce the words."
Trap: (D) and (E) are the two options from the normal side of the sort. (D) misstates the age range — 3 years sits squarely inside the 2-to-5-year window — and (E) describes ordinary variation rather than a referral criterion. The parent instruction meanwhile is to "speak slowly and relaxed" and "take time to listen attentively," never to interrupt, complete the child's sentences, or place "undue emphasis on a child's stuttering," which "may exacerbate the problem."

Topic: preschooler nutrition, dental health, and injury prevention

Q: A nurse teaches the 5-2-1-0 framework to the parents of a preschooler. Which statements correctly state the framework? Select all that apply.
A) 5 servings of fruits and vegetables per day
B) 2 hours or less of screen time per day
C) 1 hour of physical activity per day
D) 0 or limited servings of sugar-sweetened beverages
E) 5 hours of physical activity per week and 2 servings of fruits and vegetables per day
Answer: A, B, C, D
Rationale: The Nursing Alert gives the framework as "5 servings of fruits and vegetables per day, 2 hours or less of screen time per day, 1 hour of physical activity per day, and 0 (or limited) servings of sugar-sweetened beverages." Fruit juice for ages 1 to 6 years is separately limited to 4 to 6 oz/day, because excessive juice and other sugar-sweetened beverages are associated with "dental caries" and "adverse cardiometabolic effects."
Trap: (E) is the one option from the wrong side of the sort, and it keeps both digits while swapping their units — which is exactly how this mnemonic fails in practice. Each digit attaches to a different quantity, and the order is fixed.

Q: A parent is distressed that their 4-year-old "eats nothing." The child's growth is tracking normally. Which counseling reflects the chapter?
A) Quality is much more important than quantity, children self-regulate their caloric intake across meals, a preschooler eats approximately half of an adult's portion, and keeping a weekly record of everything the child eats with measured amounts gives a more accurate estimate
B) The child should be required to finish a full portion at each meal
C) The child should be offered only preferred foods until the appetite improves
D) A caloric supplement should be started, since 4-year-olds cannot self-regulate intake
Answer: A
Rationale: "In general, the quality is much more important than the quantity, which nurses should stress during nutritional counseling." "There is some evidence that children self-regulate their caloric intake. If they eat less at one meal, they compensate at another meal or snack." Preschoolers "eat only slightly more than toddlers, or approximately half of an adult's portion." The intervention is to advise "parents to keep a weekly record of everything the child eats," measuring the amount to "provide a more accurate estimate of food intake at each meal" — after which parents "are usually amazed at how much the child consumed."
Trap: (B) is the pressure that turns a normal phase into a contest. At 4 years children "seem to enter another period of finicky eating," and by 5 years they "are more agreeable to trying new foods, especially if encouraged by an adult who allows the child to help with food preparation."

Q: A parent of a 4-year-old asks who should handle the child's oral care now that fine motor skills have improved. Which response is correct?
A) The child still requires assistance and supervision with brushing, and parents should perform flossing
B) The child can brush and floss independently at this age
C) Flossing is unnecessary until the permanent dentition erupts
D) Eruption of the deciduous teeth is still in progress and will not be complete until age 6
Answer: A
Rationale: "Although preschoolers' fine motor control is improved, they still require assistance and supervision with brushing, and parents should perform flossing." "By the beginning of the preschool period, the eruption of the deciduous (primary) teeth is complete," and permanent dentition may begin erupting at 5 years. Professional care and routine prophylaxis, especially fluoride supplements, should continue, with frequency individualized by risk assessment.
Trap: (D) inverts the dentition timeline: deciduous eruption is complete at the start of this period rather than at its end. Note also that if oral trauma occurs, "prompt evaluation by a dentist is necessary," because preserving the space of an avulsed tooth matters for eruption of the secondary tooth.

Q: A nurse plans injury-prevention teaching for the parents of a 4-year-old. Which statement reflects the chapter?
A) Preschoolers are less prone to falls than toddlers and prevention can now include education about safety and hazards, but pedestrian motor vehicle injuries increase and poisoning is still a danger
B) Poisoning risk has passed, because putting objects in the mouth has ceased
C) Falls increase during the preschool years as children become more daring
D) Education is ineffective at this age, so every environmental safeguard must remain in place
Answer: A
Rationale: "Because of improved gross and fine motor skills, coordination, and balance, preschoolers are less prone to falls than toddlers." "Putting objects in the mouth as part of exploration has all but ceased, but poisoning is still a danger." "Pedestrian motor vehicle injuries increase because of activities such as playing in the street, riding tricycles, running after balls, or forgetting safety regulations when crossing streets." Prevention "can now include education regarding safety and potential hazards," and outlet caps "may be discontinued, with verbal explanations given of why danger exists and how to avoid it."
Trap: (B) draws the intuitive inference from the end of mouthing behavior, and the chapter blocks it in the same sentence. Note too that "Because preschoolers are great imitators, it is essential that parents set a good example by 'practicing what they preach,'" since children "quickly observe discrepancies between what they are told to do and what they observe."

Topic: giftedness in the preschooler

Q: A 5-year-old scores 132 on an intelligence test, and the parents ask whether that score settles the question of whether she is gifted. Which response reflects the chapter?
A) Giftedness is commonly recognized as a minimum intelligence quotient of 130, but giftedness may be present in both academic and nonacademic areas, so not all gifted children are identified when intelligence or achievement tests alone are used
B) An intelligence quotient of 130 or above is the sole and sufficient criterion for giftedness
C) Giftedness is recognized at a minimum intelligence quotient of 100, which this score exceeds
D) Intelligence testing has no role in identifying giftedness
Answer: A
Rationale: "Although the definition of gifted varies, giftedness is commonly recognized as a minimum intelligence quotient (IQ) of 130." But "giftedness may be present in both academic and nonacademic areas. Therefore not all gifted children are identified when IQ tests or achievement tests alone are used," and identification "based solely on test scores may result in the tragic loss of the opportunity to develop a child's full potential." The broader gifted-talented view includes "specific academic aptitudes, advanced memory skills, creative thinking, ability in the visual or performing arts, and psychomotor ability, either individually or in combination."
Trap: (B) states the true half of the pair and then draws the conclusion the chapter warns against. Both halves are examinable and both are true at once: 130 is the recognized threshold, and a threshold used alone under-identifies. An item can test either half, so holding only one of them is how this gets missed.

Q: A teacher says a 5-year-old with a diagnosed learning disability is "obviously not gifted, given the diagnosis." Which response is correct?
A) Giftedness can exist in children who have learning disabilities, a pattern termed twice-exceptional
B) A learning disability rules out giftedness by definition
C) The child should be retested once the learning disability has been remediated
D) Giftedness is identified only through academic aptitude, so the teacher is correct
Answer: A
Rationale: The chapter names this directly: "giftedness can exist in children who have learning disabilities," and such students are described as twice-exceptional.
Trap: (B) treats the two as mutually exclusive, which is the assumption that makes twice-exceptional children invisible, because each condition masks the other. (D) narrows identification to the one channel the chapter says is insufficient on its own; giftedness "may be present in both academic and nonacademic areas."

Q: Parents of a gifted 5-year-old say they hesitate to set limits because the child is "smarter than we are," and they are frustrated that she reads fluently but cannot tie her shoes. Which counseling reflects the chapter?
A) Gifted children are children first and have the same needs for love, security, and consistent boundaries as other youngsters; advanced skill in one area may cause adults to exaggerate abilities in all areas, and slower achievement in a particular skill may be mislabeled as lack of trying when it represents the natural progression of abilities
B) Limits should be relaxed for gifted children, since their reasoning exceeds their parents'
C) Uneven skill development indicates that the giftedness assessment was in error
D) A gifted child should be expected to perform at an advanced level across all domains
Answer: A
Rationale: Parents "may be intimidated by having a child smarter than themselves," but "gifted children are children first and have the same needs for love, security, and consistent boundaries as other youngsters." Advanced skills in one area "may also cause adults to exaggerate their abilities in all areas and thus expect excessively mature behavior," and parents "may mislabel slower achievement in a particular skill as lack of trying, when really it represents children's natural progression of abilities."
Trap: (D) is the over-generalization the chapter names as its own distinct parenting challenge, and it is what produces the frustration described in this stem. (B) waives the boundaries at exactly the wrong moment: high energy level and persistence "can lead to discipline problems similar to those seen in children with difficult temperaments."
