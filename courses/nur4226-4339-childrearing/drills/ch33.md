# Chapter 33 — The Child With Musculoskeletal or Articular Dysfunction

Source: `sources/Ch33_Fact_Bank.md` (Hockenberry, Wilson, & Rodgers, *Wong's Nursing Care of
Infants and Children*, 11th ed., ch. 33, printed pp. 1239–1297).

**Blueprint weight: 5 questions of 121, depth `standard`.** That buys **40 questions across 10
topics**, the count this course has used for every five-item chapter.

**Where the weight went.** At 59 printed pages this is the longest chapter in the course, and the
allocation is roughly proportional to its length rather than to its number of named disorders.
Immobility, casts, traction, and fractures — the general management half, pp. 1242–1266 — take 15
questions, because that material generates the priority and first-action items the exam asks
about and because it applies to every disorder that follows. Sports medicine takes 6, hips and
feet 8, and the two chronic rheumatic diseases 5.

**What is drilled from both ends.** The mirror pairs the fact bank flags, written as opposed
options: elevation, which is correct for a freshly casted limb and wrong once compartment
syndrome is suspected; Buck against Russell traction; ice for acute soft tissue injury against
moist heat for chronic arthritis; heat exhaustion against heatstroke, where the treatment flips
from no external cooling to active cooling; Legg-Calve-Perthes against slipped capital femoral
epiphysis, where rest and containment on one side face a 24-hour surgical window on the other;
metatarsus adductus against clubfoot; and a warm swollen joint against a red hot one, which is
the single sentence separating juvenile idiopathic arthritis from septic arthritis. **The
chapter's own cross-cut is drilled as its own item: knee pain in a child with a normal knee
belongs to the hip.**

**What the weight could not buy.** Left undrilled, deliberately: the AVPU scale and the toe-to-head
secondary survey; Table 33.1's full intervention list; distraction and the Ilizarov fixator; the
orthosis families and the reciprocal gait orthosis; stump shaping and phantom limb pain;
congenital limb deficiency and prosthesis timing; the Salter-Harris types individually and Box
33.4's fracture types; Box 33.7's nine causes of nonunion; contusions, dislocations, and radial
head subluxation; the female athlete triad, sports nutrition beyond hydration, and performance
enhancing substances; torticollis, kyphosis, lordosis, spondylolysis, and spondylolisthesis;
skeletal tuberculosis; Box 33.11's seven ILAR categories of juvenile idiopathic arthritis; and
Box 33.13's eleven lupus classification criteria. That is a great deal of real material. Five
blueprint questions do not reach it, and the honest record of what was cut belongs here rather
than in a footnote.

**Three of the chapter's own five NCLEX review items are drilled directly**: the immobilization
effects select-all, the 2 AM compartment syndrome priority item, and the item on the school
defibrillator. The Pavlik harness item is drilled as a select-all carrying the same teaching
content. The female athlete triad item is the one left out.

**On the GAP register.** Nothing in this chapter's register is flagged "most likely to be tested."
Four source defects are respected rather than reproduced. The chapter gives **two different ice
limits** — no more than 20 minutes in the fracture Emergency Treatment box (p. 1264) and never
more than 30 minutes in the sprain section (p. 1269) — and the select-all here uses the sprain
section's own figure, with the discrepancy named in the trap. The chapter also gives **two CPR
breath timings**, 1 second in the box and 2 seconds in the narrative; no question depends on
either. It calls **kyphosis and lordosis "lateral,"** which they are not, so no question asks
about the plane of those two deformities. And it **cross-references spinal cord injuries to
Chapter 33**, the chapter the reader is already in, when they are in Chapter 34.

**Verified counts: 40 questions, 10 topics, 7 select-alls.** Every select-all is drawn from a real
category list in the source: the hemorrhage-control sequence with its tourniquet rule, the
physiologic effects of immobilization, the Family-Centered Care cast care list, the osteogenesis
imperfecta handling instructions, the RICE measures, the Pavlik harness skin-care instructions,
and the juvenile idiopathic arthritis activity teaching. None was manufactured from a taxonomy —
the Salter-Harris types and the ILAR categories would both have made easy select-alls and neither
is used, because sorting a classification alphabetically is not a discrimination a nurse makes.

Topic: musculoskeletal trauma emergency assessment
Subtopic: airway and resuscitation at the scene

Q: A school nurse finds a 10-year-old unresponsive at the base of a climbing structure after a fall. How should the airway be opened?
A) With a modified jaw thrust, without head tilt or chin lift
B) With a head tilt–chin lift, the standard maneuver for any child
C) With a head tilt–chin lift, followed by a jaw thrust if it fails
D) Not at all until emergency medical services arrive
Answer: A
Rationale: "When spinal trauma is a possibility, open the airway using the modified jaw thrust maneuver, which is accomplished by grasping the angles of the victim's lower jaw and lifting with both hands, one on each side, and displacing the mandible upward and outward (without head tilt or chin lift)" (p. 1241). The box restates it: in a child "with head, trunk, or multisystem trauma, modified jaw thrust is preferred method."
Trap: (B) is the correct maneuver only once trauma has been excluded, and it cannot be excluded here: "Spinal cord injury is always suspected in a patient with head, trunk, or multisystem trauma," and only in a fully equipped trauma center "can spinal cord injury be ruled out or diagnosed. Therefore the patient is treated as if injury were present" (p. 1241). (D) leaves the airway closed while waiting.

Q: A bystander finds an unresponsive, pulseless 6-year-old after an unwitnessed collapse. What is the correct sequence?
A) Perform 2 minutes of CPR, then activate emergency medical services and call for a defibrillator
B) Activate emergency medical services first, then begin CPR
C) Activate emergency medical services and wait for the defibrillator before starting compressions
D) Give two rescue breaths, then activate emergency medical services
Answer: A
Rationale: "Activate the EMS system if child appears to be pubertal or older or if witnessed sudden collapse. If child has not reached puberty, perform 2 minutes of cardiopulmonary resuscitation (CPR) (as per assessment); then activate EMS and call for an automatic external defibrillator (AED)" (p. 1241). Compressions run "at a rate of at least 100 compressions per minute," and interruptions are kept under 10 seconds.
Trap: (B) is the correct sequence for a pubertal child or a witnessed sudden collapse, and this child is neither, which is the entire point of the age split. (C) delays the only intervention maintaining perfusion.

Subtopic: bleeding and impaled objects

Q: A teenager has a deep thigh laceration with arterial bleeding and a piece of metal protruding from the wound. Direct pressure, elevation, and arterial pressure-point compression have failed, and a tourniquet is applied. Which statements are correct? Select all that apply.
A) The tourniquet is a last resort, used only after the other measures fail
B) Once applied, the tourniquet is not loosened
C) The protruding object is left in place
D) Keeping the tourniquet in place gives a better chance of survival, even if it costs the limb
E) The tourniquet should be briefly released every 15 minutes to perfuse the limb
Answer: A, B, C, D
Rationale: The hemorrhage sequence is direct pressure, elevation, arterial pressure point, then "Apply tourniquet only as a last resort. Once a tourniquet is applied, it should not be loosened" (p. 1241), and "Do not remove objects protruding from child's body." The reasoning is systemic rather than local: "If the tourniquet is loosened or removed, the toxins can be released into the circulation in high concentrations and may induce a systemic, deadly tourniquet shock. With the tourniquet in place, the patient has a better chance of survival, even though it may mean the loss of a limb" (p. 1242).
Trap: (E) is the wrong option and it is the intuitive one, since periodic release sounds like limb-sparing care. It is the exact action the chapter forbids, and the harm it names is to the whole patient, not to the leg.

Topic: physiologic and psychologic effects of immobilization
Subtopic: what immobility does to each system

Q: A 9-year-old is immobilized for several weeks after significant trauma. Which effects should the nurse anticipate? Select all that apply.
A) Orthostatic intolerance
B) Deep vein thrombosis
C) Pneumonia from pooled secretions
D) Renal calculi
E) Diarrhea
F) Constipation and fecal impaction
Answer: A, B, C, D, F
Rationale: "Immobility has three major cardiovascular consequences: orthostatic intolerance, increased workload of the heart, and thrombus formation" (p. 1243). Respirations become "slower and more shallow" and "A weak and ineffectual cough reflex contributes to stasis of secretions," with atelectasis and pneumonia as the complications (p. 1244). "The urinary system is designed to function in an upright posture," and settled particulate matter "may serve as nuclei for calculi formation or as foci for infection." "Slowing of stool in the colon causes the feces to become hard (fecal impaction)."
Trap: (E) is the wrong option and it inverts the gastrointestinal effect, since immobility slows the gut rather than speeding it. This is the chapter's own NCLEX question 1, where diarrhea and a sense of euphoria are the two excluded options — the documented psychologic response is anxiety, depression, and regression, not elation.

Q: A child has been on bed rest for 5 days after a spinal injury, with no range-of-motion exercises. Which statement is accurate?
A) Inactive muscle loses strength at about 3% per day, and contractures develop unless range of motion is reestablished within 3 to 7 days
B) Muscle strength is preserved for the first 2 weeks of bed rest
C) Contractures take at least 6 weeks of immobility to develop
D) Contractures affect the extensor muscles first, because they are the stronger group
Answer: A
Rationale: "Inactive muscle loses strength at the rate of 3% per day," and "unless range of motion is reestablished within 3 to 7 days, contractures will develop." The frequent sites are "hip flexion, knee flexion, plantar flexion of the feet, and shoulder stiffness" (p. 1243).
Trap: (D) reverses the muscle balance that produces the deformity, since "body flexor muscles are stronger than extensor muscles" (p. 1243) — which is why every site the chapter lists is a flexion contracture. (B) and (C) both grant the child time the physiology does not give.

Q: A nurse turns an immobilized child and finds a red area over the sacrum that is still red 40 minutes later. What does this indicate?
A) A pressure area is developing, and treatment should begin now
B) Normal reactive hyperemia, which requires no action
C) An early sign of deep vein thrombosis
D) A contact reaction to the bed linen, requiring only a linen change
Answer: A
Rationale: Normal reactive hyperemia "should disappear within 5 to 15 minutes," and "Prolonged redness (>30 minutes) indicates that a pressure area is developing and treatment should begin" (p. 1245). The visible change understates the damage: "The skin changes observed may represent the top of a cone-shaped area with widespread tissue destruction, beneath which tissue rapidly ulcerates and creates a large pressure ulcer that sometimes extends to the bone."
Trap: (B) is correct for redness that fades within 15 minutes, and the 30-minute mark is the entire discriminator. The other areas to check by name are the occiput, trochanter, and heel.

Subtopic: the psychologic cost

Q: A hospitalized school-age child in traction has become tearful, has begun bed-wetting again, and wants to be fed. What is the most useful nursing response?
A) Accept the regression as expected, and involve the child in planning the daily routine and choosing clothes
B) Tell the child that behaving like a baby will not help him get better
C) Restrict visitors so the child can rest and settle
D) Take over all of the child's care to reduce his frustration
Answer: A
Rationale: "Children seek attention by reverting to earlier developmental behaviors, such as wanting to be fed, bed-wetting, and baby talk" (p. 1246), and the chapter names the intervention: "One of the most useful interventions to help children cope with immobility is participation in their own care. Self-care to the maximum extent possible is usually well received by children. They can help plan their daily routine, select their diet (when possible), and choose the clothes they are to wear" (p. 1247).
Trap: (D) is kindly meant and removes the one thing that helps, because immobilization has already taken the child's normal outlet: "Children respond to anxiety with increased activity. Removal of this power deprives them of necessary input and a natural outlet for their feelings and fantasies" (p. 1246). (B) shames a normal response and adds to the guilt the chapter says to correct with "It just happened—it was an accident."

Topic: cast care and neurovascular assessment in children
Subtopic: compartment syndrome

Q: A 12-year-old whose long-leg fiberglass cast was applied this morning reports pain rated 10 out of 10 at 2 AM. The pillows have been pushed off the bed, the toes are edematous, and a finger will not slip under the cast edge. What is the priority action?
A) Notify the surgeon of the findings immediately
B) Give the ordered opioid, elevate the limb on pillows, and reassess in an hour
C) Reassess in 2 to 3 hours to see whether the pain settles
D) Withhold analgesia so the pain can be evaluated accurately
Answer: A
Rationale: This is compartment syndrome: pain out of proportion, edema, and a cast acting as a tourniquet. "Permanent muscle and tissue damage can occur within a few hours" (p. 1252), and the Nursing Alert directs the nurse to "Immediately report any observations that include the five Ps of ischemia: pain, especially with passive range of motion; pallor; pulselessness (an ominous and late sign); paresthesia; and paralysis."
Trap: (B) contains two real interventions and still fails, because it does not report and because "Elevation of the affected extremity is not recommended and may further decrease blood flow to the area" once compartment syndrome is suspected (p. 1265). Elevation is right for a freshly casted limb and wrong here, which is the pairing this item is built on. (C) spends time the tissue does not have.

Q: A child with a forearm fracture reports burning pain and numbness in the fingers. The radial pulse is palpable and capillary refill is brisk. What should the nurse conclude?
A) Compartment syndrome is still possible, and sensory deficit is the most reliable finding
B) Circulation is intact, so compartment syndrome is ruled out
C) Numbness is expected after any fracture and needs no action
D) The finding is reassuring, because pulselessness is an early sign of compartment syndrome
Answer: A
Rationale: "A palpable peripheral pulse and brisk capillary refill may be present despite increasing compartmental pressure," and "Sensory deficit in the affected limb is reported to be the most reliable physical finding of compartment syndrome" (p. 1265). Manifestations "may occur as early as 30 minutes after the ischemia develops."
Trap: (D) inverts the timing and is the most dangerous option, since pulselessness is named as "an ominous and late sign" (p. 1252). (B) draws a reassuring conclusion from the one finding that cannot support it. The clock is short: ischemia lasting as little as 6 hours can cause contracture, and "A great deal of muscle damage occurs after 12 to 24 hours."

Subtopic: living with the cast

Q: A toddler is in a plaster hip spica cast. Which instructions should the nurse give? Select all that apply.
A) Leave the cast uncovered so it dries from the inside out
B) Handle a wet cast with the palms of the hands
C) Turn the child at least every 2 hours so the cast dries evenly
D) Use a heated fan or hair dryer to speed the drying
E) Report the cast if it becomes too loose
Answer: A, B, C, E
Rationale: "The cast must remain uncovered to allow it to dry from the inside out. A wet plaster cast should be supported by a pillow covered with plastic and handled by the palms of the hands to avoid indenting the cast and creating pressure areas. Turning the child in a plaster cast at least every 2 hours will help dry a body cast evenly" (p. 1252). Parents are also told to seek care "if the cast becomes too loose because a loose cast no longer serves its purpose."
Trap: (D) is the wrong option and it fails twice over: "Heated fans or dryers are not used because they cause the cast to dry on the outside and remain wet beneath. They may also cause burns from heat conduction by the cast to the underlying tissue" (p. 1252).

Q: A cast is being applied to a 5-year-old's forearm. Which statement about the procedure is accurate?
A) Both plaster and synthetic materials generate heat as they set, and warmer water increases the burn risk
B) Only plaster generates heat; synthetic material stays cool
C) Warm water should be used, because it makes application more comfortable
D) The cast saw used at removal will cut skin on contact, so the child should be warned
Answer: A
Rationale: "Both types of casting material produce heat from a chemical reaction activated by water immediately after application" (p. 1250), and Table 33.2 states that "the more rapid the drying time, the greater the likelihood of burning; therefore it is recommended to use tepid or cool water to slow drying time and decrease chemical heat produced; the warmer the water, the faster the material dries or hardens" (p. 1251).
Trap: (D) is the reverse of what a preschooler should be told, and the explanation that defuses the fear is specific: because a cast saw "works by vibration," it "cuts only the hard surface of the cast" and "will not cut when placed lightly on the skin" (p. 1252). Children describe the feeling as tickly. This matters at this age because toddlers and preschoolers "do not have easily defined body boundaries."

Topic: traction and mobilization devices in children
Subtopic: maintaining traction

Q: A child in skeletal traction needs to be repositioned higher in the bed. What should the nurse do?
A) Reposition the child without lifting or releasing the traction weights
B) Lift the weights briefly while a second nurse moves the child
C) Remove the weights, reposition, and then reapply them
D) Leave the child as is, since a child in skeletal traction is never repositioned
Answer: A
Rationale: Nursing Alert: "Skeletal traction is never released by the nurse (except under direct supervision by the provider). This precaution includes not lifting the weights that are applying traction (e.g., for moving the child in bed or repositioning)" (p. 1255). The reason is in the physics: "The continuous maintenance of traction is important during this phase because releasing the traction allows the muscle to contract normally and again cause malpositioning of the bone ends" (p. 1254).
Trap: (B) and (C) both look like reasonable ways to make a routine nursing task easier, and both undo the muscle fatigue traction exists to produce. (D) overcorrects into immobility, which brings its own set of complications.

Q: A child is in Russell traction with a padded sling under the knee. Which nursing observation is most specific to this device?
A) Confirming that hip flexion remains at the prescribed angle, and watching for footdrop
B) Confirming that the legs remain fully extended
C) Confirming that the halo pin sites are clean and dry
D) Confirming that the weights rest on the floor between treatments
Answer: A
Rationale: Russell traction "uses skin traction on the lower leg and a padded sling under the knee," and "Hip flexion must remain at the prescribed angle to prevent fracture malalignment." The nurse also watches that "damage to the peroneal nerve under the knee does not cause footdrop" (p. 1254).
Trap: (B) describes Buck extension, "a type of skin traction applied with the legs in an extended position" (p. 1254) — the two skin tractions differ precisely in whether the hip and knee are flexed. (D) would release the traction altogether; countertraction is adjusted by elevating the foot of the bed, not by resting the weights.

Subtopic: traumatic amputation

Q: A child arrives in the emergency department after a traumatic finger amputation, and the severed part has been brought in. How should it be handled?
A) Rinse gently with normal saline, wrap loosely in sterile gauze, place in a watertight bag, and chill the bag in ice water without packing it in ice
B) Place the part directly on ice to keep it as cold as possible
C) Immerse the part in normal saline in an open container at room temperature
D) Scrub the part clean and wrap it tightly in dry gauze
Answer: A
Rationale: The Nursing Alert gives the sequence: "1. Rinse limb gently with normal saline. 2. Loosely wrap limb in sterile gauze. 3. Place wrapped limb in a watertight bag. 4. Chill bag, without freezing, in ice water. Do not pack in ice because this may harm tissue. 5. Label with child's name, date, and time, and transport with the child to the hospital" (p. 1256).
Trap: (B) is the instinctive version and the alert excludes it by name, because direct contact with ice damages the very tissue the surgeon needs. (D) reverses both handling instructions at once, since rinsing is gentle and wrapping is loose.

Topic: pediatric fractures bone healing and bone fragility
Subtopic: how children's fractures differ

Q: A 3-year-old fell from a slide and is walking with a limp, refusing to bear full weight on one leg. A parent says nothing can be broken because she is walking. How should the nurse respond?
A) Walking does not rule out a fracture, and a small child who refuses to walk or crawl should be strongly suspected of having one
B) Walking on a leg does rule out a fracture in a child this age
C) Radiographs in young children are highly reliable, so a negative film will settle it
D) Pain would be severe and constant if a fracture were present
Answer: A
Rationale: Nursing Alert: "A fracture should be strongly suspected in a small child who refuses to walk or crawl. However, the fact that a child walks on a suspected fractured extremity does not rule out a fracture" (p. 1264). The anatomy explains it: "the fracture is remarkably stable because of the usually intact periosteum. The child may even be able to use an affected arm or walk on a fractured leg" (p. 1263).
Trap: (C) overstates the imaging, since much of a young child's skeleton "is composed of radiolucent growth cartilage that does not appear on radiographs," and films "are sometimes less reliable than gross deformity and point tenderness in predicting extremity fractures" (p. 1263).

Q: A 2-year-old and a 15-year-old each sustain a femoral shaft fracture. What should the nurse expect?
A) The toddler will heal considerably faster, because healing time is inversely related to age
B) Both will heal in about the same time, since bone healing is age independent
C) The adolescent will heal faster, because of greater bone mass
D) Neither will heal without internal fixation, since nonunion is common in children
Answer: A
Rationale: "Healing is more rapid in children, and the rapidity is inversely related to the child's age. The younger the child, the more rapid the healing process. Nonunion of bone fragments is uncommon" (p. 1260). Femoral shaft healing times are given by age as 2 to 3 weeks in the neonatal period, 4 weeks in early childhood, 6 to 8 weeks in later childhood, and 8 to 12 weeks in adolescence (p. 1263).
Trap: (D) inverts the pediatric advantage, since nonunion is specifically called uncommon. Remodeling runs the same direction: "The younger the child and the closer the proximity of the fracture to the growth plate, the greater the degree of remodeling that is able to take place" (p. 1263).

Q: An adolescent's ankle is forcibly inverted during a game. Which statement explains why the injury pattern in a growing child differs from an adult's?
A) Ligaments are stronger than bone in a growing child, and the growth plate is the weakest part, so injury occurs at the physis
B) Ligaments are weaker than bone at every age, so a ligament tear is the expected injury
C) The growth plate is the strongest part of a child's bone and protects the joint from injury
D) Children's bones are more brittle than adults', so a complete fracture is expected
Answer: A
Rationale: "Ligaments are stronger than bone, and the physis (growth plate) is the weakest part of the bone. The more typical site of injury is at the growth plate" (p. 1269). Before physeal closure, joint injuries are "more likely to cause separation of the epiphysis from the metaphysis (growth plate injury) than dislocation" (p. 1268).
Trap: (D) reverses the mechanical property that defines pediatric fractures: "The pliable bones of the growing child are more porous than those of the adult, which allows them to bend, buckle, and break in a 'greenstick' manner" (p. 1260). (C) contradicts the sentence that opens the Salter-Harris classification, which names the physis as the weakest point of long bones and therefore "a frequent site of injury during childhood trauma."

Subtopic: osteogenesis imperfecta

Q: An infant with osteogenesis imperfecta is admitted. Which nursing actions are appropriate? Select all that apply.
A) Lift gently by the buttocks or support with pillows for diaper changes, never by the ankles
B) Obtain blood pressures with a manual cuff
C) Handle and hold the infant with compassion rather than avoiding contact
D) Screen a child with current or healing fractures for osteogenesis imperfecta before concluding abuse
E) Anticipate intellectual disability along with the skeletal findings
Answer: A, B, C, D
Rationale: "Even changing a diaper may cause a fracture in severely affected infants. These children should never be held by the ankles when being diapered but should be gently lifted by the buttocks or supported with pillows." "It is recommended that blood pressure be obtained with a manual cuff to prevent fractures." And the counterweight is stated explicitly: "nurses should not be afraid to touch or handle the infant or child with OI. Such children need compassionate handling and care as much as any other patient" (p. 1287). On the differential: "Children with current fractures or healing fractures should be screened for OI; the assumption that abuse or neglect is the cause of fractures in children must be carefully evaluated by a multidisciplinary team."
Trap: (E) is the wrong option and the chapter says the opposite plainly: these children, "although smaller in stature than same-age children, have normal intelligence and should receive adequate information and instructions regarding their care" (p. 1287).

Topic: sports injury heat illness and the young athlete
Subtopic: sprains and overuse

Q: An adolescent felt a pop in the knee, had immediate pain that quickly subsided, has little swelling, and says the joint feels loose. How should the nurse interpret this?
A) A severe (grade III) sprain, because laxity rather than pain indicates severity
B) A mild (grade I) sprain, because pain and swelling are minimal
C) A muscle strain, because laxity is a common strain finding
D) A contusion, because the pain settled quickly
Answer: A
Rationale: A grade III sprain involves "total loss of ligamentous continuity … Pain is immediate but subsides because none of the pain fibers is being stretched. Swelling may be minimal because hemorrhage extravasates outside of the area into soft tissues" (p. 1269). "The presence of joint laxity is the most valid indicator of the severity of a sprain," and the athlete "complains that the joint 'feels loose' or as if 'something is coming apart' … Pain is seldom the principal subjective symptom."
Trap: (B) is the intuitive reading and it is exactly backward, since less pain and less swelling is worse here rather than better. (C) misapplies laxity, because strains are graded the same way "except that the degree of laxity does not apply," and complaints of laxity are rare even in a severe strain.

Q: A child sustains an ankle injury during a soccer game. Which immediate measures are correct? Select all that apply.
A) Apply ice with a layer of cloth between the pack and the skin
B) Compress and elevate the ankle several inches above the level of the heart
C) Avoid any liniment or heat-producing preparation before the ankle is examined
D) Keep each ice application intermittent, never exceeding 30 minutes at a time
E) Apply a heating pad first, to relax the muscle before evaluation
Answer: A, B, C, D
Rationale: "Soft tissue injuries should be iced immediately," with "A single layer of the wrap or cloth … placed over the injured area to protect the skin under the ice pack." "Ice therapy should be intermittent, and ice should never be applied for more than 30 minutes at a time to prevent tissue damage." "The point of injury must be kept several inches above the level of the heart for therapy to be effective." And parents are cautioned "against using any form of liniment or other heat-producing preparation before examination" (p. 1269).
Trap: (E) is the wrong option, and the reason is concrete: "If the injury requires casting or splinting, the heat generated in the enclosed space can produce extreme discomfort and may even cause tissue damage" (p. 1269). Note that the chapter carries two ice limits — 20 minutes in the fracture Emergency Treatment box (p. 1264) and 30 minutes here — and the emergency figure is the more conservative of the two.

Q: A 14-year-old cross-country runner is diagnosed with a tibial stress fracture and told to rest. What does that mean here?
A) Reduced activity with alternative exercise such as pool running, cycling, or swimming, not bed rest or casting
B) Complete bed rest until the pain resolves
C) Immobilization in a long-leg cast for 6 weeks
D) Continuing the same training, since stress fractures heal with use
Answer: A
Rationale: "Rest is the primary therapy, usually interpreted as reduced activity and the use of alternative exercise—not bed rest or immobilization with casting." "It is important to keep the child or adolescent mobile, and training can be continued," with pool running named as the worked example because it "uses the same movements as running but without the weight bearing" (p. 1270).
Trap: (D) is the version the athlete will prefer, and the chapter forbids it by name: athletes "should seek assistance when an injury is suspected and not 'work through' injuries caused by overuse—for example, shin splints, stress fractures, tendinitis, and apophysitis" (p. 1267). Note too that early films are often unhelpful, since "callus formation is not yet evident."

Subtopic: heat illness and sudden cardiac events

Q: A 13-year-old collapses during football practice. The skin is hot and dry, the core temperature is 105°F, and the athlete is confused. What is the correct management?
A) Move to a cool environment, remove clothing, apply cool water and fans, cool to 102°F, and transport emergently without giving an antipyretic
B) Move to a cool environment, provide rest and oral fluids; external cooling is not necessary
C) Give acetaminophen to bring the temperature down, then reassess
D) Give salt tablets with oral fluids, since this is sodium depletion
Answer: A
Rationale: Heatstroke "represents a failure of normal thermoregulatory mechanisms," with rapid onset, a temperature "typically higher than 104°F (40°C)," disorientation, and sweating that "may not be present." Treatment is "relocation to a cool environment, removal of clothing, application of cool water (wet towels or immersion), and use of fans," and "The child should be emergently transported to the nearest hospital." Cooling continues "until core temperature reaches 102°F (38.9°C) to prevent overcooling. Antipyretics are not used because they are metabolized by the liver, which is already not functioning properly" (p. 1271).
Trap: (B) is the treatment for heat exhaustion, where the sensorium is clear and "External cooling methods are not necessary" (p. 1271); the altered mentation and the temperature are what move this case into a different syndrome. (C) reaches for the one drug class the chapter excludes, and (D) treats heat cramps, whose mechanism is sodium depletion with normal mentation and usually normal vital signs.

Q: An adolescent athlete asks the school nurse how to prepare for a hot afternoon tournament. Which advice is correct?
A) Drink 5 to 8 ounces of water every 15 to 20 minutes, without waiting for thirst
B) Use thirst as the guide, since it reliably signals early fluid deficit
C) Take salt tablets beforehand to replace what will be lost in sweat
D) Use an energy drink, which improves endurance in adolescent athletes
Answer: A
Rationale: "Water is recommended as the best drink for most athletes, and current recommendations are to take 5 to 8 oz of water every 15 to 20 minutes" (p. 1271), and "Although thirst is experienced early in dehydration, it is unreliable as an indicator of fluid deficit." "Fluids should never be restricted during activity."
Trap: (C) is refused by name: "The use of salt tablets or table salt is unnecessary and may actually be harmful," and "it is more important to replace water than sodium and chloride" (pp. 1271–1272). (D) is also refused: "Energy drinks are not recommended in children and adolescents participating in sports activities, and there is no evidence of an increase in performance."

Q: After a 14-year-old athlete's sudden death, a school board asks the nurse whether an automatic external defibrillator would help. Which statement is accurate?
A) An automatic external defibrillator can be effective in resuscitating a child or adolescent with a shockable rhythm
B) Automatic external defibrillators should be used only by trained health care personnel
C) Automatic external defibrillators are not designed for use in children
D) A school program would benefit adult staff but not student athletes
Answer: A
Rationale: "Appropriate use of an automatic external defibrillator (AED) by civilian bystanders or health care workers may save the life of an athlete," and a school-based program "provides a high survival rate for student athletes and nonathletes" (p. 1274). The trauma box directs bystanders to turn on the defibrillator and follow its directions for children (p. 1241). The most frequent medical cause of sudden death during sport is hypertrophic cardiomyopathy.
Trap: (B) and (C) are the two beliefs that keep a defibrillator locked in an office, and the source defeats both in a single sentence by naming civilian bystanders and by giving the survival figure for student athletes specifically. This is the chapter's own NCLEX question 4.

Topic: scoliosis and spinal deformities in adolescents
Subtopic: detection and bracing

Q: A 12-year-old girl who is still growing has a 30-degree idiopathic thoracic curve. What management should the nurse expect?
A) Bracing, with the goal of slowing progression rather than curing the curve
B) Observation only, since curves under 45 degrees never progress
C) Spinal fusion, since any curve over 25 degrees is surgical
D) Exercise and chiropractic treatment in place of a brace
Answer: A
Rationale: "For moderate curves (25 to 45 degrees) in the growing child and adolescent, bracing may be the treatment of choice" (p. 1276), while "Surgical intervention may be required for correction of severe curves or those measuring 45 degrees or more" (p. 1277). The honest framing belongs in the teaching: "Historically bracing has not been shown to be curative; the goal is to slow the progression of the curvature to allow skeletal growth and maturity."
Trap: (D) offers the substitutions families ask about, and the evidence does not support them: evidence for exercise and chiropractic treatment is "very limited," transcutaneous electrical nerve stimulation "has proved to be an ineffective treatment," and "Exercises are of benefit when used in conjunction with bracing" rather than instead of it (p. 1277). (B) collapses three separate degree bands into one.

Q: During scoliosis screening a 12-year-old is found to have a left thoracic curve, back pain, and a hairy patch over the sacrum. What do these findings suggest?
A) A possible intraspinal abnormality, for which an MRI is usually obtained
B) Typical adolescent idiopathic scoliosis, managed by observation
C) A postural variation requiring no follow-up
D) A compensated curve, since the head will align with the gluteal cleft
Answer: A
Rationale: "The presence of pain, sacral dimpling or hairy patches, cutaneous vascular changes, absent or abnormal reflexes, bowel or bladder incontinence, or left thoracic curve may indicate an intraspinal abnormality such as syringomyelia, diastematomyelia, or tethered cord syndrome. An MRI scan is usually obtained for evaluation" (p. 1276).
Trap: (B) is what these findings look like at a glance, and each of the three in the stem sits on the list that excludes it. (D) confuses a red flag with an ordinary finding: "Often a primary curve and a compensatory curve will place the head in alignment with the gluteal cleft. With an uncompensated curve, however, the head and hips are not aligned." Screening itself is scheduled by sex — girls at 10 and 12 years, boys once at 13 or 14.

Subtopic: after spinal fusion

Q: An adolescent 3 days after posterior spinal fusion develops epigastric pain, belching, nausea, and copious vomiting that worsens when supine. What should the nurse suspect and do?
A) Superior mesenteric artery syndrome; reposition to left lateral decubitus or prone and notify the provider
B) Expected postoperative ileus; increase the opioid dose for comfort
C) Wound infection; obtain a culture of the incision
D) Delayed neurologic injury; test lower extremity strength only
Answer: A
Rationale: Superior mesenteric artery syndrome "may occur several days after spinal surgery; this involves duodenal compression by the aorta and superior mesenteric artery and may result in acute partial or complete duodenal obstruction. Clinical manifestations include epigastric pain, belching, nausea, and copious vomiting; symptoms are aggravated in the supine position and often relieved with the patient in a left lateral decubitus or prone position" (p. 1278).
Trap: (B) names a complication that is also on this chapter's postoperative list, and it misses the positional pattern that identifies this one. (D) points at a genuine risk, since "delayed paralysis may develop that requires surgical intervention," but nothing in the stem is neurologic and neurologic checks here are continuous rather than a reaction to vomiting. Every position change is a log roll, to protect the fusion and instrumentation.

Topic: congenital hip and foot deformities in infants
Subtopic: developmental dysplasia of the hip

Q: A 4-month-old is being examined for possible developmental dysplasia of the hip. Which finding is the most sensitive at this age?
A) Limited hip abduction
B) A positive Ortolani sign
C) A positive Barlow sign
D) An audible clunk during hip examination
Answer: A
Rationale: "The Ortolani and Barlow maneuvers are most reliable from birth to 4 weeks of age. Adduction contractures develop at about 6 to 10 weeks of age, and the Ortolani sign disappears. After this time, the most sensitive test is limited hip abduction" (p. 1280). Other findings at this age include the Galeazzi sign and asymmetric thigh and gluteal folds.
Trap: (B) and (C) are the two maneuvers every student learns first, and both have already expired by 4 months — a stage swap the chapter sets up in one sentence. (D) is dismissed outright: "The audible clunk is not pathologic," arising from breaking surface tension, knee rotation, or snapping gluteal tendons.

Q: Parents of a 2-day-old newly diagnosed with developmental dysplasia of the hip are being taught about the Pavlik harness. Which instructions are correct? Select all that apply.
A) Check two to three times a day for red areas under the straps and at skin folds
B) Do not adjust the harness
C) Always place the diaper under the straps
D) Avoid lotions and powders under the straps
E) Remove the harness whenever the infant seems uncomfortable
Answer: A, B, C, D
Rationale: The skin care instructions are printed verbatim: "Check frequently (at least two to three times per day) for red areas under the straps and at skin folds"; "Gently massage healthy skin under the straps once a day to stimulate circulation"; "In general, avoid lotions and powders because they can cake and irritate the skin"; "Always place the diaper under the straps." And "Parents should be instructed not to adjust the harness" (p. 1281).
Trap: (E) is the wrong option, because the harness is "worn continuously until the hip is stable on both clinical and ultrasound examination, usually within 6 to 12 weeks," and whether it may be removed at all "is determined individually on the basis of the provider's recommendations" (p. 1281). Interruptions cost more than they appear to: treatment begun before 2 months of age "achieves the highest rate of success."

Q: An 11-month-old is diagnosed with developmental dysplasia of the hip. What treatment should the nurse expect?
A) Closed reduction with a spica cast for approximately 12 weeks
B) A Pavlik harness worn continuously for 6 to 12 weeks
C) Observation, since the hip will stabilize once the child walks
D) Open reduction with pelvic osteotomy, which is standard at this age
Answer: A
Rationale: "In the child 6 to 24 months of age, the Pavlik harness has a low success rate. Therefore surgical treatment with a closed reduction is recommended, and the child is placed in a spica cast for approximately 12 weeks"; an open reduction follows only if the hip is not reducible (p. 1281).
Trap: (B) is the newborn treatment, which is exactly why this chapter's own review item on hip dysplasia turns on the child's age. (C) ignores the governing principle: "The longer treatment is delayed, the more severe the deformity, the more difficult the treatment, and the less favorable the prognosis."

Subtopic: foot deformities

Q: A newborn's forefoot is adducted with a kidney-shaped lateral border, but the heel and ankle are neutral and ankle range of motion is normal. The parents ask about corrective shoes. What should the nurse explain?
A) This is metatarsus adductus, which usually corrects on its own by about age 4; bracing and corrective shoes have not been shown to be effective
B) This is clubfoot, and serial casting will begin within the first month of life
C) Corrective shoes will realign the forefoot within a few months
D) Surgery is usually required during the first year
Answer: A
Rationale: Metatarsus adductus is "the most common congenital foot deformity," and the source names the confusion itself: "Unlike a clubfoot, with which it is often confused, the angulation occurs at the tarsometatarsal joint, while the heel and ankle remain in a neutral position. Ankle range of motion is normal" (p. 1283). "The majority of cases spontaneously correct with no treatment over the first 4 years of life. Interventions such as stretching and bracing or corrective shoes have not been shown to be effective."
Trap: (B) applies the Ponseti protocol to the wrong deformity, since clubfoot adds "forefoot adduction, cavus, hindfoot varus, and ankle equinus" and this newborn's ankle is normal. Both deformities carry the same additional instruction: "A thorough hip examination should be performed" because of the associated risk of hip dysplasia.

Topic: hip disorders and orthopedic infections in older children
Subtopic: the limping child

Q: A 6-year-old boy has an intermittent limp and complains of knee pain, but the knee examination is entirely normal. What should the nurse suspect?
A) A hip disorder such as Legg-Calve-Perthes disease, which commonly refers pain to the knee
B) A knee sprain that will resolve with rest
C) Growing pains, which characteristically localize to the knee
D) Juvenile idiopathic arthritis of the knee
Answer: A
Rationale: Legg-Calve-Perthes disease is "a self-limiting disorder in which there is avascular necrosis of the femoral head," and "most cases occur as an isolated event in boys between 4 and 8 years of age." Onset "is usually insidious, and the history may reveal only intermittent appearance of a limp on the affected side," while "The pain may be experienced in the hip, along the entire thigh, or in the vicinity of the knee joint" (p. 1282). Initial therapy is "rest or activity restrictions and limited weight bearing."
Trap: (D) would give a warm, swollen joint with morning stiffness, and this knee is normal on examination. Knee pain with a normal knee is the cross-cut that sends the examiner to the hip, and it applies to slipped capital femoral epiphysis too, where pain may be felt in "hip, groin, thigh, or knee" (p. 1283).

Q: A 13-year-old boy with obesity has hip pain, holds the affected leg flexed, abducted, and externally rotated, and cannot bear weight. Slipped capital femoral epiphysis is diagnosed. What should the nurse anticipate?
A) Immediate non–weight bearing, with surgery generally within 24 hours of the onset of acute symptoms
B) A trial of rest and NSAIDs with weight bearing as tolerated
C) Abduction bracing for containment of the femoral head
D) Discharge home with follow-up in 2 weeks
Answer: A
Rationale: "Once the diagnosis is established, the child should be non–weight bearing to prevent further slippage. SCFE is an emergency and requires early diagnosis and treatment," and "Most surgeons prefer to take the child to surgery within 24 hours of the onset of acute symptoms and avoid further risk of avascular necrosis" (p. 1283). The child typically "lies stiff with the lower extremity flexed, abducted, and externally rotated because of the pain."
Trap: (C) borrows the containment strategy used for Legg-Calve-Perthes, where rest, activity restriction, and abduction bracing really are the initial management (p. 1282). The two hip disorders look similar in the hallway and differ entirely in urgency. Note also that obesity is a strong association rather than a requirement: "SCFE can also occur in children who are not obese."

Subtopic: bone and joint infection

Q: A 4-year-old has a fever and a knee that is swollen, warm, erythematous, and exquisitely painful, and resists any movement of it. What should the nurse anticipate?
A) Joint aspiration with Gram stain and culture, followed by surgical decompression and irrigation
B) Moist heat and range-of-motion exercises, as for arthritis
C) Radiographs to confirm the diagnosis before any intervention
D) Observation for 48 hours to see whether the swelling resolves
Answer: A
Rationale: In septic arthritis, "The affected joint is aspirated and the specimen evaluated by Gram stain, culture, and determination of leukocyte count," and "Surgical decompression of the joint along with irrigation and debridement is recommended" (p. 1286). The picture is "severe joint pain, swelling, warmth of overlying tissue, and occasionally erythema. The child is resistant to any joint movement," and "The most common causative organism is S. aureus."
Trap: (B) is the correct comfort measure for juvenile idiopathic arthritis and dangerously wrong here, and the chapter supplies the one-sentence discriminator: "Erythema is not typical, and a warm, painful red joint is always suspect for infection" (p. 1288). (C) waits on a study that cannot help yet, since "Early radiographic findings are limited to soft tissue swelling."

Q: A 9-year-old with suspected osteomyelitis has a radiograph showing only soft tissue swelling. What should the nurse understand about the workup and treatment?
A) Radiographic changes beyond soft tissue swelling appear only after 2 to 3 weeks, so cultures are obtained and intravenous antibiotics begun without waiting for the film to change
B) The unremarkable radiograph rules out osteomyelitis
C) A bone scan will confirm or exclude the diagnosis
D) Antibiotics should be withheld until the organism is definitively identified
Answer: A
Rationale: "Radiographic signs, except for soft tissue swelling, are evident only after 2 to 3 weeks" (p. 1285). "Organism identification and antibiotic susceptibility testing are essential for effective therapy," so cultures of aspirated subperiosteal pus, blood, joint fluid, and infected skin are taken first, and "After culture specimens are obtained, empiric therapy is started with IV antibiotics covering the most likely organisms," continued "for at least 4 weeks." Surgical aspiration and drainage "should be performed as soon as possible."
Trap: (C) overreads a study the chapter qualifies: a bone scan "is useful in locating multiple sites; however, it is not a diagnostic test" (p. 1285). (D) gets the sequence half right and stops in the wrong place — cultures precede antibiotics, but antibiotics do not wait for the result.

Topic: juvenile idiopathic arthritis and lupus in children
Subtopic: juvenile idiopathic arthritis

Q: A 4-year-old with oligoarticular juvenile idiopathic arthritis and a positive antinuclear antibody is due for follow-up. Which examination is essential, and why?
A) Regular slit-lamp examination, because chronic anterior uveitis is usually asymptomatic and can cause permanent vision loss
B) Annual visual acuity screening, which will detect uveitis early
C) Fundoscopic examination for cotton wool spots
D) No eye examination unless the child reports eye pain or redness
Answer: A
Rationale: "Slit-lamp ophthalmologic examinations at regular intervals are required to diagnose chronic anterior uveitis (iridocyclitis) … however, most cases are asymptomatic. Consequently, routine slit-lamp examinations are critical. The children at greatest risk for development of uveitis have pauciarticular disease and a positive antinuclear antibody" (p. 1289). Uveitis "may cause permanent vision loss if undiagnosed and not aggressively treated."
Trap: (D) follows ordinary clinical logic and misses the point entirely, because this complication announces itself only after the damage is done. (C) borrows the ophthalmologic findings of lupus — "Cotton wool spots, papilledema, retinopathy" (p. 1292) — which belong to the other rheumatic disease in this chapter.

Q: Which teaching points are correct for a school-age child with juvenile idiopathic arthritis? Select all that apply.
A) Attend school even on days when the joints hurt
B) Use moist heat, most practically a bath, for pain and stiffness
C) Continue regular play, exercise programs, and sports unless there is a specific risk of injury
D) Discourage daytime naps, because inactivity provokes stiffness
E) Apply ice to painful joints as the standard comfort measure
Answer: A, B, C, D
Rationale: "School-aged children should attend school, even on days when there is joint pain. Staying home will not improve arthritis" (p. 1291). "Application of heat has been beneficial to children with arthritis. Moist heat is best for relieving pain and stiffness, and the most efficient and practical method is via the bathtub" (p. 1291). "Unless there is a specific risk of injury related to arthritis, the child should not be restricted from regular play, dance, exercise programs, and even individual and team sports" (p. 1290), and "Daytime naps are discouraged, especially because inactivity provokes stiffness."
Trap: (E) is the wrong option and it is correct for a different problem in the same chapter, since ice belongs to acute soft tissue injury under RICE (p. 1269) while chronic arthritis calls for heat. Reaching for the familiar sports-injury measure is the error this pairing is designed to catch.

Q: An adolescent with juvenile idiopathic arthritis is starting weekly low-dose methotrexate. Which instruction set is correct?
A) Take a daily folic acid supplement, take the dose at bedtime to reduce nausea, avoid alcohol, and expect regular liver enzyme and blood count monitoring
B) Take the dose daily, and expect no laboratory monitoring
C) Continue the drug through any illness, including varicella
D) Add over-the-counter ibuprofen for episodes of fever
Answer: A
Rationale: "A daily folic acid supplement can help reduce the occurrence of oral ulcers. Taking methotrexate at bedtime may help reduce nausea." "Alcohol consumption … increases the risk of hepatotoxicity," and "Laboratory monitoring of liver enzyme levels and blood counts is crucial" (p. 1289). "Weekly low-dose methotrexate therapy is usually the first DMARD used."
Trap: (C) contradicts a specific instruction: "During some illnesses, especially varicella, methotrexate should be discontinued because it can suppress the immune response" (p. 1289). (D) adds the drug class the teaching excludes, since patients are told to "avoid additional over-the-counter NSAIDs and to take acetaminophen for episodes of fever."

Subtopic: systemic lupus erythematosus

Q: An adolescent with systemic lupus erythematosus feels well and asks why she needs urine testing at every visit. What is the best explanation?
A) Lupus nephritis is usually asymptomatic, so urine and renal function monitoring is how it is detected
B) The urine test tracks the progression of the malar rash
C) Urine testing is required only while she is on corticosteroids
D) She may stop the testing once she has been symptom free for a year
Answer: A
Rationale: "Lupus nephritis is usually asymptomatic; consequently, monitoring of urine and renal function is required to detect disease. Kidney biopsy is required for lupus nephritis classification" (pp. 1292–1293). The same silent-complication logic runs through this disease, which is why the nurse should also "Assess school performance and emotional stability at each visit as possible indicators of central nervous system involvement" (p. 1293).
Trap: (D) ties monitoring to symptoms in a disease whose most serious organ involvement produces none, which is exactly the reasoning the surveillance schedule exists to displace. (C) attaches the monitoring to a drug rather than to the disease.

Q: A 16-year-old is being evaluated for lupus-like symptoms. Which history question is most important for the nurse to ask?
A) Whether she is taking minocycline for acne
B) Whether she has recently changed her diet
C) Whether she uses a sunscreen with an SPF of 30 or higher
D) Whether she has had a recent tetanus booster
Answer: A
Rationale: "Some commonly used drugs such as minocycline, procainamide, hydralazine, and chlorpromazine can cause lupus-like symptoms. Minocycline, a common acne treatment, may not be considered important by the teenager and omitted from the history," and the distinction matters because "Drug-induced lupus resolves with time after the triggering medication has been discontinued" (p. 1293).
Trap: (C) names a real and heavily emphasized teaching point — sunscreen with an SPF of 30 or more, hats, protective clothing, and the "slip, slop, slap" rule (p. 1294) — but it belongs to management after a diagnosis rather than to the differential. What the history has to surface here is a drug the teenager does not think of as medicine.
