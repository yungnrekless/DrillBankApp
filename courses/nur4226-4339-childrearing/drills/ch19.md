# Chapter 19 — Impact of Chronic Illness, Disability, or End-of-Life Care for the Child and Family

Source: `courses/nur4226-4339-childrearing/sources/Ch19_Fact_Bank.md`
(Hockenberry, Wilson & Rodgers, *Wong's Nursing Care of Infants and Children*, 11th ed.,
Section VIII, Chapter 19, printed pp. 589–631.)

**Blueprint weight: ~7 questions of 202, depth `standard`.** Band is 30–45 questions; this
file carries **45** across **11 topics**, one topic at 5 and ten at 4. Eleven overshoots the
6–10 topic aim by one, deliberately: this chapter is two chapters wearing one number — pp.
589–605 are about *living* with a chronic condition, pp. 605–628 are about *dying* — and
fusing a topic across that seam would produce a dashboard entry that cannot tell you what to
open. Six topics sit on the chronic-illness side, five on the palliative side.

**Where the weight went.** The chronic-illness half is built out of enumerated boxes, so the
questions are built out of them too: Box 19.2's eight adaptive tasks, Box 19.3's seven
anticipated stress points, the approach/avoidance two-column list, Box 19.6's five coping
patterns, Box 19.7's ten overprotection characteristics, and the five elements of
normalization. The palliative half is built out of the developmental ladder and the pain
rules, and those carry the weight there.

**Drilled from both ends** (the chapter's own confusable pairs, mirrored as adjacent items):
approach versus avoidance coping; overprotection versus gradual acceptance; Participation
versus Control as normalization elements; palliative care versus hospice; tolerance versus
addiction; irreversibility versus nonfunctionality. Religion appears on **both** sides of the
coping list and is drilled as its own item, because the fact bank flags it as the subtlest
pair in the box.

**What ~7 blueprint items could not buy.** Left undrilled: Table 19.2's full developmental
effects grid; Table 19.4's ethical dilemmas; Box 19.13's symptom taxonomy (its categories are
the source's own filing, not physiologic, and drilling them teaches the table rather than the
care); safe transportation and the car-seat modifications; the insurance facts; Box 19.16 and
Box 19.17 on intervention after sudden death and SIDS home visits; and the whole of §47, the
nurse's own reactions and self-care. Each is real content the exam weight does not reach.
**§46, Care of the Grieving Family, is absent from the fact bank itself** — omitted upstream
at the user's direction — and was therefore not available to drill and was not filled in from
memory.

**Two source defects are drilled as the fact bank resolves them, not as the source prints
them.** Terminal pain medication is drilled as **regular schedule plus breakthrough doses**
(p. 614), not the as-needed wording the chapter's own NCLEX item keys correct. And no item
asks for *the* age of mature understanding of death, because the chapter gives 7, 9, and 9-or-10
on three different pages; the developmental items ask for the **content** of the concept
instead, which is consistent throughout.

**Select-all count: 6** (1 in 7.5), each built on a list the source genuinely sorts two ways
— box membership versus non-membership for the adaptive tasks, the approach/avoidance columns,
overprotection versus gradual acceptance, adaptive versus maladaptive coping patterns, the
three conjunctive conditions of double effect against a plausible fourth, and continue versus
discontinue in the terminal phase. Box 19.11's five subconcepts and Box 19.14's signs are
taxonomies rather than two-way sorts and were written as single-answer items.

Topic: family adaptation to childhood chronic illness
Subtopic: the adaptive tasks of parents

Q: A nurse is planning anticipatory guidance for parents whose 4-year-old was diagnosed last week with a complex chronic condition, and wants to frame the guidance around the adaptive tasks the chapter names. Which of the following are among those tasks? Select all that apply.
A) Meet the developmental needs of the other family members
B) Educate others about the child's condition
C) Pursue every available treatment until a cure is achieved
D) Cope with ongoing stress and periodic crises
E) Eliminate the family's financial burden before discharge
Answer: A, B, D
Rationale: Box 19.2 lists eight adaptive tasks from Canam, among them "Meet the developmental needs of other family members," "Educate others about the child's condition," and "Cope with ongoing stress and periodic crises" (p. 592); achieving a cure and eliminating financial burden appear nowhere in the box.
Trap: (C) and (E) pull because they sound like what a good parent would want, but the box's tasks are all adaptation to the condition rather than escape from it, and (A) and (B) are the two real members most often dropped from recall because they point away from the ill child.

Q: The parents of a child with spina bifida ask the nurse which upcoming events are likely to be hardest on them, so they can prepare. Drawing on the chapter's anticipated stress points, which event should the nurse name?
A) The start of schooling, particularly if placement will not be in a regular class
B) The next scheduled surgical revision of the shunt
C) An unplanned admission for a urinary tract infection
D) A relapse requiring a change in the bracing regimen
Answer: A
Rationale: Box 19.3's seven anticipated parental stress points are developmental and life-stage transitions — diagnosis, developmental milestones, start of schooling, reaching the ultimate attainment, adolescence, future placement, and death of the child (p. 592); the box specifically notes that placement outside a regular class is "particularly stressful" (p. 592).
Trap: (B), (C), and (D) are all medical events, and medical events are not on this list even though the chapter discusses all of them elsewhere; the reflex is to equate stress with acute illness rather than with transition.

Q: A nurse is comparing two families. The first cares for a ventilator-dependent child but has extended family nearby, stable income, and a strong marriage. The second cares for a child with well-controlled asthma but is a single parent working two jobs with no nearby relatives. Which family should the nurse identify as being at greater risk for poor adjustment?
A) The second family, because the functional burden weighs demands against the family's resources
B) The first family, because the severity of the condition predicts the level of adjustment
C) The first family, because technology dependence always exceeds a family's coping capacity
D) Neither, because adjustment cannot be predicted before the family is assessed in the home
Answer: A
Rationale: The chapter states directly that "The level of adjustment is significantly influenced by the functional burden on the family" and gives essentially this comparison, noting that a high-technology family with many resources "may adjust more successfully" than a family with "a less serious condition and few resources to counterbalance" (p. 596).
Trap: (B) is the intuitive answer and the one the chapter names and rejects in the same sentence — "it is easy to assume that families of children with the most severe illnesses or disabilities would have the poorest adjustment" (p. 596); severity is one part of the picture, not the predictor.

Q: The 9-year-old sister of a child with a chronic condition tells her mother she does not want her brother coming along to her friend's sleepover. The mother asks the nurse whether she should insist. What should the nurse advise?
A) Respect the sister's reluctance to include her brother in the activity
B) Require the sister to include her brother so she does not learn to exclude him
C) Explain to the sister that her brother's condition means he needs her more than her friends do
D) Cancel the sleepover so that neither child feels singled out
Answer: A
Rationale: Box 19.4 lists "Respect a sibling's reluctance to be with or to include the child with special needs in activities" among the ways to promote healthy sibling relationships (p. 593), alongside the instruction to let siblings settle their own differences.
Trap: (B) is the reflex answer because forcing inclusion looks like fairness, but this box repeatedly protects the well sibling's own life; (C) compounds the error by making the sister responsible for her brother's needs, which the same box caps by telling parents to "Limit caregiving responsibilities" (p. 593).

Topic: approach and avoidance coping in chronic illness
Subtopic: sorting a single behavior

Q: During a clinic visit the father of a child with cystic fibrosis says quietly, "I know there may come a day when we lose him." The nurse should interpret this statement as:
A) An approach behavior indicating movement toward adjustment
B) An avoidance behavior indicating maladaptation to the crisis
C) A sign of hopelessness requiring referral for depression screening
D) Premature grieving that will interfere with the father's caregiving
Answer: A
Rationale: "Verbalizes possible loss of child" is listed among the eleven approach behaviors, which are defined as "coping mechanisms that result in movement toward adjustment and resolution of the crisis" (p. 594); expressing sorrow and realizing the reason for the emotion is also listed there.
Trap: (C) and (D) pull hard because naming the possible death sounds like giving up, but in this chapter a parent who says it out loud is coping well, and only the avoidance definition carries the word maladaptation.

Q: At the same visit, the mother of a different child with cystic fibrosis describes in detail the college she has already chosen for her daughter, but cannot say what the current treatment plan is. The nurse should interpret this as:
A) An avoidance behavior, because the future plans carry little emphasis on the present
B) An approach behavior, because planning realistically for the future is adaptive
C) An approach behavior, because hopefulness mobilizes goal-directed action
D) A neutral finding, because parents commonly cope by focusing on long-range goals
Answer: A
Rationale: The list pairs "Plans realistically for the future" as an approach behavior against "Entertains unrealistic future plans for child with little emphasis on the present" as an avoidance behavior (p. 594); the discriminator is the absent present, not the presence of a plan.
Trap: (B) is the mirror error of the previous item — the surface topic is identical and only the direction differs, which is exactly how this list builds its own distractors; (C) borrows the chapter's real definition of hopefulness (p. 598) to make the wrong answer sound sourced.

Q: Two parents in the same support group describe their faith. The first says their child's illness has taught the family what matters and they draw strength from their church. The second has stopped giving the prescribed enzymes, saying that healing is in God's hands now. How should the nurse classify these two statements?
A) The first is an approach behavior and the second is an avoidance behavior
B) Both are approach behaviors, because religion sustains hope in this chapter
C) Both are avoidance behaviors, because faith substitutes for medical management
D) The first is an avoidance behavior and the second is an approach behavior
Answer: A
Rationale: "Endows the chronic illness or complex condition with meaning" is an approach behavior, while "Places complete faith in religion to point of relinquishing own responsibility" is an avoidance behavior (p. 594); the discriminator is whether the family still acts.
Trap: (B) and (C) both pull because religion appears on both sides of the same box and readers expect a list to assign a topic to one column; faith that sustains action is approach, faith that replaces action is avoidance.

Q: A nurse observes three avoidance behaviors in a parent during one admission and is deciding whether to document the family as being at risk. What should most influence that judgment?
A) The family's available supports and resources
B) The total number of avoidance behaviors observed
C) Whether any approach behaviors were also observed
D) How long the child has carried the diagnosis
Answer: A
Rationale: The chapter refuses to let avoidance behaviors be scored by counting, stating that "several avoidance behaviors in an emotionally healthy family may denote significantly less risk ... than an equal number of avoidance behaviors in an individual who has few available supports" (p. 594).
Trap: (B) is the reflex because a checklist invites tallying, and the chapter anticipates exactly that; the same count means different things in a supported family and an unsupported one.

Q: A nurse is documenting the coping behaviors of a family whose child has a new diagnosis of a complex chronic condition. Which observations should be recorded as approach behaviors? Select all that apply.
A) The mother asks detailed questions about the diagnosis and the child's present condition
B) The father telephones a parent-to-parent contact the nurse provided last week
C) The mother says she cannot accept that the disease has progressed since the last visit
D) The father acknowledges that his son already senses how serious the illness is
E) The parents describe searching nightly for new treatment centers without regard to benefit
Answer: A, B, D
Rationale: Asking for information about diagnosis and condition, seeking help and support from others, and acknowledging the child's awareness of diagnosis and prognosis are all listed approach behaviors (p. 594).
Trap: (C) and (E) are the avoidance twins of listed approach behaviors — "Is unable to adjust to or accept a change in progression of disease" and "Continually looks for new cures with no perspective toward possible benefit" (p. 594) — and (E) pulls hardest because relentless searching reads as devotion.

Topic: parental reactions to a child's chronic condition

Q: The mother of a 6-year-old with a seizure disorder carries him to the bathroom although he can walk, has never disciplined him, and says she cannot bear to see him frustrated. Which parental reaction does this describe?
A) Overprotection
B) Rejection
C) Denial
D) Gradual acceptance
Answer: A
Rationale: The chapter defines overprotection as parents who "fear letting the child achieve any new skill, avoid all discipline, and cater to every desire to prevent frustration" (p. 595).
Trap: (D) is the near miss, because both overprotection and gradual acceptance involve a parent structuring the child's life; the discriminator is the single word realistic, since gradual acceptance places "necessary and realistic restrictions" while overprotection places none.

Q: A different mother of a 6-year-old with a seizure disorder expects him to dress himself and carry his plate to the sink, gives him the same bedtime as his sister, and has enrolled him in a swim class with a helmet policy. Which parental reaction does this describe?
A) Gradual acceptance
B) Overprotection
C) Denial
D) Rejection
Answer: A
Rationale: Gradual acceptance is defined as parents who "place necessary and realistic restrictions on the child, encourage self-care activities, and promote reasonable physical and social abilities" (p. 595), and it is the only one of the four reactions the chapter treats as adaptive.
Trap: (C) is the mirror error of the previous item — a reader who has just keyed overprotection looks for the opposite extreme and lands on denial, reading the ordinary expectations as a parent acting "as if the disorder does not exist" (p. 595); the helmet policy is what rules that out.

Q: A father brings his daughter to every appointment, keeps her immaculately groomed, and manages her tube feedings without error, but does not speak to her during the visit and criticizes her sharply when she drops a toy. The nurse should recognize this pattern as:
A) Rejection, which may coexist with adequate physical care
B) Not rejection, because the physical care provided is adequate
C) Denial, because the father is avoiding emotional acknowledgment of the disorder
D) Overprotection, because the father manages all of the child's care himself
Answer: A
Rationale: The chapter defines rejection as parents who "detach themselves emotionally from the child but usually provide adequate physical care or constantly nag and scold the child" (p. 595) — and this father shows both faces the definition names.
Trap: (B) is the designed distractor and uses the good physical care as evidence against the diagnosis; in this chapter adequate physical care does not rule out rejection, it is part of the description.

Q: A nurse is reviewing a home visit note describing a parent's interactions with a child who has a chronic condition. Which observations are characteristics of parental overprotection? Select all that apply.
A) Makes decisions without considering the child's wishes, including keeping the child home from school
B) Sets goals for the child that are either far too high or far too low
C) Calls attention to every activity the child attempts and praises each one lavishly
D) Expects the child to complete the same household chores as the siblings
E) Restricts play because of fear the child will be injured
Answer: A, B, C, E
Rationale: Box 19.7 lists being "dictatorial and arbitrary," setting goals "too high or too low," hovering and overdoing praise, and restricting play among the ten characteristics of parental overprotection (p. 603).
Trap: (D) is the one non-member and it is the direct antidote — applying the same family rules to the child as to the well siblings is the Expectation element of normalization (p. 601); (A) and (B) are the members that break the stereotype, since overprotection here includes authoritarian control and mis-set goals, not only indulgence.

Topic: normalization and mainstreaming in chronic illness

Q: An 11-year-old with a chronic illness tells the nurse that nothing about his life is up to him anymore and that he just does what he is told. Which element of normalization should the nurse target?
A) Control
B) Participation
C) Sharing
D) Preparation
Answer: A
Rationale: Control is defined as identifying "areas where child can be in control so that feelings of uncertainty, passivity, and helplessness are decreased," with the example of the child choosing when to rest according to his energy level (p. 601).
Trap: (B) is the close miss and is the pair this chapter flags as most confusable; when the stem emphasizes the child's helplessness the answer is Control, and (D) pulls only because preparation also reduces uncertainty, but Preparation is about advance warning of changes, not about who decides.

Q: A nurse arranges for the same 11-year-old to be responsible for drawing up and recording his own evening medication, and includes him in the discussion of when the home treatments will be scheduled. Which element of normalization does this represent?
A) Participation
B) Control
C) Expectation
D) Sharing
Answer: A
Rationale: Participation is defined as including the child "in as many decisions as possible, especially those relating to his or her care regimen," with the example that "The child is responsible for taking medications or scheduling home treatments" (p. 601).
Trap: (B) is the mirror error of the previous item — the two elements differ by what the child is being given, a role in the regimen versus a domain of his own, and the example here is verbatim from the Participation row.

Q: The mother of a child with a complex chronic condition tells the nurse she has stopped giving her son chores and no longer disciplines him, because it seems unfair to hold him to the same standard as his sisters. Which response is best?
A) Explain that applying the same family rules to all the children supports his development and prevents resentment among the siblings
B) Agree that her son's condition justifies different rules while he is symptomatic
C) Suggest she assign the sisters fewer chores so the children's loads are equal
D) Reassure her that discipline can be resumed once the condition stabilizes
Answer: A
Rationale: Expectation directs the parent to "Apply the same family rules to the child with a complex chronic illness as to the well siblings or peers," with the child "disciplined ... expected to fulfill household responsibilities, and attend school in accordance with abilities" (p. 601); the chapter adds that "Resentment and hostility can arise among siblings if different standards are applied to each child" (p. 604).
Trap: (B) and (D) both pull because deferring discipline sounds compassionate, and (D) adds a plausible-sounding condition; but inconsistent or absent discipline with "different rules to the siblings" is a listed characteristic of parental overprotection (p. 603).

Q: The parents of a 2-year-old with a developmental disability ask the nurse what document will coordinate the services their daughter receives now and who qualifies for them. What should the nurse explain?
A) An individualized family service plan, because early intervention serves children from birth to 3 years old
B) An individualized educational program, because it covers children from birth through the school years
C) An individualized family service plan, which begins at age 3 when preschool services start
D) An individualized educational program, because their daughter has a diagnosed disability
Answer: A
Rationale: Early intervention is defined as effort "to assist children from birth to 3 years old with disabilities and who are developmentally vulnerable," and the chapter names both documents the nurse may help develop — the educational program and the family service plan (p. 591).
Trap: (B) and (D) pull because educational sounds like the general-purpose term; read the middle word instead — family service plan is the birth-to-3 document, and (C) shifts the age boundary, which is the other likely distractor since birth-to-3 is easily remembered as birth-to-5.

Topic: the child's own coping with a chronic condition

Q: A school nurse is reviewing the coping styles of several students with chronic conditions. Which patterns does the chapter identify as adaptive? Select all that apply.
A) Concentrates on what she can do rather than on what is missing and is as independent as possible
B) Takes her medications and treatments reliably and has begun managing them herself
C) Talks with adults and peers about problems and notes that others have it worse
D) Sees herself as less worthy than other children and needlessly restricts her own activities
E) Acts out irritably in ways that are self-initiated but do not achieve what she wants
Answer: A, B, C
Rationale: Box 19.6 names five coping patterns and the chapter identifies exactly two as maladaptive — "Feels different and withdraws" and "Is irritable, is moody, and acts out" — leaving competence and optimism, compliance with treatment, and seeking support as the adaptive triad (p. 598).
Trap: (E) is the option designed to look adaptive, because its box entry opens "Uses proactive and self-initiated coping behaviors" and buries the disqualifier mid-sentence; proactive is not the same as effective in this box.

Q: A 12-year-old with a chronic condition frequently initiates confrontations with staff and refuses treatments, which he later says he did to make a point. His nurse notes that he is clearly taking the initiative rather than withdrawing. How should this pattern be classified?
A) Maladaptive, because the behaviors are not ego enhancing and do not produce the outcomes he wants
B) Adaptive, because self-initiated coping is preferable to passive withdrawal
C) Adaptive, because asserting himself demonstrates developing independence
D) Maladaptive, because any refusal of treatment indicates nonadherence
Answer: A
Rationale: The box describes this pattern as using "proactive and self-initiated coping behaviors, although usually counterproductive in that the behaviors are not ego enhancing or socially responsible and do not result in desired outcomes" (p. 598), and names it one of the two maladaptive patterns.
Trap: (B) and (C) each quote a real-sounding half of the entry, which is exactly how a distractor is built from this box; (D) reaches the right verdict for the wrong reason, since compliance is a separate listed pattern and this one is about the quality of the initiative.

Q: A 10-year-old with a chronic illness tells the nurse she feels better about her own situation after meeting a child on the unit whose condition is worse. How should the nurse interpret this?
A) It is part of an adaptive support-seeking pattern
B) It is minimizing and should be gently redirected
C) It is an avoidance behavior that distances her from her own prognosis
D) It suggests she does not understand the seriousness of her own condition
Answer: A
Rationale: "uses downward comparison (i.e., realizes that others have it worse)" is listed as part of the Seeks support pattern, which the chapter counts among the adaptive ones (p. 598).
Trap: (B) is the reflex because downward comparison reads as an unhealthy defense in ordinary usage; in this box it is support seeking and it is adaptive, and (C) borrows the vocabulary of the parental coping list to sound sourced.

Q: During an exacerbation, an adolescent who has generally managed her chronic condition well becomes visibly sad and angry. Her mother asks whether this means she is no longer coping. What should the nurse explain?
A) Expressing sadness and anger during exacerbations is part of good adaptation
B) Sustained cheerfulness is the marker of good adjustment, so the change warrants concern
C) The mood change signals that the adolescent has entered a maladaptive coping pattern
D) Emotional lability is expected in adolescence and is unrelated to the illness
Answer: A
Rationale: The well-adapted child is described as expressing "appropriate emotions, such as sadness, anxiety, and anger, at times of exacerbations but confidence and guarded optimism during periods of clinical stability" (p. 598), and Fig. 19.2's caption makes the same point.
Trap: (B) states the intuition the chapter contradicts throughout — unearned cheerfulness is a warning sign at every level of this chapter, in the parent, the father, and the nurse; the marker here is emotion matched to circumstance, with the qualifier guarded on the optimism.

Topic: disclosure at the time of a chronic diagnosis

Q: A nurse is preparing to sit with parents who will be told their infant has a chronic condition. Which is most important for the nurse to provide during that session?
A) Sufficient time
B) Written literature about the diagnosis
C) A referral to a parent support organization
D) A complete explanation of the long-term prognosis
Answer: A
Rationale: The chapter lists six things most parents want from the informing session and gives the last its own emphasis — "an opportunity to ask questions, a warm, sympathetic listener, and, most important, time" (p. 600).
Trap: (B) and (C) are both real chapter content — written summaries for unfamiliar terms and the National Self-Help Clearinghouse are named on p. 600 — which is what makes them plausible; (D) inverts the chapter's pacing rule that information is given gradually and repeatedly, never all at once.

Q: While the physician explains the diagnosis, a mother begins to cry. The nurse wants to convey support. Which action is most consistent with the chapter's guidance?
A) Have tissues available and let the mother continue expressing her feelings
B) Place a hand on the mother's shoulder and tell her everything will be all right
C) Offer to step out so the parents can have privacy with their emotions
D) Suggest the session be rescheduled until the mother is composed
Answer: A
Rationale: The chapter directs that emotional support be offered "by having tissues available if a family member cries and demonstrating through facial and body language that indeed this is a difficult and painful period" (p. 599), and warns that touch "can prematurely terminate free expression of feelings, especially when combined with statements, such as" everything will be all right (p. 599).
Trap: (B) is the single most reflexive comforting gesture in nursing and this chapter names precisely that combination as the thing that shuts a parent down; touch is not automatically therapeutic here, and the chapter also directs awareness of cultural issues regarding touching.

Q: The physician has finished explaining a child's diagnosis and prognosis to the parents. What should happen before the conference ends?
A) The child's strengths and the available treatments are stressed
B) The parents are asked to restate the prognosis to confirm understanding
C) The parents are given time alone to absorb what they have heard
D) The discussion is closed so the parents are not given false hope
Answer: A
Rationale: The chapter states that "the informing conference does not end with the presentation of devastating news" and directs that "the child's strengths, appealing behaviors, and potential for development are stressed, as are available rehabilitation efforts or treatments" (p. 600).
Trap: (B) reads as careful teaching and is the likeliest wrong pick, but confirming comprehension happens during the session with questions such as is this clear to you, not as the closing note; (D) misreads the strengths ending as false reassurance when the chapter treats it as the required close.

Topic: palliative care, hospice, and concurrent care

Q: The parents of a child newly diagnosed with a life-threatening illness ask when palliative care would begin. What should the nurse tell them?
A) It begins at diagnosis and continues regardless of whether the child receives treatment directed at the disease
B) It begins when curative treatment has been exhausted
C) It begins when the child's life expectancy is judged to be under 6 months
D) It begins when the family consents to a do-not-attempt-resuscitation order
Answer: A
Rationale: Both definitions the chapter gives start at diagnosis — palliative care runs "from diagnosis through death," and the World Health Organization definition states "It begins when illness is diagnosed, and continues regardless of whether or not a child receives treatment directed at the disease" (p. 606).
Trap: (C) describes hospice, not palliative care — the traditional view "that a child must be considered to have a life expectancy of fewer than 6 months to be referred to hospice care" (p. 613); any option that attaches a prognosis threshold has switched services.

Q: A nurse is explaining the difference between palliative care and hospice to a family. Which description belongs to hospice rather than to palliative care?
A) A community health care program whose bereavement care may continue for a year or more after the death
B) An interdisciplinary approach begun at diagnosis and coordinated across all sites of care
C) An approach in which curative therapy and symptom-directed care coexist from the point of diagnosis
D) Multidimensional assessment introduced at the time of a life-threatening diagnosis with support increasing over time
Answer: A
Rationale: Hospice is defined as "a community health care program that specializes in the care of dying patients by combining the hospice philosophy with the principles of palliative care" (p. 613), and its bereavement care "may continue for a year or more" (p. 613).
Trap: This is the mirror of the previous item, and (D) is the hardest distractor because it is the verbatim definition of concurrent care (p. 606), which is a palliative-care concept; the cue words are 6 months, community program, and bereavement care afterward for hospice, from diagnosis and regardless of treatment for palliative care.

Q: A mother says she does not want a palliative care consultation because agreeing to it would mean she is giving up on her daughter. What is the nurse's best response?
A) Explain that a focus on relieving suffering does not mean curative or disease-modifying treatments will be abandoned
B) Reassure her that she can decline the consultation and revisit it if the disease progresses
C) Agree that requesting the consultation is a significant decision and arrange for the physician to discuss it
D) Explain that accepting palliative care usually shortens the time families have together
Answer: A
Rationale: The chapter names this exact reassurance as the nurse's specific contribution: "nurses can provide families with the much-needed reassurance that a focus on relief of suffering does not mean that curative or disease-modifying treatments will be abandoned" (p. 606).
Trap: (D) states the fear as fact and is wrong twice over, since the chapter reports both that "palliative care prolongs life" and that children sent home on hospice "have gone home and lived longer than expected" (pp. 606, 613); (B) and (C) are the polite deferrals that leave the misconception in place, which the chapter warns causes "unnecessary delays in providing children the treatment they need."

Q: The parents of a child covered by Medicaid are told that enrolling in hospice will mean stopping the chemotherapy that is controlling their son's pain. What should the nurse know about this?
A) Under the Concurrent Care for Children Requirement, children covered by Medicaid or the Children's Health Insurance Program may receive both hospice and curative care
B) Hospice enrollment requires that all disease-directed therapy be discontinued
C) Concurrent hospice and curative care is available only through private insurance plans
D) The family must choose between hospice and continued treatment at each recertification
Answer: A
Rationale: The chapter cites the provision directly: "According to the Concurrent Care for Children Requirement in the Patient Protection and Affordable Care Act of 2010, children covered under either Medicaid or the Children's Health Insurance Program are eligible to receive both hospice and curative care" (p. 613).
Trap: (B) is the adult hospice rule most nurses carry with them and it is the reason this pediatric provision is worth knowing; (C) inverts the coverage, since hospice care "is covered under state Medicaid programs and by most insurance plans" (p. 613).

Topic: communication with the dying child and family

Q: The parents of a 9-year-old with a terminal illness tell the nurse they do not want their son told he is dying, even if he asks. What should the nurse do first?
A) Ask the parents how they would like their son to be told and how they want to be involved
B) Tell the son the truth when he next asks, because children deserve to be provided with the truth
C) Agree to the parents' request, since parents hold decision-making authority for a minor
D) Refer the matter to the ethics committee before any further conversation with the family
Answer: A
Rationale: The chapter's stated first move is that "the nurse should ask parents how they would like their child to be told of the prognosis and how they want to be included in their child's care" (p. 609), even though it also states plainly that "Children deserve to be provided with the truth" (p. 609).
Trap: (B) states a true sentence from the chapter and is still the wrong action, because the chapter reaches disclosure by moving the parents toward telling rather than by going around them; (C) is wrong because the chapter records that terminally ill children "develop an awareness of the seriousness of their diagnosis, even when protected from the truth" (p. 609).

Q: A nurse is preparing to join a physician who will deliver bad news to a child's parents. After ensuring a private setting, what comes next?
A) Determine what the parents already know, and then what they want to know
B) Give the diagnostic and prognostic information slowly and in simple language
C) Acknowledge the parents' emotional reactions before any information is exchanged
D) Summarize the major areas to be discussed and schedule the next meeting
Answer: A
Rationale: Table 19.5 gives six approaches in sequence: provide a conducive setting, determine what the parent knows, determine what the parent wants to know, give information, respond to reactions, and close (p. 608).
Trap: (B) is the likeliest wrong pick because giving information feels like the purpose of the meeting, but steps 2 and 3 both come before it; (D) is the real final step presented out of order, and the same table adds that step 2 includes recognizing denial but not acknowledging it at this stage.

Q: A nurse recognizes that a family's goals of care have never been discussed and that the child's condition is deteriorating. What is the nurse's first responsibility?
A) Explore the patient's and family's wishes
B) Provide the family with information about the available care options
C) Notify the physician that a family meeting is needed
D) Arrange a palliative care consultation
Answer: A
Rationale: The chapter states it directly: "The nurse's first responsibility is to explore the patient's and family's wishes," adding that this "is best done with the physician, but at times nurses need to initiate the process" (p. 608).
Trap: (B), (C), and (D) are all real nursing actions the chapter endorses elsewhere, which is what makes them plausible; (C) in particular pulls because waiting for the physician feels safe, and the chapter explicitly permits the nurse to initiate.

Q: A dying 16-year-old wants to stop further treatment; her parents want it continued. The team works with both to reach a plan neither had proposed. Which strategy does this describe?
A) The arbitrative strategy
B) The deference strategy
C) The advocative strategy
D) The collaborative decision-making preference
Answer: A
Rationale: The chapter names three strategies for parent-child disagreement — deference, in which parental preferences are deferred to; advocative, in which child preferences are advocated; and arbitrative, which "is used to work to resolve the differences between parents and the child" (p. 608).
Trap: (B) and (C) are each one-sided and are the pair most often swapped, since both names describe someone winning; (D) belongs to a different three-term list on the same page, the parental decision-making preferences that may be active, collaborative, or passive.

Topic: the child's concept of death by developmental stage

Q: A 2-year-old is dying in the hospital and his parents are visibly distraught at the bedside. Which nursing action is the priority for this child?
A) Help the parents manage their own feelings so they have emotional reserve for him
B) Explain to the toddler in simple words what is happening to his body
C) Limit the parents' time at the bedside so the toddler is not exposed to their distress
D) Shield the toddler from procedures, since the prognosis is what distresses him most
Answer: A
Rationale: Both versions of the chapter's developmental material give the same primary intervention — "Helping parents deal with their feelings allows them more emotional reserve to meet the needs of their children" (pp. 611, 624) — because toddlers "may perceive the seriousness of their condition from the parents' reactions."
Trap: (D) inverts what actually distresses a toddler, since "This age group reacts more to the pain and discomfort of a serious illness than to the probable fatal prognosis" (p. 624); (C) is the wrong solution to the right observation, because separation and disrupted routine are the toddler's central threats.

Q: A 4-year-old whose sibling died of sudden infant death syndrome is found giggling and clowning during the visitation. His parents are distressed by his behavior. What should the nurse explain?
A) Behavior such as giggling and attention seeking indicates the child's need to distance himself from an overwhelming loss
B) The behavior shows the preschooler does not yet grasp that something significant has happened
C) The behavior is disrespectful and should be redirected so the family is not further upset
D) The behavior suggests the preschooler is relieved, which is common with a resented new sibling
Answer: A
Rationale: The chapter lists "giggling, joking, attracting attention, or regressing to earlier developmental skills" as indicators of "children's need to distance themselves from tremendous loss," and elsewhere calls apparent indifference "a normal response related to their limited coping abilities" (pp. 612, 624).
Trap: (B) is the intuitive misreading and (C) acts on it; the chapter also warns that young children "may react to a less significant loss with more outward grief than to the loss of a significant person," so the size of the reaction is not a measure of the size of the loss.

Q: A dying 8-year-old has become sarcastic and openly refuses to cooperate with the nurses. How should the nurse interpret and respond to this behavior?
A) As a plea for control and power, and respond by giving him control wherever possible
B) As a behavior problem, and respond by setting firmer and more consistent limits
C) As regression, and respond by involving the parents in correcting his manner
D) As a sign of unrelieved pain, and respond by reassessing his analgesic regimen
Answer: A
Rationale: The chapter names the misreading in advance — "Health care professionals may erroneously interpret this behavior as rude, impolite, insolent, or stubborn" — and states that this verbal fight-or-flight reaction "is a plea for some control and power" (p. 612); the intervention is "allowing control where possible and appropriate."
Trap: (B) is the reflex response to rudeness and is the one the chapter specifically anticipates; (D) is plausible in a dying child and is the sort of correct-elsewhere answer that pulls hardest, but the chapter attaches this exact behavior to the school-age threat to security and ego strength.

Q: A dying 15-year-old tells the nurse that what upsets her most is losing her hair. Her mother asks whether her daughter is in denial about dying. What should the nurse explain?
A) Concern for present changes such as hair loss may genuinely exceed concern for the prognosis at this age
B) This is denial, and the adolescent should be gently redirected to discuss her prognosis
C) Adolescents have a mature concept of death, so the statement indicates she has already accepted it
D) The concern reflects unresolved magical thinking and will resolve as the illness progresses
Answer: A
Rationale: The chapter states it twice — adolescents "worry about physical changes even more than the prognosis for future recovery," and the nurse should help parents understand that "concern for present crises, such as loss of hair, may be much greater than concern for future ones, including possible death" (pp. 612, 625).
Trap: (B) treats a developmentally normal priority as pathology, which is precisely the reading the chapter corrects; (C) misuses a true statement, since adolescents do have a mature understanding of death yet are "least likely of all age groups to accept cessation of life, particularly their own."

Q: A 5-year-old whose grandmother died last week asks the nurse whether Grandma gets cold in the coffin and whether she can still hear them. Which subconcept of death has this child not yet acquired?
A) Nonfunctionality
B) Irreversibility
C) Causality
D) Universality
Answer: A
Rationale: Nonfunctionality is the understanding that "Once a living thing dies, all life-defining capabilities (e.g., eating, sleeping, seeing, hearing) of the body cease," and the immature counterpart is that "The dead continue external functions" (p. 611).
Trap: (B) is the close pair and both concern the dead body; irreversibility is about coming back, nonfunctionality is about still doing things while dead, so a child who expects Grandma home from heaven lacks irreversibility while this child lacks nonfunctionality; (C) would be the answer if the child believed her own misbehavior caused the death.

Topic: pain and symptom management at the end of life

Q: A terminally ill child's opioid dose has been increased twice this week to maintain the same level of relief. His mother asks whether her son is becoming addicted. What should the nurse explain?
A) The escalating requirement reflects tolerance, a pharmacologic change, while addiction is psychologic dependence on the side effects of opioids
B) The escalating requirement is an early sign of addiction, so a dose ceiling should be set
C) Addiction is a real risk in terminal illness and the dose should be held at its current level
D) Tolerance and addiction are the same process and both are expected at this stage
Answer: A
Rationale: The chapter distinguishes them explicitly: the child "can become tolerant of the drug, so more drug must be given to maintain the same level of pain relief," and "This is not to be confused with addiction, which is a psychologic dependence on the side effects of opioids" (p. 614).
Trap: (B) and (C) act on the fear the chapter answers flatly — "Addiction is not a factor in managing terminal pain in a child, and the nurse plays an important role in educating parents that their child will not become addicted" (p. 614); both look like escalating doses from the outside, which is why the mechanism is the discriminator.

Q: A nurse hesitates to give an ordered opioid dose for a dying child's severe pain, worried the dose exceeds what she is used to giving and might hasten death. Which principle addresses this concern?
A) The principle of double effect, which supports interventions intended to relieve suffering even when death may foreseeably be hastened
B) The principle of proportionality, which requires the dose be reduced until the risk is eliminated
C) The maximum safe dosage rule, which caps opioid escalation in pediatric patients
D) The principle of nonmaleficence, which requires withholding any dose that could shorten life
Answer: A
Rationale: The chapter names this nurse anxiety directly and answers it with double effect, which "provides an ethical standard that supports the use of interventions that have the intention of relieving pain and suffering even though there is a foreseeable possibility that death may be hastened" (p. 614).
Trap: (C) asserts a ceiling the chapter denies — "there is no maximum dosage that can be given to control pain" (p. 614) — and it is the most dangerous distractor because withholding on that reasoning is exactly what leaves children undermedicated.

Q: A nurse is reviewing the conditions under which the principle of double effect permits an action with a foreseeable bad effect. Which conditions does the chapter state? Select all that apply.
A) The action itself must be good or indifferent, and only the good consequences may be sincerely intended
B) The good effect must not be produced by the bad effect
C) There must be a compelling or proportionate reason for permitting the bad effect
D) The family must give written consent before the intervention is begun
E) The physician must document that death was not intended
Answer: A, B, C
Rationale: Box 19.12 states all three conditions and prefaces them with "if the following conditions are met," making them conjunctive (p. 614).
Trap: (D) and (E) are the plausible-sounding fourth conditions this box invites, because both sound like sound clinical governance; (B) is the condition a distractor most often omits, and it is the one that separates double effect from euthanasia, since relief must not come through the death.

Q: A terminally ill child is receiving analgesia for constant pain. Which approach to scheduling should the nurse expect?
A) Medications on a regular schedule, with extra doses available for breakthrough pain
B) Medications on an as-needed basis only, to avoid unnecessary exposure to opioids
C) Medications on a regular schedule, with no additional doses between scheduled times
D) Medications withheld until the child is able to rate the pain reliably
Answer: A
Rationale: The chapter states that "Pain medications are given on a regular schedule, and extra doses for breakthrough pain are available to maintain comfort," and gives pain control in the terminal stages "the highest priority" (p. 614).
Trap: (B) is the answer keyed correct in the chapter's own review item at p. 628, which contradicts this narrative sentence; the regular-schedule version is the defensible one and matches the WHO stepladder and the chapter's repeated finding that children remain undermedicated.

Topic: care at the time of a child's death

Q: A child is in the final hours of life and is unresponsive. His grandmother asks whether there is any point in talking to him. What should the nurse advise?
A) Encourage her to keep speaking to him in a calm, reassuring voice, because hearing is the last sense to fail
B) Suggest she sit quietly, since an unresponsive child receives no sensory input
C) Suggest she use touch instead, because tactile sensation is preserved longest
D) Encourage her to speak only if the child shows a physical response
Answer: A
Rationale: Box 19.14 lists "Hearing is last sense to fail" among the physical signs of approaching death, and Box 19.15 gives the paired action — "Encourage the family to continue to speak to the child in a calm, reassuring voice" (p. 619).
Trap: (C) inverts the same box entry, which states that tactile sensation decreases while sensitivity to light develops; the two signs are listed together, so the item can be built from either direction.

Q: A child has entered the terminal phase and the nurse is revising the plan of care. Which actions are consistent with the chapter's guidance? Select all that apply.
A) Provide frequent mouth care to prevent drying and cracking of the lips and mucous membranes
B) Discontinue routine measurement of vital signs
C) Continue the medications needed to manage symptoms and maintain comfort
D) Discontinue all medications, since treatment is no longer directed at the disease
E) Establish intravenous access for any child whose oral route becomes unreliable
Answer: A, B, C
Rationale: Box 19.15 directs frequent mouth care and good hygiene, continuing "necessary medications to manage symptoms and maintain comfort," and discontinuing "unnecessary medications and procedures (e.g., measuring vital signs)" (p. 619).
Trap: (D) overshoots the real instruction by dropping the word unnecessary, and (E) ignores the box's own qualifier — intravenous infusion "if access is easily established" or subcutaneous infusion — which matches the standing rule to use the least traumatic method of administration.

Q: The parents of a child with a do-not-attempt-resuscitation order ask whether oxygen can still be given if their daughter's breathing becomes labored. What should the nurse tell them?
A) Yes, because a do-not-attempt-resuscitation order withholds cardiopulmonary resuscitation, not care
B) No, because a do-not-attempt-resuscitation order directs that supportive measures be withheld
C) Only if the order is first rewritten to specify comfort measures
D) Only if the physician is present to authorize it at the time
Answer: A
Rationale: The chapter requires that parents choosing this order "be aware of exactly what will and will not be done for the child and be assured that this does not mean" no care, and uses precisely this example — the family may wish oxygen for difficult breathing without active resuscitation (p. 621).
Trap: (B) equates the order with withdrawal of comfort measures, which is the reflex misreading the chapter names; note also that the chapter states flatly that an order to slow or delay a code is not legal, and that the order must be reviewed regularly because the child's condition or the family's wishes may change.

Q: A previously healthy child has died unexpectedly, and the nurse is preparing to raise organ donation with the parents. Which approach is correct?
A) Raise donation in a private area as a conversation separate from the communication of the death
B) Raise donation immediately after the pronouncement so the family hears everything at once
C) Explain that donation will delay the funeral and prevent an open casket
D) Explain that the family will be responsible for the costs associated with the donation
Answer: A
Rationale: The chapter states that "Discussion of the option to donate organs is always separate from communication of impending or actual death" and directs that the request be made "in a private and quiet area of the hospital" and be "simple and direct" (p. 621).
Trap: (C) and (D) both reverse specific reassurances the nurse should have ready — "The family may have an open casket, and there is no delay in the funeral," and there is no cost to the donor family, although donation "does not eliminate funeral or cremation responsibilities" (p. 622); note also that healthy children who die unexpectedly are described as excellent candidates.
