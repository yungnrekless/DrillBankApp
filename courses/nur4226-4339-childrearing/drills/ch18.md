# Chapter 18 drills — Health Problems of the Adolescent

Authored from `sources/Ch18_Fact_Bank.md` (Hockenberry, Wilson & Rodgers, 11th ed.,
printed pp. 551–588).

Blueprint weight is **6 questions of 202 (3.0%), depth `standard`** — 43 drills
across 10 topics.

This is the longest chapter in the course: 38 printed pages across five largely
independent domains — male reproductive conditions, female reproductive and
menstrual conditions, sexually transmitted infections, reproduction, and the
behavioral conditions. At six blueprint items across that much ground, depth had to
be allocated rather than spread evenly, and the allocation follows the fact bank's
own judgment.

**Where the weight went.** **Testicular torsion**, because it is the chapter's one
true surgical emergency and its discriminators against epididymitis are exactly
what an item is built from. **The sexually transmitted infections**, which are the
densest cluster of confusable pairs in the book — the three vaginal discharges
alone generate several items. **Eating disorders**, including refeeding syndrome,
which is the single most dangerous thing a nurse can miss in this chapter and has a
specific timing window. And **suicide**, above all the confidentiality exception,
which reverses the rule taught throughout Chapter 17 and is therefore a guaranteed
trap.

**The one flagged omission was drilled rather than inherited.** The GAP register
recorded Box 18.1's causes of abnormal uterine bleeding as a deliberate omission
while noting that the **iatrogenic** list "is the one most likely to be tested," and
it preserved that list verbatim inside the register entry. It is drilled here as a
select-all rather than left for a logged miss.

**Drilled from both ends:** testicular torsion against epididymitis, primary
against secondary amenorrhea, primary against secondary dysmenorrhea, abnormal
uterine bleeding against dysfunctional uterine bleeding, the three vaginal
discharges against one another, primary against recurrent herpes, anorexia nervosa
against bulimia nervosa, and completed suicide methods against attempted ones —
two different questions with two different answers.

**What thirty-eight printed pages could not buy at 43 questions.** Left undrilled:
the gynecologic examination; male conditions other than torsion and varicocele;
Table 18.3's contraceptive methods beyond the protection column and two numeric
cautions; adolescent pregnancy management beyond the disclosure and nutrition
rules; the substance-use epidemiology tables; and the tobacco-cessation program
detail. Cephalopelvic disproportion, self-harm epidemiology, and the SLAP mnemonic
are each represented by at most a single item.

Five questions are select-all, each drawn from a list that genuinely sorts two
ways: the female athlete triad against a treatment that is the fallback rather than
first-line, iatrogenic causes of abnormal uterine bleeding against a cause that is
real but not iatrogenic, the minimum diagnostic criteria for pelvic inflammatory
disease against a supporting criterion, contraceptive methods by whether they
protect against sexually transmitted infection, and the SCOFF questions against one
the tool does not ask.

Topic: testicular torsion and male reproductive conditions

Q: A 14-year-old boy has sudden severe left scrotal pain with nausea, vomiting, and abdominal pain. He is afebrile with no dysuria. The cremasteric reflex is absent on the affected side. Which condition does this describe?
A) Testicular torsion, indicated by sudden onset, gastrointestinal symptoms, absent cremasteric reflex, and the absence of fever or urinary symptoms
B) Epididymitis, indicated by the scrotal pain and swelling
C) Varicocele, indicated by the unilateral findings
D) Inguinal hernia
Answer: A
Rationale: Testicular torsion presents with sudden severe pain, nausea, vomiting, and abdominal pain, an absent cremasteric reflex, and no fever or urinary symptoms. Epididymitis is "slow and insidious," has no gastrointestinal symptoms, and presents with dysuria, fever, and pyuria.
Trap: (B) is the paired diagnosis, and the chapter supplies the discriminator outright: the gastrointestinal symptoms belong to torsion. Onset speed and the cremasteric reflex are the other two, and this stem gives all three.

Q: The same adolescent presents 3 hours after the onset of pain. What is the appropriate management?
A) Manual detorsion may be attempted within 6 hours; beyond 6 hours, or after a failed manual attempt, prompt surgery is required
B) Schedule an outpatient ultrasound for the following morning
C) Observe for 24 hours with scrotal elevation and ice
D) Begin oral antibiotics and reassess in 48 hours
Answer: A
Rationale: Under 6 hours, manual detorsion may be attempted; beyond 6 hours, or after a failed manual attempt, prompt surgery is indicated. The Nursing Alert calls for immediate medical evaluation of a red, painful, swollen scrotum.
Trap: (B), (C), and (D) all introduce delay, and each is plausible as ordinary clinical caution. Torsion is the one condition in this chapter where scheduling anything is the wrong answer — the 6-hour window is the whole reason the evaluation is immediate.

Q: A 16-year-old has a painless scrotal mass described as feeling like a bag of worms on the left. It becomes distended when he stands and decreases when he lies down, and the left testicle is smaller than the right. Which condition is this?
A) Varicocele, the most common treatable cause of male-related impaired fertility
B) Testicular torsion
C) Epididymitis
D) Hydrocele
Answer: A
Rationale: A varicocele is typically left-sided and wormlike. Normally the left testicle is larger, but with a varicocele "the left is smaller." The mass "decreases in size when recumbent" and "becomes distended when upright," and it is "the most common treatable cause of male-related impaired fertility."
Trap: (D) shares the painless-mass presentation. The two positional findings are the discriminator, and note that they run opposite to the usual description of a hernia — this mass is smaller lying down, not larger.

Q: A parent asks why their son's scrotal pain warrants an emergency visit rather than a next-day appointment. Which explanation is correct?
A) Testicular torsion is a surgical emergency in which delay beyond 6 hours changes the management from possible manual detorsion to prompt surgery, and a red, painful, swollen scrotum requires immediate medical evaluation
B) Scrotal pain is rarely urgent in adolescents
C) The urgency relates to the risk of transmitting infection
D) Emergency evaluation is needed only if fever is present
Answer: A
Rationale: A red, painful, swollen scrotum requires immediate medical evaluation. Within 6 hours manual detorsion may be attempted; beyond that window, or after a failed attempt, prompt surgery is required.
Trap: (D) makes fever the trigger, and fever is precisely what torsion lacks — it belongs to epididymitis instead. Waiting for a fever before seeking care would rule out the emergency by waiting for a sign of the non-emergency.

Topic: amenorrhea and dysmenorrhea

Q: A 15-year-old has never had a menstrual period, although she developed breasts at age 11. Which classification and first step apply?
A) Primary amenorrhea, since there is no uterine bleeding by age 15 with secondary sex characteristics present and no menarche 4 years after thelarche; pregnancy should be ruled out first
B) Secondary amenorrhea, since her cycles have stopped
C) Primary amenorrhea; begin with follicle-stimulating hormone and thyroid studies
D) Normal variation requiring no evaluation until age 18
Answer: A
Rationale: Primary amenorrhea is defined as no secondary sex characteristics by 13, no uterine bleeding with secondary characteristics by 15, or no menarche 4 years after thelarche. "Pregnancy is the most common cause of secondary amenorrhea and should be ruled out in both types of amenorrhea even if the adolescent denies sexual activity."
Trap: (C) identifies the classification correctly and then skips the first step. Pregnancy testing precedes hormonal workup in both types of amenorrhea, and the chapter specifies that this holds even when the adolescent denies sexual activity.

Q: A 17-year-old who previously had regular cycles has had no period for 4 months. Which classification applies?
A) Secondary amenorrhea, defined as absence for more than 3 cycles or irregular menses for 6 months
B) Primary amenorrhea, since she is over 15
C) Secondary amenorrhea, defined as absence for more than 12 months
D) Oligomenorrhea, which is a separate diagnosis
Answer: A
Rationale: Secondary amenorrhea is the absence of menses for more than 3 cycles or irregular menses for 6 months. Primary amenorrhea applies to adolescents who have not yet established menses at all.
Trap: (B) applies the primary criteria to an adolescent who has already menstruated, which is the distinction between the two categories. Each has three defining numbers, and the six of them together are the most swappable set in this section.

Q: A 16-year-old runner has reduced caloric intake, has not menstruated in 5 months, and has a low bone mineral density. Which components and treatment apply? Select all that apply.
A) The female athlete triad comprises reduced caloric intake, amenorrhea, and low bone mineral density
B) A 20% increase in caloric intake can reverse the amenorrhea
C) Weight gain plus resistance training reverses the bone component
D) Oral contraceptives are the first-line treatment for the amenorrhea
E) Pregnancy should still be ruled out as a cause of the amenorrhea
Answer: A, B, C, E
Rationale: The female athlete triad comprises reduced caloric intake, amenorrhea, and low bone mineral density. A 20% increase in caloric intake can reverse the amenorrhea, and weight gain plus resistance training reverses the bone component. Pregnancy is ruled out in both types of amenorrhea regardless of reported sexual activity.
Trap: (D) is the one option from the wrong side of the sort. Oral contraceptives are the fallback rather than the first answer, used only when the athlete will not comply with the caloric increase or remains amenorrheic despite compliance. Treating the symptom with hormones leaves the caloric deficit that caused it.

Q: A 15-year-old has suprapubic cramping that begins with the onset of her menses and lasts about a day. A second 15-year-old has a dull ache radiating to her back and thighs with bloating and pelvic fullness. How should these be classified?
A) The first has primary dysmenorrhea, which is prostaglandin-mediated and requires ovulatory cycles; the second has secondary dysmenorrhea, which reflects underlying pathology
B) Both have primary dysmenorrhea
C) The first has secondary dysmenorrhea and the second primary
D) Neither pattern is dysmenorrhea
Answer: A
Rationale: Primary dysmenorrhea occurs in ovulatory cycles, is mediated by prostaglandin F2-alpha, and produces suprapubic cramping beginning at the onset of menses and lasting 8 to 48 hours. Secondary dysmenorrhea reflects an underlying pathology and produces dull aching that radiates to the back or thighs with bloating or pelvic fullness.
Trap: (C) is the inversion. The discriminating features are the radiation and the bloating — cramping confined to the suprapubic area and timed to the onset of bleeding is primary, while aching that travels and is accompanied by fullness points to pathology.

Q: An adolescent with primary dysmenorrhea asks when to take her ibuprofen. Which instruction is correct?
A) Nonsteroidal anti-inflammatory drugs are most effective if started several days before menses or at least by the onset of bleeding
B) Take the medication once cramping becomes uncomfortable
C) Take the medication only after the first full day of bleeding
D) Begin oral contraceptives instead, since nonsteroidal anti-inflammatory drugs are ineffective
Answer: A
Rationale: Nonsteroidal anti-inflammatory drugs are "most effective if started several days before menses or at least by the onset of bleeding." The escalation is scripted: try a different drug in the class first, and only after a 6-month unsuccessful trial of a second agent do oral contraceptives enter.
Trap: (B) is how most people take an analgesic and it defeats the mechanism. These drugs work by blocking prostaglandin production rather than by relieving established pain, so starting after the cramping begins means the prostaglandins responsible are already circulating.

Topic: abnormal uterine bleeding, premenstrual syndrome, and endometriosis

Q: A 16-year-old has irregular heavy bleeding, and an extensive workup has found no pathologic cause. Which term applies?
A) Dysfunctional uterine bleeding, which is abnormal uterine bleeding without a pathogenic cause and is diagnosed only after ruling out all other causes
B) The two terms are synonymous
C) Abnormal uterine bleeding, which by definition excludes an identified cause
D) Primary dysmenorrhea
Answer: A
Rationale: Dysfunctional uterine bleeding is abnormal uterine bleeding without a pathogenic cause. Abnormal uterine bleeding is "most commonly caused by anovulation," and the dysfunctional designation "is made only after ruling out all other causes."
Trap: (B) treats the two as interchangeable, which is the commonest error with this pair. Abnormal uterine bleeding is the umbrella finding; the dysfunctional label is a diagnosis of exclusion reached only after the workup in this stem has come back negative.

Q: A nurse reviews Box 18.1's iatrogenic causes of abnormal uterine bleeding. Which agents does the chapter list? Select all that apply.
A) Anticoagulants
B) Exogenous hormone use, including oral contraceptives and menopausal hormone therapy
C) Selective serotonin reuptake inhibitors, and tamoxifen
D) Intrauterine devices, and herbal preparations such as ginseng
E) Pregnancy-related conditions such as ectopic pregnancy
Answer: A, B, C, D
Rationale: The iatrogenic causes listed are "medications with estrogenic activity, anticoagulants, exogenous hormone use (oral contraceptives, menopausal hormone therapy), selective serotonin reuptake inhibitors, tamoxifen, intrauterine devices, herbal preparation (ginseng)."
Trap: (E) is the one option from the wrong side of the sort. Pregnancy-related conditions are a real cause of abnormal uterine bleeding and are one of the box's eight categories, but they are not iatrogenic — the distinction this item turns on is whether the cause was introduced by treatment.

Q: A 17-year-old reports irritability, bloating, and breast tenderness that begin after ovulation, worsen in the week before her period, and are gone by the time her period ends. Which condition does this describe?
A) Premenstrual syndrome, which begins at ovulation, recurs in the luteal phase, and disappears by the end of menstruation
B) Premenstrual dysphoric disorder, which is diagnosed on this symptom pattern alone
C) Generalized anxiety disorder, since the symptoms include irritability
D) Symptoms present throughout the month, which is characteristic of premenstrual syndrome
Answer: A
Rationale: Premenstrual syndrome starts at ovulation, recurs in the luteal phase, and disappears by the end of menstruation. Premenstrual dysphoric disorder is the severe variant, affects 2% to 6%, and is a distinct DSM-5 diagnosis. Selective serotonin reuptake inhibitors are first-line pharmacologic therapy for both.
Trap: (D) states the opposite of the defining feature and is worth recognizing as a distractor pattern: symptoms that persist all month are not premenstrual syndrome, because the cyclic resolution is what makes the diagnosis.

Q: A 15-year-old with endometriosis has pelvic pain and is not seeking pregnancy. Which management reflects the chapter?
A) For adolescents under 16, continuous combined hormone therapy plus nonsteroidal anti-inflammatory drugs; gonadotropin-releasing hormone agonists are reserved for severe symptoms because of bone mineralization effects and are limited to 6 months
B) A gonadotropin-releasing hormone agonist as first-line therapy at any age
C) No treatment, since she does not wish to become pregnant
D) Immediate surgical management
Answer: A
Rationale: For adolescents under 16 the treatment is continuous combined hormone therapy plus nonsteroidal anti-inflammatory drugs. Gonadotropin-releasing hormone agonists are reserved for severe symptoms because of bone mineralization effects and are limited to 6 months.
Trap: (C) applies a real rule to the wrong patient. "Women without pain who do not want to become pregnant need no treatment" — but this adolescent has pain, which is the condition that rule excludes.

Topic: vaginal infections

Q: A 17-year-old has a thin, white-gray discharge with a fishy odor. Microscopy shows clue cells, the pH is 5.0, and the whiff test is positive. Which infection is this, and does her partner need treatment?
A) Bacterial vaginosis; treatment of sexual partners is not recommended routinely
B) Trichomoniasis; the partner must be treated to prevent recurrence
C) Candidiasis; the partner does not require treatment
D) Bacterial vaginosis; the partner must be treated to prevent recurrence
Answer: A
Rationale: Bacterial vaginosis produces a thin, white, gray, or milky discharge with a fishy odor, clue cells, a pH of 4.5 or above, and a positive whiff test. For bacterial vaginosis, "treatment of sexual partners is not recommended routinely."
Trap: (D) has the right organism and the wrong partner rule, which is the half of this item that changes what the nurse does. Partner treatment is required for trichomoniasis, where the organisms "harbor in the urethra or prostate," and not routinely for bacterial vaginosis.

Q: A 16-year-old has a yellow-green frothy malodorous discharge, a vaginal pH above 5.0, and strawberry spots on the cervix. Which infection is this?
A) Bacterial vaginosis
B) Trichomoniasis, diagnosed by protozoa on a saline wet mount, with partner treatment required
C) Candidiasis, diagnosed by pseudohyphae on potassium hydroxide preparation
D) Chlamydia
Answer: B
Rationale: Trichomoniasis produces a yellow-green, frothy, malodorous discharge with strawberry spots and a pH above 5.0, with protozoa visible on a saline wet mount. Partner treatment is required because trichomonads "harbor in the urethra or prostate."
Trap: (C) names the third member of this trio, and the three discharges are the most swappable set in the chapter. Candidiasis is thick, white, and cottage cheese–like with pruritus, pseudohyphae on potassium hydroxide, a normal pH below 4.5, and a negative amine test — nearly the opposite profile on every axis.

Q: An adolescent is prescribed metronidazole for trichomoniasis. Which instruction is essential?
A) Avoid alcoholic beverages, because the combination causes abdominal distress, nausea, vomiting, and headache
B) Take the medication with alcohol to improve absorption
C) Discontinue the medication if menstruation begins
D) Alcohol restriction applies only to the treatment of bacterial vaginosis
Answer: A
Rationale: Patients taking metronidazole should "avoid drinking alcoholic beverages," because the reaction produces "abdominal distress, nausea, vomiting, and headache." This applies to bacterial vaginosis and trichomoniasis treatment alike.
Trap: (D) restricts the caution to one indication when the same drug carries it in both. Because the two conditions are treated with the same agent, a learner who files the warning under one diagnosis will miss it under the other.

Q: An adolescent being treated for a vaginal yeast infection asks whether she can use tampons during her period while finishing the medication. Which response is correct?
A) Do not use tampons, because the medication will be absorbed by the tampon, and complete the full course even during menstruation
B) Tampons are acceptable and do not affect the medication
C) Stop the medication during menstruation and resume afterward
D) Switch to an oral agent whenever menstruation begins
Answer: A
Rationale: Tampons should not be used during treatment because "the medication will be absorbed by the tampon," and the course is completed even during menstruation. A first suspected infection warrants seeing a provider; subsequent episodes may be self-treated with over-the-counter preparations.
Trap: (C) interrupts a course that the chapter says to complete, and incomplete treatment is a common source of apparent treatment failure. The two instructions belong together: keep taking it, and change the delivery method for menstrual flow.

Topic: chlamydia, gonorrhea, and pelvic inflammatory disease

Q: A sexually active 17-year-old is asymptomatic and asks why she should be screened for chlamydia. Which response is correct?
A) Chlamydia is often silent and highly destructive, and its worst outcome is pelvic inflammatory disease leading to infertility, ectopic pregnancy, and chronic pelvic pain
B) Chlamydia reliably produces a noticeable discharge in women
C) Screening is unnecessary in the absence of symptoms
D) Chlamydia produces symptoms in women but not in men
Answer: A
Rationale: Chlamydia is "often silent and highly destructive," and its worst outcome is pelvic inflammatory disease leading to infertility, ectopic pregnancy, and chronic pelvic pain. Gonorrhea shows the opposite pattern between the sexes: women are often asymptomatic, with a greenish-yellow purulent discharge when symptomatic, while men usually have symptoms.
Trap: (D) inverts the sex pattern that applies to gonorrhea and misapplies it to chlamydia. The reason asymptomatic screening exists is that the damage accrues silently, so waiting for symptoms means waiting until the tubal injury has occurred.

Q: A 16-year-old tests positive for gonorrhea. Which treatment principle applies?
A) Provide concomitant treatment for chlamydia, because coinfection is common
B) Treat gonorrhea alone and test for chlamydia in 3 months
C) Treat chlamydia only if the patient is symptomatic
D) Defer all treatment until partner testing is complete
Answer: A
Rationale: The CDC "recommends concomitant treatment for chlamydia because coinfection is common." For chlamydia, azithromycin is preferred "when compliance is a problem because only one dose is needed."
Trap: (B) defers treatment of an infection that is likely already present and silent, and the deferral period is long enough for tubal damage to occur. The point of concomitant treatment is that the coinfection rate makes empiric coverage more reliable than sequential testing in this population.

Q: A nurse counsels adolescents about partner notification after sexually transmitted infection diagnoses. Which statement is correct?
A) The look-back period is 60 days for gonorrhea and 90 days for syphilis; after chlamydia treatment the patient abstains for 7 days and is rescreened at 3 to 6 months
B) The look-back period is 90 days for gonorrhea and 60 days for syphilis
C) A single 30-day look-back applies to all sexually transmitted infections
D) No abstinence period is needed after chlamydia treatment
Answer: A
Rationale: The partner-notification window is 60 days for gonorrhea and 90 days for syphilis. After chlamydia treatment, the patient abstains for 7 days and is rescreened at 3 to 6 months.
Trap: (B) swaps the two windows, which is the commonest error because the numbers are adjacent and the infections are discussed together. These are three different intervals attached to three different infections, and distractors swap them freely.

Q: A 17-year-old is being evaluated for pelvic inflammatory disease. Which findings constitute the minimum diagnostic criteria? Select all that apply.
A) Lower abdominal tenderness
B) Adnexal tenderness
C) Uterine tenderness
D) Cervical motion tenderness
E) Oral temperature of 38.3°C or above
Answer: A, B, C, D
Rationale: The minimum criteria are "Lower abdominal tenderness, in combination with adnexal tenderness, uterine tenderness, or cervical motion tenderness."
Trap: (E) is the one option from the wrong side of the sort. Fever is a *supporting* criterion rather than a minimum one, and it carries the section's number — an oral temperature of 38.3°C or above. Requiring fever before considering the diagnosis would exclude the afebrile presentations that make up much of the caseload.

Q: A 16-year-old develops lower abdominal pain a few days after her period ended and is diagnosed with pelvic inflammatory disease. Which statements reflect the chapter?
A) Ascending infection is facilitated just after menses because the cervical os is slightly open, the cervical mucus barrier is absent, and menstrual blood is an excellent growth medium; acute care includes bed rest in semi-Fowler position and few pelvic examinations during the acute phase
B) Onset just after menses argues against the diagnosis
C) Frequent pelvic examinations during the acute phase are needed to monitor progress
D) Ambulation is encouraged during the acute phase to prevent adhesions
Answer: A
Rationale: Three factors facilitate ascent just after menses: "the cervical os is slightly open, the cervical mucus barrier is absent, and menstrual blood is an excellent medium for growth." Acute management includes bed rest in a semi-Fowler position, few pelvic examinations during the acute phase, and follow-up endocervical cultures as a test of cure.
Trap: (B) treats the timing as evidence against the diagnosis when it is a diagnostic clue in favor of it. (C) increases exactly the examinations the chapter says to limit while the infection is acute.

Topic: syphilis, herpes, and human papillomavirus

Q: A 17-year-old has a single nontender shallow genital ulcer that appeared about 2 weeks after a sexual encounter. Two months later he develops a rash that includes his palms and soles. Which infection does this describe?
A) Syphilis, with a painless chancre at 14 to 21 days and a secondary rash at 6 weeks to a few months that includes the palms and soles
B) Herpes simplex virus, with a primary outbreak followed by recurrence
C) Human papillomavirus, with condylomata acuminata
D) Chancroid
Answer: A
Rationale: The syphilitic chancre appears at 14 to 21 days as a "nontender, shallow ulcer." Secondary syphilis appears at 6 weeks to a few months with a rash "including the palms and soles," which is the classic identifier, along with condylomata lata — "broad, painless, pink-gray, wartlike" lesions.
Trap: (C) names a lesion with a confusingly similar name: condylomata *acuminata* are the warts of human papillomavirus, while condylomata *lata* belong to secondary syphilis. (B) would be painful and vesicular rather than a single painless ulcer.

Q: A patient with syphilis reports a documented penicillin allergy. Which management is correct?
A) Individuals with a penicillin allergy should be desensitized rather than offered treatment with an alternative medication
B) Substitute doxycycline as the standard alternative
C) Withhold treatment until allergy testing confirms the history
D) Treat with azithromycin, which is equivalent
Answer: A
Rationale: "Individuals with a penicillin allergy should be desensitized rather than offer treatment with an alternative medication."
Trap: (B) applies the general principle of substituting an alternative agent for an allergy, which is standard practice almost everywhere else and is specifically not the approach here. That is exactly what makes this a distractor magnet — the correct answer contradicts a habit that is otherwise reliable.

Q: Within 12 hours of receiving treatment for syphilis, a pregnant 18-year-old develops fever, headache, myalgias, and arthralgias. How should the nurse interpret and manage this?
A) This is the Jarisch-Herxheimer reaction, an expected acute febrile reaction within the first 24 hours of treatment, managed symptomatically; in the second half of pregnancy it risks preterm labor, so she should report decreased fetal movement or contractions
B) This is an allergic reaction to penicillin and treatment should be stopped
C) This indicates treatment failure requiring a different agent
D) This is unrelated to the treatment and requires infectious workup
Answer: A
Rationale: The Jarisch-Herxheimer reaction is an acute febrile reaction with headache, myalgias, and arthralgias occurring "within the first 24 hours of treatment," treated symptomatically. In the second half of pregnancy it risks preterm labor, so the patient reports decreased fetal movement or contractions.
Trap: (B) is the reflex reading of fever and malaise after a drug dose, and stopping treatment for an expected reaction leaves the syphilis untreated. The timing and the symptom cluster are what identify it as expected rather than allergic.

Q: A 19-year-old has a first herpes outbreak with lesions plus fever, chills, malaise, headache, and lymphadenopathy lasting about 2 weeks. A second patient has local tingling followed by lesions lasting 9 days without systemic symptoms. How do these differ?
A) The first is a primary infection, which lasts 2 to 3 weeks with systemic symptoms; the second is a recurrence, which is local only with prodromal tingling and lasts 8 to 10 days, with systemic symptoms usually absent
B) The first is a recurrence and the second a primary infection
C) Both are primary infections
D) Systemic symptoms indicate bacterial superinfection rather than primary herpes
Answer: A
Rationale: Primary herpes produces lesions plus fever, chills, malaise, lymphadenopathy, and headache, lasting 2 to 3 weeks. Recurrent herpes is local only, with prodromal tingling, lasting 8 to 10 days, with systemic symptoms usually absent.
Trap: (B) is the inversion. The prodromal tingling is a recurrence feature rather than a primary one, so a stem that mentions prodrome is pointing away from a first episode even though prodrome sounds like something that precedes an initial illness.

Topic: adolescent pregnancy and contraception

Q: A 16-year-old presents with amenorrhea and nausea, and the nurse suspects pregnancy. Which approach is correct?
A) Do not test for pregnancy without the adolescent's knowledge, and before testing discuss what she will do if the test is positive
B) Order the test with routine laboratory work without raising it, to avoid causing anxiety
C) Notify the parents before testing so they can be present for the result
D) Test first and discuss the implications only if the result is positive
Answer: A
Rationale: Pregnancy testing is not done without the adolescent's knowledge, and before testing the nurse should "discuss what she will do if the test is positive." After a positive result, inform her privately, normalize her reaction, and schedule follow-up in 24 to 48 hours to assist with parental notification.
Trap: (D) postpones a conversation that shapes how the result lands, and the chapter places it deliberately before the test rather than after. (C) removes the confidentiality that brought the adolescent in, and the chapter's guidance is to assist her with notifying her parents rather than to do it for her.

Q: A pregnant 16-year-old with a body mass index of 22 asks how much weight she should gain and what supplement she needs. Which response is correct?
A) A body mass index of 18.5 to 24.9 calls for a gain of 25 to 35 pounds, and folic acid 0.4 mg, or 400 micrograms, daily — ideally initiated before pregnancy for maximum benefit
B) Weight gain targets for pregnant adolescents are set by age rather than body mass index
C) A body mass index of 18.5 to 24.9 calls for a gain of 15 to 25 pounds
D) Folic acid is beneficial only when started in the second trimester
Answer: A
Rationale: A body mass index of 18.5 to 24.9 calls for a gain of 25 to 35 pounds; a body mass index of 25.0 to 29.9 calls for 15 to 25 pounds. Folic acid 0.4 mg (400 micrograms) daily is recommended, "initiated before pregnancy" for maximum benefit.
Trap: (C) applies the overweight range to a normal-weight adolescent, which is the swap built into having two adjacent ranges. (D) misses the point of folic acid timing entirely — neural tube closure occurs early enough that starting in the second trimester forgoes the benefit.

Q: A nurse counsels adolescents on contraceptive methods. Which statements reflect Table 18.3? Select all that apply.
A) Hormonal methods provide no protection against sexually transmitted infections
B) Chemical methods and barrier methods other than condoms provide no protection against sexually transmitted infections
C) The contraceptive patch is not recommended for those over 90 kg, about 198 pounds
D) Depot medroxyprogesterone should be discontinued after 2 years because of bone density effects
E) Intrauterine devices provide reliable protection against sexually transmitted infections
Answer: A, B, C, D
Rationale: Table 18.3 repeats "No STI protection" for every hormonal method, every barrier method other than condoms, and every chemical method. The patch is not recommended over 90 kg (198 lb), and depot medroxyprogesterone should be discontinued after 2 years because of bone density effects.
Trap: (E) is the one option from the wrong side of the sort. Abstinence and condoms are the only entries that carry infection protection, and every other method — including the most effective contraceptives — leaves that risk entirely unaddressed.

Q: A 17-year-old requests emergency contraception 2 days after unprotected intercourse. Which information is correct?
A) Levonorgestrel is preferred for adolescents because of its low side effect profile and increased effectiveness, is available without prescription at 17 years and older, and is effective within 120 hours; ulipristal may be more effective over 165 pounds
B) Emergency contraception is effective only within 24 hours
C) The copper intrauterine device may be placed within 30 days
D) Levonorgestrel requires a prescription at every age
Answer: A
Rationale: Levonorgestrel is best for adolescents "because of its low side effect profile and increased effectiveness," is available without prescription at 17 years and older, and is effective within 120 hours. Ulipristal may be more effective over 165 pounds, and the copper intrauterine device window is 7 days.
Trap: (B) collapses a 5-day window to a single day, which would turn an available option into an unavailable one for the adolescent in this stem. (C) stretches the copper intrauterine device window from 7 days to 30.

Topic: sexual assault

Q: A 15-year-old discloses consensual sexual contact with a 20-year-old partner. What is the correct classification and action?
A) This is statutory rape — consensual contact by someone 18 or older with a person under the age of consent — and all statutory rape requires reporting to child protective services or local law enforcement
B) No report is required, since the contact was consensual
C) This is acquaintance rape, and reporting is at the adolescent's discretion
D) Reporting is required only if the adolescent requests it
Answer: A
Rationale: Statutory rape is consensual contact by someone 18 years or older with a person under the age of consent. All statutory rape "require[s] reporting to child protective services or local law enforcement." Rape is forced contact, and acquaintance or date rape occurs between parties who know each other.
Trap: (B) treats consent as decisive, and consent is built into the definition of statutory rape rather than excluding it. That is what makes this category counterintuitive: the act is consensual by definition and mandatory to report anyway.

Q: An adolescent arrives at the emergency department shortly after a sexual assault and asks to shower before being examined. What should the nurse do?
A) Explain that she should not bathe or shower because physical evidence deteriorates rapidly, and ensure that nongenital areas are examined as well
B) Allow her to shower, since her comfort takes priority over evidence
C) Allow a brief shower provided she does not wash the genital area
D) Defer the examination entirely until she feels ready, however long that takes
Answer: A
Rationale: The victim should not bathe or shower because "Physical evidence deteriorates rapidly," and nongenital areas are examined as well. The first two messages the nurse conveys are that the victim is all right and that she is not being blamed.
Trap: (B) honors an understandable request at the cost of evidence that cannot be recovered afterward. The nurse's task is to explain why rather than simply to refuse — which is why the two reassurance messages come first.

Q: An 18-year-old discloses a sexual assault that occurred 3 weeks ago and apologizes for not coming sooner. What is the best response?
A) Recognize that it is common for rape victims to delay seeking help, especially in cases of acquaintance or date rape, and focus on the fact that the victim is seeking assistance now
B) Explain that the delay has compromised the case and limits what can be done
C) Ask why she waited, to establish the timeline for documentation
D) Treat the delay as a reason to question the account
Answer: A
Rationale: "It is common for rape victims to delay seeking help, especially in cases of acquaintance or date rape." The supportive response focuses "on the fact that the victim is seeking assistance now."
Trap: (D) reads an expected behavior as a credibility problem, which is precisely the response that keeps victims from presenting at all. (C) asks a question whose framing implies fault, at a point where the nurse's first messages are meant to convey that the victim is not being blamed.

Topic: eating disorders

Q: A 16-year-old is introverted, described by her parents as a "model child" and high achiever, avoids intimacy, weighs 82% of expected body weight, and denies that anything is wrong. Which disorder does this profile fit?
A) Anorexia nervosa, characterized by rigid control, denial of illness, weight below 85% of expected, and typically not being sexually active
B) Bulimia nervosa, characterized by impulsivity and acting out
C) Binge-eating disorder
D) Avoidant restrictive food intake disorder
Answer: A
Rationale: Table 18.4 contrasts the two profiles. Anorexia nervosa: avoids intimacy, introverted, a "model" child, high achiever, rigid control, denies illness, below 85% of expected weight, usually not sexually active. Bulimia nervosa: seeks intimacy, extroverted, acts out, impulsive, variable school performance, loses control, recognizes illness, within 5 to 15 pounds of normal or overweight, often sexually active.
Trap: (B) is the paired disorder, and the profiles differ on nearly every listed axis. The shorthand worth holding is that anorexia turns away from food and bulimia turns toward it — and the same orientation runs through the personality features, control, and illness recognition.

Q: A severely malnourished 15-year-old with anorexia nervosa is admitted and refeeding is begun. What is the greatest danger and its mechanism?
A) Refeeding syndrome, with the risk of heart failure greatest in the first 2 weeks, caused by phosphate shifting from extracellular to intracellular; prevention is slower refeeding, replacing phosphorus, and avoiding high sodium
B) Refeeding syndrome, caused by excess potassium intake, greatest in the second month
C) Immediate aspiration, which is the principal risk of refeeding
D) Hyperglycemia, which is the principal danger of refeeding
Answer: A
Rationale: The risk of heart failure is greatest in the first 2 weeks of refeeding, with a risk window of 2 to 3 weeks for patients about 10% below ideal body weight. The cause is phosphate shifting from extracellular to intracellular. Prevention is slower refeeding, replacing phosphorus, and avoiding high sodium, with monitoring of electrolytes, edema, and signs of congestive heart failure.
Trap: (B) has the right syndrome with the wrong electrolyte and the wrong window. Phosphate is the mechanism, and the danger is front-loaded into the first 2 weeks — which is counterintuitive, because that is the period in which the patient appears to be improving.

Q: A treatment team sets a weight-gain goal for an adolescent with anorexia nervosa. Which rate is appropriate?
A) About 1 kilogram, or 2 pounds, per week; pushing for more rapid gain can increase anxiety or depression and result in bulimia or more severe relapses
B) 3 to 4 kilograms per week, to restore weight as quickly as possible
C) Weight gain should not be a treatment goal
D) Any rate is acceptable provided refeeding syndrome is prevented
Answer: A
Rationale: The target is about 1 kilogram, 2 pounds, per week. "Pushing for more rapid weight gain can result in increasing anxiety or depression and result in bulimia or more severe relapses." Inpatients are supervised "during the meal and for 1 to 3 hours after."
Trap: (D) treats refeeding syndrome as the only constraint on pace, and the chapter names a second one that is psychiatric rather than metabolic. Gaining too fast can convert one eating disorder into another, which no electrolyte monitoring would detect.

Q: A nurse screens an adolescent with the SCOFF questionnaire. Which items does it include, and what score is significant? Select all that apply.
A) Do you make yourself Sick because you feel uncomfortably full
B) Do you worry you have lost Control over how much you eat
C) Have you recently lost more than One stone, 14 pounds or 6.4 kilograms, in a 3-month period
D) Would you say that Food dominates your life, and do you believe yourself to be Fat when others say you are too thin
E) Have you ever been told by a physician that you have an eating disorder
Answer: A, B, C, D
Rationale: SCOFF asks about being Sick, losing Control, losing One stone — 14 pounds or 6.4 kilograms — in 3 months, believing oneself Fat, and Food dominating life. A score of 2 or more indicates a likely case.
Trap: (E) is the one option from the wrong side of the sort, and a screening tool that required a prior diagnosis would identify nobody new. The threshold is worth holding alongside the items: two affirmative answers is the cut-point, not three or four.

Q: A 17-year-old with suspected bulimia nervosa has a normal physical examination, a low erythrocyte sedimentation rate, and scarred, cut knuckles. How should the nurse interpret these findings?
A) The medical history is most important for diagnosing eating disorders because the physical examination may be normal, especially early; a low sedimentation rate is consistent with an eating disorder, and scarred knuckles are the Russell sign from the maxillary incisors during self-induced vomiting
B) A normal physical examination rules out an eating disorder
C) An elevated sedimentation rate would be expected in an eating disorder
D) The Russell sign refers to enamel erosion of the anterior teeth
Answer: A
Rationale: "The medical history is most important for diagnosing eating disorders because the physical examination may be normal, especially early in the illness." Sedimentation rate and C-reactive protein "should be low in an eating disorder." The Russell sign is scarred, cut knuckles from contact with the maxillary incisors during self-induced vomiting.
Trap: (D) moves the sign from the hands to the mouth. Enamel erosion of the anterior teeth and parotid or submandibular swelling are real findings in bulimia nervosa, but they are separate from the Russell sign, which is on the knuckles. (C) inverts an inflammatory marker that runs opposite to intuition here.

Topic: substance abuse, self-harm, and adolescent suicide

Q: A nurse is planning substance-use education for a middle school. Which statement reflects the chapter?
A) Inhalants are the only substances with a higher incidence of use among young adolescents, because they are readily available, and dusters contain Freon, which can cause fatal cardiac dysrhythmias along with rapid loss of consciousness and respiratory arrest
B) Inhalant use rises steadily through adolescence and peaks in the late teens
C) Inhalants are relatively harmless because they are household products
D) Inhalant exposure produces only mild transient symptoms
Answer: A
Rationale: "Inhalants are the only substances that have a higher incidence of use among young adolescents," because of their availability. "Dusters contain Freon, a substance that can cause fatal cardiac dysrhythmias," alongside "rapid loss of consciousness and respiratory arrest."
Trap: (B) applies the general pattern of adolescent substance use, which does rise with age for most drugs, to the one category that runs the other way. That inversion is why inhalant education belongs in middle school rather than high school.

Q: An adolescent who used cocaine sleeps for an extended period and then reports lack of energy, irritability, appetite changes, and irregular sleep. How should the nurse interpret this?
A) The long sleep after a high is the crash and should not be confused with physical withdrawal; withdrawal itself presents as lack of energy and motivation, irritability, appetite changes, psychomotor delay, and irregular sleep
B) The long sleep is the defining feature of physical withdrawal
C) Stimulants produce strong physical dependence with a dramatic withdrawal syndrome
D) These symptoms indicate a primary depressive disorder unrelated to substance use
Answer: A
Rationale: Cocaine withdrawal presents as "lack of energy and motivation, irritability, appetite changes, psychomotor delay, irregular sleep." The crash — a long period of sleep after a high — "should not be confused" with physical withdrawal. Stimulants generally produce strong psychologic but not strong physical dependence.
Trap: (C) expects the dramatic physical withdrawal associated with other drug classes, and stimulants do not produce it. The withdrawal here looks like depression rather than like a physiologic crisis, which is why it is easy to misattribute.

Q: A 16-year-old is found to be cutting her forearms without any intent to die. Which statement reflects the chapter's definition?
A) Self-harm is self-injury without the intent to die; the definition excludes indirect self-injury such as eating disorders and drug abuse, and socially accepted body modification such as tattoos, and it peaks at 15 to 16 and declines by 18
B) Self-harm and suicide attempt are the same phenomenon
C) Eating disorders and drug abuse are classified as self-harm
D) Self-harm typically begins after age 18
Answer: A
Rationale: Self-harm is self-injury "without the intent to die," and the definition explicitly excludes indirect self-injury such as eating disorders and drug abuse, and socially accepted body modification. It peaks at 15 to 16 and declines by 18.
Trap: (B) collapses two distinct behaviors, and the distinction is the intent. Treating every episode of self-harm as a suicide attempt misreads what the behavior is doing for the adolescent — though it remains a reason for assessment rather than reassurance.

Q: A nurse is identifying the strongest risk factor for adolescent suicide. Which is it?
A) The presence of an active psychiatric disorder
B) A recent romantic breakup
C) A family history of suicide
D) Academic failure
Answer: A
Rationale: The single most important risk factor is "the presence of an active psychiatric disorder." Alcohol is associated with more than 50% of suicides, and a previous attempt is present in "as many as half" of completed adolescent suicides.
Trap: (B) names the precipitant that families and media most often identify, and precipitants are not the same as risk factors. A breakup may be the proximate trigger, but the underlying psychiatric disorder is what converts a painful event into a lethal one.

Q: A 17-year-old who has been severely depressed for months appears suddenly cheerful and calm, and tells the nurse in confidence that he has a plan to end his life. Which two actions are correct?
A) Recognize that sudden improvement after deep depression may mean the adolescent has decided and found the means; confidentiality cannot be honored for self-destructive behavior, so the suicidal behavior is reported to the family and other professionals, and the adolescent is told that this will be done
B) Accept the improvement as genuine recovery and honor the confidentiality he requested
C) Report the disclosure without informing him, to preserve the therapeutic relationship
D) Honor confidentiality unless he attempts suicide
Answer: A
Rationale: "It is a mistake to be lulled into a false sense of security when an adolescent's depression is apparently relieved. The improvement in attitude may mean that the adolescent has made the decision and found the means to carry out the threat." And: "In the case of self-destructive behaviors, confidentiality cannot be honored. Suicidal behavior is reported to the family and other professionals, and adolescents are informed that this will be done."
Trap: (C) gets the reporting right and omits the second half — the adolescent is told the report will be made, which is what keeps the earlier confidentiality discussion honest. (B) misses both halves, and this confidentiality exception reverses the rule taught throughout Chapter 17, which is precisely what makes it a reliable trap.
