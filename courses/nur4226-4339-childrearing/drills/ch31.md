Source: `sources/Ch31_Fact_Bank.md` (Hockenberry, Wilson, & Rodgers, *Wong's Nursing Care of
Infants and Children*, 11th ed., ch. 31, printed pp. 1170–1208).

**Blueprint weight: 6 questions of 121, depth `standard`.** That buys **43 questions across 10
topics**, matching the six-item chapters already banked for this course.

**Where the weight went.** The chapter is 39 printed pages and roughly 15 of them — pp. 1192–1206
— are diabetes mellitus. The allocation follows that shape rather than giving each gland an equal
share: **11 of the 43 questions are diabetes**, split into insulin therapy and monitoring on one
side and the acute glucose emergencies on the other. Diabetes insipidus and SIADH take 5, because

chapter. The adrenal cortex takes 8 across two topics. The rarer glands — parathyroid,
pheochromocytoma — take 3 each, which is enough for their discriminators and not enough for their
detail.

mirror: DI against SIADH, central DI against nephrogenic DI, Hashimoto against Graves, hypo-
against hyperparathyroidism, Cushing against Addison, gigantism against acromegaly, CAH against

against each other on purpose: **neck flexed after thyroidectomy, neck hyperextended in a newborn
with a goiter.** So is the sequence trap inside insulin mixing, where the air goes into the vials
in the reverse order from the insulin.

**What the weight could not buy.** Left undrilled, deliberately: the opening physiology survey

Topic: growth failure evaluation and growth hormone therapy
Subtopic: distinguishing the causes of short stature

Q: A 7-year-old boy is referred for short stature. He grew normally through his first year and has since followed a curve below the 3rd percentile. He is chubby, his body proportions are normal for his age, and he looks younger than 7. Intelligence testing is normal. Which feature most helps the nurse distinguish growth hormone deficiency from failure to thrive?
A) His well-nourished, even chubby, appearance
B) His height below the 3rd percentile
C) His normal intelligence testing
D) His normal skeletal proportions
Answer: A
Rationale: "Height may be stunted more than weight because, with good nutrition, these children can become overweight or even obese," and the chapter names the consequence outright: "Their well-nourished appearance is an important diagnostic clue to differentiation from other disorders such as failure to thrive" (p. 1173).
Trap: (B) is the reflex pick because it is the finding that brought the child in, but a height below the 3rd percentile is where every short-stature workup starts and separates nothing; (D) is equally true of a malnourished child, since "Skeletal proportions are normal for the age" in GH deficiency but proportion is not what undernutrition disturbs first.

Q: Two 9-year-old girls are evaluated for short stature. One has a markedly delayed bone age; the other's bone age matches her chronologic age. How should the nurse interpret the two films?
A) The girl whose bone age is not delayed has the more concerning finding
B) The girl with the profoundly delayed bone age has a confirmed endocrine diagnosis
C) Neither film adds anything, because bone age is not part of a growth workup
D) The delayed bone age means that girl's short stature is now irreversible
Answer: A
Rationale: "a bone age that is not delayed in a short child is of much greater concern and may, in fact, be of some diagnostic value under certain circumstances" (p. 1175). Most conditions that slow linear growth also retard skeletal maturation, so a delayed film is the expected and nonspecific result.
Trap: (B) is the intuitive inversion, because an abnormal test feels more diagnostic than a normal one, and Box 31.5 forecloses it: "Observation of even a profoundly delayed bone age is never diagnostic or even indicative of a specific diagnosis" (p. 1175). (D) reverses the meaning entirely, since delay indicates the short stature is to some extent still recoverable "because linear growth will continue until epiphyseal fusion is complete."

Subtopic: growth hormone replacement

Q: A 10-year-old is beginning recombinant growth hormone. Which combination of instruction and expectation should the nurse give the family?
A) Give the injection at bedtime, and expect the fastest growth in the first year with the child still remaining shorter than peers
B) Give the injection at bedtime, and expect the child to reach an average adult height for the family
C) Give the injection with breakfast, and expect growth to accelerate further with each year of treatment
D) Give the injection at bedtime, and expect therapy to continue to age 18 regardless of bone age
Answer: A
Rationale: The Drug Alert states that "GH is most effective when it is administered at bedtime," because physiologic release normally follows "during the first 45 to 90 minutes after the onset of sleep" (p. 1176). "Maximum growth velocity occurs in the first year of treatment," after which "growth rates decline" (pp. 1175–1176), and the honest ceiling is that "individuals who receive treatment remain shorter than their peers" (p. 1176).
Trap: (B) gets the timing right and the promise wrong, which is the harder error to catch, because a family hearing the drug's name expects a normal adult height and the nurse who confirms it has set up the disappointment the chapter warns about; (C) inverts the growth-velocity curve, which is steepest at the start.

Q: An adolescent girl has been on growth hormone for 5 years. Her growth rate is now under 1 inch per year and her bone age is 14.5 years. Her 15-year-old brother, also on growth hormone, has a bone age of 14 years. Which statement is correct?
A) The girl meets a criterion to stop therapy that her brother does not yet meet
B) Both of them now meet the criteria to stop therapy
C) Neither meets the criteria, because bone age is not used in this decision
D) The brother meets the criterion, since boys finish growing earlier than girls
Answer: A
Rationale: "Growth rates of less than 1 inch per year and a bone age of more than 14 years in girls and more than 16 years in boys are often used as criteria to stop GH therapy" (p. 1176). Her bone age is past 14; his is well short of 16. The decision itself is shared: "The child, family, and health care team make the decision jointly to stop GH therapy."
Trap: (D) is the reflex error because girls are taught to mature earlier, so a reader who remembers the direction without the numbers attaches the earlier stopping point to the wrong child; (B) applies one threshold to both sexes, which is the same swap in a different shape.

Topic: pituitary hyperfunction and precocious puberty
Subtopic: growth hormone excess

Q: Two patients have growth hormone-secreting pituitary adenomas: an 8-year-old and a 30-year-old. The child is growing rapidly in height with proportional weight gain and an enlarging head circumference. The adult has a broadening jaw, enlarged hands, and separating teeth, with no change in height. What accounts for the difference?
A) The epiphyses are still open in the child and have closed in the adult
B) The child's tumor secretes a larger quantity of growth hormone
C) The adult's tumor is also secreting thyroid-stimulating hormone
D) The adult has had the tumor for a longer period
Answer: A
Rationale: "Excess GH before closure of the epiphyseal shafts results in proportional overgrowth of the long bones," while "If oversecretion of GH occurs after epiphyseal closure, growth is in the transverse direction, producing a condition known as acromegaly" (p. 1176). Gigantism also produces "Proportional enlargement of head circumference."
Trap: (D) pulls hardest because acromegaly genuinely is diagnosed late — patients are identified "as much as 10 years after their symptoms first appear" (p. 1177) — so the duration in the option is a true fact recruited to explain the wrong thing. The single variable is whether the growth plates are open.

Q: An adolescent diagnosed with acromegaly after epiphyseal closure asks whether treatment will bring his height back down. Which response is accurate?
A) Treatment will not reduce your height, but it will prevent further excess growth
B) Treatment will gradually return your height toward your predicted adult height
C) Your height can be reduced, but only with transsphenoidal resection of the tumor
D) Height is never affected in acromegaly, so there is nothing to reduce
Answer: A
Rationale: "Although medical treatment of acromegaly will not reduce a patient's height, it will prevent further excess growth" (p. 1177).
Trap: (C) is the plausible-but-adjacent version, and it is the most convincing wrong answer because it names the right operation: "Transsphenoid surgery (TSS) is the most common surgical treatment for pituitary adenoma" (p. 1177). Naming a real procedure makes a false promise sound informed.

Subtopic: precocious puberty

Q: A 7-year-old girl and a 7-year-old boy are each being evaluated for central precocious puberty. Which statement should guide the nurse's expectations for the two workups?
A) The boy is far more likely to have a CNS lesion, but both children still require a brain MRI
B) The girl is far more likely to have a CNS lesion, so only she requires a brain MRI
C) Neither child needs imaging if the rest of the physical examination is normal
D) The boy is far more likely to have a CNS lesion, so imaging is reserved for boys
Answer: A
Rationale: "CPP occurs more frequently in girls and is usually idiopathic, with 95% demonstrating no causative factor," whereas "A CNS insult or structural abnormality occurs in more than 75% of boys with CPP" (p. 1178). The imaging rule is nevertheless universal: "In every case, an MRI of the brain should be performed to assess for hypothalamic brain tumor."
Trap: (D) is more tempting than (B)'s outright inversion, because it gets the epidemiology exactly right and then draws the wrong conclusion from it. Probability shapes the level of suspicion, not the imaging rule, and the phrase the chapter uses is "In every case."

Q: The mother of an 8-year-old girl with central precocious puberty asks how to manage her daughter's development. Which statements should the nurse include? Select all that apply.
A) Dress and activities should match her chronologic age rather than her physical development
B) Her sexual interest and mental age are those of an 8-year-old
C) She may be fertile, even at this age
D) Estrogen-containing birth control pills should be started now as a precaution
E) The hardest period is usually the school years before adolescence
Answer: A, B, C, E
Rationale: "Dress and activities for the physically precocious child should be appropriate to the chronologic age"; "Sexual interest is not usually advanced beyond the child's chronologic age, and parents need to understand that the child's mental age is congruent with the chronologic age"; "the nurse should emphasize to parents that the child may be fertile"; and "The most difficult time for the child is usually the school years before adolescence. After puberty, physical differences from peers are no longer present" (p. 1178).
Trap: (D) is the one wrong option, and it is wrong for a physiologic reason rather than a moral one: "hormonal forms of birth control, such as estrogen pills, prematurely initiate epiphyseal closure, resulting in stunted linear growth" (p. 1178). The chapter also states that "Usually no form of contraception is necessary unless the child is sexually active," so the option fails twice.

Topic: diabetes insipidus and siadh in children
Subtopic: recognizing diabetes insipidus

Q: A 5-month-old is brought in for persistent irritability and crying. The mother reports the baby quiets when given a bottle of plain water but keeps fussing after formula feedings. The infant has lost weight and soaks through an unusual number of diapers. Which condition does this picture suggest?
A) Diabetes insipidus
B) Syndrome of inappropriate antidiuretic hormone
C) Type 1 diabetes mellitus
D) Congenital hypothyroidism
Answer: A
Rationale: "In the infant the initial symptom is irritability that is relieved with feedings of water but not milk" (p. 1178), and the chapter repeats the clue under nursing care management: "persistent irritability and crying in an infant that is relieved only by bottle-feedings of water" (p. 1179). The infant is also "prone to severe dehydration, electrolyte imbalance, hyperthermia, azotemia, and potential circulatory collapse."
Trap: (C) pulls because polyuria, thirst, and weight loss belong to both diseases, and the water-not-milk detail is the only thing in the stem that separates them. Teaching families that "DI is a different condition from DM" is itself a stated nursing action (p. 1179), which is a fair signal that the confusion is expected.

Q: A school-age child is undergoing a water deprivation test. Which finding requires the nurse to terminate the fluid restriction?
A) Weight loss of 4% of body weight
B) Urine that remains dilute despite the restriction
C) The child complaining of intense thirst
D) A urine output that exceeds the measured intake
Answer: A
Rationale: "A weight loss between 3% and 5% indicates significant dehydration and requires termination of the fluid restriction" (p. 1179). The test requires "strict monitoring of fluid intake and urinary output, measurement of urine concentration (specific gravity or osmolality), and frequent weight checks."
Trap: (B) and (D) are the abnormal results the test exists to produce, since in DI "fluid restriction has little or no effect on urine formation but causes weight loss from dehydration" (p. 1178) — they are findings to record, not reasons to stop. Thirst is expected throughout, and the Nursing Alert warns that small children need close observation "to prevent them from drinking, even from toilet bowls, flower vases, or other unlikely sources of fluid" (p. 1179).

Q: A child with polyuria and polydipsia is given a test dose of aqueous vasopressin, and the urine osmolality does not change. What does this result indicate?
A) Nephrogenic diabetes insipidus, in which the renal tubules do not respond to the hormone
B) Central diabetes insipidus, in which too little antidiuretic hormone is produced
C) Syndrome of inappropriate antidiuretic hormone
D) Compulsive water drinking from a psychogenic cause
Answer: A
Rationale: "Unresponsiveness to exogenous vasopressin usually indicates nephrogenic DI" (p. 1179), a form described as "a rare hereditary disorder affecting primarily males and caused by unresponsiveness of the renal tubules to the hormone" (p. 1178). The mirror result confirms the other form: "a rise in urine osmolality after vasopressin administration indicates central DI."
Trap: (B) is the mirror error and names the commoner disease, so the reflex is to answer with the diagnosis rather than with the test result; the test exists precisely because hormone replacement works in one form and is useless in the other. (D) is a genuine differential the chapter raises, but it is settled by psychologic consultation, not by the vasopressin response.

Subtopic: treatment and its mirror image

Q: A parent of a child taking twice-daily DDAVP calls to report that the child urinates more than usual in the early evening, before the bedtime dose. What should the nurse explain?
A) Some breakthrough urination in the evening is deliberate, guarding against overmedication
B) The dose is too low and should be increased tonight
C) The child most likely missed the morning dose
D) The medication should be changed to the parenteral route
Answer: A
Rationale: Breakthrough "urination is allowed during the evening hours as a precaution against overmedication" (p. 1179). The twice-daily schedule is timed at bedtime "to allow the child to sleep through the night and in the morning to allow fewer interruptions in the school day."
Trap: (B) is the intuitive answer, because the symptom the drug was given to control has reappeared, and it is the one that causes harm: "The signs of overmedication are similar to manifestations associated with the syndrome of inappropriate ADH (SIADH)" (p. 1179). Over-treated diabetes insipidus becomes the opposite disorder.

Q: Two days after a craniotomy, a child develops a serum sodium of 121 mEq/L, rising urine osmolality, weight gain, nausea, and irritability. Which is the immediate management of choice?
A) Fluid restriction
B) Intranasal DDAVP
C) Free water boluses through the nasogastric tube
D) A rapid infusion of hypertonic saline
Answer: A
Rationale: "Fluid restriction is the immediate management of choice" in SIADH (p. 1179). "Excess ADH causes free water to be reabsorbed from the kidneys. As increased free water circulates, serum osmolality goes down, and urine osmolality inappropriately increases," and the danger is cerebral: "When hyponatremia occurs acutely, swelling of the brain occurs."
Trap: (B) is the mirror error, since DDAVP is the diabetes insipidus treatment (p. 1179) and giving more antidiuretic hormone here deepens the retention; (D) names a real therapy but skips a step, because "Severe SIADH may require hypertonic saline infusion that is only given in the hospital setting under close supervision" — it follows fluid restriction rather than replacing it.

Topic: pediatric hypothyroidism goiter and hashimoto disease
Subtopic: acquired hypothyroidism

Q: The parents of an 11-year-old newly diagnosed with acquired hypothyroidism ask whether their daughter will have lasting intellectual problems. Which response is accurate?
A) Intellectual disability is not associated with hypothyroidism acquired at this age, because brain growth is nearly complete by 2 to 3 years
B) Intellectual disability is likely unless replacement begins within about two weeks
C) The intellectual outcome depends entirely on how high the TSH is at diagnosis
D) About half of children with any form of hypothyroidism have intellectual disability
Answer: A
Rationale: "Because brain growth is nearly complete by 2 to 3 years of age, intellectual disability and neurologic sequelae are not associated with juvenile hypothyroidism" (p. 1180). Box 31.7 supplies the reason: thyroid hormone "Affects central nervous system development and cerebration during first 2 to 3 years," a window that has already closed for this child.
Trap: (B) moves the consequence of the congenital form onto an acquired case, which is the stage swap this pair is built on: "If left untreated, congenital hypothyroidism causes mental retardation" (p. 1180), and that is why newborn screening exists.

Q: A child with severe acquired hypothyroidism is started on L-thyroxine, and the dose is to be increased in steps over 4 to 8 weeks. A parent asks why the full dose is not simply given now. Which explanation is correct?
A) Gradual increases avoid producing symptoms of hyperthyroidism
B) The thyroid gland needs time to begin producing its own hormone again
C) A full dose would suppress the pituitary permanently
D) The drug must be titrated to the child's weight, which takes several weeks to establish
Answer: A
Rationale: "In children with severe symptoms, the restoration thyroid function is achieved more gradually with administration of increasing amounts of L-thyroxine over a period of 4 to 8 weeks. This is done to avoid symptoms of hyperthyroidism" (p. 1180).
Trap: (B) sounds physiologic and describes the wrong organ's recovery — that is the rationale for tapering exogenous steroids so the pituitary can resume secreting ACTH (p. 1188), not for ramping thyroid replacement. (D) invents a titration reason the chapter does not give.

Subtopic: goiter and chronic lymphocytic thyroiditis

Q: A 13-year-old is found on routine examination to have a symmetric, firm, nontender enlargement of the thyroid gland. What can the nurse conclude from the enlargement alone?
A) Nothing about thyroid function, since a goiter occurs with deficient, excessive, or normal hormone secretion
B) That the child is hypothyroid
C) That the child is hyperthyroid
D) That the gland is malignant until proven otherwise
Answer: A
Rationale: "A goiter is an enlargement or hypertrophy of the thyroid gland. It may occur with deficient (hypothyroid), excessive (hyperthyroid), or normal (euthyroid) TH secretion" (p. 1181). In chronic lymphocytic thyroiditis specifically, "Most children are euthyroid, but some display symptoms of hypothyroidism. Others have signs suggestive of hyperthyroidism."
Trap: (B) is the reflex pick because goiter is most often taught beside iodine deficiency; (D) takes only half of the nodule rule, which has two halves that must be kept together: "Nodules are rarely caused by a cancerous tumor but always require evaluation" (p. 1181).

Q: A 14-year-old is diagnosed with chronic lymphocytic thyroiditis (Hashimoto disease). Which conditions does the chapter name as clustering in the families of these children? Select all that apply.
A) Graves disease
B) Type 1 diabetes mellitus
C) Addison disease
D) Pernicious anemia
E) Cystic fibrosis
Answer: A, B, C, D
Rationale: "In families this disease is closely related to other thyroid disorders (e.g., Graves disease, idiopathic hypothyroidism, idiopathic myxedema) and autoimmune disorders (e.g., pernicious anemia, Addison disease, type 1 DM, and hypoparathyroidism)" (p. 1181). The mechanism is shared: "Thyroid cell damage and death are autoimmune mediated by T cells and cytokines."
Trap: (E) is the wrong option and it is chosen on the wrong principle. Cystic fibrosis does run in families, so a reader selecting on heritability takes it; the thread holding this list together is autoimmunity, not inheritance.

Topic: graves disease and thyroidectomy care in adolescents
Subtopic: antithyroid drug therapy

Q: A 13-year-old started methimazole for Graves disease six weeks ago and calls the clinic with a sore throat and a temperature of 38.9°C. What is the nurse's correct response?
A) Have the child examined now, because sore throat and fever accompany agranulocytosis
B) Advise acetaminophen and fluids, and call back if there is no improvement in three days
C) Reassure the family that this is an expected effect of the drug in the first months
D) Instruct the family to double the next dose, because fever raises thyroid hormone demand
Answer: A
Rationale: "The most serious side effect of ATDs is agranulocytosis (severe leukopenia), which generally occurs within the initial weeks or months of therapy. It is usually accompanied by a sore throat and fever" (p. 1182). The Drug Alert repeats it: because these symptoms "accompany the grave complication of leukopenia, these children should be examined if such symptoms occur" (p. 1183). Treatment is "immediate discontinuation of the drug and the administration of antibiotics and glucocorticoids until symptoms resolve."
Trap: (B) is the advice a sore throat earns in any other child, which is exactly why the alert is printed twice in two pages; (C) inverts the meaning of the timing, since the first weeks to months are when the risk is highest, not when the symptom is harmless.

Q: An adolescent scheduled for subtotal thyroidectomy has been taking oral iodine for several weeks preoperatively and admits she stopped because of the taste. Which risk should the nurse address first?
A) Thyroid crisis, which sudden discontinuation can precipitate
B) Delayed wound healing after the operation
C) Hypocalcemia before the surgery
D) Loss of the preparation's antibacterial effect
Answer: A
Rationale: "Compliance with iodine therapy is essential to avoid the danger of thyroid crisis after sudden discontinuation" (p. 1183). Thyroid storm "may be precipitated by acute infection, surgical emergency, or discontinuation of antithyroid therapy," and presents as "acute onset of severe irritability and restlessness, vomiting, diarrhea, hyperthermia, hypertension, severe tachycardia, and prostration," with "rapid progression to delirium, coma, and death" (p. 1182).
Trap: (C) names the right complication at the wrong moment, since hypoparathyroidism follows removal of the gland rather than preceding it (p. 1183). The palatability problem has its own answer and it is not stopping the drug: mix the preparation "with a strong-tasting fruit juice, such as grape or punch flavors," and give it "through a straw."

Subtopic: after thyroidectomy

Q: A child returns from a subtotal thyroidectomy. How should the nurse position the neck, and why?
A) Slightly flexed, to avoid strain on the sutures
B) Hyperextended, to keep the airway open
C) Rotated to one side, to encourage drainage
D) In whatever position the child prefers, since position does not affect the suture line
Answer: A
Rationale: "Postoperative care involves positioning with the neck slightly flexed to avoid strain on the sutures and observation for bleeding and complications," and "The children learn to support the neck in this position when they sit up" (p. 1183).
Trap: (B) is the mirror error and it is drawn from a real instruction elsewhere in the same chapter: in a newborn born with a goiter, "Hyperextension of the neck often facilitates breathing" (p. 1181). Same organ, opposite position, and what changes the answer is whether there is a suture line to protect or an airway to open.

Q: On the first day after thyroidectomy, a 15-year-old who was cheerful before surgery is now anxious and tearful and says she feels low. Vital signs are stable and the dressing is dry. What should the nurse suspect?
A) Early hypocalcemia from hypoparathyroidism
B) A normal emotional reaction to having had surgery
C) Damage to the laryngeal nerve
D) Thyroid storm
Answer: A
Rationale: The Nursing Alert states that "The earliest indication of hypoparathyroidism may be anxiety and mental depression, followed by paresthesia and evidence of heightened neuromuscular excitability" — Chvostek sign, Trousseau sign, and tetany (p. 1183). "The nurse should observe for signs of hypoparathyroidism, which causes hypocalcemia, in the immediate postoperative period."
Trap: (B) is the humane reading and the dangerous one, because the mood change here arrives before the classic calcium signs and is easy to accept at face value; (C) would announce itself as "severe stridor or hoarseness" (p. 1183) rather than as a change in affect.

Topic: parathyroid disorders and calcium balance in children
Subtopic: reading the calcium and phosphorus pair

Q: A child's laboratory results show a decreased serum calcium with an increased serum phosphorus. Which group of findings should the nurse expect?
A) Muscle cramps, tingling of the hands and feet, and a positive Trousseau sign
B) Muscle weakness, constipation, and confusion
C) Polyuria, renal colic, and hypertension
D) Spontaneous fractures and vague bone pain
Answer: A
Rationale: "The diagnosis of hypoparathyroidism is made on the basis of clinical manifestations associated with decreased serum calcium and increased serum phosphorus" (p. 1184). "Muscle cramps are an early symptom, progressing to numbness, stiffness, and tingling in the hands and feet. A positive Chvostek or Trousseau sign or laryngeal spasms may be present" (p. 1183).
Trap: (B), (C), and (D) are all genuine findings of the opposite disorder — Box 31.9 lists weakness and fatigability, constipation, confusion and delusions, polyuria and polydipsia, renal colic, hypertension, and spontaneous fractures under hyperparathyroidism (p. 1184). Low calcium excites the neuromuscular system; high calcium sedates it.

Q: A child with chronic renal failure develops secondary hyperparathyroidism. Which treatment should the nurse expect?
A) Oral calcium salts, high-dose vitamin D, and a low-phosphorus diet
B) Measures to lower the serum calcium, since hyperparathyroidism causes hypercalcemia
C) Immediate parathyroidectomy
D) Fluid restriction with a high-phosphorus diet
Answer: A
Rationale: Because the underlying renal disorder is irreversible, "treatment is aimed at raising serum calcium levels to inhibit the stimulatory effect of low levels on the parathyroid. This includes oral administration of calcium salts, high doses of vitamin D to enhance calcium absorption, a low-phosphorus diet, and administration of a phosphorus-mobilizing aluminum hydroxide to reduce phosphate absorption" (p. 1184).
Trap: (B) is the reflex inversion and the most harmful option, because the name of the disease points at hypercalcemia while the secondary form is being driven by a low calcium the treatment must raise; (C) belongs to the primary form, where "The most common cause of primary hyperparathyroidism is adenoma of the gland."

Q: A child is returning from a parathyroidectomy. Which items and precautions should the nurse have in place? Select all that apply.
A) Injectable calcium gluconate at the bedside
B) Seizure precautions
C) Observation for stridor, hoarseness, and a feeling of tightness in the throat
D) A tracheostomy set near the bed
E) Withholding all calcium-containing foods
Answer: A, B, C, D
Rationale: "Because hypocalcemia is a potential complication, observing for signs of tetany, instituting seizure precautions, and having calcium gluconate available for emergency use are part of the nursing care" (p. 1185). The hypoparathyroidism section supplies the rest: watch for "signs of laryngospasm such as stridor, hoarseness, and a feeling of tightness in the throat," and "A tracheostomy set and injectable calcium gluconate should be located near the bedside for emergency use" (p. 1184).
Trap: (E) is the wrong option and it inverts the postoperative problem. Removing overactive parathyroid tissue drops the serum calcium, so what follows surgery is the risk of tetany, not of hypercalcemia — the same reversal that makes hypoparathyroidism a watched complication after thyroidectomy (p. 1183).

Topic: adrenocortical insufficiency and cushing syndrome in children
Subtopic: acute adrenal crisis

Q: A toddler with meningococcemia develops hypotension, cold clammy skin, and spreading purpura despite adequate antibiotic therapy, and adrenal crisis is suspected. What is the correct first action?
A) Institute cortisol replacement immediately, since improvement with cortisol confirms the diagnosis
B) Draw plasma cortisol and ACTH levels and wait for the results before giving any steroid
C) Give a bolus of dextrose-free normal saline and reassess in one hour
D) Obtain an adrenal ultrasound to look for hemorrhage into the gland
Answer: A
Rationale: "There is no rapid, definitive test for confirmation of acute adrenocortical insufficiency," and cortisol and ACTH levels are "too time consuming to be practical for initial diagnosis" (p. 1186). "Because there is no real danger in administering a cortisol preparation for a short period, it is important to institute treatment immediately. Improvement with cortisol therapy confirms the diagnosis."
Trap: (B) is the option that sounds rigorous and is the one the chapter rules out; the same permission is restated for families as "there is no physical harm in treating for suspected adrenal insufficiency that is not present, but the consequence of not treating acute adrenal insufficiency can be fatal" (p. 1191). (C) gets the fluid right and the composition wrong, because "Normal saline containing 5% glucose is given parenterally to replace lost fluid, electrolytes, and glucose" — cortisol deficiency causes hypoglycemia, so the resuscitation fluid treats two problems at once.

Q: A school-age child with Addison disease develops a fever and an ear infection. What should the nurse teach the parents about the hydrocortisone dose?
A) Triple the dose, to meet the body's increased need for glucocorticoids
B) Keep the dose unchanged, since this is a minor illness
C) Halve the dose, because fever already raises endogenous cortisol
D) Hold the dose until the fever resolves
Answer: A
Rationale: "During stressful situations (e.g., fever, infection, emotional upset, or surgery), the dosage must be tripled to accommodate the body's increased need for glucocorticoids," and "Failure to meet this requirement will precipitate an acute crisis" (p. 1187).
Trap: (B) is the quietest wrong answer and the commonest one, because nothing in the stem looks like an emergency yet and an ear infection is an ordinary childhood event. The chapter sets the threshold lower than it feels by naming emotional upset in the same list as surgery, and (C) inverts the physiology entirely: the failing adrenal is what cannot raise cortisol under stress.

Q: A parent calls to say their child with chronic adrenocortical insufficiency has vomited the morning hydrocortisone dose twice and is now sleepy. What should the nurse advise?
A) Treat this as an emergency, giving the prefilled hydrocortisone injection if trained and seeking care immediately
B) Wait two hours and retry the oral dose with crackers
C) Skip today's dose and resume the schedule tomorrow
D) Give a double oral dose with an antacid to reduce gastric irritation
Answer: A
Rationale: "Sudden termination of the drug because of inadequate supplies or inability to ingest the oral form because of vomiting places the child in danger of an acute adrenal crisis" (pp. 1187–1188). "Ideally, families will have a prefilled syringe of hydrocortisone and have training to administer this drug during a crisis," and "Any evidence of acute insufficiency is reported to the practitioner immediately" (p. 1188).
Trap: (D) is built from a true fact used in the wrong situation: cortisone does cause "gastric irritation, which is minimized by ingestion with food or the use of an antacid" (p. 1188), but a route that is already failing cannot be rescued by what is mixed into it. (C) treats a missed dose as recoverable, which is true of almost every other drug a child takes.

Subtopic: cortisol excess

Q: A child on prolonged high-dose corticosteroid therapy is being assessed. Which set of findings is expected?
A) Hypertension, hypokalemia, alkalosis, and hyperglycemia
B) Hypotension, hyperkalemia, hyponatremia, and hypoglycemia
C) Hypertension, hyperkalemia, acidosis, and hypoglycemia
D) Hypotension, hypokalemia, alkalosis, and hyperglycemia
Answer: A
Rationale: Table 31.1 pairs each sign with its cause: hypertension and arteriosclerosis arise from "Increased salt and water retention (hypervolemia)," hypokalemia and alkalosis from "Increased excretion of potassium and hydrogen ions," and glycosuria from "Increased gluconeogenesis by liver and decreased rate of glucose utilization by cells" (p. 1188).
Trap: (B) is the complete mirror image and names the adrenal-insufficiency signature of hyponatremia with hyperkalemia and hypoglycemia (pp. 1172, 1186). Both pictures come from the same axis, so a reader who remembers "a steroid problem" without the direction will take it. (C) and (D) each swap a single value, which is how a half-remembered pair fails in practice.

Q: A child whose cushingoid appearance developed during long-term prednisone therapy is having the drug discontinued. Which points should the nurse include? Select all that apply.
A) The dose is tapered gradually rather than stopped
B) Abrupt withdrawal can precipitate acute adrenal insufficiency
C) While the steroid continues, it is given early in the morning
D) The cushingoid appearance is reversible once the steroids are discontinued
E) The dose should be moved to the evening so it does not interfere with the school day
Answer: A, B, C, D
Rationale: "This condition is reversible after the steroids are gradually discontinued," and "Abrupt withdrawal will precipitate acute adrenal insufficiency" (p. 1188). Tapering allows "the anterior pituitary an opportunity to secrete increasing amounts of ACTH to stimulate the adrenals to produce cortisol." On timing: "Giving the drug early in the day maintains the normal diurnal pattern of cortisol secretion" (p. 1189).
Trap: (E) is the wrong option and it comes with the friendliest rationale, which is what makes it selectable: "If given during the evening, it is more likely to produce symptoms because endogenous cortisol levels are already low, and the additional supply exerts more pronounced effects" (p. 1189). Convenience is not the variable being managed; the diurnal rhythm is.

Topic: congenital adrenal hyperplasia and pheochromocytoma
Subtopic: congenital adrenal hyperplasia

Q: A newborn girl has ambiguous genitalia and congenital adrenal hyperplasia is suspected. Which diagnostic studies should the nurse expect? Select all that apply.
A) Newborn screening measurement of 17-hydroxyprogesterone
B) Serum 17-ketosteroid levels
C) Pelvic ultrasonography
D) Serum electrolytes
E) Serum calcium and phosphorus studies
Answer: A, B, C, D
Rationale: "Newborn screening is currently done in all 50 states by measurement of the cortisol precursor 17-hydroxyprogesterone"; "Definitive diagnosis is confirmed by evidence of increased 17-ketosteroid levels in most types of CAH"; ultrasonography "readily identifies the absence or presence of female reproductive organs or male testes in a newborn or child with ambiguous genitalia"; and "In complete 21-hydroxylase deficiency, blood electrolytes demonstrate loss of sodium and chloride and elevation of potassium" (p. 1190).
Trap: (E) is the wrong option and it belongs to a different gland: "Blood studies to identify elevated calcium and decreased phosphorus levels are routinely performed" is the hyperparathyroidism workup (p. 1184). Every option here is a real sentence from this chapter, which is what makes the misplaced one hard to see.

Q: A 5-year-old boy has pubic hair, an enlarged penis, acne, and a deepening voice, but his testes remain small. Which explanation fits these findings?
A) The androgen is adrenal rather than gonadal in origin, as in congenital adrenal hyperplasia
B) He has central precocious puberty, with early activation of the hypothalamic-pituitary-gonadal axis
C) He has premature pubarche, an incomplete variant with no other signs of puberty
D) He has a growth hormone-secreting pituitary adenoma
Answer: A
Rationale: "in contrast to precocious puberty, breasts do not develop in girls, and they remain amenorrheic and infertile. In boys, the testes remain small, and spermatogenesis does not occur" (p. 1190), because the androgen driving virilization comes from the adrenal cortex.
Trap: (B) is the reflex answer and the testes are what defeat it: in central precocious puberty the axis drives the gonads, producing "early maturation and development of the gonads, with secretion of sex hormones, development of secondary sexual characteristics, and sometimes production of mature sperm and ova" (p. 1177). (C) fails on the stem's own breadth, since premature pubarche is "early development of sexual hair" appearing "without other signs of puberty" (p. 1178) and this child has several.

Subtopic: catecholamine-secreting tumors

Q: A child with episodic hypertension, headache, tachycardia, weight loss, and diaphoresis is being evaluated for pheochromocytoma. Which action must the nurse avoid?
A) Palpating the abdominal mass
B) Monitoring blood glucose and urine acetone daily
C) Keeping the environment restful and free of emotional stress
D) Encouraging the parents to room in and participate in care
Answer: A
Rationale: The Nursing Alert is explicit: "Do not palpate the mass. Preoperative palpation of the mass releases catecholamines, which can stimulate severe hypertension and tachyarrhythmias" (p. 1191).
Trap: The prohibited action hides inside a list of correct ones, because (B), (C), and (D) are all stated interventions — "Note daily blood glucose levels, urine acetone, and any signs of hyperglycemia and report immediately," an environment "made conducive to rest and free of emotional stress," and parents "encouraged to room-in with their child and to participate in care" (p. 1191). Abdominal palpation is routine assessment everywhere else, which is why it has to be ruled out by name.

Topic: type 1 diabetes insulin therapy and glucose monitoring
Subtopic: recognizing type 1 diabetes

Q: A 9-year-old is admitted after two weeks of weight loss despite constant hunger, bed-wetting after years of dryness, and unrelenting thirst. Which statement about the likely type of diabetes is correct?
A) This abrupt presentation with the three Ps and weight loss describes type 1, in which insulin deficiency is absolute
B) This describes type 2, in which insulin deficiency is absolute
C) This describes type 2, because type 2 accounts for 85% to 90% of the diabetic population
D) Presentation cannot suggest the type; only islet cell antibodies can
Answer: A
Rationale: Table 31.2 gives type 1 an abrupt onset with "Three Ps common, polyuria, polydipsia, polyphagia" in an underweight child, against type 2's gradual onset in an overweight one whose presenting symptoms "May be related to long-term complications" (p. 1192). Type 1 destruction of the insulin-producing cells "usually leads to absolute insulin deficiency," while type 2 combines resistance with "relative (rather than absolute) insulin deficiency."
Trap: (C) quotes the table accurately and applies it to the wrong population. The 85% to 90% figure describes the entire diabetic population, while the narrative on the same page says that "In children younger than 10 years old, most diabetes cases are type 1." A stem about a child wants type 1; a stem about diabetes generally wants type 2.

Subtopic: administering insulin

Q: A parent is drawing 6 units of regular insulin and 14 units of NPH into one syringe. Which sequence is correct?
A) Air into the NPH vial, air into the regular vial, withdraw the regular insulin, then withdraw the NPH
B) Air into the regular vial, air into the NPH vial, withdraw the NPH, then withdraw the regular insulin
C) Withdraw the NPH first, so the cloudy insulin is not contaminated by the clear
D) Draw the two insulins into separate syringes, since they cannot be mixed
Answer: A
Rationale: The four steps as printed: "(1) inject the measured amount of air (equivalent to the dosage) into the long-acting insulin; (2) inject the measured amount of air into the rapid-acting (clear) insulin without removing the needle; (3) withdraw the clear insulin; and (4) insert the needle (already containing the clear insulin) into the long-acting (cloudy) insulin and then withdraw the desired amount" (p. 1203). The Nursing Alert gives the reason: withdrawing the clear insulin first "avoids contaminating the short-acting insulin with the longer-acting insulin."
Trap: (C) is the mirror error and it protects the wrong vial. Note that the air sequence runs opposite to the insulin sequence, so a family who has memorized only "clear before cloudy" can still reverse the air steps, and that is exactly the item (B) is built from.

Q: An adolescent is playing in a tennis tournament this afternoon and asks where to inject her morning insulin. Which guidance is correct?
A) Choose a site other than the exercising extremity, because exercise enhances absorption from exercised muscles
B) Use the arm she serves with, so the insulin is available faster during play
C) Use the abdomen, because abdominal absorption is the slowest of the sites
D) Site does not matter as long as she rotates within one area
Answer: A
Rationale: "absorption is also altered by vigorous exercise, which enhances absorption from exercised muscles. Therefore it is recommended that a site be chosen other than the exercising extremity (e.g., avoiding legs and arms when playing in a tennis tournament)" (p. 1203).
Trap: (C) reverses Table 31.6, which gives the abdomen a very fast rate with a very short duration and the buttock a very slow rate with a very long duration (p. 1203); (D) discards the reason rotation exists, which is that "insulin absorption is slowed by fat pads that develop in overused injection areas" (p. 1202).

Subtopic: monitoring and targets

Q: An 8-year-old's home log shows glucose values almost entirely within range, but the hemoglobin A1C is 10.5%. How should the nurse interpret this?
A) The A1C reflects the average glucose of the previous 2 to 3 months and detects nonadherence, so the log and the A1C disagree
B) The A1C is unreliable in children under 10 and should be repeated
C) The log is the accurate record, since the A1C reflects only the past week
D) An A1C of 10.5% is good control for a school-age child
Answer: A
Rationale: Glucose attaches irreversibly to hemoglobin for the red cell's roughly 120-day life, so the value "reflects the average blood glucose levels over the previous 2 to 3 months," and the test is used for "detecting incorrect testing, monitoring the effectiveness of changes in treatment, defining patients' goals, and detecting nonadherence" (p. 1196).
Trap: (D) fails against the chapter's own grading, where "levels of 10% or higher" are poor control and the school-age target is under 8% (p. 1196); (C) shortens the window to the interval a family can actually influence in the days before a clinic visit, which is precisely the wish the test is designed to defeat.

Q: A nurse is reviewing glucose goals for a 4-year-old and for a 16-year-old with type 1 diabetes. Which statement is correct?
A) The preschooler is allowed to run higher, because of high risk and vulnerability to hypoglycemia
B) The preschooler should be held to the tighter target, to protect the developing brain from hyperglycemia
C) Both should be held to a premeal range of 90 to 130 mg/dl
D) Targets are set by duration of diabetes rather than by age
Answer: A
Rationale: Table 31.3 gives children under 6 a premeal range of 100 to 180 mg/dl, a bedtime range of 110 to 200 mg/dl, and an A1C at or below 8.5%, with the stated rationale "High risk and vulnerability to hypoglycemia." Adolescents over 12 are held to 90 to 130 mg/dl before meals and an A1C under 7.5% (p. 1196).
Trap: (B) is the intuitive inversion, since tighter control sounds more protective for the youngest patient, and it misreads the rationale column: the danger being guarded against in a small child is the low, not the high. (C) applies the adolescent row to a preschooler, which is the same swap with the numbers attached.

Topic: hypoglycemia hyperglycemia and diabetic ketoacidosis in children
Subtopic: telling the two apart

Q: A nurse is teaching parents to recognize hypoglycemia in their child. Which findings should they be taught to attribute to a low blood glucose? Select all that apply.
A) An onset over minutes rather than days
B) Pallor and sweating
C) Shakiness, hunger, and headache
D) Tremors, progressing late to dilated pupils and seizure
E) Deep, rapid Kussmaul respirations
Answer: A, B, C, D
Rationale: Table 31.5 gives hypoglycemia a rapid onset in minutes, pallor with sweating, a shaky feeling with hunger, headache and dizziness, tremors, and the late signs "Hyperreflexia, dilated pupils, seizure" with shock and coma (p. 1197). The adrenergic wave — "tachycardia, tremors, sweating, irritability, aggression, and hunger" — arrives before the neuroglycopenic one.
Trap: (E) is the one wrong option and it belongs to the opposite state: hyperglycemia produces "Deep, rapid (Kussmaul)" respirations while hypoglycemia leaves them shallow or normal (p. 1197). Because both states open with a change in behavior, the chapter's own instruction is not to reason it out but to test: "the simplest way to differentiate between the two is to test the blood glucose level."

Q: A school-age child's fasting glucose has been high every morning. The evening intermediate-acting insulin has been increased twice with no improvement. A 2 AM glucose is drawn and is 48 mg/dl. What does this indicate, and how is it treated?
A) The Somogyi effect, treated by decreasing the nocturnal insulin dose
B) Insulin waning, treated by increasing the nocturnal insulin dose further
C) The dawn phenomenon, for which no change is required
D) Missed doses, which should be addressed by questioning the family about adherence
Answer: A
Rationale: "The Somogyi effect may occur at any time but often entails an elevated blood glucose level at bedtime and a drop at 2 AM, with a rebound rise following," produced by "counterregulatory hormones (epinephrine, GH, and corticosteroids), which are stimulated by hypoglycemia." The treatment is "decreasing the nocturnal insulin dose to prevent the 2 AM hypoglycemia" (p. 1198).
Trap: (B) is the mirror error and it makes the child worse with every increase. Insulin waning is "a progressive rise in blood glucose levels from bedtime to morning" with no low at any point, and it is the one "treated by increasing the nocturnal insulin dose" (p. 1198). All three causes of morning hyperglycemia look identical at breakfast, and only the middle-of-the-night value separates them.

Subtopic: acute management

Q: A 6-year-old with type 1 diabetes is found unresponsive at school with jerking movements of the arms. The school nurse has a glucometer, orange juice, and a glucagon emergency kit at hand. What should be done?
A) Administer glucagon and position the child on the side
B) Give 4 ounces of orange juice and recheck the glucose in 15 minutes
C) Check the blood glucose and wait for the result before taking any action
D) Give nothing and call for transport, since the cause is not established
Answer: A
Rationale: The Emergency Treatment box directs that a severe reaction with unresponsiveness, unconsciousness, or seizures be treated by administering "glucagon as prescribed," followed by a planned meal or snack when the child can eat (p. 1204). The Nursing Alert adds that "Vomiting may occur after administration of glucagon; therefore precautions against aspiration must be taken (e.g., placing the child on the side) because the child often becomes unconscious" (p. 1198).
Trap: (B) is the correct treatment for the mild and moderate levels of that same box, and giving it here risks aspiration in a child who cannot protect an airway; only the severity changes the route, not the substance. (C) delays treatment for a test, against the chapter's stated default: "When in doubt, it is best to assume hypoglycemia and treat" (p. 1198).

Q: A father calls about his 5-year-old with type 1 diabetes, who has a cold, is eating very little, and has ketones in the urine. Which response is best?
A) Encourage calorie-free fluids, continue the insulin, and check glucose and ketones every 3 hours
B) Hold the insulin until the child is eating normally again
C) Give an extra dose of insulin now and call back tomorrow
D) Bring the child to the emergency department immediately
Answer: A
Rationale: "The goals during an illness are to restore euglycemia, treat urinary ketones, and maintain hydration"; "Monitor blood glucose levels and urinary ketones every 3 hours"; and the priority is stated plainly: "Although insulin and diet are important tools in sick-day care, fluids are the most important intervention. Fluids must be encouraged to prevent dehydration and to flush out ketones" (p. 1198).
Trap: (B) follows a sensible-sounding rule about matching insulin to food intake and is flatly contradicted: "Insulin should never be omitted during an illness, although dosage requirements may increase, decrease, or remain unchanged" (p. 1198). (D) is premature against the chapter's own thresholds for calling, which are vomiting more than once, glucose remaining above 240 mg/dl, or ketones that stay high, none of which the stem establishes.

Q: A 10-year-old is admitted in diabetic ketoacidosis. The serum potassium is 4.8 mEq/L, the child has not yet voided, and a 0.9% saline bolus is infusing. What is correct about potassium replacement?
A) Potassium waits until the level is known to be normal or low, voiding is observed, and insulin has been given, and it is never given as a rapid bolus
B) Potassium should be given now as an IV bolus, since total body potassium is depleted
C) Potassium is unnecessary, because the serum level is normal
D) Potassium is added only once the level falls below 3.0 mEq/L
Answer: A
Rationale: The Nursing Alert states that "Potassium must never be given until the serum potassium level is known to be normal or low and urinary voiding is observed," and "Never give potassium as a rapid IV bolus, or cardiac arrest may result" (p. 1198). Replacement begins once renal function is established, meaning the child is "voiding at least 25 ml/hr," and insulin has been given.
Trap: (C) is the trap the chapter builds deliberately two pages earlier: "The total body potassium is then decreased even though the serum potassium level may be elevated as a result of the decreased fluid volume in which it circulates" (p. 1193), so a reassuring value does not mean there is no deficit. (B) has the physiology right and the route lethal.

Q: A child in diabetic ketoacidosis is estimated to be 10% dehydrated. Which approach to replacing the fluid deficit is current?
A) Replace the deficit evenly over 36 to 48 hours, to reduce the risk of cerebral edema
B) Replace 50% of the deficit over the first 8 to 12 hours and the remainder over the next 16 to 24 hours
C) Replace the entire deficit within the first 4 hours, to restore perfusion quickly
D) Withhold fluids until the insulin infusion has brought the glucose below 300 mg/dl
Answer: A
Rationale: "Current trends suggest more cautious fluid management to reduce the risk of cerebral edema. Therefore the fluid deficit should be replaced evenly over a period of 36 to 48 hours" (p. 1198). The initial hydrating solution is 0.9% saline, and insulin follows the rehydration bolus "because serum glucose levels fall rapidly after volume expansion" (pp. 1198–1199).
Trap: (B) is the schedule the chapter prints and then supersedes — "Traditionally, deficits have been replaced at a rate of 50% over the first 8 to 12 hours" (p. 1198) — so it is the option that is correct in an older text and wrong here, which makes it the most defensible-looking distractor. (D) reverses the order of fluids and insulin.
