chapter says decides it; epiglottitis against bacterial tracheitis, on drooling; acute otitis

Topic: pediatric airway structure and respiratory assessment

Q: A nursing student asks why a small amount of airway edema causes so much more distress in an infant than in an adult. What is the correct explanation?
A) If airway diameter decreases, resistance increases exponentially, and airway size is the dominant determinant of resistance
B) Resistance rises in direct proportion to the reduction in diameter
C) Chest wall tissue accounts for most airway resistance in infants
D) Infants have fewer alveoli, which raises airway resistance directly
Answer: A
Rationale: The chapter states that "Resistance is determined primarily by airway size," that flow resistance in the airways is the most important of the three sources, and that while flow rate, gas viscosity, and airway length raise resistance proportionally, "If airway diameter decreases, resistance increases exponentially" (p. 884).
Trap: (B) is the proportional relationship that holds for the other three determinants and not for diameter, which is exactly what makes a small change in caliber so consequential; chest wall tissue accounts for about 20% and lung tissue about 15% of resistance, so (C) inverts the ranking.

Q: A nurse notes subcostal retractions in an infant with bronchiolitis. What does this finding indicate?
A) A flattened diaphragm
B) Severe upper airway obstruction
C) Reduced chest wall compliance
D) Pleural effusion
Answer: A
Rationale: The chapter states that subcostal retraction "indicates a flattened diaphragm," and reserves retractions extending to the supraclavicular areas and suprasternal notch for severe airway obstruction (pp. 884–885).
Trap: (B) names the finding that belongs to a different retraction location, and the distinction matters because location grades severity; (C) reverses the developmental fact, since compliance is normally *high* in the newborn and infant "because of a more pliant (flexible) rib cage."

Q: A child is brought in for a barking, brassy, nonproductive cough. Which conditions does the chapter associate with this specific cough quality?
A) Croup and foreign body aspiration
B) Pertussis and measles
C) Pneumonia and cystic fibrosis
D) Asthma and bronchiolitis
Answer: A
Rationale: The chapter states that "A brassy, nonproductive cough is part of the symptomatology of croup and foreign body aspiration," while a paroxysmal cough with an inspiratory whoop is typical of pertussis and a severe cough accompanies measles and cystic fibrosis (p. 885).
Trap: (C) names the two conditions the chapter pairs with a severe cough rather than a brassy one; note also the chapter's caution that "Because there are no cough receptors in the alveoli, a cough may be absent in a child with pneumonia in the early stages."

Q: A nurse is reviewing the respiratory tract's own defenses with a student. Which does the chapter name? Select all that apply.
A) Lymphoid tissues, including the tonsils and adenoids
B) The mucous blanket and ciliary action
C) The epiglottis and the epiglottis reflex
D) Cough and position changes
E) Bronchial smooth muscle tone
Answer: A, B, C, D
Rationale: The chapter names eight defenses: lymphoid tissues, the mucous blanket, ciliary action, the epiglottis and its reflex, cough, position changes, lymphatics, and humoral defenses including immunoglobulin A secreted by the bronchial epithelium (pp. 886–887).
Trap: (E) is a real airway property that is not on the defense list, and the practical point of the list is what breaks it — the chapter names "Drying of the mucous membranes," which "inhibits the activity of humoral defenses, such as immunoglobulins," alongside malnutrition, fatigue, and crowding.

Topic: streptococcal pharyngitis and its sequelae

Q: A 12-year-old has a positive rapid streptococcal test and is prescribed oral penicillin. What should the nurse tell the family about why the full course matters?
A) Children with this infection are at increased risk for rheumatic fever and glomerulonephritis
B) Completing the course confers lasting immunity to future streptococcal infections
C) A follow-up throat culture is required after every course
D) Untreated infection leads to adult rheumatoid arthritis
Answer: A
Rationale: The chapter states that streptococcal pharyngitis "is not in itself a serious disease, but affected children are at risk for serious sequelae," naming acute rheumatic fever appearing "in an average of 18 days" and acute glomerulonephritis appearing "in about 10 days" (p. 893).
Trap: (B) and (D) are the distractors in the chapter's own review question, and both are simply false; note that the sequelae are not equivalent — penicillin prevents rheumatic fever but "does not prevent acute glomerulonephritis in susceptible children," though it may prevent spread of a nephrogenic strain to the family.

Q: A child with streptococcal pharyngitis started penicillin yesterday morning and feels better. When may the child return to school?
A) After a full 24-hour period of antibiotic therapy
B) After 48 hours of antibiotic therapy
C) After the 10-day course is complete
D) As soon as the fever has resolved
Answer: A
Rationale: The chapter states that "Children with streptococcal infection are noninfectious to others 24 hours after initiation of antibiotic therapy," and should not return to school or day care "until they have been taking antibiotics for a full 24-hour period" (p. 894).
Trap: (C) keeps a well child home for the whole course, and (D) uses a symptom rather than the treatment clock; the family should also discard and replace the toothbrush after 24 hours of antibiotics.

Q: A 2-year-old has a sore throat with nasal discharge, conjunctivitis, hoarseness, and a cough. How should the nurse interpret the likelihood of streptococcal infection?
A) Suspicion is low, because these symptoms are more suggestive of a viral cause and the child is under 3 years old
B) Suspicion is high, because sore throat plus fever is the classic presentation
C) Suspicion is high, because conjunctivitis accompanies streptococcal infection in young children
D) Suspicion cannot be estimated clinically and testing is mandatory in all cases
Answer: A
Rationale: Table 26.2 lists children under 3 years and symptoms more suggestive of a viral cause — nasal discharge, conjunctivitis, hoarseness, cough, diarrhea, mouth ulcers, stomatitis — as the situations in which suspicion is low; the chapter adds that "80% to 90% of all cases of acute pharyngitis are viral" (p. 893).
Trap: (B) uses features the table does list as reasons to check, but ignores the two that argue against it here; the chapter's own instruction is to suspect strep "in children over the age of 2 years who have pharyngitis even if no exudate is present."

Q: A child's rapid antigen test for group A streptococcus is negative, but the child has classic signs of the infection. What should the nurse anticipate?
A) A confirmatory throat culture, because rapid test kits have questionable sensitivity
B) No further testing, because a negative rapid test excludes the infection
C) Empiric antibiotics without further testing
D) Repeat rapid antigen testing in 24 hours
Answer: A
Rationale: The chapter states that rapid kits have "questionable sensitivity," so "a confirmatory throat culture is recommended in patients who have a negative test result with a rapid diagnostic test kit and classic signs of the infection are present" (p. 893).
Trap: (B) treats a test the chapter calls insensitive as conclusive; note the converse caution too — because some children are carriers, "a positive culture or antigen test is not always conclusive evidence of active disease."

Topic: tonsillectomy and adenoidectomy care

Q: A 5-year-old is 4 hours post-tonsillectomy. The nurse observes the sleeping child swallowing repeatedly. What should the nurse do?
A) Notify the surgeon immediately, because continuous swallowing is the most obvious early sign of bleeding
B) Offer the child a drink, since the swallowing indicates thirst
C) Document the finding and reassess in 1 hour
D) Reposition the child and continue routine observation
Answer: A
Rationale: The Nursing Alert states that "The most obvious early sign of bleeding is the child's continuous swallowing of the trickling blood. While the child is sleeping, note the frequency of swallowing. If continuous bleeding is suspected, notify the surgeon immediately" (p. 896).
Trap: (B) and (C) both wait, and the chapter names the other hemorrhage signs to underline why waiting is wrong — tachycardia, pallor, frequent throat clearing, vomiting bright red blood, and restlessness, with "Decreasing blood pressure is a much later sign of shock."

Q: A nurse is giving discharge instructions after a tonsillectomy and adenoidectomy. Which instructions are correct? Select all that apply.
A) Observe for continuous swallowing
B) Encourage sips of cool, clear liquids
C) Observe for restlessness or difficulty breathing
D) Administer an analgesic such as acetaminophen
E) Give codeine elixir for pain at regular intervals
Answer: A, B, C, D
Rationale: These four are the keyed set in the chapter's own review question, and each is supported by the post-operative section — continuous swallowing as the earliest bleeding sign, cool clear fluids, restlessness among the hemorrhage and obstruction signs, and regular analgesia for at least the first 24 to 48 hours (p. 896).
Trap: (E) is contraindicated by a Drug Alert — "Codeine is contraindicated in pediatric patients post tonsillectomy and adenoidectomy" — because ultrarapid metabolizers convert it to three to four times the expected morphine, which has caused hypopnea, apnea, and death; the other wrong option in the source's item is encouraging coughing.

Q: The parent of a child 6 days post-tonsillectomy calls because she sees a cream-colored membrane on the tonsillar bed. What should the nurse tell her?
A) This is an expected finding for 5 to 10 days after surgery
B) This indicates infection and the child should be seen today
C) This indicates bleeding and the child should go to the emergency department
D) This indicates the child has been drinking liquids that are too warm
Answer: A
Rationale: The chapter states that "A cream-colored membrane is often visible on the tonsillar bed postoperatively for 5 to 10 days postsurgery; reassure parents this is an expected finding" (p. 896).
Trap: (B) and (C) escalate a normal finding, which is what the explicit instruction to reassure parents exists to prevent; also expected are dark brown old blood in emesis and between the teeth, objectionable mouth odor, and slight ear pain with low-grade fever.

Q: A child 2 days post-tonsillectomy asks for a milkshake. What should the nurse advise?
A) Withhold milk products until clear fluids are retained, because they coat the mouth and throat and prompt throat clearing
B) Allow it, since cold dairy products soothe the surgical site
C) Allow it only if it is red or brown in color to mask any blood
D) Withhold all oral intake until the tenth postoperative day
Answer: A
Rationale: The chapter directs that milk, ice cream, or pudding is withheld "until clear fluids are retained because milk products coat the mouth and throat, causing the child to clear the throat, which may initiate bleeding" (p. 896).
Trap: (C) inverts a real rule — fluids with a red or brown color are *avoided*, precisely so fresh or old blood in emesis can be distinguished from the ingested liquid; citrus juice is also poorly tolerated, and soft foods begin on the first or second postoperative day.

Q: A child is 8 days post-tonsillectomy and vomits bright red blood. What does the chapter say about this timing?
A) Hemorrhage may occur up to 10 days after surgery as tissue sloughs during healing
B) Bleeding after 48 hours is always from a new source unrelated to the surgery
C) Bleeding this late indicates the child has an undiagnosed coagulopathy
D) Bleeding cannot occur after the first postoperative day
Answer: A
Rationale: The chapter states that "Hemorrhage may occur up to 10 days after surgery as a result of tissue sloughing from the healing process," and that most children resume normal activity within 1 to 2 weeks (p. 896).
Trap: (D) and (B) both misplace the risk window, which is the reason discharge teaching must extend past the first days; the chapter also directs medical evaluation for persistent severe earache, fever, or cough.

Topic: otitis media and otitis externa

Q: A 3-month-old is irritable, cries constantly, refuses to nurse after 2 to 3 minutes, has rhinitis, and a rectal temperature of 101.8 degrees Fahrenheit. What should the nurse suspect?
A) Acute otitis media
B) Otitis media with effusion
C) Acute otitis externa
D) Respiratory syncytial virus infection
Answer: A
Rationale: The chapter describes infants with acute otitis media as irritable, holding or pulling at their ears, with a temperature as high as 104 degrees Fahrenheit common and loss of appetite because "sucking or chewing aggravates the pain" (pp. 898–901).
Trap: (B) is excluded because in effusion "Severe pain or fever is usually absent... the child may not appear ill"; the brief feeding attempts are the specific cue here, since sucking worsens middle ear pain, and this is the chain the chapter's own review question tests.

Q: A nurse is using a pneumatic otoscope. Which finding indicates otitis media with effusion rather than acute otitis media?
A) An immobile or orange-colored tympanic membrane without acute signs of illness
B) Moderate to severe bulging of the tympanic membrane
C) Intense erythema of the tympanic membrane
D) Acute onset of ear drainage not due to otitis externa
Answer: A
Rationale: The chapter states that "An immobile tympanic membrane or an orange-colored membrane indicates OME," while acute otitis media is diagnosed with moderate to severe bulging, acute onset of drainage, mild bulging with pain of less than 48 hours, or intense erythema (pp. 900–901).
Trap: (B), (C), and (D) are all acute otitis media criteria; the consequence of the distinction is management, since "Antibiotics are not required for initial treatment of OME" while they are first-line for acute infection.

Q: A 3-year-old is diagnosed with acute otitis media. Which prescription should the nurse expect?
A) High-dose amoxicillin, 80 to 90 mg/kg/day divided twice daily, for 5 to 7 days
B) High-dose amoxicillin for 10 days
C) Antibiotic ear drops instilled three times daily
D) An oral decongestant with an antihistamine
Answer: A
Rationale: The chapter gives high-dose amoxicillin at 80 to 90 mg/kg/day divided twice daily "for 5 to 7 days with children 2 years and older, and 10 days with younger children, children with underlying medical conditions, craniofacial anomalies, tympanic membrane perforation or children with chronic otitis media" (p. 900).
Trap: (B) applies the younger-child duration to a 3-year-old, which is the age tier this item tests; (C) and (D) are both ruled out, since "Antibiotic ear drops have no value in treating AOM" and "The use of steroids, decongestants, and antihistamines to treat AOM is not recommended."

Q: A parent reports that her child's tympanostomy tube has fallen out 11 months after placement. What should the nurse advise?
A) This is normal, requires no immediate intervention, and the practitioner should be notified
B) This is an emergency requiring same-day evaluation
C) This indicates the tube was placed incorrectly and will need replacement today
D) This is normal and the practitioner does not need to be told
Answer: A
Rationale: The chapter states that "The tympanostomy tube is eventually pushed out of the eardrum usually 8 to 18 months after tube placement," which "is normal and requires no immediate intervention, although they should notify the practitioner" (p. 901).
Trap: (D) drops the notification half of a two-part instruction, and (B) escalates a normal event; note also that water precautions are inconsistent in the literature, with "no need for water precautions unless the child develops recurrent drainage after swimming."

Q: An 8-year-old who swims daily has ear pain that worsens sharply when the nurse presses on the tragus. What is the likely diagnosis, and which treatment caution applies?
A) Acute otitis externa, and the vinegar-and-alcohol prevention mixture must not be used if tympanostomy tubes are present
B) Acute otitis media, and antibiotic ear drops should be started
C) Acute otitis externa, and oral amoxicillin is first-line
D) Cholesteatoma, and surgical excision is required
Answer: A
Rationale: The chapter identifies pain "accentuated by manipulation of the pinna, especially pressure on the tragus" as the discriminating sign of otitis externa, most common in 5- to 14-year-olds and peaking in summer, and states that the 50:50 acetic acid and rubbing alcohol mixture "must not be used if tympanostomy tubes are present" (p. 902).
Trap: (B) is the confusion the chapter anticipates, since children with either condition "pull at the ear"; note also that polymyxin-B, neomycin, and gentamicin drops should not be used if tympanostomy tubes are present because of ototoxicity risk.

Topic: croup syndromes

Q: A 5-year-old developed a severe sore throat after going to bed, is now drooling with difficulty swallowing, has a temperature of 102.2 degrees Fahrenheit, clear breath sounds, no cough, and appears anxious and flushed. What is the most likely diagnosis?
A) Acute epiglottitis
B) Acute laryngotracheobronchitis
C) Acute spasmodic laryngitis
D) Bacterial tracheitis
Answer: A
Rationale: The chapter names three predictive observations for epiglottitis — "absence of spontaneous cough, presence of drooling, and agitation" — and states that "The key differences between LTB and epiglottitis are the absence of cough, the presence of dysphagia, and the high degree of toxicity in children with epiglottitis" (pp. 903–904).
Trap: (B) is excluded by the absent cough alone, since laryngotracheobronchitis is defined by a barking cough; the chapter's own mnemonic is that children with epiglottitis "usually look worse than they sound, in contrast to children with LTB, who sound worse than they look."

Q: A nurse suspects epiglottitis in a 4-year-old. Which actions are appropriate? Select all that apply.
A) Keep the child calm in a position of comfort with the parent present
B) Summon personnel with advanced airway management skills
C) Administer humidified oxygen by mask or blow-by to avoid further agitation
D) Postpone diagnostic tests and invasive procedures until an airway is established
E) Visualize the epiglottis with a tongue depressor to confirm the diagnosis
Answer: A, B, C, D
Rationale: The chapter directs that a lateral neck radiograph be accompanied by "Experienced personnel with advanced airway management skills," that oxygen be given by mask or blow-by "to avoid further agitation," and that "diagnostic tests and invasive procedures be postponed on the child with suspected epiglottitis until an airway has been established" (pp. 903–904).
Trap: (E) is forbidden by a Nursing Alert — nurses "should not attempt to visualize the epiglottis directly with a tongue depressor or take a throat culture," and inspection is done only when immediate intubation or emergency tracheotomy can follow; this is the single most harmful option in the chapter.

Q: A 2-year-old has had 2 days of rhinitis and a low-grade fever and tonight developed a barking, seal-like cough with inspiratory stridor that worsens when he cries. He has no stridor at rest. What management does the chapter direct?
A) Management at home, since mild croup without stridor at rest does not require hospital treatment
B) Immediate nebulized racemic epinephrine
C) Immediate intubation
D) Oral antibiotics for 10 days
Answer: A
Rationale: The chapter states that "Mild croup (no stridor at rest) is managed at home," with cool mist as a comfort measure, and reserves nebulized racemic epinephrine for moderate to severe cases (pp. 904–905).
Trap: (B) escalates a mild case and commits the child to 3 to 4 hours of observation afterward; note the chapter's practical observation that "a ride in the car with the window down may help relieve symptoms, and the child often improves on the way to the emergency department due to exposure to cool air."

Q: A child with moderate croup receives nebulized racemic epinephrine in the emergency department and improves within 30 minutes. What should happen next?
A) Observe the child for 3 to 4 hours for any visible signs of respiratory distress
B) Discharge the child once the stridor has resolved
C) Repeat the dose every 5 minutes until the cough stops
D) Admit the child for a minimum 48-hour observation
Answer: A
Rationale: The chapter states that "The patient who has received nebulized epinephrine for croup should be observed for 3 to 4 hours for any visible signs of respiratory distress," because although improvement appears within 30 minutes, "the effects were transient"; additional doses may be given every 20 to 30 minutes as needed (p. 905).
Trap: (B) discharges a child whose improvement the chapter specifically calls transient, which is why the observation period exists; (C) compresses the repeat interval by a factor of four or more.

Q: A child with croup receives a single dose of oral dexamethasone. When should the nurse expect improvement?
A) As early as 6 hours after administration, with continued improvement over 12 to 24 hours
B) Within 30 minutes
C) Within 2 hours
D) Not until the second day of therapy
Answer: A
Rationale: The chapter states that a single dose of oral dexamethasone is "considered standard treatment for this condition" and that "The onset of action is clinically detectable as early as 6 hours after administration, with continued improvement over a period of 12 to 24 hours" (p. 905).
Trap: (B) and (C) borrow the timing of nebulized epinephrine, whose peak effect is under 2 hours; the two drugs are given together precisely because their onsets differ, one immediate and transient, the other delayed and sustained.

Q: A 6-year-old with a preceding upper respiratory infection has a croupy cough, high fever, a toxic appearance, thick purulent tracheal secretions, no drooling, and has not responded to racemic epinephrine and corticosteroids. What is the most likely diagnosis?
A) Bacterial tracheitis
B) Acute epiglottitis
C) Acute laryngotracheobronchitis
D) Acute spasmodic laryngitis
Answer: A
Rationale: The chapter describes bacterial tracheitis at a mean age of 5 to 7 years with "a history of previous URI with croupy cough, stridor unaffected by position, toxicity, absence of drooling, respiratory distress, and high fever," adding that "A prominent manifestation is the production of thick, purulent tracheal secretions" and that manifestations resemble laryngotracheobronchitis "but are unresponsive to LTB therapy" (p. 906).
Trap: (B) shares the toxicity and high fever, and the discriminator is drooling — present in epiglottitis, absent here; a croup that fails racemic epinephrine and steroids is this diagnosis rather than a worse case of the first one.

Topic: bronchiolitis and respiratory syncytial virus

Q: An infant hospitalized with respiratory syncytial virus has an oxygen saturation of 92% on room air. What does the chapter direct?
A) Continuous pulse oximetry and supplemental oxygen are not necessary at saturations of 90% or higher
B) Start supplemental oxygen to keep saturation above 95%
C) Begin continuous oximetry and titrate oxygen to 98%
D) Start nebulized albuterol to improve oxygenation
Answer: A
Rationale: The chapter states that "Continuous pulse oximetry and supplemental oxygen are not necessary if oxygen saturations are 90% or higher," and lists oxygen saturation of 90% or higher among the Quality Patient Outcomes for bronchiolitis (p. 908).
Trap: (D) is among the interventions the chapter rules out, since "Bronchodilators are not recommended and are rarely beneficial"; routine chest percussion and drainage, routine viral testing, and routine radiographs are likewise not recommended.

Q: A 2-month-old with respiratory syncytial virus is improving on room air and is being discharged. Which instructions should the nurse give? Select all that apply.
A) Continue breastfeeding
B) Observe for labored breathing or apnea
C) Instill normal saline drops in both nares and suction thoroughly before feeding and before sleep
D) Keep the infant out of day care or nursery
E) Position the infant side-lying for sleep
Answer: A, B, C, D
Rationale: These four are the keyed set in the chapter's own review question, and the feeding teaching directs instilling saline drops and suctioning with a bulb syringe "before feedings and before bedtime," offering small fluid amounts, and encouraging breastfeeding mothers to pump (pp. 908–909).
Trap: (E) reverses safe sleep positioning; the other wrong option in the source's item is stopping breastfeeding in favor of an oral electrolyte solution. Parents should also be told the cough may persist for a few weeks.

Q: A nurse is planning isolation for an infant admitted with respiratory syncytial virus. What does the chapter direct?
A) Contact and Standard Precautions, with some institutions adding Droplet Precautions
B) Airborne Precautions in a negative-pressure room
C) Standard Precautions only, since the virus does not survive on surfaces
D) No precautions, since reinfection confers immunity
Answer: A
Rationale: The chapter states that "Contact and Standard Precautions are used with hospitalized patients and some institutions use Droplet precautions in addition," and notes the virus "can survive for several hours on countertops, gloves, paper tissues, and cloth, and for 30 minutes on skin" (pp. 907–908).
Trap: (B) overapplies airborne precautions, and the chapter rules it out directly — "There is no documentation of distant spread of RSV by small-particle aerosols (airborne transmission)"; transmission is by direct contact, "mainly as a result of inoculation from hand to eye, nose, or other mucous membranes."

Q: An infant born at 27 weeks of gestation is now 4 months old in November. What prophylaxis does the chapter identify?
A) Palivizumab, 15 mg/kg/dose every 30 days from November to March
B) Ribavirin by aerosol monthly
C) The respiratory syncytial virus vaccine at 2, 4, and 6 months
D) No prophylaxis, since the infant is past the neonatal period
Answer: A
Rationale: The chapter names infants born before 29 weeks of gestation among palivizumab candidates and gives the regimen as 15 mg/kg/dose every 30 days, November to March (p. 908).
Trap: (B) names the only approved specific therapy for hospitalized children, whose use "is controversial because of concerns about the high cost, aerosol route of administration, potential toxic effects among exposed health care personnel, and conflicting results of efficacy trials" — it is treatment, not prophylaxis. Note the Drug Alert that reconstituted lyophilized palivizumab must be given within 6 hours because it is preservative free.

Q: An infant with bronchiolitis has a respiratory rate of 74 breaths per minute, listlessness, and poor air exchange. How should the nurse interpret this?
A) These are signs of severe illness requiring immediate attention
B) These indicate the infant is entering the recovery phase
C) These are expected findings at the peak of illness and need no change in care
D) These indicate a bacterial superinfection and antibiotics should be started
Answer: A
Rationale: Box 26.6 stages the signs, listing "Tachypnea >70 breaths/min," listlessness, apneic spells, "Poor air exchange; poor breath sounds," and cyanosis under severe illness (p. 907).
Trap: (D) reaches for antibiotics, which "are not part of the treatment of RSV unless there is a coexisting bacterial infection such as OM or pneumonia"; note that bronchiolitis peaks on day 3 to 5, so severity at that point is not by itself reassuring.

Topic: childhood pneumonia

Q: A 7-year-old has had 2 weeks of malaise and a persistent cough, with patchy infiltrates on radiograph. Which organism is most likely, and what treatment does the chapter name?
A) *Mycoplasma pneumoniae*, treated with erythromycin, azithromycin, or clarithromycin
B) *Streptococcus pneumoniae*, treated with high-dose amoxicillin
C) Respiratory syncytial virus, treated symptomatically
D) *Staphylococcus aureus*, treated with vancomycin
Answer: A
Rationale: The chapter states that *Mycoplasma pneumoniae* "is the most common bacterial pathogen of community-acquired pneumonia in children 5 years of age or older," with an incubation of 2 to 3 weeks and a cough that may last several weeks, treated with erythromycin, azithromycin, and clarithromycin (p. 910).
Trap: (B) names the most common bacterial pathogen overall in both children and adults, which is what makes it the strongest distractor; the age of the child is the discriminator, and erythromycin is named as the drug of choice for older children and adolescents because of its activity against this organism.

Q: A 3-year-old with pneumonia develops abrupt worsening after a viral upper respiratory infection, appears ill with a high fever, and has lobar consolidation. How does this differ from viral pneumonia?
A) Bacterial pneumonia has abrupt onset following a viral infection, high fever, an ill appearance, and lobar consolidation, and is treated with antibiotics
B) Viral pneumonia produces lobar consolidation while bacterial pneumonia produces patchy infiltrates
C) Bacterial pneumonia occurs more frequently than viral pneumonia in children
D) The two cannot be distinguished clinically or radiographically
Answer: A
Rationale: The chapter describes bacterial pneumonia as having an onset that "is abrupt and generally follows a viral infection that disturbs the natural defense mechanisms of the upper respiratory tract," while viral pneumonia shows "diffuse or patchy infiltration with a peribronchial distribution" and is treated symptomatically (pp. 909–910).
Trap: (B) reverses the radiographic patterns, and (C) reverses the frequency, since viral pneumonias "occur more frequently than bacterial pneumonias and are seen in children of all age-groups."

Q: A child with pneumonia complains of pain on one side of the chest. Which position does the chapter recommend?
A) Lying on the affected side, which splints the chest and reduces pleural rubbing
B) Lying on the unaffected side, to allow the affected lung to expand
C) Supine and flat, to equalize pressure
D) Prone, to improve posterior lung drainage
Answer: A
Rationale: The chapter states that "Lying on the affected side ('good lung up') splints the chest on that side and reduces pleural rubbing that often causes discomfort" (p. 912).
Trap: (B) is the intuition that follows from wanting to ventilate the sick lung, and it is not what the chapter directs for this particular problem, which is pleuritic pain rather than oxygenation.

Q: A 4-year-old has had pneumonia three times in 18 months. What does the chapter direct?
A) Further evaluation for cystic fibrosis or an immunodeficiency disease
B) Prophylactic antibiotics through the winter season
C) Reassurance, since recurrent pneumonia is common at this age
D) Repeat pneumococcal vaccination
Answer: A
Rationale: The chapter states that "The infant or child with recurrent pneumonia should be further evaluated for CF or an immunodeficiency disease" (p. 911).
Trap: (C) normalizes a pattern the chapter treats as a diagnostic signal; note separately that "A history of recurrent intractable pneumonia is reason to consider an FB in an airway," so a retained foreign body belongs on the same differential.

Topic: tuberculosis screening and treatment in children

Q: A 3-year-old whose household contact has human immunodeficiency virus infection has a tuberculin skin test showing 6 mm of induration. How should this be interpreted?
A) Positive, because 5 mm or more is positive in children with immunosuppressive conditions or close contact with contagious tuberculosis
B) Negative, because the threshold for any child is 10 mm
C) Negative, because the threshold for a child under 4 years is 15 mm
D) Indeterminate, requiring repeat testing in 6 weeks
Answer: A
Rationale: Box 26.12 sets 5 mm or more as positive for children in close contact with known or suspected contagious tuberculosis, children suspected to have disease, and children receiving immunosuppressive therapy or with immunosuppressive conditions including human immunodeficiency virus infection (p. 915).
Trap: (C) applies the 15 mm threshold, which belongs only to children 4 years or older with no risk factors; the whole structure of the box is that the cutoff is set by risk, not by the number alone, and this is the classic misapplication.

Q: A nurse reads a tuberculin skin test at 48 hours and finds a 14-mm area of redness with 4 mm of palpable induration. How should this be recorded?
A) 4 mm of induration, because the transverse diameter of induration is measured rather than erythema
B) 14 mm, because the reaction includes the erythematous area
C) 18 mm, the sum of both measurements
D) The test is uninterpretable and must be repeated
Answer: A
Rationale: The chapter states that "The size of the transverse diameter of induration, not the erythema, is measured," and that the reaction is determined in 48 to 72 hours (p. 914).
Trap: (B) would convert a negative result in most risk categories into a positive one; the technique detail that supports correct reading is that injection should raise "A wheal 6 to 10 mm in diameter... between the layers of the skin," and the procedure is repeated if no wheal forms.

Q: A nurse is reviewing causes of a false-negative tuberculin skin test. Which does the chapter name? Select all that apply.
A) Recent live-virus vaccine administration
B) Corticosteroid or other immunosuppressive therapy
C) Severe malnutrition
D) Testing before sensitivity develops, as in a child younger than 2 years
E) Prior bacille Calmette-Guerin vaccination
Answer: A, B, C, D
Rationale: Box 26.13 lists intercurrent viral diseases, live-virus vaccines, corticosteroids and other immunosuppressive agents, immunodeficiency disease, severe malnutrition, malignancies, testing before sensitivity develops, outdated material, faulty technique, overwhelming infection, improper reading, and early infection (p. 915).
Trap: (E) causes a false *positive* rather than a false negative, which is why interferon-gamma release assays "are the preferred tests to perform on asymptomatic children over 5 years of age who have received the bacille Calmette-Guerin vaccine and have a borderline positive or negative TST."

Q: A child is diagnosed with latent tuberculosis infection. What treatment does the chapter describe?
A) Isoniazid daily for 9 months, or twice weekly by directly observed therapy for 9 months
B) Isoniazid, rifampin, and pyrazinamide for 6 months
C) Rifampin daily for 9 months
D) No treatment, since latent infection is not contagious
Answer: A
Rationale: The chapter gives latent infection treatment as "a daily dose of isoniazid (INH) for 9 months or alternatively twice a week with directly observed therapy (DOT) for 9 months," with rifampin for 4 months if the organism is isoniazid-resistant (p. 915).
Trap: (B) is a regimen for active pulmonary or extrapulmonary disease rather than latent infection; (C) attaches the isoniazid duration to rifampin, which is given for 4 months.

Q: A 7-year-old with asymptomatic tuberculosis has started therapy. What should the nurse tell the family about school?
A) The child can and should attend school once therapy has started and clinical symptoms have been reduced
B) The child must remain out of school for the full duration of therapy
C) The child may attend school but must wear a mask at all times
D) The child must be in an airborne isolation room until sputum cultures are negative
Answer: A
Rationale: The chapter states that "Asymptomatic children can lead an essentially unrestricted life. They can and should attend school (or day care) once they have started therapy and clinical symptoms have been reduced," and that most children with tuberculosis, especially under 10 years, are not contagious (pp. 916–917).
Trap: (D) applies the rule for children and adolescents with infectious pulmonary disease and acid-fast bacilli on sputum smear; note the one activity restriction the chapter does impose — older children are restricted from vigorous competitive games and contact sports during the active stage of primary disease.

Topic: foreign body aspiration and inhalation injury

Q: A 2-year-old suddenly cannot speak, becomes cyanotic, and collapses. What should the nurse do?
A) Act immediately with abdominal thrusts, since these three signs indicate true choking and the child can die within 4 minutes
B) Perform back blows and chest thrusts, which are correct at this age
C) Observe briefly, since half of children with foreign body aspiration are asymptomatic
D) Obtain a chest radiograph before intervening
Answer: A
Rationale: The Nursing Alert names the three signs of true choking — the child "(1) cannot speak, (2) becomes cyanotic, and (3) collapses" — and states that the child "can die within 4 minutes"; the chapter directs "abdominal thrusts for children over 1 year old and back blows and chest thrusts for children less than 1 year old" (pp. 918–919).
Trap: (B) applies the infant maneuver to a 2-year-old, which is the age-tiering error this pair invites; (C) misuses a true statistic about asymptomatic children to justify waiting in a child who is visibly obstructed.

Q: A toddler aspirated a peanut. Which bronchus is the most likely site of obstruction?
A) The right, because of its shorter length and straighter angle
B) The left, because of its greater diameter
C) Either equally, since the angles are symmetric in young children
D) Neither; most foreign bodies lodge in the trachea
Answer: A
Rationale: The chapter states that "The right bronchus, with its shorter length and straighter angle, is the usual site of bronchial obstruction," and that "Most inhaled FBs lodge in a mainstem or lobar bronchus" (p. 917).
Trap: (D) contradicts the stated distribution; note also that nuts, hot dogs, round candy, and peanuts "together make up more than 40% of all aspirated food items," and that the high fat content of potato chips and peanuts adds a risk of lipoid pneumonia.

Q: A 3-year-old has had three episodes of pneumonia in the same lobe over 4 months. What should the nurse consider?
A) A retained airway foreign body, since recurrent intractable pneumonia is reason to consider one
B) Asthma, since recurrent wheezing suggests reactive airway disease
C) Croup, since the episodes are recurrent
D) Nothing further, since a normal chest radiograph excludes a foreign body
Answer: A
Rationale: The chapter states that "A history of recurrent intractable pneumonia is reason to consider an FB in an airway," and notes that a foreign body obstruction "may be mistaken for croup or asthma" (pp. 917–918).
Trap: (D) relies on a test the chapter says is unreliable here — "Up to half of children with tracheal foreign bodies have a normal chest radiography study," and radiographs are "of limited value in localizing vegetable matter and some plastic items." Bronchoscopy is required for definitive diagnosis and removal.

Q: A child rescued from a house fire has an oxygen saturation of 96% by pulse oximetry. How should the nurse interpret this?
A) The reading is unreliable, because pulse oximetry is normal in carbon monoxide poisoning
B) Oxygenation is adequate and no further intervention is needed
C) The reading confirms that no inhalation injury occurred
D) The reading should be repeated on the other hand to confirm
Answer: A
Rationale: The chapter notes that pulse oximetry is normal in carbon monoxide poisoning, so the child "can be dying of tissue hypoxia with a reassuring SpO2," and that carbon monoxide has 230 times the affinity of oxygen for hemoglobin (p. 923).
Trap: (B) is the exact reasoning the trap is built to catch, and it is fatal here; treatment is 100% oxygen, which halves the carboxyhemoglobin level in 40 to 60 minutes, with hyperbaric oxygen considered above 25% in children.

Topic: asthma classification and monitoring

Q: A 7-year-old has daytime asthma symptoms once a week but wakes with symptoms five nights a month. How should the severity be classified?
A) Mild persistent, because severity is assigned by the single most severe feature present
B) Intermittent, because daytime symptoms occur less than twice a week
C) Moderate persistent, because nighttime symptoms are present at all
D) Severity cannot be assigned without spirometry
Answer: A
Rationale: The chapter states that "The presence of one clinical feature of severity is sufficient to place a patient in that category. An individual should be assigned to the most severe grade in which any feature occurs"; for ages 5 to 11, nighttime symptoms three or four times a month fall in the mild persistent step (pp. 929–930).
Trap: (B) classifies by the daytime feature alone and would under-treat this child; the stepwise logic is that "if control of asthma is not maintained at one level or step, pharmacologic therapy for the next step up should be considered."

Q: A child's peak expiratory flow reading is 45% of personal best. What does the chapter direct?
A) This is the red zone, and a short-acting bronchodilator should be administered
B) This is the yellow zone, and maintenance therapy may need to be increased
C) This is the red zone, and the practitioner should be called before any medication is given
D) This is the green zone, and the routine plan should continue
Answer: A
Rationale: The chapter defines the red zone as below 50% of personal best, signaling "a medical alert. Severe airway narrowing may be occurring. A short-acting bronchodilator should be administered," with the practitioner notified if the reading does not return to yellow or green (p. 933).
Trap: (C) reverses the order and delays the drug while the child obstructs; note that the zones are percentages of the child's own personal best established over a 2- to 3-week stable period, not of a predicted-for-age value.

Q: A parent asks how to record the daily peak flow reading. What should the nurse teach?
A) Repeat the routine three times, waiting at least 30 seconds between, and record the highest of the three
B) Repeat three times and record the average of the three
C) Record a single reading taken first thing in the morning
D) Repeat three times and record the lowest reading, which is the most conservative
Answer: A
Rationale: The Family-Centered Care box directs repeating the entire routine three times, waiting at least 30 seconds between each, and states "Record the highest of the three readings, not the average" (p. 933).
Trap: (B) is the intuitive statistical choice and is explicitly excluded; the same box directs using the same meter over time, because "different brands can give significantly different values," and notes that the meter "should not be used to diagnose asthma severity."

Q: A nurse is teaching a family to identify asthma triggers. Which does the chapter list? Select all that apply.
A) Cold air and changes in weather
B) Strong emotions such as fear, anger, laughing, and crying
C) Sulfite preservatives in food
D) Gastroesophageal reflux
E) High-fiber foods
Answer: A, B, C, D
Rationale: Box 26.16 lists outdoor and indoor allergens, irritants, occupational chemicals, exercise, cold air, changes in weather or temperature, environmental change, colds and infections, animals, medications, "Strong emotions - Fear, anger, laughing, crying," conditions including gastroesophageal reflux and tracheoesophageal fistula, food additives such as sulfite preservatives, and foods such as nuts and dairy (p. 930).
Trap: (E) is the non-member; note the qualifier that makes a good distractor elsewhere — "Although allergens play an important role in asthma, 20% to 40% of children with asthma have no evidence of allergic disease."

Q: A child with asthma wants to join a sports team. Which activity does the chapter identify as best tolerated, and why?
A) Swimming, because the air is fully saturated with moisture and because of the type of breathing required
B) Distance running, because endurance training strengthens respiratory muscles
C) Soccer, because intermittent activity limits airway cooling
D) No sport, because exercise should be avoided in asthma
Answer: A
Rationale: The chapter states that "Swimming is well tolerated by children with EIB because they are breathing air fully saturated with moisture and because of the type of breathing required in swimming," and that exercise-induced bronchospasm "occurs rarely in activities that require short bursts of energy... and more commonly in those that involve endurance exercise" (p. 937).
Trap: (D) contradicts the chapter directly — "Exercise is advantageous for children with asthma," and prophylactic treatment before exercise "usually permits full participation in strenuous exertion"; (B) and (C) both name endurance sports, which are the harder ones.

Topic: asthma drug therapy

Q: A parent asks whether the child's salmeterol inhaler can be used when wheezing starts. What should the nurse explain?
A) Long-acting beta2-agonists are never used to treat acute symptoms or exacerbations, and are never used as monotherapy
B) It may be used for acute symptoms if the short-acting inhaler is unavailable
C) It may be used for acute symptoms but only twice daily
D) It replaces the inhaled corticosteroid during an exacerbation
Answer: A
Rationale: A Drug Alert states that "Salmeterol and Foradil are never used to treat acute symptoms or exacerbations," and the chapter adds that these agents "must be added to antiinflammatory therapy and never as monotherapy" because they "can increase the risk of severely worsening asthma symptoms, potentially leading to hospitalizations and death" (pp. 935–937).
Trap: (B) makes an exception the chapter does not allow, and the consequence is that the child uses a slow drug for an emergency; these agents are also not used in patients younger than 4 years.

Q: A 7-year-old is starting an inhaled corticosteroid by metered-dose inhaler. Which teaching point is essential?
A) Always attach a spacer when an inhaled corticosteroid is given, to prevent yeast infections in the mouth
B) Inhale as rapidly and deeply as possible with each puff
C) Take two puffs back to back without waiting between them
D) Rinse the inhaler in water weekly to keep it clean
Answer: A
Rationale: The chapter states that "The MDI should always be attached to a spacer when an inhaled corticosteroid is administered to prevent yeast infections in the mouth," and names cough, dysphonia, and oral thrush as the side effects of inhaled agents (p. 935).
Trap: (B) is the technique for a dry powder inhaler, where the child "needs to inhale as quickly and as deeply as possible"; with a metered-dose inhaler the breath is slow, over 3 to 5 seconds, held 5 to 10 seconds. (D) would destroy the device, since "Placing dry powder inhalers or MDIs with hydrofluoroalkanes in water will destroy these inhalers."

Q: A nurse is sorting asthma medications for a teaching session. Which is a quick-relief rather than a long-term control medication?
A) A short-acting beta2-agonist such as albuterol
B) Cromolyn sodium
C) Montelukast
D) An inhaled corticosteroid such as fluticasone
Answer: A
Rationale: The chapter splits the drugs into long-term control — inhaled corticosteroids, cromolyn and nedocromil, long-acting beta2-agonists, methylxanthines, and leukotriene modifiers — and quick relief: short-acting beta2-agonists, anticholinergics, and systemic corticosteroids (p. 935).
Trap: (B) and (C) are the two most often misfiled, and the chapter rules both out explicitly — cromolyn "does not result in immediate relief of symptoms," and leukotriene modifiers "are not used to treat acute episodes."

Q: A child using an inhaled short-acting beta-agonist has needed it five times a day for the past week. What should the nurse advise?
A) Seek medical supervision, since these agents should not be taken more than three or four times daily for acute symptoms without it
B) Continue as needed, since these agents have no daily limit
C) Double the dose so fewer administrations are needed
D) Switch to the long-acting agent for daily use
Answer: A
Rationale: The chapter states that "Inhaled beta-adrenergic agents should not be taken more than three or four times daily for acute symptoms without medical supervision" (pp. 935–937).
Trap: (D) substitutes a drug that must never be used for acute symptoms; escalating rescue use is a severity signal that should move the child up a treatment step, which is the logic of the stepwise approach.

Q: A 14-year-old is starting omalizumab for moderate to severe persistent allergic asthma. What should the nurse include in the plan?
A) Observe the patient for 2 hours after each of the first three doses and 30 minutes thereafter, because of a black box warning for anaphylaxis
B) Expect symptom improvement within 48 hours
C) Discontinue the inhaled corticosteroid once the drug is started
D) Administer it only during acute exacerbations
Answer: A
Rationale: The chapter states that omalizumab carries a black box warning for anaphylaxis and directs observing the recipient "for 2 hours after the first three doses are administered and 30 minutes thereafter for subsequent doses," and that it is approved for children 12 years and older (p. 937).
Trap: (B) is far too fast, since "Efficacy of omalizumab is not immediate and takes 12 to 16 weeks"; the drug is indicated precisely for asthma "not controlled by inhaled corticosteroids," so it is added rather than substituted.

Topic: acute asthma exacerbation and status asthmaticus

Q: A child in severe respiratory distress from asthma had loud wheezing on arrival, and the nurse now hears almost no breath sounds while the respiratory rate has risen sharply. What does this indicate?
A) Impending respiratory failure and imminent asphyxia, requiring immediate intervention
B) Improvement, since the wheezing has resolved
C) A response to the bronchodilator that should be reassessed in 30 minutes
D) Pneumothorax, which should be confirmed by radiograph first
Answer: A
Rationale: A Nursing Alert states that "Shortness of breath with air movement in the chest restricted to the point of absent breath sounds accompanied by a sudden rise in respiratory rate is an ominous sign indicating respiratory failure and imminent asphyxia" (pp. 930–933).
Trap: (B) is the misreading the chapter names in advance — with severe obstruction "breath sounds and crackles may be inaudible... This represents a lack of air movement and may be misinterpreted as improvement by unknowing examiners."

Q: A child in status asthmaticus who has been agitated suddenly becomes quiet. How should the nurse interpret this?
A) The child may be seriously hypoxic and requires immediate intervention
B) The child is settling and should be allowed to rest
C) The sedating effect of the beta2-agonist is taking hold
D) The child is responding to the calm environment
Answer: A
Rationale: A Nursing Alert states that "the child who suddenly becomes agitated, or the agitated child who suddenly becomes quiet, may be seriously hypoxic and requires immediate intervention," and that the child "who sweats profusely, remains sitting upright, and refuses to lie down is in severe respiratory distress" (pp. 937–938).
Trap: (C) reverses the expected drug effect, since beta2-agonist and corticosteroid side effects to watch for are "tachycardia, restlessness, irritability, hyperactivity" rather than sedation.

Q: A child arrives in status asthmaticus. Which initial measures does the chapter direct? Select all that apply.
A) Humidified oxygen to maintain saturation greater than 90%
B) Three treatments of inhaled short-acting beta2-agonist spaced 20 to 30 minutes apart
C) A systemic corticosteroid to decrease the effects of inflammation
D) Continuous cardiorespiratory and pulse oximetry monitoring
E) Antibiotics, which are routinely given in status asthmaticus
Answer: A, B, C, D
Rationale: The chapter directs humidified oxygen to keep saturation above 90%, "Three treatments of beta2-agonists spaced 20 to 30 minutes apart... as initial therapy," a systemic corticosteroid, and continuous cardiorespiratory and pulse oximetry monitoring (pp. 937–938).
Trap: (E) is ruled out — antibiotics "should not be used to treat acute asthma attacks except when a bacterial infection resulting from another condition such as pneumonia or sinusitis is present"; other adjuncts the chapter names are ipratropium, heliox, magnesium sulfate, and ketamine.

Q: A family asks what most reduces the risk of a child dying from asthma. What does the chapter identify?
A) A written asthma action plan, reviewed at least every 6 months in moderate to severe disease
B) Continuous use of a long-acting beta2-agonist
C) Annual spirometry alone
D) Avoiding all physical activity
Answer: A
Rationale: The chapter states that a written asthma action plan "can significantly reduce the risk of asthma death," that all children should have one, and that it should be reviewed at least every 6 months in children with moderate to severe disease (pp. 938–939).
Trap: (D) contradicts the chapter's endorsement of exercise; the reason the action plan matters so much is that "Most asthma deaths in children occur in the home, school, or community before lifesaving medical care can be administered," with adolescents the most vulnerable group.

Topic: cystic fibrosis

Q: A 6-week-old has a sweat chloride of 72 mEq/L on two separate samples. How should the nurse interpret this?
A) Diagnostic of cystic fibrosis, since a concentration greater than 60 mEq/L is diagnostic
B) Normal, since values under 80 mEq/L are within range in infants
C) Suggestive but not diagnostic, since the infant is under 3 months
D) Uninterpretable, since a single value cannot be used
Answer: A
Rationale: The chapter states that "Normally sweat chloride content is less than 40 mEq/L, with a mean of 18 mEq/L. A chloride concentration greater than 60 mEq/L is diagnostic of CF; in infants younger than 3 months, a sweat chloride concentration greater than 40 mEq/L is highly suggestive of CF," and two separate samples are collected for reliability (p. 946).
Trap: (C) applies the *suggestive* threshold for young infants to a value that already exceeds the diagnostic one; the value here clears both bars, and two samples were obtained as the test requires.

Q: A newborn presents with abdominal distention, vomiting, failure to pass stools, and rapid dehydration. What should the nurse suspect?
A) Meconium ileus, the earliest postnatal manifestation of cystic fibrosis, occurring in 10% to 15% of affected newborns
B) Hirschsprung disease, which cannot be distinguished from this presentation
C) Pyloric stenosis, given the vomiting and dehydration
D) Necrotizing enterocolitis
Answer: A
Rationale: The chapter identifies meconium ileus as "the earliest postnatal manifestation," occurring in "10% to 15% of newborns with the disease," presenting with "abdominal distention, vomiting, failure to pass stools, and rapid development of dehydration with associated electrolyte imbalance" (p. 946).
Trap: (C) is excluded by the failure to pass stools and the newborn timing, since pyloric stenosis typically begins around 3 weeks; all 50 states now screen newborns using immunoreactive trypsinogen analysis on a dried blood spot.

Q: A nurse is teaching a family about pancreatic enzyme replacement. Which instructions are correct? Select all that apply.
A) Give enzymes with all meals and snacks, within 30 minutes of eating
B) Do not exceed 2500 units of lipase per kilogram per meal in a child over 12 months
C) Adjust the dose to achieve normal growth and one or two stools per day
D) Add extra enzymes when high-fat foods are eaten
E) Crush the enteric-coated beads to make them easier to swallow
Answer: A, B, C, D
Rationale: The chapter directs that enzymes be given with meals and snacks within 30 minutes of eating, caps replacement at 2500 units lipase/kg/meal over 12 months of age, titrates to normal growth and one or two stools per day, and encourages extra enzymes with high-fat foods (p. 949).
Trap: (E) is explicitly forbidden — "The enteric-coated beads should not be chewed or crushed because destroying the enteric coating can lead to inactivation of the enzymes and excoriation of oral mucosa"; capsules may instead be opened and sprinkled on a small amount of food.

Q: A nurse is scheduling a child's cystic fibrosis therapies for the morning. In what order should they occur?
A) Bronchodilator, then airway clearance therapy, then aerosolized antibiotic
B) Aerosolized antibiotic, then airway clearance therapy, then bronchodilator
C) Airway clearance therapy, then bronchodilator, then aerosolized antibiotic
D) All three simultaneously by nebulizer
Answer: A
Rationale: The chapter states that a bronchodilator "is administered before ACTs when the patient exhibits evidence of reactive airway disease or wheezing," and that aerosolized tobramycin "is usually administered by jet or ultrasonic nebulizers after ACT is performed" (pp. 947–948).
Trap: (B) and (C) each misplace one agent, and the logic is that the bronchodilator opens the airways before clearance while the antibiotic is deposited into airways that have already been cleared; airway clearance itself "should not be performed before or immediately after meals."

Q: An adolescent male with cystic fibrosis asks whether he will be able to have a normal sex life. What should the nurse explain?
A) More than 95% of males with the disease are sterile, but sexual function is normal and normal sexual relationships can be expected
B) Both fertility and sexual function are typically impaired
C) Fertility is normal but sexual function is often impaired
D) The question should be deferred to the physician
Answer: A
Rationale: The chapter states that "More than 95% of males with CF are sterile due to obliteration or atresia of the epididymis, vas deferens, and seminal vesicles," and that males "must be informed at some point that they will often be unable to produce offspring. It is important that the distinction be made between sterility and impotence. Normal sexual relationships can be expected" (pp. 945, 950).
Trap: (B) is the misunderstanding the chapter names explicitly and directs the nurse to correct; females may be able to bear children but should be informed of the respiratory burden of pregnancy, and their children will be carriers.

Q: A child with cystic fibrosis has a subtle drop in oxygen saturation along with tachypnea, tachycardia, and pallor. What should the nurse suspect?
A) Pneumothorax, since its signs are nonspecific and a subtle saturation drop may be an early sign
B) Pulmonary infection, since saturation falls with any exacerbation
C) Carbon dioxide retention from excess oxygen administration
D) Hemoptysis, which always precedes these findings
Answer: A
Rationale: A Nursing Alert states that "Signs of a pneumothorax are usually nonspecific and include tachypnea, tachycardia, dyspnea, pallor, and cyanosis. A subtle drop in oxygen saturation (measured by pulse oximetry) may be an early sign of pneumothorax" (p. 948).
Trap: (B) is the more common explanation for a saturation drop in this disease, which is what makes the alert necessary; note separately that oxygen "must be used cautiously because many children with CF have chronic carbon dioxide retention."

Topic: obstructive sleep apnea and respiratory failure

Q: A 5-year-old snores nightly, and the parents report breathing pauses and daytime irritability. The tonsils appear only mildly enlarged. What should the nurse explain?
A) Tonsil size does not correlate with the degree of obstructive sleep apnea, and polysomnography is needed to distinguish it from primary snoring
B) Mildly enlarged tonsils exclude obstructive sleep apnea
C) The history alone is sufficient to diagnose obstructive sleep apnea
D) Snoring in a 5-year-old is normal and requires no evaluation
Answer: A
Rationale: The chapter states that "Adenotonsillar hypertrophy is a common cause of OSA, but tonsil size does not correlate with the degree of OSA," and that an overnight sleep study "can distinguish between OSA and primary snoring" (pp. 952–953).
Trap: (C) diagnoses from a snoring history alone, which the chapter rules out; untreated complications include growth failure, cor pulmonale, hypertension, behavioral problems, attention-deficit/hyperactivity disorder, and death, so the evaluation matters.

Q: A monitor shows that an infant has no airflow but visible chest wall movement. Which type of apnea is this?
A) Obstructive
B) Central
C) Mixed
D) Periodic breathing
Answer: A
Rationale: The chapter defines the three types as central, "in which both airflow and chest wall movement are absent"; obstructive, "in which airflow is absent but chest wall motion is present"; and mixed, "in which both central and obstructive components are present" (p. 953).
Trap: (B) is the exact inverse and is the likeliest swap; the distinction matters because the two have entirely different causes and treatments, one neurologic and one mechanical.

Q: A nurse is assessing a child at risk for respiratory failure. Which does the chapter name as the cardinal signs? Select all that apply.
A) Restlessness
B) Tachypnea
C) Tachycardia
D) Diaphoresis
E) Cyanosis
Answer: A, B, C, D
Rationale: Box 26.17 lists restlessness, tachypnea, tachycardia, and diaphoresis as the cardinal signs, with mood changes, headache, hypertension, and increased cardiac and urinary output among the early but less obvious ones (p. 954).
Trap: (E) belongs to the more severe hypoxia group along with hypotension, depressed respirations, bradycardia, arrhythmias, somnolence, stupor, and coma — waiting for it means acting after the cardinal signs have been present for some time.

Q: A nurse is caring for a child with impending respiratory failure. Why does the chapter emphasize frequent assessment of fever and pain?
A) Because both, like cold stress, dramatically increase oxygen requirements and therefore respiratory effort, especially in younger children
B) Because both indicate an infectious cause requiring antibiotics
C) Because both are contraindications to supplemental oxygen
D) Because both must be documented before intubation can proceed
Answer: A
Rationale: The chapter states that "Because one goal of therapy is to control the body's oxygen demands, assessments of fever and pain should be frequent. Both conditions (as well as cold stress) can dramatically increase oxygen requirements, especially in younger children, and therefore increase respiratory effort" (p. 954).
Trap: (B) treats the findings as diagnostic clues rather than as metabolic loads, which is the framing this chapter uses; note that respiratory failure "is the most common cause of cardiopulmonary arrest in children," and the interventions that may avert an arrest are supplemental oxygen, opening the airway, positioning, stimulation, suctioning, and early intubation.
