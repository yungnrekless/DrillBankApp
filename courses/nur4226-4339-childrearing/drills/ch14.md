# Chapter 14 drills — Health Problems of Early Childhood

Authored from `sources/Ch14_Fact_Bank.md` (Hockenberry, Wilson & Rodgers, 11th ed.,
chapter author Brigit M. Carter, printed pp. 440–459).

Blueprint weight is **6 questions of 202 (3.0%), depth `standard`** — 43 drills
across 10 topics, on the same six-item footing as Chapter 13.

Unlike chapters 10, 12, and 13, this is **not a developmental chapter**. It is
three self-contained clinical blocks bolted to the early-childhood age range —
**sleep problems, ingestion of injurious agents including lead, and child
maltreatment** — and the topics here keep them separate because that is how they
are tested. Nothing in this chapter compares across stages, so the topic names
carry no age qualifier except where the age itself is the fact.

**Where the weight went.** The poisoning material is **protocol content**, which is
the easiest thing in the book to build items from: the emergency sequence, the two
different reasons emesis is contraindicated, the antidote pairings, the toxic
doses, and **Table 14.2's blood lead action ladder** — a table of thresholds and
actions, which is the most item-shaped structure in the chapter. Maltreatment is
**definitional and observational**, so it is drilled the way it is tested: "which
finding should the nurse recognize as suggestive of…"

**Drilled from both ends:** nightmares against sleep terrors (six discriminators,
and the nursing response inverts), corrosives against hydrocarbons (same
contraindication, different rationale, and items test the rationale), acetaminophen
stage 2 against iron stage 2 (both are false-recovery windows), acute against
chronic salicylate toxicity, elemental mercury swallowed against inhaled, and BAL
against DMSA on the two things that separate them — peanut allergy and iron.

**What twenty printed pages could not buy at 43 questions.** Left undrilled: Box
14.1's poisonous and nonpoisonous plant lists; Box 14.3's full lead source list and
the ethnic-remedy details; the twenty-item home lead-reduction checklist beyond the
wet-cleaning and cold-water rules; the MSBP four determining considerations beyond
the confirmatory one; the forensic chain-of-custody detail; the discharge and
foster-placement guidance; and the sexual-abuse prevention rehearsal scripts. The
GAP register flags **Box 14.1** as the first place to look if a drill turns on a
specific plant — no flagged-as-testable omission needed expanding before this
chapter was drilled.

Four questions are select-all, one for each list that genuinely sorts two ways:
activated charcoal's criteria against the drug it does not bind, the education owed
at a blood lead level of 5 or above against a discredited screen, Box 14.5's
warning signs against an ordinary accidental pattern, and the findings that mimic
abuse against one that is actually an abuse pattern. That is below the usual
one-in-seven ratio. The chapter is full of lists, but most of them — the four
stages of acetaminophen toxicity, the five of iron, the six types of sexual abuse,
Box 14.6's four parallel columns — are **sequences or taxonomies rather than
two-way sorts**, and a select-all built from those teaches recall of a list instead
of the discrimination that actually decides an action. Those are drilled as
single-answer scenarios instead.

Topic: preschool sleep problems, nightmares, and sleep terrors

Q: A 4-year-old wakes screaming about 90 minutes after falling asleep, sits up thrashing with eyes bulging and profuse sweating, does not appear to recognize her mother, and pushes her away when held. By morning she remembers nothing. Which problem is this?
A) A nightmare, occurring during REM sleep
B) A sleep terror, a partial arousal from stage IV non-REM sleep
C) A seizure requiring immediate evaluation
D) Trained night crying from an inappropriate sleep association
Answer: B
Rationale: Table 14.1 defines a sleep terror as "A partial arousal from very deep sleep (stage IV, non-REM) sleep," occurring "usually 1-4 hours after falling asleep, when non-REM sleep is deepest." The child "is not very aware" of another's presence, "is not comforted, and may push person away and scream and thrash more if held or restrained," and has "no memory of dream or of yelling or thrashing."
Trap: (A) is the reflex label for any frightening night waking. A nightmare happens in REM sleep in the second half of the night, the child wakes fully, remembers the dream, and — the discriminator that matters most at the bedside — "is aware of and reassured by another's presence."

Q: A 5-year-old wakes at 5 AM crying, tells his father about a frightening dream, clings to him, is visibly comforted by his presence, and then takes a long time to settle back to sleep. Which problem is this, and what is the correct response?
A) A sleep terror; observe without interfering until the child calms
B) A nightmare; accept the dream as a real fear, sit with the child, and offer comfort, assurance, and a sense of protection
C) A nightmare; return the child to bed immediately without discussion, to avoid reinforcing the waking
D) A sleep terror; wake the child fully to break the episode
Answer: B
Rationale: A nightmare is "A scary dream; takes place during REM sleep and is followed by full waking," occurring "in second half of night, when dreams are most intense," with distress "after dream is over." Return to sleep "may be considerably delayed because of persistent fear." The interventions are to "Accept dream as real fear" and "Sit with child; offer comfort, assurance, and sense of protection."
Trap: (A) is the mirror error of the previous item, and applying the sleep-terror response here would leave a fully awake, frightened child without the comfort that works. The chapter also directs avoiding "forcing child back to his or her own bed," which is what (C) does.

Q: During a sleep terror, a 4-year-old is thrashing in bed but is not near the edge and is in no danger of injury. What should the nurse instruct the parents to do?
A) Observe the child for a few minutes without interfering, until the child becomes calm or wakes fully
B) Wake the child fully as quickly as possible to end the episode
C) Hold and restrain the child firmly until the thrashing stops
D) Arrange professional counseling, since sleep terrors indicate an underlying disorder
Answer: A
Rationale: The intervention for sleep terrors is to "Observe child for a few minutes, without interfering, until child becomes calm or wakes fully," and to "Intervene only if necessary to protect child from injury," guiding the child back to bed if needed. Parents should be told that sleep terrors are "a normal, common phenomenon in preschoolers that requires relatively little intervention."
Trap: (C) is the instinctive parental response and it makes the episode worse — the child "may push person away and scream and thrash more if held or restrained." (D) misroutes the referral: professional counseling belongs to "recurrent nightmares unresponsive to" comfort measures, not to sleep terrors.

Q: Parents report that their 4-year-old stalls at bedtime with elaborate requests and comes out of the room repeatedly. Which guidance reflects the chapter?
A) Ignore attention-seeking behavior and do not take the child into the parents' bed or allow the child to stay up past a reasonable hour; a light in the room, a transitional object, and a drink of water by the bed are acceptable
B) Allow the child into the parents' bed on difficult nights, so that everyone sleeps
C) Remove all objects from the room, including night-lights, to eliminate stimulation
D) Extend the bedtime by an hour, since the resistance shows the child is not tired
Answer: A
Rationale: Parents "should ignore attention-seeking behavior, and the child should not be taken into the parents' bed or allowed to stay up past a reasonable hour." Helpful measures are "keeping a light on in the room, providing transitional objects such as a favorite toy, or leaving a drink of water by the bed," along with soothing limited rituals such as a bath or story, and consistently carrying through the routine.
Trap: (B) is the exhausted-parent answer and the one the chapter names as the thing not to do. Note also the media link: there is "a direct correlation between sleep problems in preschool children and evening media use, as well as daytime exposure to violent media content."

Topic: poisoning emergency management and gastric decontamination

Q: A parent telephones after finding their 2-year-old with an open bottle of pills. What should the nurse advise the parent to do first?
A) Call the poison control center before initiating any intervention
B) Induce vomiting immediately, before the pills are absorbed
C) Give milk to dilute the medication, then observe at home
D) Drive to the emergency department without calling anyone
Answer: A
Rationale: Parents are advised "to call the poison control center (PCC) before initiating any intervention," and to post the PCC number, 800-222-1222, near each phone. Based on that telephone assessment, the PCC "counsels the parents to begin treatment at home or to take the child to an emergency facility."
Trap: (B) is the obsolete reflex — the American Academy of Pediatrics "no longer recommends" syrup of ipecac for routine poison treatment. Read the setting carefully on items like this: for a parent at home the first action is to call the PCC, while for a nurse at the bedside the governing principle is different, as the next item shows.

Q: A 3-year-old arrives in the emergency department after a household poisoning, pale and lethargic with a weak pulse. What is the first and most important principle guiding the nurse's care?
A) Identify the poison before initiating any treatment, so the correct antidote is given
B) Treat the child first, not the poison, with immediate concern for life support and the CABs of resuscitation
C) Administer activated charcoal as soon as the child arrives
D) Perform gastric lavage before assessing vital signs
Answer: B
Rationale: "The first and most important principle in dealing with a poisoning is to treat the child first, not the poison." This "requires an immediate concern for life support," with vital signs taken, mental status assessed, and respiratory or circulatory support instituted. Because shock complicates several household poisons, "particularly corrosives," measures begin with circulation, airway, and breathing support — "the CABs of resuscitation."
Trap: (A) inverts the priority and is attractive because identifying the poison really is one of the four steps in the emergency sequence; it is not the first. The child is stabilized first, and evidence of the poison — "container, vomitus, urine" — is saved along the way.

Q: A nurse is preparing to give activated charcoal to a child who ingested a toxic substance 40 minutes ago. Which statements about activated charcoal are correct? Select all that apply.
A) It may be considered when the time to administration is within 1 hour after the ingestion
B) The child must have an intact or protected airway
C) It may be mixed with chocolate milk, fruit syrup, or a cola drink and served through a straw in an opaque covered container
D) It effectively absorbs iron, making it the treatment of choice for iron overdose
E) Complications include vomiting with potential aspiration, constipation, and intestinal obstruction with multiple doses
Answer: A, B, C, E
Rationale: Activated charcoal "is an odorless, tasteless, fine black powder that absorbs many compounds, creating a stable complex." It may be considered when the child may have ingested large amounts of carbamazepine, dapsone, phenobarbital, quinine, or theophylline; when administration is "within 1 hour after the poison ingestion"; and when the child "has an intact or protected airway." Acceptance is improved by mixing it with "chocolate milk, fruit syrup, or cola drinks" and serving it "through a straw in an opaque container with a cover."
Trap: (D) is the one option from the wrong side of the sort, and it is the most commonly swapped fact in this section: "Activated charcoal does not absorb iron." Iron overdose is managed with whole bowel irrigation if tablets are visible on x-ray, and with deferoxamine chelation.

Q: A parent asks whether they should keep syrup of ipecac in the medicine cabinet for emergencies. Which response is correct?
A) The American Academy of Pediatrics no longer recommends its use for routine treatment of poison ingestion and suggests disposing of bottles of syrup of ipecac from the home
B) Every home with young children should keep a bottle and use it for any ingestion
C) It should be kept but used only for corrosive ingestions
D) It should be kept for hydrocarbon ingestions, where emptying the stomach is critical
Answer: A
Rationale: The Nursing Alert states that "Syrup of ipecac is not recommended for routine poison treatment intervention in the home." It is an emetic acting "through irritation of the gastric mucosa and by stimulation of the vomiting center," but the AAP "no longer recommends its use for routine treatment of poison ingestion and suggests disposal of bottles of syrup of ipecac from the home."
Trap: (C) and (D) name the two ingestions in which inducing emesis is most specifically contraindicated — corrosives because vomiting further damages the mucosa, hydrocarbons because of aspiration risk. Both make ipecac worse than useless rather than selectively useful.

Q: A child has ingested an unknown quantity of a toxin. Which statement about gastric lavage reflects current practice?
A) There is no conclusive evidence that gastric lavage decreases morbidity, and it is no longer recommended to be performed routinely, if at all
B) Gastric lavage is the standard first-line intervention for all pediatric ingestions
C) Gastric lavage is most effective when performed more than 4 hours after ingestion
D) Gastric lavage requires no airway protection in a child who is alert
Answer: A
Rationale: Gastric lavage "can be associated with serious complications (gastrointestinal perforation, hypoxia, aspiration)." "There is no conclusive evidence that gastric lavage decreases morbidity, and it is no longer recommended to be performed routinely, if at all." It "may be of little benefit if used later than 1 hour after ingestion," and when used requires "a protected airway, possible sedation, and the largest diameter tube that can be inserted."
Trap: (C) inverts the timing window, which runs the same direction as activated charcoal's — sooner is better, and after an hour the benefit largely disappears. Gastrointestinal decontamination as a whole "is not routinely recommended for most childhood poisonings."

Topic: corrosive, hydrocarbon, and mercury exposure

Q: A 2-year-old has swallowed liquid drain cleaner. The mouth and lips are swollen and white, and the child is drooling and unable to clear secretions. Which action is contraindicated, and why?
A) Inducing emesis, because vomiting further damages the mucosa
B) Maintaining a patent airway, because manipulation increases edema
C) Administering analgesics, because they mask the extent of injury
D) Giving oral fluids once tolerated, because they dilute the gastric contents
Answer: A
Rationale: For corrosives — "strong acids or alkalis" — "Inducing emesis is contraindicated (vomiting further damages the mucosa)." Treatment maintains a patent airway as needed, administers analgesics, and gives oral fluids when tolerated. The swollen white mucous membranes and edema of the lips, tongue, and pharynx in this stem are the chapter's own signs of impending respiratory obstruction.
Trap: (B), (C), and (D) each name a treatment the chapter actually directs, which is what makes this item turn on knowing the one contraindication rather than on general caution. Note also that "Liquid corrosives are easily ingested and cause more damage than granular/solid preparations."

Q: A parent asks whether to give vinegar to neutralize an alkaline cleaner their child has swallowed. Which response is correct?
A) Do not neutralize; neutralization can cause an exothermic reaction, producing heat and adding a thermal burn to the chemical burn
B) Vinegar is appropriate for an alkali, and baking soda for an acid
C) Neutralization is safe provided the volume is kept under 120 ml
D) Neutralization should be attempted only after inducing emesis
Answer: A
Rationale: The chapter directs "Do not neutralize," because neutralization "can cause an exothermic reaction (which produces heat and causes increased symptoms or produces a thermal burn in addition to a chemical burn)." If the PCC or medical advice is not immediately available, "it may be appropriate to dilute corrosive with water or milk (usually ≤120 ml [4 oz])."
Trap: (C) attaches the correct volume figure to the wrong action — 120 ml is the ceiling for *dilution* with water or milk, not a safe dose of a neutralizing agent. (B) is the chemistry-class answer, and it is exactly the exothermic reaction the chapter warns about.

Q: A 3-year-old has swallowed lamp oil and is coughing and gagging. What is the immediate danger, and what does it mean for treatment?
A) Aspiration — even small amounts can cause bronchitis and chemical pneumonia — so inducing emesis is generally contraindicated, and if lavage must be performed a cuffed endotracheal tube should be in place first
B) Corrosive injury to the esophagus, so immediate dilution with milk is required
C) Systemic absorption, so emesis should be induced without delay
D) Hypoglycemia, so intravenous dextrose is the priority
Answer: A
Rationale: For hydrocarbons — gasoline, kerosene, lamp oil, mineral seal oil, lighter fluid, turpentine, paint thinner — the "Immediate danger is aspiration (even small amounts can cause bronchitis and chemical pneumonia)." "Inducing emesis is generally contraindicated," and "if gastric lavage must be performed, a cuffed endotracheal tube should be in place before lavage because of a high risk of aspiration." Symptomatic treatment of chemical pneumonia "includes high humidity, oxygen, hydration, and acetaminophen."
Trap: (B) applies the corrosive picture to a hydrocarbon. Emesis is contraindicated for both, but for opposite reasons — mucosal damage for corrosives, aspiration for hydrocarbons — and items on this pair test the rationale rather than the action.

Q: A child breaks a mercury thermometer and some of the liquid mercury is swallowed; more is spilled on the floor. Which statement is correct?
A) Elemental mercury is nontoxic if ingested and the gastrointestinal tract is healthy, but mercury is volatile at room temperature and enters the bloodstream after it is inhaled, so the spill should be cleaned up quickly with disposable towels and rubber gloves
B) The swallowed mercury is the emergency, and emesis should be induced at once
C) Mercury is harmless in both routes and requires no intervention
D) The spill may be safely vacuumed to disperse the droplets
Answer: A
Rationale: "Elemental mercury (also called metallic mercury or quicksilver) is nontoxic if ingested and if the gastrointestinal tract is healthy (e.g., has no fistulas). However, mercury is volatile at room temperature and enters the bloodstream after it is inhaled." The Nursing Alert directs: "clean up spilled mercury quickly, using disposable towels and rubber gloves and washing the hands well afterward." The classic form of mercury poisoning is acrodynia, meaning painful extremities.
Trap: (B) follows the intuition that swallowing a heavy metal must be the dangerous route, and this is one of the few places in the chapter where that intuition inverts. The hazard is the vapor, which is why the cleanup instruction — and not the ingestion — carries the urgency.

Topic: acetaminophen, salicylate, and iron poisoning

Q: A 3-year-old ingested a large quantity of acetaminophen 36 hours ago. The child vomited initially but now appears well, and the parents want to go home. How should the nurse interpret this?
A) The child has recovered, and discharge is appropriate
B) This is stage 2, in which the patient improves between 24 and 72 hours; treatment with N-acetylcysteine is still indicated
C) This is stage 3, and liver failure is already established
D) The improvement rules out a toxic ingestion
Answer: B
Rationale: Acetaminophen poisoning is "the most common accidental drug poisoning in children," with a toxic dose of "150 mg/kg or greater in children." Stage 1, from 0 to 24 hours, brings nausea, vomiting, sweating, and pallor; in stage 2, from 24 to 72 hours, "Patient improves," possibly with right upper quadrant pain; stage 3, at 72 to 96 hours, brings jaundice, confusion, coagulation abnormalities, and sometimes renal failure.
Trap: (A) is the answer the child's appearance invites, and it is why stage 2 exists as a named stage. The antidote is N-acetylcysteine, "equally effective given intravenously or orally," diluted in fruit juice or soda when oral "because of the antidote's offensive odor," and given as one loading dose followed by 17 additional doses.

Q: A 2-year-old ingested iron tablets 4 hours ago and has had no vomiting, diarrhea, or abdominal pain. The parents ask whether the child is out of danger. Which response is accurate?
A) If the child does not develop gastrointestinal symptoms in 6 hours, toxicity is unlikely — but the child is not yet at that point and requires continued observation
B) The absence of symptoms at 4 hours confirms that no significant amount was ingested
C) Iron toxicity produces no gastrointestinal symptoms at any stage
D) The child should be discharged and returned only if symptoms develop
Answer: A
Rationale: In stage 1 of iron poisoning, within 6 hours, the findings are vomiting, hematemesis, diarrhea, bloody stools, and abdominal pain; the chapter states that "if child does not develop gastrointestinal symptoms in 6 hours, toxicity is unlikely." Stage 2 is a "Latency period — up to 24 hours of apparent improvement," after which stage 3 brings metabolic acidosis, bleeding, seizures, and shock.
Trap: (B) applies the 6-hour rule two hours early, which is the error that sends a child home during the window in which symptoms still declare themselves. Note that iron has the same false-recovery trap as acetaminophen: apparent improvement in stage 2 is a stage, not a resolution.

Q: A child with severe iron poisoning is receiving intravenous deferoxamine. The urine turns red-orange, and shortly afterward the child develops facial flushing, urticaria, tachycardia, and hypotension. What should the nurse do?
A) Recognize both findings as expected and continue the infusion
B) Recognize the red-orange urine as expected, but stop the infusion, maintain the intravenous line with normal saline, and notify the practitioner immediately
C) Stop the infusion because of the urine color change, which indicates hemolysis
D) Slow the infusion and administer activated charcoal to bind the remaining iron
Answer: B
Rationale: Chelation therapy with deferoxamine "may turn urine red to orange," which is an expected effect. However, if intravenous deferoxamine is given too rapidly, "hypotension, facial flushing, rash, urticaria, tachycardia, and shock may occur; stop the infusion, maintain the IV line with normal saline, and notify the practitioner immediately."
Trap: (C) reacts to the harmless finding and misses the dangerous one, which is the point of pairing them in one stem. (D) proposes an agent that does not work here at all: "Activated charcoal does not absorb iron."

Q: A 4-year-old has been given adult aspirin by a grandparent for several days for a presumed viral illness, and now has subtle confusion and a nosebleed. Which statement reflects the chapter?
A) Chronic salicylate poisoning, more than 100 mg/kg/day for 2 or more days, has a subtle onset with nonspecific symptoms often mistaken for viral illness and can be more serious than acute ingestion
B) Chronic salicylate exposure is always less serious than a single large ingestion
C) Bleeding is not a feature of salicylate toxicity at any dose
D) The early findings of acute salicylate poisoning are bradycardia and hypoventilation
Answer: A
Rationale: Chronic salicylate toxicity is ">100 mg/kg/day for ≥2 days" and "can be more serious than acute ingestion," with the "same as listed above but subtle onset and nonspecific symptoms (often mistaken for viral illness)" plus bleeding tendencies. Acute early findings are nausea, hyperventilation, vomiting, and tinnitus.
Trap: (D) inverts the two most characteristic early acute findings — the child hyperventilates rather than hypoventilates, and tinnitus is the other early cue. (B) is the intuition this fact overturns, and it is why a history of several days of dosing matters more than the size of any single dose.

Q: A child with severe salicylate toxicity requires enhanced elimination. Which interventions does the chapter name?
A) Activated charcoal as soon as possible, sodium bicarbonate to correct metabolic acidosis with urinary alkalinization to enhance elimination, external cooling for hyperpyrexia, and hemodialysis rather than peritoneal dialysis in severe cases
B) Gastric lavage, which reliably removes aspirin concretions
C) Peritoneal dialysis, which is preferred over hemodialysis in children
D) Aspirin reversal with vitamin K alone, which corrects both the acidosis and the bleeding
Answer: A
Rationale: "Activated charcoal is given as soon as possible (unless contraindicated by altered mental status)." "Sodium bicarbonate transfusions are used to correct metabolic acidosis, and urinary alkalinization may be effective in enhancing elimination; hypokalemia may interfere with achieving urinary alkalinization." Use "external cooling for hyperpyrexia," vitamin K for bleeding, and "In severe cases, hemodialysis (not peritoneal dialysis) is used."
Trap: (B) is explicitly refuted: "Lavage will not remove concretions of ASA." (C) reverses the dialysis preference, which the chapter states in a parenthesis precisely because it is the swappable half.

Topic: lead exposure sources and pathophysiology

Q: A parent insists their toddler cannot have lead poisoning because he has never been seen eating paint chips. Which response is correct?
A) A child does not need to eat loose paint chips to be exposed; normal hand-to-mouth behavior, coupled with lead dust in the environment that has settled over decades, is the usual method of poisoning
B) Eating paint chips is the only meaningful route of childhood lead exposure
C) Lead exposure occurs only through inhalation during renovation
D) Lead poisoning cannot occur in a home built after 1950
Answer: A
Rationale: "A child does not need to eat loose paint chips to be exposed to the toxin; normal hand-to-mouth behavior, coupled with the presence of lead dust in the environment that has settled over decades, is the usual method of poisoning." In most cases of acute childhood lead poisoning "the source is nonintact lead-based paint in an older home or lead-contaminated bare soil in the yard." Ingestion "happens during normal day-to-day play and mouthing activities," while inhalation "usually occurs during renovation and remodeling."
Trap: (D) attaches the wrong date to the wrong rule: lead in paint was *decreased* in 1950 and *banned* in 1978, so a home built in 1960 can still carry lead paint. Those two dates are also the ones the personal risk questions use, which is where the swap does the most damage.

Q: A nurse is teaching about how lead behaves in a child's body. Which statement is accurate?
A) Lead ultimately settles in the bones and teeth, where it remains inert and in storage, making up approximately 75% to 90% of the body burden, and at the cellular level it competes with calcium
B) Lead is stored primarily in the liver and is cleared within weeks
C) Lead affects only the hematologic system
D) A child's developing nervous system is less vulnerable to lead than an adult's
Answer: A
Rationale: Lead "moves via an equilibration process between the blood, the soft tissues and organs, and the bones and teeth. Lead ultimately settles in the bones and teeth, where it remains inert and in storage. This makes up the largest portion of the body burden, approximately 75% to 90%." At the cellular level "it competes with molecules of calcium, interfering with the regulating action of calcium." Lead "can affect any part of the body, including the renal, hematologic, and neurologic systems."
Trap: (D) inverts the chapter's central concern: of most concern "is the developing brain and nervous system, which are more vulnerable than those of older children and adults." That vulnerability is the entire reason for screening children rather than adults.

Q: A 3-year-old with an elevated blood lead level is also found to be iron deficient. Why does this combination matter, and what does it mean for teaching?
A) Children who are iron deficient absorb lead more readily, and lead can interfere with iron binding onto the heme molecule, sometimes creating a picture of anemia even when the child is not iron deficient; teaching therefore includes adequate calcium and iron and regular meals
B) Iron deficiency protects against lead absorption, so supplementation should be withheld
C) The anemia proves the child is iron deficient, so lead can be excluded as a cause
D) Nutrition has no bearing on lead absorption
Answer: A
Rationale: "Children who are iron deficient absorb lead more readily than those with sufficient iron stores. Lead can interfere with the binding of iron onto the heme molecule. This sometimes creates a picture of anemia even though the child is not iron deficient." Education for a child with a blood lead level of 5 mcg/dl or above includes the "Importance of good nutrition in reducing the absorption and effects of lead," with "adequate intake of calcium and iron and importance of regular meals."
Trap: (C) reads the anemia backward. Lead can produce the appearance of iron-deficiency anemia in a child whose iron stores are adequate, so the blood count cannot be used to rule lead in or out — only a venous blood lead level can.

Q: A child's blood lead level has been elevated for months. The parents ask whether the damage can be undone once the lead is removed. Which response is accurate?
A) Although most of the pathophysiologic effects of lead are reversible, the most serious consequences are on the central nervous system, and low-dose exposure may also cause permanent neurologic deficits
B) All effects of lead reverse completely once the blood lead level normalizes
C) Only high-dose exposure carries any risk of permanent injury
D) Lead poisoning at this age produces no cognitive effects
Answer: A
Rationale: "Although most of the pathophysiologic effects of lead are reversible, the most serious consequences of both high and low lead exposure are the effects on the central nervous system." In lead encephalopathy "permanent brain damage can result in cognitive impairment, behavior changes, possible paralysis, and seizures." "However, low-dose exposure may also cause permanent neurologic deficits." Even mild and moderate poisoning can cause "aggression, hyperactivity, impulsivity, delinquency, disinterest, and withdrawal."
Trap: (C) is the reassuring half-truth most families hold, and the chapter refuses it in a single sentence. Long-term signs include "developmental delays, lowered intelligence quotient (IQ), reading skill deficits, visual-spatial problems, visual-motor problems, learning disabilities, and lower academic success."

Topic: lead screening and the blood lead level action ladder

Q: A healthy 18-month-old is at a well-child visit and has no symptoms. The parents ask why lead testing is being suggested. Which response is correct?
A) Children with lead poisoning rarely have symptoms even at levels requiring chelation therapy, and universal screening should be done at 1 and 2 years of age
B) Screening is indicated only for children with behavioral symptoms or pica
C) Screening begins at 5 years of age, when school entry is required
D) A fingerstick sample is diagnostic and no further testing is needed
Answer: A
Rationale: "Children with lead poisoning rarely have symptoms even at levels requiring chelation therapy." Universal screening "should be done at 1 and 2 years old. Any child between 3 and 6 years old who has not been previously screened should also be tested. All children with risk factors should be screened more often."
Trap: (B) waits for symptoms that usually never come, which is precisely the reasoning screening exists to override. (D) misstates the diagnostic standard: "A diagnosis of lead poisoning is based only on the lead testing of a venous blood specimen from a venipuncture," collected carefully "to avoid contamination by lead on the skin."

Q: A family cannot be screened under a universal program, so targeted screening is used. Which questions does the chapter give as the personal risk questions?
A) Does the child live in or regularly visit a house built before 1950; a house built before 1978 with recent or ongoing renovations within the past 6 months; or have a sibling or playmate who has or had lead poisoning
B) Does the child live in a house built before 1978; a house with well water; or attend day care
C) Does the child have pica; a family history of anemia; or a sibling in school
D) Does the child live in a house built before 1920; a house painted within the past year; or have a pet
Answer: A
Rationale: Children should be screened if their family cannot answer "no" to three questions: "Does your child live in or regularly visit a house that was built before 1950?"; "Does your child live in or regularly visit a house built before 1978 with recent or ongoing renovations or remodeling within the past 6 months?"; and "Does your child have a sibling or playmate who has or had lead poisoning?"
Trap: (B) collapses the two dates into one and drops the renovation qualifier, which is what makes the second question different from the first. The 1950 question asks about age of housing alone; the 1978 question asks about age plus recent disturbance of the paint.

Q: A 2-year-old's venous blood lead level returns at 52 mcg/dl. Which actions does Table 14.2 direct?
A) Provide lead education, refer to a clinical center specializing in lead poisoning, provide diagnostic testing within 24 to 48 hours, perform clinical evaluation and management within 48 hours, provide appropriate chelation therapy, and ensure aggressive environmental intervention
B) Provide education and rescreen in 1 year
C) Provide education and retest within 1 month, with no chelation at this level
D) Withhold chelation until the child develops symptoms
Answer: A
Rationale: For a blood lead level of 45 to 69 mcg/dl, Table 14.2 directs providing lead education, referral to a clinical center specializing in lead poisoning with coordination of care, "diagnostic testing within 24-48 hours," clinical evaluation and management "within 48 hours," "appropriate chelation therapy," "aggressive environmental intervention," and follow-up testing "at least once per month."
Trap: (D) is the error the chapter blocks twice over: children with lead poisoning "rarely have symptoms even at levels requiring chelation therapy," and a level of 70 or above requires immediate testing and chelation "whether symptoms are present or not." (B) is the action for a level below 5.

Q: A 3-year-old's blood lead level is 8 mcg/dl. Which elements belong in the education and follow-up for this child? Select all that apply.
A) Explanation of the child's blood lead level and what it means, and the potential adverse health effects of an elevated level
B) The importance of wet cleaning to remove lead dust from floors, windowsills, and other surfaces
C) The hazards of improper removal of lead paint, such as dry sanding, scraping, or open-flame burning
D) Follow-up testing within 1 month, and then every 3 to 4 months
E) Erythrocyte protoporphyrin testing as the preferred screening method going forward
Answer: A, B, C, D
Rationale: Education for every child with a blood lead level of 5 mcg/dl or above covers the level and its meaning, potential adverse health effects, sources of exposure and how to reduce it "such as the importance of wet cleaning," the importance of good nutrition, the need for follow-up testing, results of any environmental investigation, and "Hazards of improper removal of lead paint (dry sanding, scraping, or open-flame burning)." For a level of 5 to 14, Table 14.2 directs follow-up testing "within 1 month, and then every 3-4 months."
Trap: (E) is the one option from the wrong side of the sort. Erythrocyte protoporphyrin "is no longer used as a screening test" because it "is not sensitive to BLLs of less than about 16 to 25 mcg/dl" — though a level above 35 mcg/dl still indicates toxicity and reflects duration of exposure and body burden.

Topic: lead chelation therapy

Q: Parents of a child beginning chelation therapy ask whether it will undo the effects of the lead. Which response is accurate?
A) Chelation removes lead from circulating blood and some from organs and tissues, but it does not counteract any effects of the lead, and it is unclear whether it affects lead stores in bones
B) Chelation reverses the neurologic effects of lead exposure
C) A single course of chelation removes the entire body burden of lead
D) Chelation eliminates the need for environmental remediation
Answer: A
Rationale: Chelation is "the term used for removing lead from circulating blood and, theoretically, some lead from organs and tissues. It is unclear whether chelation affects lead stores in bones." Although it resembles an antidote in purpose, "chelation does not counteract any effects of the lead." Because of the equilibration process "there is often a rebound of the BLL after chelation," and "Multiple chelation treatments may be necessary."
Trap: (C) ignores the rebound the chapter describes: the level rises again as lead redistributes from tissue stores, and rebound ceases only once the body burden has fallen enough to stabilize the blood level. (D) is contradicted by the discharge requirement for "confirmation that the child will be discharged to a home without lead hazards."

Q: A child with a peanut allergy and a blood lead level of 58 mcg/dl, without symptoms, requires chelation. Which agent consideration is correct?
A) British antilewisite is contraindicated in children with peanut allergies or hepatic insufficiency and must not be given with iron; succimer is oral, is appropriate for levels of 45 to 69 mcg/dl in the absence of symptoms, and can be used in conjunction with iron
B) British antilewisite is the agent of choice here, since peanut allergy is not a contraindication
C) Succimer must never be given with iron
D) Calcium disodium edetate is contraindicated in all children with any allergy
Answer: A
Rationale: British antilewisite "is contraindicated in children with peanut allergies or hepatic insufficiency, nor should it be given in conjunction with iron"; it is given "only at a deep intramuscular site" and is used with calcium disodium edetate for high lead levels or lead encephalopathy. Succimer is oral, for "lead levels of 45 to 69 mcg/dl and an absence of symptoms," and "DMSA can be used in conjunction with iron."
Trap: (C) inverts the iron rule between the two agents, which is the single most swappable fact in this section — it is British antilewisite that must not be combined with iron, and succimer that may be. Both facts matter because iron supplementation is often indicated in the same child.

Q: A child is receiving intramuscular calcium disodium edetate. Which nursing actions are required?
A) Ensure adequate urinary output and adequate oral fluid intake, rotate injection sites to prevent painful fibrotic areas, and use a topical anesthetic cream or injected procaine to reduce injection pain
B) Restrict fluids to reduce renal workload during chelation
C) Give the drug subcutaneously to reduce pain
D) Substitute disodium edetate if calcium disodium edetate is unavailable
Answer: A
Rationale: "Adequate urinary output must be ensured with administration of calcium EDTA. Children receiving the drug intramuscularly must be able to maintain adequate oral intake of fluids." Chelating agents "are administered deeply into a large muscle mass," and "Rotation of sites is essential to prevent the formation of painful areas of fibrotic tissue." For pain, "the local anesthetic procaine is injected with the drug," and topical EMLA or LMX4 may be applied before injection.
Trap: (B) inverts a requirement that exists because "the chelates are excreted via the kidneys," so hydration is essential rather than restricted. (D) names a substitution the chapter flags as lethal: "Incidences of child death from hypocalcemia have been recorded when Na₂EDTA was substituted for CaNa₂EDTA."

Topic: child maltreatment types, scope, and predisposing factors

Q: A nurse is reviewing national child maltreatment data. Which statement is accurate?
A) Neglect is the most common form of maltreatment, accounting for 75.3% of confirmed cases, and 74.8% of reported neglect cases involve children 3 years old or younger
B) Physical abuse is the most common form of confirmed maltreatment
C) Sexual abuse accounts for the majority of confirmed cases
D) Reported statistics closely represent the true incidence of maltreatment
Answer: A
Rationale: Of 683,000 children confirmed as victims in 2015, 75.3% involved neglect, 17.2% physical abuse, 8.4% sexual abuse, and 6.9% psychologic maltreatment or emotional abuse. "Child neglect is the most common form of maltreatment, and 74.8% of reported neglect cases involve children 3 years old or younger." Of children who died, "72.9% suffered from neglect either exclusively or in combination with another type of maltreatment."
Trap: (B) is the reflex answer because physical abuse is the most visible form and the one that generates images. (D) is contradicted directly: "Reported statistics only partially represent the actual incidence of child maltreatment because many cases are believed to go unreported."

Q: A nurse is asked which children are at highest risk of physical abuse. Which statement reflects the chapter?
A) Children from birth to 1 year old are at highest risk, and the onus for child abuse is always on the abuser
B) Preschoolers are at highest risk, because of negativism and testing behavior
C) School-age children are at highest risk, because of increased independence
D) Risk is evenly distributed across childhood
Answer: A
Rationale: "Children from birth to 1 year old are at highest risk for being abused." Infants and small children "require constant attention and must have all their needs met by others," which can produce "parental or caregiver fatigue that results in striking out at the child." The chapter states plainly that "The onus for child abuse is always on the abuser."
Trap: (B) is the intuitive answer in a chapter titled Health Problems of Early Childhood, and the age band it names is the wrong one. Note that premature infants are separately at risk "because of failure of parent-child bonding during early infancy, increased physical needs, or irritability."

Q: A nurse suspects maltreatment in a family that is affluent and well educated. Which statement reflects the chapter?
A) Child maltreatment occurs across all socioeconomic, religious, cultural, racial, and ethnic groups; abuse in lower socioeconomic groups is more likely to be reported, but concealed crises may also be present in upper-class families
B) Maltreatment essentially does not occur in higher-income families
C) Reporting thresholds should be higher for educated families
D) A single predictive factor identifies families in which abuse will occur
Answer: A
Rationale: "Child maltreatment occurs across all socioeconomic, religious, cultural, racial, and ethnic groups." Although most reporting has come from lower socioeconomic populations, "child abuse is not a problem of any one societal group… abuse in these groups is more likely to be reported. However, concealed crises may also be present in upper-class families."
Trap: (D) states what the chapter denies about all three risk categories: "no single factor or group of factors is predictive of abuse. Rather, the interaction of these factors is thought to increase the risk," and predisposing characteristics "are never predictive of actual abuse."

Q: In an abusive family, one child appears to be singled out. A colleague suggests removing only that child. Which consideration does the chapter raise?
A) Removing the singled-out child often places the other siblings at risk for abuse, so no child is safe if left in the abusive environment unless the parents can learn new parenting skills
B) Removing the singled-out child reliably protects the remaining siblings
C) Siblings are never at risk once the scapegoated child is removed
D) Removal decisions are made by the nurse rather than the court
Answer: A
Rationale: "One child may be singled out in an abusive family. Removing that child from the home often places the other siblings at risk for abuse. Therefore no child is safe if left in the abusive environment unless the parents can be helped to learn new parenting skills."
Trap: (B) treats the scapegoating as fixed to one child rather than as a pattern in the household, which is the assumption this passage exists to correct. (D) misassigns authority: referrals go to Child Protective Services, and "A court proceeding may be necessary before the child can be placed outside the home."

Q: A mother repeatedly brings her toddler in with seizures that no clinician has witnessed, has some medical training, and requests extensive testing. The child improves during a hospital stay when the mother is absent. Which condition should the nurse suspect, and what confirms it?
A) Munchausen syndrome by proxy; the resolution of symptoms after separation from the perpetrator confirms the diagnosis
B) Abusive head trauma; retinal hemorrhage confirms the diagnosis
C) Physical neglect; growth failure confirms the diagnosis
D) A seizure disorder that is simply difficult to capture on monitoring
Answer: A
Rationale: Munchausen syndrome by proxy is "a rare but serious form of child abuse in which caregivers deliberately exaggerate or fabricate histories and symptoms or induce symptoms," for "the gratification of the caregiver." "In most cases, the perpetrator is the biologic mother with some degree of health care knowledge and training." Commonly presented symptoms include seizures, vomiting, diarrhea, and altered mental status, "usually witnessed only by the perpetrator," and "The resolution of symptoms after separation from the perpetrator confirms the diagnosis."
Trap: (D) is where this diagnosis usually hides, and the chapter notes that "Health care providers can become easily misled and unknowingly enable the perpetrator" — the child then "endures painful and unnecessary medical testing and procedures."

Topic: recognizing physical abuse and abusive head trauma

Q: A 4-month-old is brought in with vomiting, irritability, poor feeding, and listlessness. There is no external injury. Imaging shows subdural and subarachnoid hemorrhage, and the ophthalmology examination shows bilateral retinal hemorrhages. Which condition does this describe?
A) Abusive head trauma, in which most often there are no signs of external injury
B) Gastroenteritis with dehydration
C) A bleeding disorder, which this presentation confirms
D) Accidental head injury, since no external marks are present
Answer: A
Rationale: Abusive head trauma is "a serious form of physical abuse caused by violent shaking of infants and young children." Characteristic injuries are "intracranial bleeding (subdural and subarachnoid hematomas) and, in approximately 80% of cases, bilateral retinal hemorrhages, which are classic results of repetitive acceleration-deceleration head trauma," sometimes with rib and long-bone fractures. "Most often, there are no signs of external injury, making diagnosis difficult," and presenting symptoms such as "vomiting, irritability, poor feeding, and listlessness, are often mistaken for common infant and childhood ailments."
Trap: (B) is the diagnosis this presentation is most often given, which is exactly why the chapter lists those four symptoms. (D) treats absence of external injury as reassurance when the chapter names it as characteristic of the mechanism.

Q: A nurse is teaching new parents about preventing abusive head trauma. Which teaching best targets the mechanism the chapter names?
A) Coping mechanisms for caring for a child with inconsolable crying, since abusive head trauma most often results from the caregiver's frustration with crying
B) Instruction to never shake a baby, without further content
C) Information on choosing a safe crib
D) Guidance on infant sleep position
Answer: A
Rationale: Abusive head trauma is "most often a result of the caregiver's frustration with crying, maternal stress, or depression." The Nursing Alert directs: "Stress to parents the danger of shaking infants (shaking can cause AHT). Education must include coping mechanisms on caring for children with inconsolable crying." An estimated 1200 to 1400 children are shaken each year, "and of these victims, 25% to 30% die as a result of their injuries. The rest have lifelong complications."
Trap: (B) states the prohibition without addressing what produces the impulse, and it is the incomplete half of the teaching. Parents who already know not to shake a baby still need a plan for the hour in which the crying will not stop.

Q: A nurse evaluates a child's injuries. Which findings are among Box 14.5's warning signs of abuse? Select all that apply.
A) The history is incompatible with the pattern or degree of injury
B) Bruising or other injury is present in a nonmobile patient
C) The patient has multiple fractures of differing ages
D) A 4-year-old has a single bruise over the shin after a fall from a bicycle, and the account is consistent
E) There was a delay in seeking care
Answer: A, B, C, E
Rationale: Box 14.5 lists a history "incompatible with the pattern or degree of injury"; a vague explanation or reluctance to provide information; contradictory histories among caregivers; a mechanism "not possible given age or developmental level"; "Bruising or other injury… in a nonmobile patient"; "multiple fractures of differing ages"; "a delay in seeking care"; an inappropriate affect; and disappearance of the accompanying adult.
Trap: (D) is the one option from the wrong side of the sort — a mobile child with a single injury over a bony prominence and a consistent history is the ordinary accidental pattern. The chapter is explicit that "No one indicator can be used to diagnose maltreatment. It is a pattern or combination of indicators."

Q: A nurse is reviewing findings that can be mistaken for abuse. Which are named in the chapter as mimics that must be ruled out? Select all that apply.
A) Cupping and coin rubbing
B) Mongolian spots
C) Osteogenesis imperfecta and bleeding disorders
D) Spiral fracture from a twist-and-fall injury
E) Burns with sharply demarcated margins and no splash marks
Answer: A, B, C, D
Rationale: The chapter lists medical conditions such as "bleeding disorders, osteogenesis imperfecta, or sudden infant death syndrome"; cultural practices such as "cupping or coin rubbing"; unintentional injuries such as "burns from metal buckles on car seats, bruising from seat belts, or spiral fractures from a twist-and-fall injury"; and normal variants such as "mongolian spots and congenital anomalies of genitalia."
Trap: (E) is the one option from the wrong side of the sort, and it belongs to Box 14.6's abuse findings rather than to the mimics: "sharply demarcated areas from immersion in scalding water" with "absence of 'splash' marks and presence of symmetric burns" is the immersion-burn pattern, which is the opposite of an accidental scald.

Q: A hospitalized 3-year-old with unexplained injuries is unusually clingy toward the parent suspected of abuse, does not cry during a painful dressing change, and is indiscriminately friendly with staff. How should the nurse interpret this?
A) These are recognized responses in maltreated children; a child may be overly attached to the abusive parent, may show inappropriate reaction to injury such as failure to cry from pain, and may be indiscriminately friendly
B) The child's attachment to the parent makes abuse unlikely
C) The absence of crying indicates the injuries are not painful
D) Indiscriminate friendliness indicates the child is well adjusted
Answer: A
Rationale: Among children's responses, "no one pattern is typical," but "extremes of behavior may be observed." Children "may be overly attached to the abusive parent, possibly in the hope of preventing any upset that may precipitate anger and another attack." Box 14.6 lists "inappropriate reaction to injury, such as failure to cry from pain," "lack of reaction to frightening events," and "indiscriminate friendliness and displays of affection" among suggestive behaviors.
Trap: (B) is the reassuring inference an observer naturally draws from a child who seeks the parent out, and the chapter inverts it. The same pattern appeared in Chapter 12 as a concern rather than a comfort, and in Chapter 11 as disinhibited social engagement disorder.

Topic: sexual abuse recognition and the nursing response

Q: A 6-year-old begins to disclose sexual abuse and asks the nurse to promise not to tell anyone. What is the correct response?
A) Do not promise not to tell; tell the child that you are required by law to report the abuse
B) Promise confidentiality, so the child feels safe enough to continue
C) Promise not to tell the family, but explain that other staff will be informed
D) Stop the conversation until a parent can be present
Answer: A
Rationale: The Nursing Care Guidelines direct: "Do not promise not to tell; tell them that you are required by law to report the abuse." The guidance also directs providing a private time and place, not expressing shock or criticizing the family, using the child's own vocabulary for body parts, avoiding leading statements, reassuring the child that telling was the right thing and that the abuse is not their fault, determining immediate need for safety, and explaining what will happen when the nurse reports.
Trap: (B) is the humane instinct and it makes a promise that cannot be kept, which risks the child experiencing the mandated report as a betrayal. (D) may place the child with the person who is the source of the danger.

Q: A 7-year-old is examined after a disclosure of sexual abuse, and the genital examination is entirely normal. What does this finding mean?
A) In most cases the genital examination findings are normal, which does not mean that sexual abuse did not occur
B) A normal examination rules out sexual abuse
C) A normal examination indicates the abuse occurred more than a year ago
D) A normal examination means forensic evidence collection is unnecessary
Answer: A
Rationale: "In most cases, the genital examination findings are normal, which does not mean that sexual abuse did not occur." "Fondling or genital-to-genital contact without penetration may leave no physical findings," and "Genital tissue heals very quickly and most often without scars." The chapter also names two myths directly: "the size of the hymeneal opening is not predictive of the likelihood of sexual abuse," and because the mucosa is elastic, penetration without tissue disruption is possible, which "defies another myth that there is always evidence of female virginity."
Trap: (B) is the inference the examination invites and the reason the chapter states the negative so plainly. Forensic timing matters separately: evidence from the body "diminishes greatly after 24 hours," with "the best chance for evidence collection coming from bed linens or the child's underwear," and contact within 72 to 96 hours should prompt consideration of forensic testing.

Q: Which statements about sexual abuse definitions and offenders reflect the chapter?
A) Incest requires no blood relationship and includes stepparents and unrelated siblings, while pedophilia denotes a preference of an adult for prepubertal children rather than a type of sexual activity; a typical abuser is a man whom the victim knows
B) Incest requires a blood relationship between the parties
C) Pedophilia is defined as a specific sexual act performed against a child
D) Most offenders are strangers to the victim with prior criminal records
Answer: A
Rationale: Incest is "Any physical sexual activity between family members; blood relationship is not required (abusers can include stepparents, unrelated siblings, grandparents, uncles, and aunts)." Pedophilia "does not denote a type of sexual activity but rather the preference of an adult for prepubertal children as the means of achieving sexual excitement." "Anyone, including siblings and mothers, can be sexual abusers, but a typical abuser is a man whom the victim knows."
Trap: (D) is the stranger-danger picture, and the chapter contradicts each part: many offenders "hold full-time jobs, are active in community affairs, and may not have prior criminal records," and often work or volunteer "in positions such as teaching or coaching."

Q: A nurse is documenting findings in a case of suspected maltreatment. Which approach is correct?
A) Describe behaviors rather than interpret them, record conversations verbatim as much as possible, use a body diagram, and photograph injuries with a measurement tool
B) Record the nurse's conclusion that the child appears abused, so the record is unambiguous
C) Summarize the child's statements in the nurse's own words for clarity
D) Omit parent-child interaction, since it is subjective
Answer: A
Rationale: "Accurate and factual documentation is essential. Behaviors are described, not interpreted, and are recorded daily to establish a progress record." "Conversations among the nurse, child, and parent are recorded verbatim as much as possible." Recording includes bruise location, size, shape, and color "on drawing of body outline," distinguishing characteristics, symmetry or asymmetry, evidence of past injuries, and "description of parent-child interactions (verbal interactions, eye contact, touching, parental concern)."
Trap: (B) records a conclusion in place of the evidence supporting it, which weakens the record in precisely the setting — a court proceeding, where "Nurses may be subpoenaed" — that the documentation exists to serve. (C) discards the verbatim quality the chapter requires, and the child's own words are the evidence.
