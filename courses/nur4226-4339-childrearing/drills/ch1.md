# Chapter 1 drills — Perspectives of Pediatric Nursing

Authored from `sources/Ch1_Fact_Bank.md` (Hockenberry, Wilson & Rodgers, *Wong's
Nursing Care of Infants and Children*, 11th ed., printed pp. 1–14).

Blueprint weight is **9 questions of 202 (4.5%), depth `full`** — the second
heaviest chapter in the course and the heaviest of any non-dysfunction chapter.
That earns **74 drills across 14 topics**, high in the 55–75 band.

The fact bank's own curation note says the weight sits on three things, and the
topic split follows it: the **vocabulary of the philosophy of care**, where the
terms are one word apart and swapping them is the whole distractor strategy; the
**mortality and morbidity rankings by age group**, because "leading cause of
death in X-year-olds" is a stem the item bank writes over and over; and the
**nursing process and clinical reasoning steps**, which every later chapter's
care plan assumes without redefining.

Every invertible distinction the bank flags is drilled from both ends as adjacent
items: family-centered versus atraumatic care, enabling versus empowerment,
overweight versus obesity, neonatal versus postneonatal mortality, over- versus
underinvolvement, nonmaleficence versus beneficence, actual versus risk
diagnosis, signs and symptoms versus risk factors, PNP versus CNS, clinical
reasoning versus the nursing process. A `full` chapter has room to mirror them
all, so none were dropped.

Two deliberate exclusions, both from the GAP register:

- **No firearms rate is drilled from Table 1.1.** Every firearms cell in that
  table prints a dash, confirmed against the artwork — the table counts
  *unintentional* injury deaths and most firearm deaths are classified as
  homicide or suicide. The point that survives is *why* the row is empty, and
  that is what the one firearms item asks.
- **Suicide's rank at ages 10 to 19 is drilled from Table 1.3, not from the
  narrative.** The book contradicts itself: p. 7 calls suicide the third leading
  cause at 10 to 19, while Table 1.3 ranks it second at both 10–14 and 15–19.
  The audit confirmed the table against the printed artwork, and the table's own
  rates settle it, so the table is the keyed answer and the narrative's "third"
  is written as the distractor it is.

Rates are drilled with their denominators attached wherever the bank flags them,
because an option carrying the right number against the wrong denominator is a
distractor this chapter genuinely uses.

Topic: national health promotion initiatives for children
Subtopic: screen time and media guidance

Q: A mother of a 12-month-old says she lets the baby watch cartoons on a tablet for about 20 minutes while she cooks, and asks whether that is acceptable. Based on the 2016 AAP media guidelines, how should the nurse respond?
A) Twenty minutes daily is within the recommended limit for this age
B) No screen time is recommended before 18 months of age, except for video calling with a grandparent or loved one
C) Screen time is acceptable at this age as long as the content is educational
D) Screen time should be avoided entirely until the child is 5 years old
Answer: B
Rationale: The AAP 2016 guidelines quoted on p. 2 state that "For infants less than 18 months of age, no screen time is recommended except for video calling with a grandparent or loved one." The guidelines deliberately "shift the importance from what is on the screen to who is viewing the information with the young child."
Trap: (C) is the reflex pick because "educational content" is the compromise parents expect a nurse to offer, and it is exactly the framing the 2016 revision abandoned. Under 18 months the recommendation is about the interaction, not the content.

Q: The same mother returns two years later with the child, now 3 years old, and asks the same question. What is the accurate response?
A) No screen time is recommended until 5 years of age
B) Screen time may now be unlimited as long as a parent is in the room
C) Technology should be used sparingly before 5 years of age, and a parent should always participate during screen-time viewing
D) Screen time limits no longer apply once the child can speak in sentences
Answer: C
Rationale: The same guidance continues past 18 months in a different form: "Parents should be advised to use technology sparingly before 5 years of age and to always participate during screen-time viewing" (p. 2).
Trap: (A) is the mirror error of the previous item — it carries the under-18-month prohibition forward to an age where the rule has changed to sparing, co-viewed use. (B) drops the "sparingly" half and keeps only the participation half.

Subtopic: nutrition and oral health promotion

Q: A nurse is teaching new parents about feeding. Which statement about when eating habits are set is accurate?
A) Lifelong eating habits are established during the first 3 years of life
B) Lifelong eating habits are established by 18 months old
C) Lifelong eating habits are established once the child begins school
D) Lifelong eating habits are established during adolescence, when food choices become independent
Answer: A
Rationale: The chapter states flatly that "Children establish lifelong eating habits during the first 3 years of life" (p. 2), which is why the health promotion theme of nutrition is placed in infancy and toddlerhood rather than later.
Trap: (B) is not a random wrong number — it is the book's own distractor in review question 5, and it pulls because 18 months is the screen-time threshold from the same page. Two different rules, two different ages.

Q: A mother of a 15-year-old is frustrated that her son eats well at home but lives on fast food with friends. What does the chapter say explains this?
A) Adolescents lose the ability to recognize satiety cues during the growth spurt
B) Eating preferences are not established until adolescence, so his habits are still forming
C) During adolescence parental influence diminishes and food choices are made in relation to peer acceptability and sociability
D) Adolescents require higher caloric density than family meals typically provide
Answer: C
Rationale: "Most eating preferences and attitudes related to food are established by family influences and culture. During adolescence, parental influence diminishes and the adolescent makes food choices related to peer acceptability and sociability" (p. 2).
Trap: (B) inverts the chapter's own sequence — preferences are established early by family and culture, and adolescence is when a *different* influence takes over, not when the establishing begins.

Q: A nurse is planning oral health teaching for a family whose infant has just cut a first tooth. Which action reflects the chapter's guidance?
A) Defer oral hygiene teaching until the primary teeth are fully erupted at about 30 months
B) Begin teaching now, including drinking fluoridated water and instituting early dental preventive care
C) Begin teaching at the 3-year visit, when the child can cooperate with brushing
D) Teach that bottled water can be substituted for fluoridated tap water without concern
Answer: B
Rationale: The nurse's teaching starts "beginning with the first tooth eruption; drinking fluoridated water, including bottled water; and instituting early dental preventive care" (pp. 2–3). "Preventing dental caries and developing healthy oral hygiene habits must occur early in childhood."
Trap: (D) misreads the parenthetical. The chapter names bottled water because it must *also* be fluoridated, not because it is an acceptable non-fluoridated substitute.

Topic: obesity and overweight bmi definitions

Q: A 10-year-old's BMI plots at the 96th percentile for age and sex. How is this classified?
A) Overweight
B) Obese
C) Within normal limits, since the percentile is below the 97th
D) At risk for overweight, pending a repeat measurement
Answer: B
Rationale: Obesity in children and adolescents is defined as "a body mass index (BMI) at or greater than the 95th percentile for youth of the same age and gender" (p. 3). The 96th percentile is above that cut point.
Trap: (A) is the reflex pick from a learner who remembers that two categories exist but places the boundary a tier too high. The 95th percentile is the obesity line, not the top of the overweight range.

Q: A sibling of that child plots at the 88th percentile. How is this classified?
A) Obese
B) Overweight
C) Normal weight
D) Underweight for age
Answer: B
Rationale: Overweight is "a BMI at or above the 85th percentile and below the 95th percentile for children and teens of the same age and sex" (p. 3). The 88th percentile falls inside that band.
Trap: (A) is the mirror error of the previous item, and the fact bank names it the classic one — a distractor placing obesity at the 85th percentile. The 85th percentile opens the overweight band; it does not reach obesity.

Q: A provider documents that an obese adolescent has "cardiometabolic changes." Which cluster does that term name?
A) Hypertension, altered glucose metabolism, dyslipidemia, and abdominal obesity
B) Hypertension, tachycardia, hyperthyroidism, and truncal obesity
C) Insulin resistance, hypotension, elevated hemoglobin, and hepatic steatosis
D) Dyslipidemia, bradycardia, hypercalcemia, and central adiposity
Answer: A
Rationale: Cardiometabolic changes are defined as "a cluster of cardiovascular factors that include hypertension, altered glucose metabolism, dyslipidemia, and abdominal obesity" (p. 3).
Trap: (B) keeps two real members and swaps in two plausible cardiac findings. Each of the four terms in the printed definition is doing work, so an option that gets half right is still wrong.

Q: A school nurse is designing an obesity prevention program and wants to target the age band where the chapter says the evidence is strongest. Which group should be enrolled?
A) Children 2 to 5 years old
B) Children 6 to 12 years old
C) Adolescents 13 to 18 years old
D) Any age, since the evidence does not favor a particular band
Answer: B
Rationale: "Lifestyle interventions show promise in preventing obesity and decreasing occurrence if targeted at children 6 to 12 years old" (p. 3). For scale, over 30% of America's children are overweight and 17% are obese, and the Healthy People target is to reduce the incidence of overweight children from 20% in all ethnic groups to less than 6%.
Trap: (A) pulls because "earlier is better" is true of the eating-habits rule on the previous page, and the two get merged. Habits form in the first 3 years; the intervention evidence sits at 6 to 12.

Topic: childhood injury epidemiology by age

Q: A nursing student asks what kills and disables the most children in the United States. What is the accurate answer?
A) Congenital anomalies
B) Cancer
C) Injuries
D) Infectious disease
Answer: C
Rationale: "Injuries are the most common cause of death and disability to children in the United States," and "Each year, injuries kill or disable more children older than 1 year old than all childhood diseases combined" (pp. 3, 9). Table 1.3 shows injuries ranked first in every age interval from 1 through 19 years.
Trap: (A) is true for infants under 1 year, where congenital anomalies lead Table 1.2, and that is exactly why it pulls. The stem asks about children, and the injury claim is explicitly stated for children older than 1 year.

Q: An 8-month-old boy dies at home. Which type of unintentional injury ranks first as a cause of death in his age group?
A) Motor vehicle
B) Drowning
C) Mechanical suffocation
D) Choking
Answer: C
Rationale: Table 1.1 ranks mechanical suffocation first among unintentional injuries in infants under 1 year in both sexes, at 25.0 per 100,000 in males and 21.4 in females. The narrative confirms it: "During infancy, more boys die of aspiration or suffocation than do girls."
Trap: (A) is the reflex pick because motor vehicle deaths lead every *other* age band in the table, from 5 years up. Infancy is the one interval where it does not.

Q: A 3-year-old boy drowns in a backyard pool. Where does drowning rank among unintentional injury deaths for males in his age group?
A) First
B) Second
C) Third
D) Fifth
Answer: A
Rationale: Table 1.1 ranks drowning first for males ages 1 to 4 at 3.4 per 100,000, ahead of motor vehicle at 3.0.
Trap: (B) is picked by learners who remember motor vehicle leads "everywhere" and demote drowning by one. Ages 1 to 4 in males is the interval where drowning takes the top rank.

Q: A 3-year-old girl dies in a motor vehicle collision. Where does motor vehicle injury rank among unintentional injury deaths for females in her age group?
A) First
B) Second
C) Fourth
D) It does not appear in the top five for this age group
Answer: A
Rationale: Table 1.1 ranks motor vehicle first for females ages 1 to 4 at 2.4 per 100,000, ahead of drowning at 1.8.
Trap: (B) is the mirror error of the previous item. The 1-to-4 band is the one interval in Table 1.1 where the leading injury differs by sex — drowning in males, motor vehicle in females — so carrying the male answer across is the error the pair is built to catch.

Q: A community health nurse is planning injury prevention for a group of 5- to 9-year-olds and wants to address the activity with the greatest fatality risk in this age band. Which should she target?
A) Bicycling, with emphasis on helmet use for traumatic head injury
B) Swimming, with emphasis on adult supervision
C) Firearm storage in the home
D) Pedestrian safety in driveways
Answer: A
Rationale: "Children ages 5 to 9 years are at greatest risk of bicycling fatalities. The majority of bicycling deaths are from traumatic head injuries" (pp. 3–5).
Trap: (D) is drawn from the same paragraph but belongs to a different group — driveway injuries "typically involve small children and large vehicles backing up," which is a toddler problem, not a school-age one.

Q: A student notices that every firearms cell in Table 1.1 prints a dash, in both sexes and all four age groups, and asks whether the extraction lost the data. What is the correct explanation?
A) The row was truncated when the table was reproduced
B) Firearm deaths in children are too rare to generate a rate
C) The table reports unintentional injury deaths, and most firearm deaths are classified as homicide or suicide
D) Firearm deaths are reported only for ages 15 to 24 and were omitted from the other columns
Answer: C
Rationale: The GAP register records this as closed against the printed artwork: Table 1.1 prints a dash in every firearms cell. The table counts *unintentional* injuries, and firearm deaths are largely classified elsewhere. The narrative's separate claim that "improper use of firearms is a major cause of death among males," and Fig. 1.4's ranking of firearms fourth among injury deaths in children 5 to 14, are counting a different universe.
Trap: (B) is the intuitive read of an empty cell and is flatly contradicted by the narrative on the same pages. An empty cell in this table means "counted somewhere else," not "did not happen."

Topic: injury risk factors and cognitive stage
Subtopic: who gets injured

Q: A nurse is identifying children at elevated injury risk on a busy pediatric unit. Which findings does Box 1.3 name as risk factors for childhood injury? (Select all that apply.)
A) Male sex, attributed mainly to behavioral characteristics, especially aggression
B) A difficult temperament profile, especially persistence, high activity level, and negative reactions to new situations
C) A history of previous injury, especially if the initial injury required hospitalization
D) High birth order within a small family
E) Poverty, family stress, young maternal age, and low maternal education
Answer: A, B, C, E
Rationale: Box 1.3 lists sex ("Preponderance of males; difference mainly the result of behavioral characteristics, especially aggression"), temperament, stress, alcohol and drug use, history of previous injury, developmental characteristics, anatomic characteristics, and an "other" cluster of "Poverty, family stress (e.g., maternal illness, recent environmental change), substandard alternative child care, young maternal age, low maternal education, multiple siblings."
Trap: (D) is the one to reject, and it is close enough to be tempting — the box names *multiple siblings*, not birth order, and it appears in the socioeconomic cluster rather than as a standalone risk.

Q: A father asks why his 8-month-old, who has just begun crawling, is suddenly at risk for poisoning when nothing in the house has changed. Which explanation reflects the infant's cognitive stage?
A) Object permanence leads the infant to search actively for hidden containers
B) Sensorimotor exploration means the infant explores the environment through taste and touch and has a natural tendency to place objects in the mouth
C) Transductive reasoning prevents the infant from learning that a substance was harmful last time
D) The infant imitates adult behavior seen during medication administration
Answer: B
Rationale: Box 1.3 maps the infant's sensorimotor stage — "explores environment through taste and touch" — to aspiration and poisoning in the crawling infant who "has a natural tendency to place objects in the mouth."
Trap: (A) and (C) are both real entries from the same box, but both belong to the *young child*, not the infant. The box is organized by stage, and lifting a mechanism one row down is the error it invites.

Q: A mother cannot understand why her 3-year-old, who was scolded for climbing the bookshelf, immediately climbed the kitchen counter. Which cognitive characteristic explains this?
A) Formal operations, with a resulting feeling of invulnerability
B) Sensorimotor exploration through taste and touch
C) Transductive reasoning, so the child may fail to learn from experience and perceives one climb as a different type of danger from another
D) Transitional cognitive processes, so the child attempts dangerous acts without detailed planning
Answer: C
Rationale: Box 1.3 gives transductive reasoning for the young child as "may fail to learn from experiences (e.g., perceives falling from a step as a different type of danger from climbing a tree)," alongside magical and egocentric thinking that leaves the child "unable to comprehend danger to self or others."
Trap: (D) is the school-age entry and is the nearest neighbor — it also describes acting without planning. The discriminator is that the young child does not *transfer* the lesson at all, while the school-age child cannot fully trace causal relationships.

Q: A 16-year-old is brought in after being injured attempting a stunt his friends dared him to try. Which cognitive characteristic best accounts for this?
A) Formal operations, with preoccupation with abstract thinking that loses sight of reality and may lead to a feeling of invulnerability
B) Magical and egocentric thinking
C) Object permanence and cause-and-effect immaturity
D) Absorption in play that makes environmental hazards go unnoticed
Answer: A
Rationale: Box 1.3 pairs the adolescent's formal operations with a feeling of invulnerability, and adds that "The need to conform and gain acceptance compels older children and adolescents to accept challenges and dares."
Trap: (D) is the school-age entry — absorption with play "makes them oblivious to environmental hazards such as street traffic or water." That is inattention to a hazard, not a dare accepted in full awareness of it.

Q: Which pairings of developmental stage with a resulting injury are correct as the chapter's own review question presents them? (Select all that apply.)
A) A crawling infant aspirates after placing an object in the mouth
B) A toddler who runs and climbs is susceptible to burns, falls, and collisions
C) An older child accepts a peer's dare and is injured
D) A newborn rolls over and falls from an elevated surface
Answer: A, B, C
Rationale: These are the three keyed options in the chapter's review question 1 (p. 13), answer key "1. B, C, E."
Trap: (D) is the option to reject, and it fails on physiology rather than on stage matching — newborns do not roll over. The chapter's other rejected option, a preschooler riding a two-wheel bike recklessly, fails the same way: the behavior does not belong to the stage named.

Q: A 2-year-old is struck by a car in a parking lot and sustains a skull fracture. Which anatomic characteristic named in Box 1.3 predisposed this child to cranial injury?
A) A large spleen and liver with a wide costal arch
B) A large head relative to body size
C) A small and light body that may be thrown easily
D) Incomplete ossification of the long bones
Answer: B
Rationale: Box 1.3's anatomic characteristics list gives "Large head - Predisposes to cranial injury," alongside "Large spleen and liver with wide costal arch - Predisposes to direct trauma to these organs" and "Small and light body - May be thrown easily, especially inside a moving vehicle."
Trap: (D) is not in the box at all. It sounds like pediatric anatomy and is the kind of plausible invention this chapter's distractors use; only three anatomic characteristics are named, and this is not one of them.

Topic: violence and mental health epidemiology

Q: A parent who keeps a handgun in the home asks whether it really changes the family's risk. By approximately how much does the presence of a gun in a household increase the risk of suicide?
A) Twofold
B) Threefold
C) Fivefold
D) Tenfold
Answer: C
Rationale: "The presence of a gun in a household increases the risk of suicide by about fivefold and the risk of homicide by about threefold" (pp. 5–6).
Trap: (B) is the reflex pick because it is the other multiplier in the same sentence. The two numbers are attached to different outcomes and the fact bank flags swapping them explicitly.

Q: The same parent asks about the risk of homicide. By approximately how much does a gun in the household raise it?
A) Threefold
B) Fivefold
C) Sevenfold
D) It does not change homicide risk, only suicide risk
Answer: A
Rationale: The same sentence gives homicide risk as increased "by about threefold" (pp. 5–6).
Trap: (B) is the mirror error of the previous item, and it is the more dangerous direction to hold backwards because the larger multiplier attaches to the outcome parents are less likely to anticipate.

Q: A nurse works with children in a neighborhood with frequent community violence. A colleague says the children there "are used to it by now." What is the accurate response?
A) Habituation does reduce the stress response after repeated exposure
B) Multiple exposures to episodes of violence do not inoculate children against the negative effects, and continued exposure can result in lasting symptoms of stress
C) Only children who are directly victimized develop lasting symptoms
D) Witnessing violence is protective if the child has a stable caregiver present
Answer: B
Rationale: The chapter states that "multiple exposures to episodes of violence do not inoculate children against the negative effects; continued exposure can result in lasting symptoms of stress," and names as "the silent and underrecognized victims" the children who witness acts of community violence.
Trap: (C) pulls because it matches the intuition that witnesses are unharmed, which is precisely the claim the chapter singles out as wrong. Witnessing is the exposure it is warning about.

Q: A school nurse quotes the chapter's prevalence figures for child mental health. Which pair is accurate?
A) One out of five children experiences mental health problems, and one out of 10 has a serious emotional problem affecting daily functioning
B) One out of 10 children experiences mental health problems, and one out of 20 has a serious emotional problem
C) One out of three children experiences mental health problems, and one out of five has a serious emotional problem
D) One out of five children has a serious emotional problem affecting daily functioning
Answer: A
Rationale: "One out of five children experiences mental health problems, and 1 out of 10 has a serious emotional problem that affects daily functioning" (p. 6).
Trap: (D) keeps the right numerator and attaches it to the wrong severity tier. One in five is the broad figure; the serious tier is half that.

Topic: infant mortality rates and causes
Subtopic: rates and their denominators

Q: A nurse is explaining vital statistics to a student. How is the infant mortality rate defined?
A) The number of deaths during the first year of life per 1000 live births
B) The number of deaths during the first year of life per 100,000 live births
C) The number of deaths during the first 28 days of life per 1000 live births
D) The number of deaths under 5 years of age per 1000 population
Answer: A
Rationale: The infant mortality rate is "the number of deaths during the first year of life per 1000 live births" (p. 6). For 2014 it was 5.82.
Trap: (B) carries the right numerator and the wrong denominator, which the fact bank flags as a live distractor in this chapter — Table 1.2's cause-specific rates are per 100,000 live births, while the summary rate is per 1000. An option with the right number and the wrong denominator is still wrong.

Q: A 3-week-old dies in the neonatal intensive care unit. Which mortality category does this death fall into, and what was its 2014 rate?
A) Postneonatal mortality, 1.88 per 1000 live births
B) Neonatal mortality, 3.94 per 1000 live births
C) Neonatal mortality, 5.82 per 1000 live births
D) Perinatal mortality, 3.94 per 100,000 live births
Answer: B
Rationale: Neonatal mortality covers deaths at less than 28 days of life; the 2014 neonatal mortality rate was 3.94 per 1000 live births (p. 6). Three weeks is inside that window.
Trap: (C) attaches the overall infant mortality rate to the neonatal band. The 5.82 figure is the total of which 3.94 and 1.88 are the two parts.

Q: A 6-month-old dies at home. Which mortality category does this death fall into, and what was its 2014 rate?
A) Neonatal mortality, 3.94 per 1000 live births
B) Postneonatal mortality, 1.88 per 1000 live births
C) Postneonatal mortality, 5.82 per 1000 live births
D) Childhood mortality, 1.88 per 100,000 population
Answer: B
Rationale: Postneonatal mortality covers deaths from "28 days to 11 months"; the 2014 rate was 1.88 per 1000 live births (p. 6).
Trap: (A) is the mirror error of the previous item — the boundary between the two subdivisions is 28 days, not 6 months, and both are subdivisions of the same infant mortality rate.

Subtopic: causes and disparities

Q: A student asks what most determines whether a newborn survives the neonatal period in a technologically developed country. What does the chapter identify?
A) Gestational age at delivery
B) Birth weight
C) Maternal age
D) Mode of delivery
Answer: B
Rationale: "Birth weight is considered the major determinant of neonatal death in technologically developed countries. The relatively high incidence of LBW (<2500 g [5.5 pounds]) in the United States is considered a key factor in its higher neonatal mortality rate compared with other countries" (p. 6). The preventive strategy the chapter names is "Access to and the use of high-quality prenatal care."
Trap: (A) is nearly right and is the reason this item exists — the chapter names birth weight specifically, and low birth weight is what the international comparison turns on.

Q: Which cause ranks first among the 10 leading causes of infant death in the United States?
A) Sudden infant death syndrome
B) Disorders relating to short gestation and unspecified low birth weight
C) Congenital anomalies
D) Accidents (unintentional injuries)
Answer: C
Rationale: Table 1.2 ranks congenital anomalies first at 20.4% of infant deaths, a rate of 119.0 per 100,000 live births, ahead of short gestation and unspecified low birth weight at 18.0%. Together with maternal complications of pregnancy and SIDS, the top four "accounted for about half (53%) of all deaths of infants younger than 1 year old."
Trap: (A) is the reflex pick because SIDS gets disproportionate public attention; it ranks fourth in Table 1.2 at 6.7%.

Q: A nurse is describing racial disparity in infant mortality. Which statement is accurate?
A) African American infants have twice the infant mortality rate of Caucasian infants, and the low birth weight rate is also much higher
B) African American infants have a slightly higher rate, and the low birth weight rate is comparable
C) Infant mortality rates for Hispanics and Asian-Pacific Islanders have risen sharply over the past two decades
D) Infant mortality is now equivalent across racial groups in the United States
Answer: A
Rationale: "Infant mortality for Caucasians is considerably lower than for all other races in the United States, with African Americans having twice the rate of Caucasians. The LBW rate is also much higher for African American infants than for any other group." Rates for Hispanics and Asian-Pacific Islanders "have decreased dramatically during the past two decades" (pp. 6–7).
Trap: (C) reverses the direction of a real trend from the same paragraph. Those two groups' rates fell; the disparity the chapter emphasizes is the African American one.

Topic: childhood mortality and morbidity patterns

Q: A student assumes that death rates fall steadily from birth through adolescence. Which age group actually has the lowest rate of death?
A) Infants under 1 year
B) Children 1 to 4 years
C) Children 5 to 14 years
D) Adolescents 15 to 19 years
Answer: C
Rationale: "Death rates for children older than 1 year of age have always been lower than those for infants. Children ages 5 to 14 years have the lowest rate of death. However, a sharp rise occurs during later adolescence, primarily from injuries, homicide, and suicide" (p. 7).
Trap: (D) is picked by learners who assume the trend keeps falling with age. It reverses in later adolescence — Table 1.3 gives all-cause rates of 11.5 at 5 to 9 and 14.0 at 10 to 14, then 45.5 at 15 to 19.

Q: A nurse reviews Table 1.3 and is asked which cause of death ranks first at ages 1 to 4, 5 to 9, 10 to 14, and 15 to 19. What is the answer?
A) The leading cause changes with each age interval
B) Injuries rank first in all four intervals
C) Congenital anomalies rank first in the younger two intervals and injuries in the older two
D) Cancer ranks first in all four intervals
Answer: B
Rationale: Table 1.3 ranks injuries first in every interval from 1 through 19 years, at rates of 7.6, 3.6, 3.6, and 17.7 per 100,000 population.
Trap: (C) is plausible because congenital anomalies really do rank second at 1 to 4 and then fall away — to rank 3 at 5 to 9, rank 4 at 10 to 14, and off the list by 15 to 19. Falling down the table is not the same as leading it.

Q: According to Table 1.3, where does suicide rank as a cause of death in children 10 to 14 years old?
A) It does not appear in the top five at this age
B) Second
C) Third
D) Fifth
Answer: B
Rationale: Table 1.3 ranks suicide second at 10 to 14 years, at 2.1 per 100,000, ahead of cancer at 2.0 and congenital anomalies at 0.8 — and second again at 15 to 19, at 8.7 ahead of homicide at 6.7. Suicide first enters the top five at age 10.
Trap: (C) is the book's own narrative, which calls suicide "the third leading cause of death among children and adolescents 10 to 19 years old" — and the GAP register resolves the contradiction against it. The printed artwork was checked: the table really does rank suicide second in both intervals, and no way of combining the intervals puts it third. Answer this stem from the table.

Q: A nurse in an urgent care clinic is asked what the single most common illness of childhood is. What is the accurate answer?
A) Otitis media
B) Gastroenteritis
C) The common cold
D) Asthma
Answer: C
Rationale: "The chief illness of childhood is the common cold." Respiratory illness "accounts for approximately 50% of all acute conditions, 11% are caused by infections and parasitic disease, and 15% are caused by injuries" (p. 7).
Trap: (A) is the reflex pick because otitis media is the classic pediatric diagnosis and the most commonly *treated* one. The chapter's claim is about the chief illness overall, and respiratory conditions carry half of all acute conditions.

Q: Which groups does the chapter identify as having increased morbidity? (Select all that apply.)
A) Homeless children and children living in poverty
B) Low birth weight children and children with chronic illnesses
C) Foreign-born adopted children
D) Children in day care centers
E) Firstborn children in two-parent households
Answer: A, B, C, D
Rationale: The chapter names "homeless children, children living in poverty, LBW children, children with chronic illnesses, foreign-born adopted children, and children in day care centers," attributing the pattern to "barriers to health care" plus "improved survival of children with chronic health problems, particularly infants of VLBW" (p. 7).
Trap: (E) is the option to reject. Note also that morbidity "is not distributed randomly in children," and that "Children who have had a particular type of problem are more likely to have that problem again" — recurrence is the pattern, not chance.

Topic: family-centered care as a philosophy of care

Q: A nurse manager is explaining the premise underlying family-centered care to new staff. Which statement captures it?
A) The family is the constant in a child's life, whereas the service systems and support personnel within those systems fluctuate
B) The nurse is the constant in a hospitalized child's life, and the family visits within that structure
C) The family's role is to consent to care that the health care team designs
D) Care is centered on the child, with the family informed of decisions after they are made
Answer: A
Rationale: Family-centered care "recognizes the family as the constant in a child's life," and Box 1.4's first key element is "Incorporating into policy and practice the recognition that the family is the constant in a child's life, whereas the service systems and support personnel within those systems fluctuate." The definition itself is "an approach to the planning, delivery, and evaluation of health care that is grounded in mutually beneficial partnerships among health care providers, patients, and families" (p. 7).
Trap: (C) sounds like partnership and is not. Consent after the fact is the model family-centered care replaces; the partnership is in the planning, delivery, *and* evaluation.

Q: A nurse arranges for the parents of a newly diagnosed child to be trained in the dressing changes they will be doing at home, and schedules the sessions around their work hours. Which concept does this illustrate?
A) Empowerment
B) Enabling
C) Advocacy
D) Anticipatory guidance
Answer: B
Rationale: "Professionals enable families by creating opportunities and means for all family members to display their current abilities and competencies and to acquire new ones to meet the needs of the child and family" (p. 7). Creating the opportunity and the means is enabling.
Trap: (A) is the reflex pick and is the other half of the pair. The axis is who is acting: enabling is what the professional creates, empowerment is what the family comes to hold.

Q: The same parents later tell the nurse they finally feel in charge of their child's care again and can see that their own efforts made the difference. Which concept does this illustrate?
A) Enabling
B) Empowerment
C) Coordination
D) Nonmaleficence
Answer: B
Rationale: Empowerment "describes the interaction of professionals with families in such a way that families maintain or acquire a sense of control over their family lives and acknowledge positive changes that result from helping behaviors that foster their own strengths, abilities, and actions" (p. 7).
Trap: (A) is the mirror error of the previous item. The tell is the sense of control residing in the family and the family recognizing its own contribution — that is empowerment, not the opportunity that made it possible.

Q: Which are key elements of family-centered care as Box 1.4 states them? (Select all that apply.)
A) Facilitating family-professional collaboration at all levels of hospital, home, and community care
B) Exchanging complete and unbiased information between family members and professionals in a supportive manner at all times
C) Encouraging and facilitating family-to-family support and networking
D) Ensuring that service and support systems are flexible, accessible, and comprehensive
E) Limiting information to what the family requests, to avoid overwhelming them during a crisis
Answer: A, B, C, D
Rationale: Box 1.4 names collaboration at all levels — care of the individual child, program development and evaluation, and policy formation — plus unbiased information exchange, recognition of cultural diversity and different coping methods, family-to-family networking, flexible systems, and "Appreciating families as families and children as children."
Trap: (E) is the one to reject and it is written to sound protective. The printed element is "complete and unbiased information... at all times," which is the opposite of rationing disclosure by perceived readiness.

Q: A nurse notices that a colleague performs all of a chronically ill child's care herself so the exhausted parents can rest, and describes this as family-centered. How should this be evaluated?
A) It is family-centered, because it responds to a family need
B) It is not family-centered; the positive action is developing teaching skills to instruct families rather than doing everything for them
C) It is family-centered only if the parents requested it
D) It is atraumatic care rather than family-centered care
Answer: B
Rationale: Among the positive actions listed in the therapeutic relationship guidelines is developing "teaching skills to instruct families rather than doing everything for them," and working "to find ways to decrease their dependence on health care providers." The fact bank flags this exact distractor: an option describing the nurse taking over care to spare the family is wrong even though it sounds kind.
Trap: (A) is the reflex pick because the action is genuinely kind and the stem supplies a sympathetic motive. Family-centered care builds the family's competence; substituting for it increases dependence.

Topic: atraumatic care

Q: A child life specialist prepares a preschooler for an IV insertion with a doll, and the nurse arranges for the mother to hold the child during the procedure. Which philosophy of care do these actions reflect?
A) Family-centered care
B) Atraumatic care
C) Evidence-based practice
D) Anticipatory guidance
Answer: B
Rationale: Atraumatic care is "the provision of therapeutic care in settings, by personnel, and through the use of interventions that eliminate or minimize the psychologic and physical distress experienced by children and their families in the health care system" (p. 7). Preparing the child and keeping the parent present are its named interventions.
Trap: (A) is the reflex pick because a parent is involved, and the fact bank flags this swap directly. Family-centered care is about partnership and the family as constant; atraumatic care is about eliminating or minimizing distress. Both may be present, but distress reduction is what the stem describes.

Q: A nurse is asked to state the overriding goal of atraumatic care. What is it?
A) Prevent or minimize the child's separation from the family
B) First, do no harm
C) Promote a sense of control
D) Prevent or minimize bodily injury and pain
Answer: B
Rationale: The overriding goal is "First, do no harm" (pp. 7–8). This is the chapter's own review question 4, answered "Do no harm."
Trap: (A), (C), and (D) are the three *principles* that provide the framework for reaching that goal — and using all three principles as the distractors is precisely how the book writes the item. A principle is the means; the goal is the harm standard itself.

Q: Which are the three principles that provide the framework for atraumatic care? (Select all that apply.)
A) Prevent or minimize the child's separation from the family
B) Promote a sense of control
C) Prevent or minimize bodily injury and pain
D) Eliminate the family's financial burden of hospitalization
Answer: A, B, C
Rationale: The three principles are listed on pp. 7–8 as preventing or minimizing separation from the family, promoting a sense of control, and preventing or minimizing bodily injury and pain. Named examples include fostering the parent-child relationship during hospitalization, preparing the child before unfamiliar procedures, controlling pain, allowing privacy, providing play for expression of fear and aggression, offering choices, and respecting cultural differences.
Trap: (D) is the one to reject. Financial support appears in family-centered care's Box 1.4 as one form of support for coping, not among the atraumatic care principles.

Q: A nurse summarizes atraumatic care as concerned with "the where, who, why, and how of any procedure performed on a child." Which four components does that map onto?
A) Setting, personnel, therapeutic care, and interventions
B) Assessment, diagnosis, planning, and evaluation
C) Autonomy, nonmaleficence, beneficence, and justice
D) Development, nutrition, oral health, and safety
Answer: A
Rationale: The definition names setting ("the place in which that care is given - the home, the hospital, or any other health care setting"), personnel ("anyone directly involved in providing therapeutic care"), interventions (ranging "from psychologic approaches, such as preparing children for procedures, to physical interventions, such as providing space for a parent to room in with a child"), and therapeutic care ("the prevention, diagnosis, treatment, or palliation of acute or chronic conditions").
Trap: (C) is the ethical decision-making set from p. 10, and it pulls because it is also a memorized group of four from the same chapter. The chapter has several four-item lists and they are not interchangeable.

Q: A hospitalized school-age child is sleepless because of continuous overhead lighting and equipment alarms. A nurse says this is an environmental annoyance rather than an atraumatic care concern. How should this be evaluated?
A) Correct, since atraumatic care addresses procedural pain rather than the environment
B) Incorrect; physical distress in the definition includes sleeplessness and disturbances from sensory stimuli such as temperature extremes, loud noises, bright lights, or darkness
C) Correct, since sleeplessness is a psychologic rather than a physical form of distress
D) Incorrect; this is a family-centered care concern rather than an atraumatic care concern
Answer: B
Rationale: Physical distress "may range from sleeplessness and immobilization to disturbances from sensory stimuli, such as pain, temperature extremes, loud noises, bright lights, or darkness" (p. 8). Psychologic distress separately "may include anxiety, fear, anger, disappointment, sadness, shame, or guilt."
Trap: (A) is the reflex pick and reflects the common narrowing of atraumatic care to pain management. The fact bank flags it: atraumatic care is not only about pain, and the setting is one of its four named components.

Topic: therapeutic versus nontherapeutic nurse-family relationships

Q: A new graduate says, "I realize that caring for the child means I can visit them on my days off if they ask me." What does this comment indicate?
A) A therapeutic relationship with appropriate emotional availability
B) Overinvolvement, which is a nontherapeutic pattern
C) Appropriate family advocacy
D) Underinvolvement compensated by extra contact
Answer: B
Rationale: Spending off-duty time with families is listed among the overinvolvement behaviors in the Nursing Care Guidelines on exploring your relationships, along with working overtime to care for the family, calling frequently to check on them, showing favoritism, buying clothes, toys, or food, and competing with other staff for a family's affection. This is the chapter's own review question 3, keyed to this comment.
Trap: (A) is the reflex pick because the comment sounds devoted rather than problematic, which is what makes overinvolvement hard to self-identify. The boundary in a therapeutic relationship is "positive and professional," not absent.

Q: A different nurse routinely tells parents that visiting hours must be cut short because the unit is too busy, and describes her practice as focused on the technical care the child needs. What does this indicate?
A) Overinvolvement
B) Underinvolvement, which is equally nontherapeutic
C) An appropriate boundary
D) Effective time management under staffing constraints
Answer: B
Rationale: The underinvolvement column names nurses who "restrict parent or visitor access to children, using excuses such as the unit is too busy" and who focus on technical aspects and lose "sight of the person who is the patient."
Trap: (A) is the mirror error of the previous item, and (C) is the more tempting one — the fact bank warns that a distractor treating only overinvolvement as a problem misses half the concept. Both directions are nontherapeutic.

Q: Which behaviors belong to the overinvolvement pattern described in the Nursing Care Guidelines? (Select all that apply.)
A) Restricting parent access to children because the unit is busy
B) Buying clothes, toys, or food for the family
C) Competing with other staff for a family's affection
D) Attempting to influence families' decisions rather than facilitating their informed decision making
E) Other staff commenting on your closeness to a family
Answer: B, C, D, E
Rationale: All four keyed options are listed under overinvolvement, along with working overtime to care for the family, spending off-duty time with them, calling frequently, and showing favoritism.
Trap: (A) is the one to reject and it is the single underinvolvement item in the list. The sorting axis is direction, not severity — both columns are nontherapeutic, so an option cannot be rejected merely for sounding harmful.

Q: A nurse becomes critical of parents who do not visit their hospitalized child and finds herself competing with them for the child's affection. Which pattern does the chapter name for this?
A) Pure overinvolvement with the family
B) Pure underinvolvement with the family
C) Being overinvolved with children and underinvolved with their parents
D) A therapeutic relationship under strain
Answer: C
Rationale: The chapter names this third pattern separately, shown by becoming "critical when parents do not visit their children" or competing with parents for their children's affection (p. 8).
Trap: (A) is close and is the reason this item is here — the nurse is genuinely overinvolved, but only with one half of the family unit, and the chapter treats the split as its own named pattern rather than as a variant of overinvolvement.

Q: A nurse recognizes that her relationship with a family has shifted from therapeutic toward a personal friendship. What does the chapter direct her to do?
A) End contact with the family abruptly to reestablish the boundary
B) Continue as before, since a warm relationship benefits the family
C) Recognize the change and arrange for a more objective colleague to take her place
D) Report the family to the nurse manager for boundary violation
Answer: C
Rationale: Among the positive actions listed is recognizing when "a therapeutic relationship can change to a social relationship or an intimate friendship" and arranging for a more objective colleague to take your place. The chapter also names the ability to "'step back' and withdraw emotionally, if not physically, when emotional overload occurs, yet remain committed."
Trap: (A) is the overcorrection, and it pulls because abrupt withdrawal looks like the decisive fix. The direction is continuity of care through a colleague, not severed contact — and the withdrawal the chapter describes is emotional, not necessarily physical.

Topic: pediatric nurse roles and ethical decision making
Subtopic: the nurse's roles

Q: A nurse is asked what the best approach to disease prevention in children is. What does the chapter state?
A) Screening at every well-child visit
B) Education and anticipatory guidance
C) Immunization compliance monitoring
D) Early referral to specialty services
Answer: B
Rationale: "The best approach to prevention is education and anticipatory guidance" (pp. 8–9). The care plan must include "a thorough assessment of all aspects of child growth and development, including nutrition, immunizations, safety, dental care, socialization, discipline, and education."
Trap: (A) is defensible practice and is not what the chapter says. Screening detects; the chapter's stated best approach acts before there is anything to detect.

Q: A nurse who feels competent at counseling recognizes mid-conversation that a family's crisis exceeds what she can manage. What does the chapter identify as the risk if she proceeds anyway?
A) The family may become dependent on her for future support
B) She may not only prevent the child from dealing with a crisis but also impede future success with a qualified professional
C) She may violate the institution's scope-of-practice policy
D) There is little risk, since any supportive listening is beneficial
Answer: B
Rationale: The chapter's own example is a nurse who feels competent in counseling but is inadequate at it, who "may not only prevent the child from dealing with a crisis but also impede future success with a qualified professional." It states plainly that "Failure to recognize limitations can be nontherapeutic at best and destructive at worst," and that "A nurse working in isolation rarely serves the child's best interests."
Trap: (D) is the reflex pick because supportive listening is normally safe. The chapter's point is that inadequate counseling does lasting damage to the child's later engagement with help, which is more than a missed opportunity.

Subtopic: ethical decision making

Q: A team withholds a treatment that would cause significant suffering with little expected benefit. Which moral value most directly underlies this decision?
A) Beneficence
B) Nonmaleficence
C) Autonomy
D) Justice
Answer: B
Rationale: Nonmaleficence is "the obligation to minimize or prevent harm" (p. 10).
Trap: (A) is the reflex pick because both concern the patient's good and the two words are learned together. Preventing harm is nonmaleficence; actively promoting well-being is beneficence.

Q: The same team initiates a therapy expected to substantially improve a child's function. Which moral value most directly underlies this decision?
A) Nonmaleficence
B) Beneficence
C) Justice
D) Autonomy
Answer: B
Rationale: Beneficence is "the obligation to promote the patient's well-being" (p. 10). The other two of the four values are autonomy, "the patient's right to be self-governing," and justice, "the concept of fairness."
Trap: (A) is the mirror error of the previous item. Both items describe defensible care; the axis is whether the obligation being met is to avoid harm or to produce good.

Q: Two experienced nurses reach different conclusions about the same pediatric case, and each can justify her position. How does the chapter frame this?
A) One of them has misapplied the ethical framework and needs correction
B) Ethical dilemmas arise when competing moral considerations underlie various alternatives, and team members may reach different but morally defensible decisions by assigning different weights to competing moral values
C) The disagreement should be resolved by institutional policy without further discussion
D) Nurses should defer to the physician's determination in ethical conflicts
Answer: B
Rationale: "Ethical dilemmas arise when competing moral considerations underlie various alternatives. Parents, nurses, physicians, and other health care team members may reach different but morally defensible decisions by assigning different weights to competing moral values" (p. 10). The nurse decides "within the framework of societal mores, professional practice standards, the law, institutional rules, the family's value system and religious traditions, and the nurse's personal values."
Trap: (A) is the reflex pick because disagreement usually implies error. The chapter's definition of a dilemma is that more than one defensible answer exists — the weights differ, not the competence.

Topic: evidence-based practice and the grade criteria

Q: A nurse notices that unit practice for a common procedure varies and asks whether a better approach exists. She frames the population, intervention, comparison, outcome, and time. Which activity is she performing?
A) Quality improvement auditing
B) Formulating a PICOT question as the first step of evidence-based practice
C) Nursing diagnosis
D) Root cause analysis
Answer: B
Rationale: The chapter defines the "population/patient problem, intervention, comparison, outcome, and time (PICOT) question to clearly define the problem of interest" (p. 10). Evidence-based practice is "the collection, interpretation, and integration of valid, important, and applicable patient-reported, nurse-observed, and research-derived information," and it "implies questioning why something is effective and whether a better approach exists."
Trap: (A) pulls because both start from noticing variation. The tell is the structured question format, which belongs to EBP rather than to an audit.

Q: A practice guideline cites consistent evidence from well-performed randomized controlled trials. How is this rated on the GRADE criteria?
A) High quality
B) Moderate quality
C) Low quality
D) Very low quality
Answer: A
Rationale: Table 1.4 defines high-quality evidence as "Consistent evidence from well-performed RCTs or exceptionally strong evidence from unbiased observational studies."
Trap: (B) pulls because learners hedge toward the middle tier when a rating scale has four levels. Moderate is reserved for RCTs "with important limitations (inconsistent results, flaws in methodology, indirect evidence, or imprecise results)."

Q: A second guideline rests on unsystematic clinical observations for one of its critical outcomes. How is this rated on the GRADE criteria?
A) Low quality
B) Very low quality
C) Moderate quality
D) It cannot be rated
Answer: B
Rationale: Table 1.4 defines very low quality as "Evidence for at least one of the critical outcomes from unsystematic clinical observations or very indirect evidence." Low quality requires at least observational studies, RCTs with serious flaws, or indirect evidence.
Trap: (A) is the mirror error of the previous item and is the near neighbor on the scale. The discriminator is *unsystematic* observation, which drops it below the low tier.

Q: A guideline states that the desirable effects of an intervention are closely balanced with its undesirable effects. How is the resulting recommendation graded?
A) Strong
B) Weak
C) High
D) It is not graded until the balance is resolved
Answer: B
Rationale: In Table 1.4, a weak recommendation means "Desirable effects closely balanced with undesirable effects," while a strong recommendation means "Desirable effects clearly outweigh undesirable effects, or vice versa."
Trap: (C) confuses the two halves of the GRADE table — high, moderate, low, and very low grade the *evidence*; strong and weak grade the *recommendation*. Mixing the two scales is the error this item targets.

Q: A nurse manager wants to support evidence-based practice on her unit. Besides current resources such as online search engines and journals, what does the chapter name as necessary?
A) A dedicated research nurse on staff
B) Paid time away from patient care to participate in activities that promote evidence-based practice
C) Institutional review board approval for all practice changes
D) A minimum proportion of master's-prepared staff
Answer: B
Rationale: Two resources are named: "appropriate, recent resources such as online search engines and journals," and time — "In some institutions, nurses are given paid time away from performing patient care to participate in activities that promote EBP. This requires an organizational environment that values EBP" (p. 10).
Trap: (A) is a real support and is not what the chapter names. The two named resources are information access and protected time, and the second is the one the chapter ties to organizational values.

Topic: clinical reasoning and the nursing process

Q: A student is asked to list the steps of the nursing process as this textbook states them. Which sequence is correct?
A) Assessment, diagnosis outcomes identification, planning, implementation, evaluation
B) Assessment, planning, diagnosis, implementation, documentation, evaluation
C) Assessment, diagnosis, planning, implementation, documentation
D) Data collection, analysis, intervention, reassessment
Answer: A
Rationale: The book states the model as "assessment, diagnosis outcomes identification, planning, implementation, and evaluation" (p. 11).
Trap: (B) and (C) both insert documentation as a step, which is the specific error the chapter guards against: "Although documentation is not one of the steps of the nursing process, it is essential for evaluation."

Q: A nurse argues that no step of the nursing process can be completed without documentation. How does the chapter address this?
A) It agrees; documentation is required at every step
B) The nurse can assess, diagnose, identify problems, plan, and implement without documentation, but evaluation is best performed with written evidence of progress toward outcomes
C) Documentation is the sixth step of the nursing process
D) Documentation applies only to the implementation phase
Answer: B
Rationale: "The nurse can assess, diagnose, and identify problems; plan; and implement without documentation. However, evaluation is best performed with written evidence of progress toward outcomes" (p. 12).
Trap: (A) overstates a true-sounding claim, and the fact bank flags it: the book says evaluation is what suffers, not that the other phases become impossible.

Q: A nurse writes the diagnosis "Risk for Infection" for a child with a new central line who currently shows no signs of infection. What does the word "risk" signify?
A) An actual health problem of low severity
B) A potential health problem — a risk state requiring nursing intervention to prevent an actual problem from developing
C) That the diagnosis is provisional pending laboratory confirmation
D) That the problem belongs to the family rather than the child
Answer: B
Rationale: A potential health problem is "a risk state that requires nursing intervention to prevent the development of an actual problem," and "To differentiate actual from potential health problems, the word risk is included in the nursing diagnosis statement (e.g., Risk for Infection)" (p. 11). Interventions for a risk state are directed toward reducing risk factors.
Trap: (A) treats risk as a severity grade rather than a status marker. It is the presence or absence of the problem that the word distinguishes, not how bad it is.

Q: The same child now has fever, erythema at the insertion site, and a rising white count. How does the diagnosis change, and what drives interventions now?
A) It remains a risk diagnosis, and interventions continue to target risk factors
B) It becomes an actual problem, supported by signs and symptoms — a cluster of cues and defining characteristics — and interventions target illness management
C) It becomes a medical rather than a nursing diagnosis, so nursing interventions no longer apply
D) It remains a risk diagnosis until the culture returns
Answer: B
Rationale: Signs and symptoms are "a cluster of cues and defining characteristics that are derived from patient assessment and indicate actual health problems," whereas risk factors "predispose a child and family to a dysfunctional health pattern and are limited to individuals at greater risk than the population as a whole" (p. 11).
Trap: (A) is the mirror error of the previous item — carrying the risk framing forward after defining characteristics have appeared. The presence of cues, not the presence of a culture result, is what converts a risk state to an actual problem.

Q: A nurse cannot decide between two diagnostic categories for a child. Which concept does the chapter offer to resolve this?
A) The critical defining characteristic, which is essential for the diagnosis to be made and helps differentiate between diagnostic categories
B) The risk factor with the highest prevalence
C) The outcome statement, which determines the diagnosis retrospectively
D) The medical diagnosis, which takes precedence
Answer: A
Rationale: A critical defining characteristic is a defining characteristic "essential for the diagnosis to be made"; these "help differentiate between diagnostic categories" (p. 11).
Trap: (C) inverts the sequence. An outcome is "the projected or expected change in a patient's health status, clinical condition, or behavior that occurs after nursing interventions have been instituted" — it follows the diagnosis rather than establishing it.

Q: A student says clinical reasoning and the nursing process are two names for the same thing. How should this be corrected?
A) They are unrelated; clinical reasoning belongs to medicine and the nursing process to nursing
B) Clinical reasoning is a cognitive process that gathers and analyzes data and considers alternative actions, while the nursing process is a method of problem identification and problem solving that describes what the nurse actually does; both rest on the scientific method of inquiry
C) Clinical reasoning replaced the nursing process in current practice
D) The nursing process is the cognitive component of clinical reasoning
Answer: B
Rationale: Clinical reasoning is "a cognitive process that uses formal and informal thinking to gather and analyze patient data, evaluate the significant of the information, and consider alternative actions" — quoted with the source's own wording — while the nursing process is "a method of problem identification and problem solving that describes what the nurse actually does." Clinical reasoning "is based on the scientific method of inquiry, which is also the basis for the nursing process" (p. 11).
Trap: (C) is the reflex pick for a learner who has heard clinical reasoning described as the newer framing. The fact bank flags any distractor calling one a substitute for the other as a misstatement of the relationship; they are related, not sequential.

Q: A nurse completes an admission assessment and considers it finished. How does the chapter characterize assessment within the nursing process?
A) It is the first step and is complete once baseline data are recorded
B) It is a continuous process that operates at all phases of problem solving and is the foundation for decision making, making the process dynamic rather than static
C) It recurs only at the evaluation phase
D) It is complete when the nursing diagnosis is assigned
Answer: B
Rationale: Assessment is "a continuous process that operates at all phases of problem solving and is the foundation for decision making... the purposeful collection, classification, and analysis of data from a variety of sources," considering "the patient's biophysical, psychologic, sociocultural, and spiritual background." The chapter adds that continual assessment throughout all phases makes the process "dynamic rather than static" (pp. 11–12).
Trap: (A) is the textbook-diagram reading of the nursing process, where assessment is a box with an arrow leaving it. The chapter explicitly rejects that as static.

Topic: pediatric quality outcome measures

Q: Which are among the six national quality priorities as the chapter lists them? (Select all that apply.)
A) Making care safer by reducing harm caused in the delivery of care
B) Ensuring that each person and family is engaged as partners in their care
C) Promoting effective communication and coordination of care
D) Making quality care more affordable by developing and spreading new health care delivery models
E) Increasing the proportion of care delivered by advanced practice nurses
Answer: A, B, C, D
Rationale: The six priorities from the National Strategy for Quality Improvement in Health Care (2015) are the four keyed here, plus "Promoting the most effective prevention and treatment practices for the leading causes of mortality, starting with cardiovascular disease" and "Working with communities to promote wide use of best practices to enable healthy living" (pp. 12–13).
Trap: (E) is the one to reject. It names a staffing change rather than a care outcome, which is the axis the whole list is built on.

Q: A quality report tracks the percentage of children 3 months to 18 years diagnosed with an upper respiratory infection who were **not** dispensed an antibiotic prescription on or 3 days after the episode. What does a higher percentage indicate?
A) Poorer performance, since fewer children received treatment
B) Better performance, since the measure rewards withholding antibiotics for a viral illness
C) Nothing, since the measure tracks diagnosis rather than treatment
D) Better access to care
Answer: B
Rationale: Table 1.5 defines appropriate treatment for children with URI as the percentage "who were diagnosed with upper respiratory infection (URI) and were not dispensed an antibiotic prescription on or 3 days after the episode." The measure is constructed so that the desirable action is non-prescription.
Trap: (A) is the reflex pick because most quality measures count services delivered, and this one counts a service correctly withheld. Reading the direction of a measure before interpreting the number is the skill being tested.

Q: A second measure in the same report tracks children 2 to 18 years diagnosed with pharyngitis who were ordered an antibiotic. What does the measure require in addition?
A) That a follow-up visit occurred within 3 days
B) That a group A streptococcus test was received for the episode
C) That the antibiotic was a narrow-spectrum agent
D) That the child had no antibiotic prescription in the preceding 30 days
Answer: B
Rationale: Table 1.5 defines appropriate testing for children with pharyngitis as the percentage "who were diagnosed with pharyngitis, ordered an antibiotic, and received a group A streptococcus (strep) test for the episode."
Trap: (C) is clinically sensible antibiotic stewardship and is not what this measure counts. The pharyngitis measure is about confirming the indication, while the URI measure is about avoiding the prescription entirely.

Q: A nurse asks what the childhood immunization status quality measure is anchored to. What is the endpoint?
A) Completion of the listed vaccine series by the child's second birthday
B) Completion of the listed vaccine series before school entry
C) Completion of the listed vaccine series by 18 months of age
D) Any immunization received during the reporting year
Answer: A
Rationale: Table 1.5 defines the measure as the percentage of children 2 years of age who had the listed series — four DTaP, three IPV, one MMR, three HiB, three Hep B, one VZV, four PCV, one Hep A, two or three rotavirus, and two influenza vaccines — "by their second birthday."
Trap: (C) pulls because 18 months is a real pediatric milestone in this chapter, but it belongs to the screen-time guidance. The immunization measure is anchored at 2 years.
