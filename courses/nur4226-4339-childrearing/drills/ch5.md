# Chapter 5 drills — Pain Assessment and Management in Children

Authored from `sources/Ch5_Fact_Bank.md` (Hockenberry, Wilson & Rodgers, 11th ed.,
printed pp. 137–168).

Blueprint weight is **7 questions of 202 (3.5%), depth `standard`** — 45 drills
across 10 topics, at the top of the 30–45 band.

The blueprint names the topic as *Pain Assessment in Children* while the chapter
is titled *Pain Assessment and Management*, and the fact bank weights it
accordingly: **assessment exhaustively, management at the level of the rules that
get tested.** This drill set keeps that ratio — six of the ten topics are
assessment.

The item bank for this chapter is built almost entirely on **matching a tool to a
child** — which scale for a preterm infant, which for a 4-year-old, which for a
ventilated child, which for a child with cognitive impairment — and on **the age
boundaries between them.** So the scale topics carry the most questions, and
nearly every one turns on an age range or a score range rather than on what the
tool measures. Two boundaries repay memorizing because they are the hinge of
several items: the infant scales are indexed in **gestational weeks**, not months,
and the self-report scales split at 3, 5, and 8 years.

Everything else is organized around the principle the chapter states outright and
the fact bank calls the single most important sentence in it: **self-report is the
most reliable indicator of pain**, and observational measures are the fallback
when self-report is unavailable. The distractor that inverts this — treating an
observational score as more objective than a verbal child's own report — appears
in several traps below.

**What a `standard` band could not buy.** The fact bank itself excludes opioid
dosing (Table 5.9), the coanalgesic and side-effect-management tables, the
routes-and-methods detail, regional and epidural technique, procedural sedation
levels, and the disease-specific pain sections (abdominal, musculoskeletal,
neuropathic, burn, cancer, sickle cell), which belong to their own body-system
chapters. **No specific mg/kg opioid dose is drilled here**, following the bank's
judgment that a partial dosing table is more dangerous than none. Acetaminophen
and NSAID dosing is kept, because those are the first-step drugs. The
Adolescent Pediatric Pain Tool's scoring detail and the full biobehavioral
classification table are represented by single items rather than enumerated.

Topic: pain physiology and consequences of untreated pain

Q: A nurse hesitates to medicate a postoperative child, reasoning that pain is unpleasant but not harmful. How should this be corrected?
A) The reasoning is sound if the pain is short lived
B) Pain triggers physiologic stress responses with negative consequences involving multiple systems; long-term amplification of the sympathetic nervous system hinders healing, and unrelieved pain may prolong the stress response and adversely affect recovery
C) The concern applies only to neonates
D) Pain becomes harmful only once it becomes chronic
Answer: B
Rationale: "Pain triggers a number of physiologic stress responses in the body, and these may lead to negative consequences involving multiple systems. Long-term amplification of the sympathetic nervous system hinders healing. Unrelieved pain, whether from trauma, surgery, or disease, may prolong stress response and adversely affect a child's recovery" (pp. 137–138).
Trap: (D) reverses the direction of the relationship the chapter draws. Poorly controlled **acute** pain "predisposes patients to chronic pain syndromes" — acute pain left untreated is one of the ways chronic pain begins, not a separate and lesser problem.

Q: A preterm infant who has had many heel sticks now cries and becomes rigid during routine repositioning and diaper changes. What explains this?
A) The infant has become manipulative and is seeking attention
B) The wind-up phenomenon — after noxious stimuli, multiple levels of the spinal cord experience altered excitability, which may cause normal nonpainful stimuli such as routine nursing care and handling to be perceived as noxious
C) Normal irritability of prematurity, unrelated to prior procedures
D) An adverse effect of analgesia
Answer: B
Rationale: The wind-up phenomenon is "attributed to a decreased pain threshold and chronic pain"; after noxious stimuli "multiple levels of the spinal cord experience an altered excitability," which "may cause normal nonpainful stimuli, such as routine nursing care and handling, to be perceived as noxious stimuli" (p. 138). Neonates "have more generalized spinal reflex responses and lower thresholds to stimuli than older children."
Trap: (C) treats an acquired, mechanistic change as constitutional temperament, and it removes the reason to act. Wind-up is why repeated procedural pain in a preterm infant is a compounding problem rather than a series of isolated events.

Q: A nurse describes bandage removal, nasogastric tube placement, and urinary catheter placement as routine rather than painful. How should this be evaluated?
A) Accurate; these are noninvasive and are not associated with pain
B) Inaccurate; the chapter names each of these explicitly as very painful to children, and warns that nurses may find it easy to overlook such experiences as part of normal care
C) Accurate for children over 5 years old
D) Inaccurate only for the nasogastric tube
Answer: B
Rationale: "Nurses may find it easy to overlook certain painful experiences as part of normal care and perform invasive procedures without any attempts to minimize pain. Something as minor and ordinary as bandage removal, nasogastric tube placement, or urinary catheter placement is very painful to children" (p. 138). Premature infants "are particularly vulnerable to repeated acute pain episodes from routine and frequent procedures."
Trap: (A) is the working assumption the chapter is written to dislodge — the risk is not disbelieving a child's pain report but never asking, because the procedure was not categorized as painful in the first place.

Q: A child reports pain at a site where no tissue injury can be identified, and a nurse suggests the pain is not real. How should this be addressed?
A) The nurse is correct; pain requires an identifiable physical source
B) Once pain tracks are created in the brain, it is possible for pain to exist purely in the brain — and that pain is real, and it feels the way the child says it feels, regardless of the location of origin
C) The child should be evaluated for a psychiatric disorder before pain is treated
D) The report should be documented but not treated
Answer: B
Rationale: "Once these pain tracks are created in the brain, it is possible for pain to exist purely in the brain. This pain, however, is real and it feels the way the child says it feels — regardless of the location of origin" (p. 138). The brain neuromatrix is "a series of connected neural cells with cognitive, sensory, and affective pathways combined in a feedback loop unique to each individual."
Trap: (C) makes treatment conditional on ruling out a psychological cause, which reverses the chapter's stance. The origin of the pain does not determine whether it is real or whether it is treated.

Topic: principles of pain measurement

Q: A verbal 9-year-old rates his pain 8 out of 10 while lying quietly and watching television. His FLACC score is 1. Which assessment should guide management?
A) The FLACC score, because behavioral observation is more objective than self-report
B) The child's self-report, because self-report is the single most reliable indicator of pain and must be accepted and respected as the most important pain assessment data available
C) An average of the two scores
D) Neither; repeat both in an hour
Answer: B
Rationale: "Self-report is the single most reliable indicator of pain and must be accepted and respected by nurses as the most important pain assessment data available" (p. 145). Observational measures are the fallback for children who cannot self-report, and "Pain scores on observational measures do not correlate with children's own reports of pain intensity" (p. 144).
Trap: (A) is the central inversion of this chapter and it is the reason quiet children go untreated. An observational tool measures behavioral and physiologic responses, not perceived intensity — the two are different constructs, so a low behavioral score does not overrule a high self-report. (C) treats two incommensurable measures as averageable.

Q: A nurse is deciding whether to use an observational pain measure for a 10-year-old with chronic abdominal pain. What does the chapter say about the tool's validity here?
A) Observational measures are equally valid at all ages and for all pain types
B) Observational measures are most reliable for short, sharp procedural pain such as heel lance or injection; they are less reliable for acute pain and are not reliable or valid for chronic pain or for pain in older children
C) Observational measures are preferred for chronic pain because they avoid reporting bias
D) Observational measures are valid for chronic pain but not procedural pain
Answer: B
Rationale: "Observational pain measures are most reliable when used to measure short, sharp procedural pain, such as pain due to heel lance or injections. They are less reliable when measuring acute pain and not reliable or valid for assessing chronic pain or pain in older children" (p. 144).
Trap: (C) is the convenience answer for a busy unit and it fails on two counts at once — wrong pain type and wrong age. The related reason acute tools fail in chronic pain: "Tools that measure acute pain intensity may not be sensitive to the subtle changes and slow resolution characteristic of chronic and recurrent pain because pain expression becomes more complex."

Q: A child rated 5 on one unit and 5 on another, and a nurse concludes the pain is unchanged. What is the flaw?
A) There is no flaw; a 5 is a 5
B) Scores are not interchangeable — a pain score of 5 may mean a lot of pain on a 0 to 5 scale or very little on a 0 to 100 scale — and consistent use of the same measurement tool is necessary to standardize communication and evaluate effectiveness
C) The second score should have been doubled
D) Pain scores should never be compared across time
Answer: B
Rationale: "A pain score of 5 may mean a lot of pain (if a 0 to 5 scale is used) or very little (if a 0 to 100 scale is used)," and "Consistent use of the same measurement tools is necessary to standardize communication related to pain and evaluate the effectiveness of pain management strategies" (p. 143).
Trap: (D) overcorrects into abandoning serial comparison entirely, which is the whole purpose of consistent tool use. Comparison across time is the point; comparison across *tools* is what fails, and the deeper reason is that different tools measure different pain expressions.

Q: A nurse records only a numeric pain rating at each assessment. What else does the chapter require?
A) Nothing further; intensity is the standard measure
B) Location, frequency, duration, and precipitating, aggravating, and alleviating factors, plus pain's effect on sleep, emotional function, physical recovery or functioning, disability, role functioning, and satisfaction with pain treatment
C) Only the analgesic given and the time
D) Vital signs, which substitute for the additional dimensions
Answer: B
Rationale: "Pain assessment should be more thorough than merely obtaining a pain rating. Understanding the location, frequency, duration, and precipitating, aggravating, and alleviating factors of the pain experienced by the child is also essential," along with pain's effect on the domains listed (p. 143). Other dimensions "may change without a change in pain intensity."
Trap: (D) substitutes physiologic signs for the assessment, which is a version of the same error as trusting a behavioral score over a self-report — vital signs are a response to pain, not a measure of its dimensions.

Topic: observational pain scales

Q: A nurse is assessing pain in a nonverbal 6-month-old. Which finding is the most consistent and specific indicator?
A) Heart rate elevation
B) Facial expression, evaluated through features such as eye squeeze, brow bulge, open mouth, and taut tongue
C) Oxygen saturation
D) Duration of crying
Answer: B
Rationale: "Facial expression is the most consistent and specific indicator of pain in infants," evaluated through features such as "eye squeeze, brow bulge, open mouth, and taut tongue" (p. 143).
Trap: (A) and (C) are physiologic parameters that appear inside several scales as scored variables, which makes them plausible — but neither is singled out as the most consistent and specific indicator, and both change for many reasons other than pain.

Q: A 4-year-old is recovering in the postanesthesia care unit and is too drowsy to self-report. Which tool is validated for this situation?
A) COMFORT-B
B) FLACC
C) Wong-Baker FACES
D) CRIES
Answer: B
Rationale: "The only tool found reliable and valid for pain assessment of children 2 months to 7 years of age recovering in the postanesthesia care unit is the FLACC tool" (p. 145). FLACC scores five behaviors — Face, Legs, Activity, Cry, Consolability — each 0 to 2, for a total of 0 to 10.
Trap: (A) is the right idea in the wrong setting, and it is the item's whole point. COMFORT-B belongs to the critically ill, ventilated, nonverbal child; FLACC is the one with PACU validation. This pair sorts by setting, not by the child's alertness.

Q: A 6-year-old is mechanically ventilated and sedated in the PICU. Which tool is most appropriate, and what score suggests pain?
A) FLACC, with pain considered above 5
B) COMFORT-B, with pain considered above 17 on a 6 to 30 scale
C) Numeric Rating Scale, with pain considered above 4
D) NIPS, with pain considered above 4
Answer: B
Rationale: The COMFORT-B is "one of the most commonly used pain scales to assess critically ill ventilated children who are nonverbal due to sedation or their condition" and "is particularly helpful in children who are mechanically ventilated." Its range is 6–30, and the table's note reads "Consider pain >17" (pp. 144–145). It scores alertness, calmness/agitation, respiratory response or crying, physical movement, muscle tone, and facial tension.
Trap: (A) attaches the FLACC score range to the right kind of child but the wrong tool, and (D) uses an infant scale on a school-age child. Both score ranges and age ranges have to travel with the tool name.

Q: A nurse must choose an infant pain scale for a neonate born at 28 weeks of gestation who is being assessed after surgery. Which scale's age range excludes this infant?
A) PIPP-revised, 25–40 weeks
B) N-PASS, 23–40 weeks
C) CRIES, 32–40 weeks
D) NIPS, 26–40 weeks
Answer: C
Rationale: Table 5.4's age ranges are in **gestational weeks**: PIPP-revised 25–40, NIPS 26–40, N-PASS 23–40, COMFORT-neo 24–42, and CRIES 32–40 (p. 144). At 28 weeks this infant falls below the CRIES range.
Trap: CRIES is otherwise the intuitive pick, because it is the postoperative scale and the stem supplies a postoperative context — which is exactly why the age column has to be read alongside the type-of-pain column. (A) is the reflex rejection, since PIPP-revised sounds like the specialized tool with the narrowest window; its range comfortably includes 28 weeks.

Q: Which infant pain scales adjust the score for gestational age?
A) NIPS and CRIES
B) PIPP-revised and N-PASS
C) COMFORT-neo and CRIES
D) All five adjust for gestational age
Answer: B
Rationale: Of the five scales in Table 5.4, only PIPP-revised and N-PASS carry a gestational-age adjustment (p. 144). PIPP-revised scores heart rate, oxygen saturation, brow bulge, eye squeeze, nasolabial furrow, and behavioral state 0–3 each, for a range of 0–21; N-PASS scores vital signs, crying/irritability, facial expressions, behavioral state, and extremities/tone 0–2 each, for a pain range of 0–10.
Trap: (D) is the reasonable assumption that any scale designed for neonates must account for maturity, and it is wrong for three of the five. Note that CRIES scores Crying, requiring Oxygen, Increased vital signs, Expression, and Sleeplessness — its acronym is the mnemonic, and it is a postoperative tool for the 32-to-40-week infant.

Topic: self-report pain rating scales

Q: A 3-year-old needs a self-report pain scale. Which is validated at this age, and what is the caution?
A) Faces Pain Scale–Revised, which starts at a neutral face
B) Wong-Baker FACES, validated for children as young as 3 years — but smiling faces on pain assessment scales may lead younger children to rate the affective domain of pain rather than its intensity, so simple concrete anchor words should be used
C) Numeric Rating Scale, since 3-year-olds can count
D) Visual Analog Scale, since it is nonverbal
Answer: B
Rationale: Wong-Baker FACES uses "six cartoon faces ranging from smiling face for 'no pain' to tearful face for 'worst pain'" and is "For children as young as 3 years old." The caution: "smiling faces on pain assessment scales may lead younger children to rate the affective domain of pain; or, in other words, rate their feelings about having pain rather than report the intensity of their physical pain," mitigated by using "Simple, concrete anchor words, such as the script originally used to validate the tool" (pp. 145–146).
Trap: (A) is the right instinct about the smiling-face problem applied to the wrong age — the Faces Pain Scale–Revised starts at a neutral face precisely because of that concern, but it is validated for children 5 to 17, not for a 3-year-old.

Q: A 6-year-old needs a faces scale, and the nurse wants to avoid the affect problem. Which scale starts with a neutral rather than a smiling face, and what is its age range?
A) Wong-Baker FACES, 3 years and up
B) Faces Pain Scale–Revised, for children 5 to 17
C) Oucher, 3 to 13 years
D) Word-Graphic Rating Scale, 8 to 17 years
Answer: B
Rationale: The Faces Pain Scale–Revised uses "six cartoon faces ranging from neutral face for 'no pain' to grimacing face 'worst possible pain'" and is for children 5–17 (p. 146).
Trap: (A) is the mirror error of the previous item, and the starting face is the entire discriminator between these two scales — smiling for Wong-Baker, neutral for the revised scale. Both have six faces and both are cartoons, so nothing else separates them at a glance.

Q: A nurse plans to use the Oucher photographic scale with a 5-year-old. What must the child be able to do first?
A) Count off any two numbers or count by 10s
B) Rate six geometric shapes from largest to smallest
C) Read the descriptive words on the scale
D) Nothing; the photographic scale requires no prerequisite
Answer: B
Rationale: The Oucher provides "six photographs of a white child's face representing 'no hurt' to 'biggest hurt you could ever have;' also includes vertical scale with numbers from 0-100; scales for African American and Hispanic children have been developed," for children 3–13. For the photographic scale the child "should be able to rate six geometric shapes from largest to smallest" (p. 146).
Trap: (A) is the prerequisite for the Oucher's **numeric** scale, not its photographic one — the same tool has two halves with two different readiness tests. Note also the chapter's finding on the ethnic versions: an "Ethnically similar scale may not be preferred by child when given choice of ethnically neutral cartoon scale."

Q: An 8-year-old is being asked to rate pain. Which scale is most widely used in clinical practice, and which do children least prefer?
A) The Visual Analog Scale is most widely used; children least prefer the Numeric Rating Scale
B) The Numeric Rating Scale is most widely used because it is easy to use; research shows children ages 3 to 18 least prefer the Visual Analog Scale
C) Both are equally used and equally preferred
D) The Word-Graphic Rating Scale is most widely used
Answer: B
Rationale: "The numeric rating scales (NRSs), such as the 0 to 10 scale, are the most widely used in clinical practice because they are easy to use," and of the VAS: "Research shows that children ages 3-18 years old least prefer VAS compared with other scales" (pp. 146–147). Both are used from about 8 years.
Trap: (A) swaps the two, which is the only way this pair is tested. Both scales share an age floor of about 8 years, so the discriminator is preference and practicality, not developmental readiness.

Q: At what age do the cognitive skills required for a numeric rating scale appear, and can a younger child report pain at all?
A) Seriation appears between 7 and 10 years, so most school-age children can use a numeric scale — and even 2-year-olds can report pain
B) Numeric scales can be used from 3 years, and children under 3 cannot report pain
C) Seriation appears at 5 years, and children under 5 cannot report pain
D) Numeric scales require 12 years of age
Answer: A
Rationale: The cognitive skills of "measurement, classification, and seriation (the ability to accurately place items in rank order), become apparent between 7 and 10 years of age. Therefore most school-age children have the cognitive ability required to use a numeric rating scale." Self-report measures "are most often used for children older than 3 to 4 years, but even 2-year-olds can report pain" (p. 145).
Trap: (B) and (C) both convert a scale's readiness threshold into a claim that younger children cannot report pain. Those are different statements: the age boundary governs which *tool* fits, not whether the child has something to tell you. "Although children at 3 or 4 years of age are able to self-report pain, they do not have the cognitive ability to understand rank order," which is why faces scales exist.

Topic: pain assessment in special populations

Q: A preterm infant lies still and shows no facial grimace during a heel stick. What should the nurse conclude?
A) The infant is not experiencing pain
B) Preterm infants' responses to pain may be behaviorally blunted or absent, so it is imperative to presume that pain exists in all situations usually considered painful for adults and children, even in the absence of behavioral or physiologic signs
C) The observational score should be recorded as zero and no analgesia given
D) The infant has a high pain threshold
Answer: B
Rationale: "Preterm infants' responses to pain may be behaviorally blunted or absent," and the rule that follows is that "it is imperative to presume that pain exists in all situations that are usually considered painful for adults and children, even in the absence of behavioral or physiologic signs" (pp. 144–145).
Trap: (D) inverts the physiology. Neonates have "lower thresholds to stimuli than older children," not higher — the absence of a visible response reflects an immature or overwhelmed capacity to *show* pain, not an absence of feeling it.

Q: A neonate is receiving vecuronium. How should pain be assessed and managed?
A) Behavioral observation scales remain valid because muscle tone is scored
B) An infant receiving a muscle-paralyzing agent is incapable of a behavioral or visible pain response, yet there is ample evidence such infants are neurologically capable of feeling pain — so pain is presumed and treated
C) Paralysis eliminates pain perception, so analgesia is unnecessary
D) Pain should be assessed by heart rate alone
Answer: B
Rationale: "an infant receiving a muscle-paralyzing agent (vecuronium) is incapable of a behavioral or visible pain response; however, there is ample evidence that such infants are neurologically capable of feeling pain" (p. 145).
Trap: (C) is the dangerous version of the same misreading, and it is why the chapter states this case separately from the blunted-response one. A paralytic removes the signal, not the sensation. (A) fails because every behavioral variable, muscle tone included, depends on the movement the drug has abolished.

Q: A nonverbal 8-year-old with severe cognitive impairment is being assessed for postoperative pain. Which tools are validated, and what does the chapter require when using the revised FLACC?
A) FLACC, COMFORT-neo, and CRIES; no customization is needed
B) Revised FLACC, the Non-communicating Children's Pain Checklist–Revised, and the Paediatric Pain Profile — and when using the revised FLACC, nurses must include parent-reported pain behaviors to customize the tool to the individual child
C) Only the Numeric Rating Scale, with parental proxy reporting
D) Wong-Baker FACES, with the parent selecting the face
Answer: B
Rationale: The three validated tools are rFLACC, NCCPC-r, and PPP, and "When using the rFLACC, nurses must include parent-reported pain behaviors to customize the tool to use with individual children" (p. 145). Children with cognitive impairment "may not be able to express pain through behaviors or self-report methods validated for developmentally similar peers," and parents report their pain "was not effectively managed."
Trap: (A) offers a plain FLACC without the revised descriptors, and the difference is not cosmetic — the customization step is what makes the tool valid for this child, because the behaviors that signal pain differ from child to child.

Q: A parent of a child with cerebral palsy reports that physical therapy, assisted stretching, and putting on splints are painful. How should the nurse treat this information?
A) As parental over-reporting, since these are therapeutic rather than noxious activities
B) As accurate and expected: parents of children with cognitive impairment report that ordinary daily activities including assisted stretching and walking, independent standing, toileting, putting on splints, occupational therapy, range of motion, and physical therapy are painful
C) As a reason to discontinue therapy
D) As relevant only if the child also cries
Answer: B
Rationale: Parents reported that these ordinary daily activities were painful, and the most frequent parent-reported pain behaviors include "crying; being less active; seeking comfort; moaning; not cooperating; being irritable; being stiff, spastic, tense, or rigid; sleeping less; being difficult to satisfy or pacify; flinching or moving body part away; and being agitated or fidgety" (p. 145).
Trap: (D) makes crying the gatekeeper for believing the report, when the parent's list contains eleven behaviors and crying is only one — the child who becomes stiff, less active, or hard to pacify is reporting pain in a form a nurse unfamiliar with that child will miss.

Q: A verbal 10-year-old with autism spectrum disorder needs pain assessment. What does the chapter direct?
A) Use an observational tool, since children with autism cannot reliably self-report
B) Use self-report measures whenever possible — ask the verbal child where the pain is located and to describe how it feels; in nonverbal individuals, watch for escalation of repeated self-stimulating activities such as rocking or hand flapping
C) Rely entirely on parental report
D) Use the CRIES scale
Answer: B
Rationale: Because cognitive ability varies widely in autism spectrum disorder, "self-report measures should be used whenever possible. Ask the verbal child with ASD where the pain is located and to describe how it feels." Nonverbal individuals "may have an escalation of repeated self-stimulating activities (i.e., rocking or hand flapping) when in pain" (p. 146).
Trap: (A) generalizes from a diagnosis to a capability, which is exactly what the chapter warns against — ability varies widely, and the default remains self-report. This is the same principle as the chapter's central rule, applied to a population where it is most often set aside.

Topic: chronic and recurrent pain assessment

Q: A child has had daily abdominal pain for four months with significant functional disability and no other explanation. How is this classified?
A) Acute pain, since each episode is brief
B) Chronic primary pain — pain in one or more anatomic regions persisting or recurring for longer than 3 months, associated with significant emotional distress or functional disability, and not better explained by another chronic pain condition
C) Chronic postsurgical pain
D) Nociceptive pain
Answer: B
Rationale: Chronic pain is "Persistent or recurrent pain lasting longer than 3 months," and chronic primary pain adds the emotional distress or functional disability criterion and the exclusion clause (p. 137). The 3-month cutoff runs through every chronic subtype in the definitions table.
Trap: (D) names a mechanism rather than a time course — nociceptive pain is "pain related to tissue injury" — and it is available as a distractor whenever a stem describes a bodily location. Duration is what sorts acute from chronic here.

Q: A nurse is selecting a tool for a child with recurrent headaches and wants a measure of how pain affects everyday physical activities. Which tool fits?
A) Functional Disability Inventory, a comprehensive evaluation of the influence of pain on physical functioning
B) FLACC
C) CRIES
D) Wong-Baker FACES
Answer: A
Rationale: The Functional Disability Inventory is "a more comprehensive evaluation of the influence of pain on physical functioning" and assesses "the child's ability to perform everyday physical activities" (p. 147). For migraine specifically, PedMIDAS is "specifically designed to evaluate pain caused by migraines," and the PedsQL covers physical, emotional, social, and academic functioning for the child younger than 7 years.
Trap: (D) measures intensity, which is exactly what acute-pain tools give and what chronic pain assessment needs more than. The goal of a pain treatment program is "to improve function, quality of life, and a return to daily activities" — so function is the outcome being measured, not a secondary detail.

Q: A child is being followed for chronic pain. What else does the chapter direct the nurse to screen for?
A) Nothing beyond pain intensity and function
B) Depression and anxiety, since chronic and recurrent types of pain often occur with some level of both, using measures such as the Children's Depression Inventory and the Revised Child Anxiety and Depression Scale together
C) Only sleep disturbance
D) Substance use exclusively
Answer: B
Rationale: "Chronic and recurrent types of pain often occur with some level of depression and anxiety in children," and the two named instruments "may be used together to better measure negative affect" (p. 148).
Trap: (C) picks a single real domain out of a broader assessment. Sleep is one of the dimensions the chapter lists for any pain assessment, alongside emotional function, physical functioning, disability, role functioning, and satisfaction with treatment — screening for mood is an addition specific to chronic pain, not a substitute for the rest.

Topic: biobehavioral interventions

Q: A nurse refers to distraction and guided imagery as "nonpharmacologic" interventions. What term does the chapter prefer, and why?
A) Nonpharmacologic is the correct and preferred term
B) Biobehavioral, because it reflects the mechanism of action, whereas "nonpharmacologic" reflects the lack of pharmaceutical treatment as though medications are the gold standard
C) Alternative, because the interventions replace medication
D) Complementary, because they are secondary to medication
Answer: B
Rationale: The term biobehavioral intervention "is preferred over nonpharmacologic because it is a term that reflects the mechanism of action. The term nonpharmacologic reflects the lack of pharmaceutical treatment as though medications are the gold standard" (p. 148). And the chapter adds: "Sometimes biobehavioral interventions are as effective as, or even more effective than, pharmacologic interventions."
Trap: (C) and (D) misuse two terms the chapter defines precisely against each other: "Alternative refers to use of a nonmainstream practice to replace standard medical care. Complementary care is used together with standard medical care," and integrative medicine is "the integration of conventional and biobehavioral approaches."

Q: A nurse plans a pain treatment approach for a postoperative child. Which approach does the chapter identify as preferred?
A) Analgesics alone, with biobehavioral methods reserved for failures
B) Multimodal — combinations of analgesic and biobehavioral therapies — because by targeting different mechanisms of pain, medications combined with biobehavioral interventions are more effective at relieving pain
C) Biobehavioral methods alone, to avoid opioid exposure
D) Whichever the parent prefers, without regard to evidence
Answer: B
Rationale: Multimodal treatment, "also known as combinations of analgesic and biobehavioral therapies, is the preferred pain treatment plan" (p. 149). Biobehavioral interventions work by "interrupting the pain, fear, anxiety, and stress cycle," and they "can help reduce pain perception, make pain more tolerable, decrease anxiety, and enhance the effectiveness of analgesics or reduce the dosage required," with "few if any side effects."
Trap: (A) treats biobehavioral methods as a fallback, which is the ranking the chapter's terminology argument is written against. The mechanism is complementary rather than duplicative — different targets, additive effect.

Q: A nurse wants to teach a school-age child a distraction technique for an upcoming dressing change. When should this be taught?
A) During the procedure, when the child is motivated to use it
B) Before pain occurs or before it becomes severe — and even after learning it, children often need help using it during a painful procedure
C) After the procedure, for use next time
D) Timing is not important as long as the technique is taught
Answer: B
Rationale: "Children should learn to use a specific strategy before pain occurs or before it becomes severe." Even after learning it, "children often need help using it during a painful procedure," and it can be used afterward so "the child [has] a chance to recover, feel mastery, and cope more effectively" (p. 149).
Trap: (A) is the practical instinct and it fails for the same reason the chapter gives for severe pain generally: "Severe and sustained pain is more difficult to control." A technique first encountered mid-procedure has to compete with the distress it is meant to interrupt.

Q: A child cannot name a coping technique she has used before. What should the nurse do?
A) Select a technique for her based on her age
B) Describe several techniques and let the child select one, involving the parents in the selection process because they may be familiar with the child's usual coping style
C) Defer biobehavioral intervention and rely on medication
D) Use the technique most recently taught on the unit
Answer: B
Rationale: If the child cannot name a familiar coping technique, describe several and let the child select. "Parents should be involved in the selection process; they may be familiar with the child's usual coping style," and if the parent cannot assist, "other appropriate persons may include a grandparent, older sibling, nurse, or child-life specialist" (p. 149).
Trap: (A) removes the child from a choice the chapter deliberately gives her, and it discards the parent's knowledge of how this child actually copes. Well-researched, low-risk options the chapter names are biofeedback, guided imagery, hypnosis, mindfulness, and yoga.

Topic: analgesic selection and coanalgesics

Q: A 2-year-old has moderate postoperative pain. According to the WHO two-step strategy, what is the first-line approach?
A) Start morphine, since the pain is postoperative
B) Administer a nonopioid analgesic first; opioids are started when pain is severe or unrelieved by nonopioids, and morphine is the medicine of choice for the second step
C) Start with codeine and escalate if needed
D) Start a benzodiazepine to reduce anxiety and reassess
Answer: B
Rationale: "For children older than 3 months of age the first step is to administer a nonopioid analgesic. Opioids are started when pain is severe or unrelieved by nonopioids. Morphine is the medicine of choice for the second step" (p. 151). The WHO principles are "Using a two-step strategy; Dosing at regular intervals; Using the appropriate route of administration; Adapting treatment to the individual child."
Trap: (D) substitutes an anxiolytic for an analgesic, which the chapter rules out by name: "Benzodiazepines are frequently used to treat anxiety; however, these drugs do not provide analgesia and should not be used as a substitute for analgesics." It also raises real risk — "The concomitant use of opioids and sedatives, such as diazepam, is associated with increased risk of death."

Q: A child needs an analgesic with antiinflammatory action. Which statement is accurate?
A) Acetaminophen has antipyretic, analgesic, and antiinflammatory actions
B) NSAIDs are antipyretic, antiinflammatory, and analgesic; acetaminophen has antipyretic and analgesic actions but no antiinflammatory action
C) Both have equivalent antiinflammatory effect
D) Neither drug class has antiinflammatory action
Answer: B
Rationale: "NSAIDs are known for their antipyretic, antiinflammatory, and analgesic actions. Acetaminophen has antipyretic and analgesic actions, but no antiinflammatory action" (p. 151). Nonopioids "are usually the first analgesics for pain related to tissue injury, also known as nociceptive pain."
Trap: (A) is the reflex pick because the two drugs are used interchangeably for fever and mild pain, and the shared indications hide the one property that differs. A stem asking for an antiinflammatory and naming acetaminophen is wrong.

Q: A 4-month-old needs an oral analgesic for mild pain. Which is appropriate?
A) Ibuprofen 5-10 mg/kg/dose every 6-8 hours
B) Acetaminophen 10-15 mg/kg/dose every 4-6 hours, not to exceed five doses in 24 hours or 75 mg/kg/day
C) Choline magnesium trisalicylate 25 mg/kg every 12 hours
D) Morphine, as the second-step drug of choice
Answer: B
Rationale: Acetaminophen is dosed at "10-15 mg/kg/dose q 4-6 h PO not to exceed five doses in 24 h or 75 mg/kg/day, or 3-4000 mg/day" (p. 151).
Trap: (A) is the correct ibuprofen dose attached to a child too young for it — ibuprofen is for "Children >6 months old," and a 4-month-old falls below that floor. The dose being right is what makes the option pull; the age gate is the item.

Q: A prescriber considers codeine for a child's postoperative pain. What should the nurse know?
A) Codeine remains a preferred pediatric opioid because of its favorable safety profile
B) In 2017 the FDA issued a black box warning on the use of codeine in children because of reported deaths; children with CYP2D6 combinations associated with poor metabolism may get no analgesia, while those with ultrarapid metabolism are at risk of morphine toxicity and death
C) Codeine is contraindicated only in children under 2 years
D) The concern applies only to children with hepatic impairment
Answer: B
Rationale: "Children with CYP2D6 allele combinations associated with poor metabolism of codeine may get no analgesia, whereas children with combinations associated with ultrarapid metabolism are at risk of morphine toxicity and death," and "In 2017, the US Food and Drug Administration (FDA) issued a black box warning on the use of codeine in children because of reported deaths" (p. 152).
Trap: (A) is the historical answer — codeine "was once a commonly used opioid in children" — and it is what makes this a live error rather than an academic one. Note the double-edged nature of the pharmacogenetics: the same gene produces both a treatment failure and a fatal overdose, in opposite directions.

Q: EMLA cream is ordered before a venipuncture. What should the nurse know about its use?
A) It works within 10 to 15 minutes of application
B) It is a eutectic mixture of lidocaine 2.5% and prilocaine 2.5%, must be administered at least an hour before the painful intervention, and is used with caution in neonates
C) It may be applied to broken skin for faster onset
D) It is contraindicated in all children under 1 year
Answer: B
Rationale: EMLA is "a eutectic mixture of local anesthetics: lidocaine 2.5% and prilocaine 2.5%"; it is "effective in children when applied to intact skin, but must be administered at least an hour before the painful intervention," and is used with caution in neonates (p. 157). Table 5.14 gives its onset as 1 hour and duration as 4 hours, with methemoglobinemia as an adverse effect.
Trap: (A) collapses the planning problem this drug creates — an hour of lead time is what makes EMLA useless for an unplanned stick and is why the faster products in Table 5.14 exist. Note the contrasting product for acute versus chronic use: the fentanyl transdermal patch "is contraindicated for acute pain management" and is approved only "for use in opioid-tolerant children over 12 years of age who require chronic continuous opioid treatment."

Topic: opioid side effects tolerance and withdrawal

Q: A child receiving morphine becomes difficult to arouse. The nurse notes the respiratory rate is slightly increased and concludes the child is comfortable and sleeping. How should this be evaluated?
A) Correct; an increased respiratory rate rules out respiratory depression
B) Incorrect and dangerous — respiratory depression progresses predictably: the patient first becomes sedated and unarousable, then depth of respirations decreases while rate increases, then end-tidal carbon dioxide rises, oxygen saturation falls, and respiratory rate decreases and then ceases
C) Correct; sedation is the expected therapeutic effect
D) Incorrect only if the child has an opioid allergy
Answer: B
Rationale: Respiratory depression "is rare, but is the most serious adverse effect from analgesics," and the chapter gives the sequence exactly as stated. The nursing implication: "Sedation from opioids is to be avoided and nurses must increase their monitoring vigilance while patients sleep" (pp. 157–158).
Trap: (A) and (C) are the two halves of the same reassuring misreading, and both are specifically wrong. Unarousable sedation is the **first** step of the sequence, not a sign of comfort, and the rate rise is an early compensatory change rather than evidence of adequate ventilation.

Q: A child on scheduled opioids for 3 days develops constipation. What does the chapter direct?
A) Increase dietary fiber and add a stool softener, which is usually sufficient
B) Prevention with peristaltic agents and laxatives is more effective than treatment once constipation occurs, and dietary treatment such as increased fiber and stool softeners is usually not sufficient
C) Withhold the opioid until bowel function returns
D) No intervention; constipation resolves after 2 days of regular opioid administration
Answer: B
Rationale: "Prevention of constipation with peristaltic agents and laxatives is more effective than treatment once constipation occurs. Dietary treatment, such as increased fiber and the use of stool softeners, is usually not sufficient" (p. 159). Encourage fluids and physical activity, and "Gum chewing has also been used to effectively increase peristalsis."
Trap: (D) applies a real fact to the wrong side effect. Nausea, vomiting, pruritus, and sedation "usually subside after 2 days of regular opioid administration" — constipation does not, which is why it is the one that needs a standing preventive order.

Q: A child on morphine develops itching. A nurse plans to give diphenhydramine. How should this be evaluated?
A) Appropriate; opioid pruritus is histamine mediated
B) Diphenhydramine is often erroneously used for opioid-related pruritus — appreciable relief is the result of sedation rather than the antihistamine effect; low-dose opioid agonists such as naloxone or nalbuphine are used, and ondansetron is also effective
C) Appropriate, but only in combination with naloxone
D) Inappropriate; opioid pruritus requires stopping the opioid
Answer: B
Rationale: Pruritus is treated "with low-dose opioid agonists such as naloxone or nalbuphine." "Diphenhydramine is often erroneously used to treat opioid-related pruritus; however, appreciable relief is the result of sedation rather than the antihistamine effect." Ondansetron is also effective (p. 159).
Trap: (A) is the standard reasoning for pruritus of most causes, and here it produces a drug that "works" for the wrong reason — adding sedation to a patient already at risk of opioid-induced sedation is the specific harm this correction is aimed at.

Q: A child has received opioids for 7 days and is ready for discontinuation. What should the nurse anticipate?
A) Abrupt discontinuation is safe at this duration
B) Withdrawal symptoms can be anticipated and prevented by weaning patients from opioids that were administered for more than 5 days
C) Withdrawal is a concern only after 30 days of therapy
D) Withdrawal indicates addiction and warrants a psychiatric referral
Answer: B
Rationale: "Withdrawal symptoms can be anticipated and prevented by weaning patients from opioids that were administered for more than 5 days" (p. 159). The Withdrawal Assessment Tool–1 is used in children 7 months to 10 years old.
Trap: (D) confuses physical dependence with addiction. Physical dependence is "a normal, natural, physiologic state of 'neuroadaptation'" in which abrupt discontinuation produces withdrawal; it is neither tolerance — needing a higher dose for the same effect — nor psychologic dependence. Infants "may be at risk for physical tolerance and physical dependence but not psychologic dependence or addiction."

Q: A child is being weaned from opioids after 8 days of therapy. Which findings indicate **initial** withdrawal, as Box 5.3 separates it from later withdrawal and from the drug's own side effects? (Select all that apply.)
A) Lacrimation and rhinorrhea
B) Yawning
C) Sweating
D) Restlessness
E) Dilated pupils, piloerection, and seizure
F) Miosis and constipation
Answer: A, B, C, D
Rationale: Box 5.3 sorts these findings into distinct columns. Initial withdrawal is "Lacrimation; rhinorrhea; yawning; sweating; restlessness." Later withdrawal adds "Irritability; tremors; anorexia; dilated pupils; piloerection; nausea, vomiting; abdominal cramping, diarrhea; seizure." The general side effects of opioids are a third column entirely, including "miosis (may be sign of toxicity)," constipation, sedation, pruritus, and orthostatic hypotension (p. 159).
Trap: (F) is the direction-of-error pick, and it is the sharpest discrimination in the box — **miosis** sits among the side effects and "may be sign of toxicity," while **dilated pupils** belong to later withdrawal, so the pupils point opposite ways depending on which process is underway. (E) is genuine Box 5.3 content from the wrong column: real withdrawal signs, but the later ones, so an option can be a true withdrawal finding and still be the wrong answer to this stem. Note that none of these is tolerance, which shows instead as "Decreasing pain relief; Decreasing duration of pain relief" and is treated by increasing the dose or shortening the interval.

Topic: needlestick pain prevention

Q: A 2-month-old infant needs a heel stick. Which is the most effective biobehavioral intervention to reduce needlestick pain?
A) Swaddling
B) Breastfeeding
C) Expressed breast milk given by bottle
D) A pacifier without sucrose
Answer: B
Rationale: "The most effective biobehavioral intervention to reduce needlestick pain in infants is breastfeeding" (p. 159). Other effective methods are 24% sucrose or glucose and skin-to-skin care in infants, comfort holds for toddlers and small children, and deep breathing, distraction, hypnosis, and virtual reality for children and adolescents.
Trap: (C) is the near-miss the chapter names precisely: "Breastfeeding, but not breast milk, is as effective as sucrose and skin-to-skin care." The act, not the substance, is what works — so expressing milk into a bottle forfeits the effect.

Q: A parent asks whether the distraction the child-life specialist used actually reduced her son's pain during the blood draw. What is the accurate answer?
A) Distraction directly reduces needle pain and is the treatment of choice
B) There is strong evidence such strategies manage distress and help children cope with needle pain, but no evidence they prevent, reduce, or relieve the pain itself — local anesthetics are the most effective pharmacologic agents for that
C) Distraction has no demonstrated benefit of any kind
D) Distraction is effective only in children under 5
Answer: B
Rationale: The chapter draws this distinction explicitly: these strategies "are effective interventions to manage distress and help children and adolescents cope with needle pain, but there is no evidence these interventions prevent, reduce, or relieve needle pain." And: "Local anesthetics are the most effective pharmacologic agents for preventing and reducing needlestick pain" (p. 159).
Trap: (A) and (C) are the two overstatements in opposite directions, and (A) is the more common one. Coping and analgesia are different outcomes; a child who was calm may still have been in pain.

Q: A nurse is preparing to give two vaccines to a toddler. Which actions reflect the evidence-based strategies to reduce needlestick pain? (Select all that apply.)
A) Avoid aspiration and use a rapid injection technique
B) Administer the least painful vaccine first
C) Avoid the supine position if possible, and use a position of comfort
D) Consider hypnosis or age-appropriate distraction
E) Offer verbal reassurance, empathy, and apology throughout
Answer: A, B, C, D
Rationale: Box 5.4 lists each keyed strategy, along with simultaneous vaccine administration where possible, breastfeeding or skin-to-skin care for small infants, and topical numbing in appropriate age groups (p. 159).
Trap: (E) is the one to reject, and the fact bank flags it as counterintuitive and heavily tested: "Caregivers and nurses should avoid verbal reassurance, empathy, and apology. Caregivers can be encouraged to use physical soothing and coaching." What replaces reassurance is touch and coaching, not silence.

Q: An unplanned intravenous start is needed on a 3-year-old in the next few minutes. Which needlestick pain product's onset makes it unsuitable, and which would work in that window?
A) EMLA is unsuitable at a 1-hour onset; a cold vibration device works at a 15-second onset
B) The cold vibration device is unsuitable at a 1-hour onset; EMLA works in 2 minutes
C) A sucrose pacifier is the fastest option at 15 seconds
D) All four products have equivalent onset times
Answer: A
Rationale: Table 5.14 gives the onsets: EMLA 1 hour (4-hour duration), needle-free jet injection with buffered lidocaine 10–30 seconds (1–4 hours), sucrose pacifier 2 minutes (10 minutes), and cold vibration device 15 seconds (limit use to 3 minutes). The cold vibration device is for children 1 year and older, with cold tolerance assessed before use (p. 159).
Trap: (B) swaps EMLA's onset with the fastest product's, which is the only way this table is tested. The spread from 15 seconds to a full hour is the point of the column — the product is chosen by how much warning you had, not by potency.

Q: A nurse selects a sucrose pacifier for pain relief during immunization of a 9-month-old. How should this be evaluated?
A) Appropriate; sucrose is effective at any age under 2 years
B) Sucrose is recommended in children under 6 months of age, so this infant is above the recommended band — though a trial did find sucrose reduced distress in 16- to 19-month-olds during immunizations
C) Inappropriate; sucrose is never used after the newborn period
D) Appropriate only if combined with EMLA
Answer: B
Rationale: Box 5.4 states "Sucrose recommended in children under 6 months of age," and Table 5.14 gives the sucrose pacifier's age range as newborn to 6 months. The chapter also notes a trial in which sucrose reduced distress in 16- to 19-month-olds during immunizations (p. 159).
Trap: (C) overcorrects past the evidence the chapter itself reports. Note the two discriminations worth holding from Table 5.14: methemoglobinemia belongs to EMLA alone, and the sucrose pacifier is the only product capped at 6 months while both the jet injector and the cold vibration device start at 1 year and older.
