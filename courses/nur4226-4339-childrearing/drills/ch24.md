# Chapter 24 — The Child With Renal Dysfunction

Source: `courses/nur4226-4339-childrearing/sources/Ch24_Fact_Bank.md`
(Hockenberry, Wilson & Rodgers, *Wong's Nursing Care of Infants and Children*, 11th ed.,
Chapter 24, printed pp. 778–824. Chapter authors Patricia A. Ring and Cynthia J. Camille.)

**Blueprint weight: ~6 questions of 202, depth `standard`.** Band is 30–45 questions; this
file carries **43** across **9 topics**, averaging 4.8 each.

**Where the weight went.** The chapter's central comparison is acute poststreptococcal
glomerulonephritis against minimal change nephrotic syndrome, which the book sets out itself in
Table 24.6 and which is the single most reliably tested discrimination in pediatric renal
nursing; both diseases get a full topic. Urinary tract infection gets the largest share because
it is the most common disorder here and because the guideline specifics — specimen method,
freshness window, the pyuria requirement — are exactly the procedural detail an item bank uses.
Acute kidney injury is built around hyperkalemia, since the chapter states outright that
potassium is the most immediate threat.

**Drilled from both ends:** glomerulonephritis against nephrotic syndrome on blood pressure,
red blood cell casts, and serum lipids; the two latent periods, throat against skin; lower
against upper urinary tract infection; primary against secondary reflux; hyperkalemia in acute
kidney injury against hyperkalemia in chronic kidney disease, where the urgency reverses;
communicating against noncommunicating hydrocele; and measures that *shift* potassium against
those that *remove* it.

**The chapter's own review question 4 is not drilled as the source keys it.** Its answer key
marks as needing correction a statement that appears verbatim as correct instruction in the
text, while leaving uncorrected two statements the text contradicts outright. The fact bank
records the discrepancy rather than resolving it, so the post-repair items below are built from
the text's own rules — tub baths avoided until the catheter is removed, and fluids encouraged
rather than rationed — and nothing was written from the unreliable key.

**What ~6 blueprint items could not buy.** Left undrilled: the diagnostic-test tables and the
blood tests of renal function; renal tubular acidosis and nephrogenic diabetes insipidus; the
chronic and rapidly progressive glomerulonephritides; Alport syndrome and renal trauma; the
dialysis and transplantation procedures beyond their indications; disorders of sex development;
obstructive uropathy; and Table 24.5's renal involvement in systemic disease. The dialysis
modalities are the largest real loss, since the chapter distinguishes three of them by process.

Topic: urinary tract infection risk, diagnosis, and specimen collection

Q: A 9-month-old uncircumcised boy is being evaluated for fever without a source. What does the chapter say about his risk of urinary tract infection?
A) About 11% of uncircumcised male infants develop a urinary tract infection in the first year, compared with 0.1% of circumcised male infants
B) Circumcision status does not affect the first-year infection rate
C) Circumcised infants have the higher rate, because the exposed meatus is colonized more readily
D) The difference exists but is confined to infants older than 12 months
Answer: A
Rationale: The chapter gives the figures directly — "11% of uncircumcised male infants developing a UTI in the first year of life compared with 0.1% of circumcised male infants" (p. 786) — and notes that the foreskin is "associated with increased periurethral colonization with uropathogens."
Trap: (C) inverts the finding; note that the highest baseline prevalence overall is in uncircumcised males younger than 3 months and females younger than 12 months, so age and sex both qualify the risk.

Q: A urine culture from a 3-year-old grows 60,000 colonies of a single organism, and the urinalysis shows no white blood cells. How should this be interpreted?
A) This does not meet the diagnostic definition, because diagnosis requires both pyuria and at least 50,000 colonies per milliliter
B) This meets the definition, because the colony count exceeds 50,000 per milliliter
C) This meets the definition, because a single organism was isolated
D) This is diagnostic of pyelonephritis rather than cystitis
Answer: A
Rationale: The chapter states that "Diagnosis of UTI is made based on the presence of BOTH pyuria AND at least 50,000 colonies per ml of a single uropathic organism in an appropriately collected specimen," and that "The key to distinguishing true UTI from asymptomatic bacteriuria is the presence of pyuria" (pp. 785, 787).
Trap: (B) and (C) each satisfy one half of a two-part definition, which is precisely how asymptomatic bacteriuria gets misread as infection; pyuria is defined as at least 10 white blood cells per milliliter of uncentrifuged urine.

Q: A nurse is obtaining a urine specimen from a 4-year-old girl with recurrent infections. Which actions are correct? Select all that apply.
A) Have the child sit on the toilet facing the tank
B) Ensure the specimen reaches the laboratory within 1 hour at room temperature, or within 4 hours if refrigerated
C) Obtain the culture before any antimicrobial agent is given
D) Encourage the child to drink large volumes of water so a specimen can be obtained quickly
E) Accept a positive bag specimen as sufficient for diagnosis
Answer: A, B, C
Rationale: The chapter's Nursing Tip directs the facing-backward position because "The labia are naturally separated, decreasing the likelihood of contamination"; the specimen "must be fresh (<1 hour after voiding with storage at room temperature or <4 hours after voiding with refrigeration)"; and the guideline for febrile children 2 to 24 months is to obtain culture and urinalysis before an antimicrobial is administered (p. 787).
Trap: (D) reverses a stated rule, since "a recent high fluid intake may indicate a falsely low organism count"; (E) reverses the bag-specimen rule, which runs the counterintuitive direction — a *negative* bag urinalysis may be sufficient, while a *positive* one still requires catheterization or suprapubic aspiration.

Q: A 6-year-old has a temperature of 39.1 degrees Celsius, chills, and flank tenderness. How should the nurse interpret this?
A) These suggest pyelonephritis, since the presence of a fever typically implies upper tract involvement
B) These suggest cystitis, since flank tenderness is referred from the bladder
C) These cannot be localized without imaging
D) These suggest cystitis, since fever is common in lower tract infection
Answer: A
Rationale: The chapter assigns fever above 38 degrees Celsius, chills, and flank pain to the upper tract and states that "presence of a fever typically implies pyelonephritis," while the lower tract produces frequency and painful urination of small volumes with "fever usually absent or low grade" (p. 786).
Trap: (C) waits for imaging where the chapter localizes clinically, and it is worth noting the chapter's caution that "flank pain and tenderness may be the ONLY indication of pyelonephritis on physical examination," so an incomplete picture does not exclude it.

Q: The mother of a 4-year-old with recurrent urinary tract infections asks whether bath time is to blame. What should the nurse advise?
A) Plain tub baths have not been shown to increase risk, but the essential oils in bubble baths and shampoos irritate, and infections have been linked to hot tub or whirlpool use
B) All baths should be replaced with showers, since immersion introduces bacteria
C) Bathing has no relationship to urinary tract infection in children
D) Plain tub baths are the main modifiable risk factor and should be stopped
Answer: A
Rationale: The chapter separates these explicitly: "There is no evidence that plain tub baths increase the risk of UTI, but infections have been related to the use of hot tub or whirlpool baths," and lists "essential oils in bubble baths and shampoos" among the extrinsic contributors (p. 786).
Trap: (D) is the advice families most often receive and it is not what this chapter says; the single most important host factor named is urinary stasis, and the chapter emphasizes that "Failure to relieve the fecal retention in spite of adequate treatment of the UTI may result in recurrence."

Q: A child with febrile urinary tract infection and suspected pyelonephritis is prescribed nitrofurantoin. What should the nurse recognize?
A) Nitrofurantoin is inappropriate here, because it does not achieve therapeutic concentrations in the blood or kidney
B) Nitrofurantoin is appropriate and should be given on an empty stomach
C) Nitrofurantoin is appropriate, since it is concentrated in the urine where the infection is
D) Nitrofurantoin is inappropriate because of photosensitivity in febrile children
Answer: A
Rationale: The chapter states that "Nitrofurantoin is not used for treatment of febrile infants or patients where pyelonephritis is suspected because it is excreted in the urine and does not achieve therapeutic concentrations in the blood or kidney" (pp. 787–789).
Trap: (C) states the drug's real pharmacology and draws the wrong conclusion from it, which is what makes it the strongest distractor — urinary concentration is exactly why the drug works for cystitis and prophylaxis and fails for an upper tract infection; (B) and (D) misassign side effects, since nitrofurantoin is given with food or milk and photosensitivity belongs to trimethoprim-sulfamethoxazole.

Topic: vesicoureteral reflux

Q: A child is found to have grade II vesicoureteral reflux. What should the nurse tell the parents about the likely course?
A) About 80% of grade II reflux resolves spontaneously
B) About 30% of grade II reflux resolves spontaneously
C) Spontaneous resolution does not occur at any grade
D) About 13% of grade II reflux resolves spontaneously
Answer: A
Rationale: The chapter gives spontaneous resolution rates of 82%, 80%, 46%, 30%, and 13% for grades I through V (p. 791).
Trap: (B) and (D) are the grade IV and grade V figures, and the reason the item matters is that the rate falls off a cliff between grades II and III — four in five for the low grades, fewer than one in two from grade III onward.

Q: A child has reflux caused by high bladder pressure from posterior urethral valves. How is this classified, and what governs treatment?
A) Secondary reflux, and treatment focuses on the underlying problem rather than on correcting the reflux
B) Primary reflux, and treatment focuses on surgical reimplantation of the ureter
C) Secondary reflux, and treatment focuses on correcting the reflux surgically
D) Primary reflux, and treatment is limited to antibiotic prophylaxis
Answer: A
Rationale: Secondary reflux results from abnormally high bladder pressure — posterior urethral valves, dysfunctional voiding, or neurogenic bladder — and in secondary reflux "treatment is typically focused on the underlying problem and not correction of the VUR" (p. 791).
Trap: (C) keys the right category with the wrong management, which is the half-right answer this pair invites; primary reflux by contrast is a congenital anomaly of the ureterovesical junction, a shortened intravesical segment that cannot be compressed.

Q: A parent asks whether her child's reflux caused the kidney scarring seen on imaging. What does the chapter support?
A) Reflux increases the chance of febrile infection, but is neither necessary nor sufficient to cause pyelonephritis and renal scarring
B) Reflux is the direct cause of renal scarring in children
C) Reflux has no relationship to febrile urinary tract infection
D) Scarring occurs only in children with grade V reflux
Answer: A
Rationale: The chapter states that "VUR is neither necessary nor sufficient to cause acute pyelonephritis and renal scarring in children," while still increasing the chance of febrile infection (p. 791).
Trap: (B) overstates what the book claims and is the reading most parents arrive with; the chapter names the actual scarring risk factors as an abnormal renal ultrasound, or the combination of a high fever at or above 39 degrees Celsius with a causative organism other than *Escherichia coli*.

Q: A child has had a first febrile urinary tract infection at 14 months. Which imaging does the chapter recommend?
A) A renal ultrasound, with a voiding cystourethrogram reserved for an abnormal ultrasound or a second febrile infection
B) A voiding cystourethrogram immediately, to grade any reflux present
C) No imaging after a first infection at any age
D) A nuclear renal scan, which is the preferred initial study
Answer: A
Rationale: Current guidelines recommend a renal ultrasound after an initial febrile urinary tract infection in a child 2 to 24 months old, and "An abnormal renal ultrasound or a second febrile UTI warrants a VCUG" (pp. 787–789).
Trap: (B) is the older sequence and exposes the child to a catheterized study that the guideline now defers; note also that the RIVUR trial showed prophylaxis "substantially reduced risk of recurrence of UTI with urinary prophylaxis, but no significant effect on renal scarring."

Topic: acute poststreptococcal glomerulonephritis

Q: A 6-year-old had impetigo about 4 weeks ago and now has periorbital edema and cola-colored urine. Is the interval consistent with poststreptococcal glomerulonephritis?
A) Yes, because the latent period after a skin infection is 3 to 6 weeks
B) No, because the latent period is 1 to 2 weeks after any streptococcal infection
C) No, because skin infections do not precipitate this disease
D) Yes, but only if a throat culture is also positive
Answer: A
Rationale: The chapter states that "A latent period of 1 to 2 weeks occurs between a streptococcal infection of the throat, or 3 to 6 weeks between a skin infection and the onset of clinical manifestations" (p. 792).
Trap: (B) applies the throat interval to a skin history, which is the swap this pair invites; (D) overvalues the culture, since "Pharyngeal cultures are positive in only a few cases" and serologic tests such as the antistreptolysin O titer are what establish antecedent infection.

Q: A nurse is assessing a child being evaluated for acute glomerulonephritis. Which findings are consistent with the diagnosis? Select all that apply.
A) Mild to moderate blood pressure elevation, although severe hypertension may occur
B) Urinalysis showing hematuria, proteinuria, and increased specific gravity
C) Red blood cell casts on microscopy
D) Elevated blood urea nitrogen and creatinine in at least half of cases
E) Serum cholesterol between 450 and 1500 mg/dl
Answer: A, B, C, D
Rationale: The chapter describes "a mild to moderate elevation in blood pressure compared with normal values for age, although severe hypertension may be present," urinalysis showing hematuria, proteinuria, and increased specific gravity with granular and red blood cell casts, and azotemia "in at least 50% of cases" (pp. 792–794).
Trap: (E) is the nephrotic syndrome laboratory picture, and Table 24.6 keeps serum lipids normal in glomerulonephritis and elevated in nephrotic syndrome — lipids, blood pressure direction, and red blood cell casts are the three cleanest single discriminators between the two diseases.

Q: A child with acute glomerulonephritis develops headache, dizziness, abdominal discomfort, and vomiting. What should the nurse suspect?
A) Premonitory signs of hypertensive encephalopathy
B) Gastroenteritis complicating the illness
C) Early uremic syndrome from acute kidney injury
D) Side effects of the antibiotic prescribed for the streptococcal infection
Answer: A
Rationale: The chapter names headache, dizziness, abdominal discomfort, and vomiting as the premonitory signs of hypertensive encephalopathy, in which severe hypertension causes autoregulation of cerebral blood flow to fail, leading to hyperperfusion and cerebral edema (p. 793).
Trap: (B) is the most natural misreading of the gastrointestinal complaints, and the chapter forecloses it by making cerebral assessment a stated nursing priority — "Assessment of the child's appearance for signs of cerebral complications is an important nursing function because the severity of the acute phase is variable and unpredictable."

Q: A child with acute glomerulonephritis develops an enlarged heart and increased pulmonary vascular markings. What is the mechanism?
A) Hypervolemia rather than cardiac failure
B) Primary myocardial failure from streptococcal toxin
C) Pericardial effusion from the immune-complex deposition
D) Cardiomyopathy secondary to the hypoalbuminemia
Answer: A
Rationale: The chapter states directly that "Hypervolemia, NOT cardiac failure, causes cardiac decompensation" in this disease (p. 793).
Trap: (B) and (D) each supply a plausible-sounding cardiac mechanism, and the consequence of picking either is treating the myocardium instead of the volume; note also that the first sign of improvement overall "is an increase in urinary output with a corresponding decrease in body weight as edema resolves."

Q: A child with acute glomerulonephritis and moderate oliguria is placed on dietary restrictions. Which restriction does the chapter direct?
A) Sodium is restricted, and protein is restricted only for severe azotemia from prolonged oliguria
B) Protein is restricted in all cases to reduce the nitrogen load
C) Both protein and fluid are restricted from diagnosis regardless of output
D) No dietary restriction is needed in any case of glomerulonephritis
Answer: A
Rationale: The chapter directs a regular diet in uncomplicated cases with "no salt added to foods" for hypertension or edema, restricts potassium-rich foods during oliguria, and states that "Protein restriction is reserved only for children with severe azotemia resulting from prolonged oliguria" (pp. 794–795).
Trap: (B) applies a restriction the chapter explicitly reserves, which would compromise nutrition in most children with this disease; note also that antibiotics here do not alter the course of the nephritis and are given to prevent transmission of nephritogenic streptococci to the family.

Topic: nephrotic syndrome

Q: A 3-year-old has generalized severe edema, massive proteinuria, a serum albumin of 1.8 g/dl, and a normal blood pressure. Which condition does this describe, and which single finding best separates it from acute glomerulonephritis?
A) Minimal change nephrotic syndrome, and the absence of red blood cell casts
B) Acute glomerulonephritis, and the severity of the edema
C) Minimal change nephrotic syndrome, and the presence of periorbital edema
D) Acute glomerulonephritis, and the low serum albumin
Answer: A
Rationale: Table 24.6 assigns normal or decreased blood pressure, generalized severe edema, massive proteinuria, markedly decreased serum protein, elevated lipids, and absent red blood cell casts to minimal change nephrotic syndrome, and elevated blood pressure with red blood cell casts present to glomerulonephritis (p. 793).
Trap: (C) picks the one finding both diseases share, since the edema of both "is more prominent in the face in the morning" — which is exactly why a stem gives only that detail and expects a different discriminator to be used.

Q: A child with nephrotic syndrome has been on prednisone for 3 weeks. His parents report a rounded face, increased appetite, abdominal distention, and new facial hair, and ask whether the disease is worsening. What should the nurse explain? Select all that apply.
A) Rounding of the face is a steroid effect, not disease edema
B) Increased appetite and behavior changes are steroid effects
C) Hirsutism is a steroid effect that will gradually disappear after the drug is stopped
D) Abdominal distention may reflect either steroid effect or ascites, so the child's weight and girth guide the answer
E) These findings indicate the disease is relapsing and the steroid dose should be increased
Answer: A, B, C, D
Rationale: The chapter directs parents to distinguish steroid effects — "rounding of the face, increased appetite, behavior changes, abdominal distention, and hirsutism" — from the disease's own edema, and to be reassured that these "will disappear gradually after discontinuation of the drug"; monitoring includes daily weight and abdominal girth (pp. 798–799).
Trap: (E) reads drug effects as disease activity and escalates the drug causing them, which is the error the teaching exists to prevent; note that diuresis follows within 7 to 21 days of starting steroids and about 90% of children achieve remission on the initial course.

Q: A child has had two relapses of nephrotic syndrome while on steroids. How is this classified?
A) Steroid dependent
B) Steroid resistant
C) Frequent relapse
D) Steroid sensitive
Answer: A
Rationale: The chapter defines steroid dependence as two consecutive relapses while on steroids or within 2 weeks of stopping them, frequent relapse as two or more in 6 months or four or more in 12 months, and steroid resistance as no remission after 4 weeks of prednisone (Box 24.6, p. 797).
Trap: (B) is the term most often substituted, and the distinction matters clinically — steroid-dependent children relapse repeatedly and accumulate steroid exposure with cushingoid features and growth retardation, while steroid-resistant children "are thought to have a high risk of developing chronic kidney disease."

Q: A 4-year-old with nephrotic syndrome is due for immunizations while receiving prednisone. What should the nurse know?
A) Live vaccines are withheld during steroid therapy, but pneumococcal conjugate and polysaccharide vaccines are specifically recommended in addition to the usual schedule
B) All vaccines are withheld until steroids are discontinued
C) Live vaccines may be given, since the steroid dose in nephrotic syndrome is not immunosuppressive
D) Only the influenza vaccine is recommended beyond the usual schedule
Answer: A
Rationale: The chapter directs that in addition to usual vaccinations, PCV13 and PPSV23 should be given, and that "Live vaccines should not be given while the child is on steroid therapy" (pp. 797–798).
Trap: (B) withholds the pneumococcal protection this child most needs, since the chapter stresses susceptibility to "cellulitis, pneumonia, peritonitis, or sepsis"; both halves of this rule get tested, and giving only one is the usual error.

Q: The parent of a child with nephrotic syndrome in remission asks whether her son may return to school. What should the nurse advise?
A) He may attend school, while avoiding contact with infected playmates
B) He should be kept home until the urine has been protein free for 6 months
C) He should be kept home whenever he is receiving any steroid dose
D) He may attend school with no precautions, since remission removes the infection risk
Answer: A
Rationale: The chapter states that "Nurses should instruct parents regarding avoiding contact with infected playmates, but the child MAY attend school," and directs home urine testing daily while on medication or during illness and twice a week during remission (pp. 798–799).
Trap: (B) and (C) both house-bound a child the chapter explicitly does not, and (D) drops the precaution that remains real — these children stay particularly vulnerable to upper respiratory tract infection, and spontaneous peritonitis can occur from bacteria migrating across the bowel wall.

Topic: hemolytic uremic syndrome

Q: A 2-year-old develops pallor, bruising, and oliguria several days after bloody diarrhea. Which triad would confirm the suspected diagnosis?
A) Acute kidney injury, hemolytic anemia, and thrombocytopenia
B) Acute kidney injury, hemolytic anemia, and leukopenia
C) Acute kidney injury, thrombocytopenia, and a prolonged prothrombin time
D) Hemolytic anemia, thrombocytopenia, and hypertension
Answer: A
Rationale: The chapter states that "The triad of anemia, thrombocytopenia, and renal failure is sufficient for diagnosis" of hemolytic uremic syndrome (p. 800).
Trap: (B) and (C) each substitute a different hematologic abnormality for thrombocytopenia, which is the member most often replaced; the mechanism is that red cells are damaged passing through partially occluded glomerular arterioles and platelets aggregate within the damaged vessels.

Q: A nurse is teaching a family about preventing the infection that most often precedes hemolytic uremic syndrome. Which instructions are correct? Select all that apply.
A) Cook ground beef to an internal temperature of at least 74 degrees Celsius, measured with a meat thermometer
B) Do not rely on the absence of pink color as evidence that ground beef is safe
C) Avoid giving children unpasteurized apple juice
D) Avoid unwashed raw vegetables
E) Give an antimotility drug at the first sign of diarrhea to limit fluid loss
Answer: A, B, C, D
Rationale: The Nursing Alert directs an internal temperature of at least 74 degrees Celsius measured with a thermometer, warns that "Cooking the ground beef until no pink color is seen may not be sufficient to kill the bacteria," and discourages unpasteurized apple juice and unwashed raw vegetables (p. 801).
Trap: (E) is the action the same alert discourages, and the reason is mechanistic — slowing transit keeps the Shiga toxin in contact with the gut. The organism responsible in more than 90% of cases is Shiga toxin–producing *Escherichia coli*, most often O157:H7.

Q: A child with hemolytic uremic syndrome has been anuric for 24 hours. What does the chapter identify as the most effective treatment?
A) Early dialysis or continuous hemofiltration
B) Heparin to interrupt the intravascular coagulation
C) High-dose corticosteroids
D) Fibrinolytic therapy to clear the microthrombi
Answer: A
Rationale: The chapter states that "The most consistently effective treatment is early hemodialysis, PD, or continuous hemofiltration," instituted in any child anuric for 24 hours or with oliguria plus uremia or hypertension and seizures (p. 801).
Trap: (B), (C), and (D) are the three the chapter rules out together — "There is no substantial evidence that heparin, corticosteroids, or fibrinolytic agents are beneficial, and in some instances they may aggravate the condition"; each is attractive because it appears to target the pathophysiology directly.

Q: The parents of a child recovering from hemolytic uremic syndrome ask about long-term outlook. What should the nurse tell them?
A) Survival in the diarrhea-positive form is about 95%, but residual renal impairment ranges from 10% to 50%
B) Survival is about 95% and renal recovery is complete in nearly all survivors
C) Survival is about 50%, and survivors rarely have lasting renal impairment
D) Long-term outcome cannot be estimated for this condition
Answer: A
Rationale: The chapter states that "the survival rate of D+ HUS is approximately 95%, but residual renal impairment ranges from 10% to 50%" (p. 801).
Trap: (B) keeps the survival figure and drops the caveat that shapes follow-up care, which is the half-answer families are most often given; the condition is also named as one of the main causes of acute kidney injury in early childhood, occurring primarily between 6 months and 3 years.

Topic: acute kidney injury and hyperkalemia

Q: A child in acute kidney injury has a serum potassium of 7.2 mEq/L, a sodium of 135 mEq/L, a hemoglobin of 8 g/dl, and a urine output of 2 ml/kg/hr. Which finding requires immediate attention?
A) The serum potassium
B) The serum sodium
C) The hemoglobin
D) The urine output
Answer: A
Rationale: The chapter names a serum potassium in excess of 7 mEq/L as an emergency to report immediately, and states that "An elevated serum potassium level is the most immediate threat to the life of the child with AKI" (pp. 804–805).
Trap: (B), (C), and (D) are all normal or reassuring and are designed to look abnormal in context — a sodium of 135 is within range, transfusion is not recommended unless hemoglobin drops below 6 g/dl, and 2 ml/kg/hr is good output rather than the oliguria this disease is defined by, which is less than 1 ml/kg/hr.

Q: A child with hyperkalemia and electrocardiographic changes receives intravenous calcium gluconate. What does this accomplish?
A) It exerts a protective effect on cardiac conduction without lowering the serum potassium
B) It binds potassium in the gut for elimination
C) It shifts potassium from the extracellular to the intracellular compartment
D) It increases renal potassium excretion
Answer: A
Rationale: The chapter states that calcium gluconate given intravenously over 2 to 4 minutes with continuous electrocardiographic monitoring "exerts a protective effect on cardiac conduction," and separately that the first three measures "produce only transient protection by REDISTRIBUTING existing potassium stores; they do not remove potassium from the body" (pp. 804–805).
Trap: (C) describes what sodium bicarbonate and glucose-with-insulin do, and (B) describes sodium polystyrene sulfonate; a question asking what *protects the heart* and one asking what *eliminates* potassium have different answers, and calcium answers only the first.

Q: A fluid-overloaded, hypertensive child in acute kidney injury is prescribed sodium polystyrene sulfonate. What should the nurse anticipate?
A) A sodium ion is exchanged for each potassium ion, which may worsen fluid overload, hypertension, and cardiac failure
B) The drug removes potassium without any sodium exchange
C) The drug shifts potassium intracellularly and must be repeated every 2 hours
D) The drug is contraindicated because it cannot remove potassium from the body
Answer: A
Rationale: The chapter gives the dose as 1 g/kg orally or rectally and notes that "a sodium ion is exchanged for each potassium ion," which "may contribute to fluid overload, hypertension, and cardiac failure" (pp. 804–805).
Trap: (D) inverts the drug's role, since this agent and dialysis are the only two measures the chapter credits with actually removing potassium; (B) drops the trade-off that makes it a considered choice rather than a free one in this particular child.

Q: A child with acute kidney injury has continued oliguria. Which findings would indicate dialysis? Select all that apply.
A) Severe, persistent acidosis
B) Inability to reduce serum potassium to a safe range by other methods
C) Nausea, vomiting, and drowsiness progressing toward coma
D) Circulatory overload with hypertension and evidence of cardiac failure
E) A single serum creatinine above the reference range
Answer: A, B, C, D
Rationale: The chapter lists continued oliguria associated with severe persistent acidosis, inability to reduce potassium by other methods, clinical uremic syndrome, or circulatory overload with hypertension and cardiac failure as the indications for dialysis (p. 805).
Trap: (E) names a laboratory abnormality present in essentially every case of this disease and therefore useless as a threshold; note that plasma creatinine is described as the more sensitive measure of the intensity of azotemia, which is what makes it tempting here.

Q: A toddler develops acute kidney injury after 3 days of severe gastroenteritis. How should the nurse classify the cause, and what does it imply?
A) Prerenal, and the azotemia is rapidly reversible with prompt expansion of the extracellular fluid volume
B) Intrinsic renal, and prolonged management will be required
C) Postrenal, and relief of obstruction will restore function
D) Prerenal, and permanent tubular damage has already occurred
Answer: A
Rationale: The chapter identifies prerenal causes as reduced perfusion of an anatomically normal kidney, names dehydration from diarrheal disease or persistent vomiting as "the most common cause of prerenal failure in infants and children," and states that this azotemia "is rapidly reversible with prompt attention to expansion of the extracellular fluid volume" (pp. 802–803).
Trap: (D) keys the right category with the wrong prognosis, which is what makes it the closest miss; note also that mannitol is not scientifically justified for preventing acute kidney injury and diuretics are not recommended to prevent or treat it, only to aid management of volume overload.

Topic: chronic kidney disease and its systemic effects

Q: At what point does the chapter say chemical composition of body fluids can no longer be maintained, and at what point is dialysis or transplantation required?
A) When more than 50% of functional renal capacity is destroyed, and when the glomerular filtration rate falls below 10% to 15% of normal
B) When 25% of capacity is destroyed, and when the filtration rate falls below 50% of normal
C) When any measurable loss occurs, and when the filtration rate falls below 30% of normal
D) When more than 50% of capacity is destroyed, and when the filtration rate falls below 50% of normal
Answer: A
Rationale: The chapter states that "The kidneys are able to maintain the chemical composition of fluids within normal limits until MORE THAN 50% of functional renal capacity is destroyed," and that treatment with dialysis or transplantation "is required when the glomerular filtration rate decreases below 10% to 15% of normal" (p. 806).
Trap: (D) keeps the first threshold and inflates the second, which is the more common error because a 50% filtration rate sounds severe; the clinical consequence of the real figure is that children remain chemically compensated far longer than intuition suggests.

Q: A nurse is reviewing the mechanism of bone disease in chronic kidney disease. Which statement is accurate?
A) Diseased kidneys cannot complete synthesis of vitamin D to 1,25-dihydroxycholecalciferol, which decreases calcium absorption and contributes to secondary hyperparathyroidism
B) Diseased kidneys excrete excess phosphate, which lowers serum phosphate and leaches calcium from bone
C) Parathyroid hormone secretion falls, reducing calcium mobilization from bone
D) Bone disease in chronic kidney disease results from calcium deposition rather than demineralization
Answer: A
Rationale: Box 24.8 describes the chain: acidosis dissolves alkaline bone salts, reduced phosphate excretion elevates plasma phosphate and lowers serum calcium, the fall in calcium drives parathyroid hormone secretion and bone resorption, and the kidneys cannot complete vitamin D synthesis to its active form (p. 807).
Trap: (B) reverses the phosphate handling, which is the step the whole cascade turns on; the resulting condition is renal osteodystrophy, sometimes called renal rickets because the disorganized bone growth resembles vitamin D–resistant rickets.

Q: A school-age child is being followed for chronic kidney disease. Which finding does the chapter describe as often the first measurable sign?
A) Falling behind on the growth chart
B) A rising serum creatinine
C) Hyperkalemia with electrocardiographic changes
D) Gross hematuria
Answer: A
Rationale: The chapter states that "Growth is affected early in the development of CKD, and falling behind on the growth chart is often the first measurable sign," and that the first symptom may be loss of normal energy and increased fatigue on exertion (pp. 807–808).
Trap: (C) transfers the acute kidney injury priority to the wrong disease, and the chapter draws the contrast itself — "Dangerous hyperkalemia is uncommon in CKD until the end stage," which is the opposite of its urgency in acute injury.

Q: A 14-year-old girl with chronic kidney disease has not menstruated for 8 months. Her mother asks whether this is related. What should the nurse explain?
A) Secondary amenorrhea commonly develops in girls past puberty with chronic kidney disease
B) Menstrual changes are unrelated to renal disease and warrant a separate workup first
C) Amenorrhea occurs only in girls receiving dialysis
D) Sexual maturation is accelerated rather than delayed in chronic kidney disease
Answer: A
Rationale: The chapter states that sexual maturation may be delayed or absent and that "secondary amenorrhea commonly develops in girls past puberty," with decreased estrogen, luteinizing hormone, and progesterone causing anovulation and menstrual irregularities (p. 807).
Trap: (D) inverts the direction; note that this is one of the four named systemic consequences of chronic kidney disease, alongside bone demineralization, anemia, and growth failure, and each has its own box in the chapter.

Topic: male genitourinary tract defects

Q: The mother of a 2-year-old uncircumcised boy asks whether she should be pulling the foreskin back to clean beneath it. What should the nurse advise?
A) The foreskin should not be forcibly retracted, because it may create scarring that prevents future retraction
B) The foreskin should be fully retracted daily from birth to prevent infection
C) The foreskin should be retracted only if the child has had a urinary tract infection
D) Failure to retract by age 2 indicates a need for circumcision
Answer: A
Rationale: The chapter states that phimosis is "a normal finding in infants and young boys and usually disappears as the child grows and the distal prepuce dilates," and that "The foreskin should not be forcibly retracted because it may create scarring that can prevent future retraction" (p. 815).
Trap: (B) is the advice that produces the very scarring the rule prevents, and it can also cause paraphimosis, in which the retracted foreskin cannot be replaced — a urologic emergency requiring immediate evaluation.

Q: An infant has painless scrotal swelling that enlarges when he cries and is smaller in the morning. How should the nurse classify this?
A) A communicating hydrocele, from a patent processus vaginalis
B) A noncommunicating hydrocele, since it is painless
C) An incarcerated inguinal hernia
D) Testicular torsion
Answer: A
Rationale: A communicating hydrocele arises because the processus vaginalis did not close, "allowing for communication with the peritoneum," and "may change in size during the day or with straining," whereas a noncommunicating hydrocele is "not reducible and so do not change in size with crying or straining" (p. 815).
Trap: (B) uses the painlessness, which both types share, as the discriminator; the size change is what separates them, and it matters because a communicating hydrocele persisting past 1 year is repaired "because of the increased risk of development of an incarcerated inguinal hernia."

Q: A full-term 4-month-old has a testis that has not descended. What should the nurse tell the parents about the likely course?
A) Spontaneous descent is probable during the first 6 months of life, after which the probability decreases
B) Spontaneous descent is unlikely at any age and surgery should be scheduled now
C) Spontaneous descent commonly occurs up to 2 years of age
D) Undescended testes in full-term infants always descend without intervention
Answer: A
Rationale: The chapter states that spontaneous descent is "probable during the first 6 months of life with correction for prematurity" and that "If persistent after 6 months, probability of spontaneous descent decreases," with prevalence of 30% in preterm and 3% in full-term boys (pp. 815–816).
Trap: (C) extends the window past the point where watchful waiting is justified; note that congenital hernias and abnormal testes often accompany cryptorchid testes and that they are at risk for subsequent torsion, which is why the clock matters.

Q: A newborn has hypospadias with an incomplete foreskin. The parents ask about circumcision before discharge. What should the nurse explain?
A) Neonatal circumcision is avoided, because the foreskin may be needed for the surgical repair
B) Circumcision should be performed, since it reduces the infection risk after repair
C) Circumcision is unrelated to the repair and may proceed on the family's preference
D) Circumcision is performed at the same time as the repair at 6 to 12 months
Answer: A
Rationale: The chapter states that "Neonatal circumcision should be avoided in hypospadias where there is incomplete foreskin," because the foreskin may be needed for reconstruction, and gives 6 to 12 months as the preferred age for repair, before the child has developed body image (p. 817).
Trap: (C) treats a tissue the surgeon may need as a matter of parental preference, which is the practical consequence of missing this rule; note that cryptorchidism is present in about 10% of infants with hypospadias, and that severe hypospadias carries an increased risk of disorders of sex development.

Q: A nurse is describing the exstrophy-epispadias complex to a student. Which sequence correctly orders the three anomalies from least to most severe?
A) Epispadias, bladder exstrophy, cloacal exstrophy
B) Bladder exstrophy, epispadias, cloacal exstrophy
C) Cloacal exstrophy, bladder exstrophy, epispadias
D) Epispadias, cloacal exstrophy, bladder exstrophy
Answer: A
Rationale: The chapter presents the three in ascending severity: epispadias as failure of the urethra to close, leaving an exposed dorsal urethra; bladder exstrophy as an open inside-out bladder with epispadias and pubic diastasis; and cloacal exstrophy as bladder exstrophy plus hindgut exstrophy, anal atresia, omphalocele, and often spinal dysraphism (p. 818).
Trap: (C) reverses the order entirely, which is easy to do because cloacal sounds less dramatic than exstrophy; note that in bladder exstrophy "the upper urinary tract is usually normal," and pubic separation is generally corrected by pelvic osteotomy.

Topic: hypospadias and its postoperative care

Q: A 10-month-old is being discharged after hypospadias repair with a stent in place. Which instructions are correct? Select all that apply.
A) The drainage bag is always positioned below the level of the bladder
B) Tub baths are avoided until the catheter is removed
C) Straddle toys, sandboxes, swimming, and rough activities are avoided until the surgeon allows them
D) Fluids are encouraged, partly to counter the constipating effect of the anticholinergic medication
E) Fluids are rationed to prevent overloading the repaired urethra
Answer: A, B, C, D
Rationale: The chapter directs that "Drainage bags should always be positioned below the bladder level," that "Tub baths are avoided until the catheter is removed," that "The child should avoid straddle toys, sandboxes, swimming, and rough activities until allowed by the surgeon," and that because "Anticholinergic medication is constipating," constipation is prevented with adequate fluid and a stool softener or laxative (pp. 817–818).
Trap: (E) reverses the fluid instruction and is the statement the text contradicts most directly. Note that the chapter's own review question keys option (C) here as needing correction even though it appears verbatim as correct instruction in the text; the fact bank records that discrepancy, and these items follow the text rather than the key.

Q: A toddler with a catheter after hypospadias repair arches his back, draws his knees to his chest, and leaks urine around the catheter. What should the nurse do?
A) Recognize bladder spasms, which are expected, and administer the ordered oxybutynin
B) Report the leaking as evidence of catheter displacement
C) Irrigate the catheter to clear an obstruction
D) Remove the catheter, since leaking indicates it is no longer needed
Answer: A
Rationale: The chapter describes bladder spasms as "common and are very uncomfortable" with a catheter in place, treated with oxybutynin every 8 hours and possibly every 6, and gives this exact picture — the child "may arch his back and bring his knees to his chest and may leak urine around the catheter with a spasm" (pp. 817–818).
Trap: (B) and (D) both read an expected finding as a catheter problem, which is what the description exists to prevent; the medication is stopped once the catheter is removed.

Q: The parents of a child 2 days after hypospadias repair are alarmed that the penis is swollen, discolored, and bruised. What should the nurse tell them?
A) This appearance is expected postoperatively and will resolve with time
B) This suggests infection and the surgeon should be notified urgently
C) This suggests compromised blood supply to the repair
D) This indicates the dressing was applied too tightly and should be removed
Answer: A
Rationale: The chapter states that "the appearance of the penis postoperatively is often swollen, discolored, and/or bruised. This is expected and will resolve with time," and directs that parents be prepared for it in advance (pp. 817–818).
Trap: (B) and (C) each treat a normal appearance as a complication, and the reason the chapter prepares families ahead of discharge is precisely that this is the finding they call about; note that a caudal or penile nerve block lasts 6 to 8 hours and scheduled analgesia continues for 48 to 72 hours.

Q: A nurse is reviewing the goals of hypospadias repair with a family. Which objectives does the chapter state?
A) To allow voiding standing with a straight stream, to improve the appearance of the genitalia for psychologic reasons, and to preserve a sexually adequate organ
B) To prevent urinary tract infection, to allow circumcision later, and to correct associated cryptorchidism
C) To restore normal urinary continence and prevent reflux
D) To allow voiding standing and to normalize testosterone production
Answer: A
Rationale: The chapter states the three surgical objectives verbatim: "(1) to enhance the child's ability to void in the standing position with a straight stream, (2) to improve the physical appearance of the genitalia for psychologic reasons, and (3) to preserve a sexually adequate organ" (p. 817).
Trap: (B) and (C) each substitute plausible urologic goals the chapter does not claim for this operation; note that the complications of repair the chapter does name are urethrocutaneous fistula, urethral stricture or meatal stenosis, and urethral diverticulum.

Q: A 32-kg child requires maintenance intravenous fluid. What is the 24-hour requirement by the method the chapter gives?
A) 1740 ml
B) 1640 ml
C) 3200 ml
D) 1500 ml
Answer: A
Rationale: Box 24.4 gives the Holliday-Segar method as 100 ml/kg/day for the first 10 kg, 50 ml/kg/day for the second 10 kg, and 20 ml/kg/day for each additional kilogram — so 1000 plus 500 plus 240 equals 1740 ml/day (p. 790).
Trap: (B) results from applying 20 ml/kg to 11 rather than 12 kilograms, and (D) from stopping after two tiers; the method has three tiers, and dropping one is the usual arithmetic failure. The chapter notes this formula is for parenteral replacement and may overestimate actual intake, since fluid also comes from food.
