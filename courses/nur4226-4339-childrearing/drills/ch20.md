# Chapter 20 — Impact of Cognitive or Sensory Impairment on the Child and Family

Source: `courses/nur4226-4339-childrearing/sources/Ch20_Fact_Bank.md`
(Hockenberry, Wilson & Rodgers, *Wong's Nursing Care of Infants and Children*, 11th ed.,
Section VIII, Chapter 20, printed pp. 632–657. Chapter author Rosalind Bryant.)

**Blueprint weight: ~6 questions of 202, depth `standard`.** Band is 30–45 questions; this
file carries **43** across **10 topics**, averaging 4.3 each.

**Where the weight went.** This chapter is four conditions plus one nursing framework —
cognitive impairment, Down syndrome, fragile X, hearing loss, vision loss, and autism — and
the framework (assess, teach concretely in one-step units, promote development, normalize,
prevent) is stated once and reused for all of them. The topics follow the conditions rather
than the framework, because a red topic has to name a thing to go restudy and "promoting
development" would name four different sections at once.

**The chapter's most testable material is numeric**, so a disproportionate share of these
items turn on a figure: the fragile X repeat bands, the hearing severity bands, the acuity
cut-offs for partial sight and legal blindness, the amblyopia prevention window, and the
screening and referral ages. Every such figure is quoted from the fact bank, never estimated.

**The Down syndrome topic here deliberately leaves out the cytogenetics.** Chapter 3 already
carries `down syndrome configurations and recurrence risk`, which drills the three
configurations, the translocation carrier recurrence figures, and the maternal-age inversion.
Re-drilling those under a second topic name would split one restudy unit across two dashboard
entries, so this chapter's topic is named for what Chapter 3 does not cover — the associated
conditions, the surveillance schedule, and the atlantoaxial controversy.

**Drilled from both ends:** conductive versus sensorineural loss (loudness versus distortion,
and the treatment difference that follows); myopia versus hyperopia with their lens types;
cued speech versus sign language; hearing aid versus
cochlear implant; chemical burn versus penetrating eye injury, whose rules are exact
opposites.

**What ~6 blueprint items could not buy.** Left undrilled: Box 20.2's full clinical
manifestations of Down syndrome (eight groupings, reproduced complete in the fact bank and
far too long for the budget); Box 20.3's fragile X manifestations; Box 20.5's cataracts and
glaucoma entries; the ultraviolet-burn and foreign-object entries of the eye-injury box; the
thimerosal evidence box and its QSEN competencies; the noise-exposure prevention material and
its Nursing Alert; combined hearing and visual impairment with finger spelling; sexuality
teaching and future-care planning; and the ASD comorbidity and medication list. The three
aphasia/agnosia/dysacusis definitions survived because the fact bank flags them as a
three-way set whose distractors write themselves; the glaucoma and cataract findings did not,
and that is the single largest real loss in this file.

**Two source defects were followed, not reproduced.** The chapter prints *permutation* where
*premutation* is meant (p. 639); the item uses the intended word. And the chapter's own
review item asserts that parents of a child with Down syndrome generally believe the
experience made them stronger — a claim no sentence in this chapter supports — so nothing was
drilled from it.

**Select-all count: 6** (1 in 7.2), each built on a source list that genuinely sorts two ways:
Box 20.1's early signs against non-members, the respiratory measures for the Down syndrome
infant, Box 20.4's manifestations against ordinary temperament, the lipreading guidelines
against their opposites, the penetrating-injury actions against the chemical-burn actions,
and the ASD hospitalization measures against generic pediatric comfort. Box 20.5 and the
hearing severity table are lookup grids rather than two-way sorts and were written as
single-answer items.

Topic: cognitive impairment definition and diagnosis

Q: A nurse is reviewing the record of a 10-year-old being evaluated for intellectual disability. The child's intelligence testing and adaptive behavior assessment both show significant limitations. What third element must be present for the diagnosis?
A) Onset before 18 years of age
B) A documented genetic or metabolic cause
C) A specific intelligence quotient below a stated cutoff
D) Evidence that the limitations have persisted for at least 2 years
Answer: A
Rationale: The American Association on Intellectual and Developmental Disabilities defines intellectual disability as consisting of "three components: (1) intellectual functioning, (2) adaptive behavior, and (3) age younger than 18 years at time of diagnosis" (p. 632).
Trap: (B) pulls because the chapter spends most of its length on identifiable syndromes, but the definition is functional rather than etiologic; (C) is the error the next item addresses, since no intelligence quotient cutoff is attached to any level anywhere in this chapter.

Q: A student nurse states that intellectual disability is diagnosed on the basis of the intelligence quotient score. How should the nurse correct this?
A) Current criteria move away from relying exclusively on intelligence testing and require deficits in adaptive functioning as well
B) Intelligence testing is accurate but must be repeated at least twice before the diagnosis is made
C) Intelligence testing has been replaced entirely by measures of adaptive behavior
D) The diagnosis rests on intelligence testing, but adaptive behavior determines the level of support needed
Answer: A
Rationale: The chapter states that DSM-5 criteria "recommend moving away from exclusively relying on IQ testing toward using additional measures of adaptive functioning," and the definition requires "significant limitations in both intellectual functioning and adaptive behavior" (p. 632).
Trap: (C) overshoots by discarding intelligence testing, which the chapter still names as how intellectual functioning is measured, and (D) demotes adaptive behavior to a support-planning tool when it is one of the two required legs of the diagnosis.

Q: A nurse is screening a 9-month-old whose mother reports that the baby "just is not like my first one." Which findings are listed as early signs suggestive of cognitive impairment? Select all that apply.
A) A dysmorphic syndrome such as Down syndrome or fragile X syndrome
B) Major organ system dysfunction such as feeding or breathing difficulty
C) Language difficulties or delay
D) Irritability or nonresponsiveness to the environment
E) A birth weight below the tenth percentile for gestational age
Answer: A, B, C, D
Rationale: Box 20.1 lists seven early signs — dysmorphic syndromes, irritability or nonresponsiveness to environment, major organ system dysfunction, gross motor delay, fine motor delay, language difficulties or delay, and behavior difficulties (p. 632).
Trap: (E) is the plausible non-member, since growth restriction sounds like a developmental red flag; the two members most often missed are (A) and (B), because they are physical findings in a box that readers remember as a list of developmental delays.

Q: The same mother adds, "My sister says I should stop comparing my children." How should the nurse respond?
A) Take the comparison seriously and proceed with developmental assessment of this child
B) Reassure the mother that all children develop differently and comparison can be harmful
C) Arrange to meet the older sibling to identify familial patterns of development
D) Defer assessment until the child's next well visit to see whether the differences persist
Answer: A
Rationale: The chapter states that "Parental concerns, such as delayed development compared with siblings, need to be taken seriously" (p. 633), and the same rule recurs for hearing and for vision — parental concern is sufficient grounds for evaluation at any age (pp. 642, 647).
Trap: (B) is the reflex reassurance and is the one this chapter names and rejects; it sounds supportive and it ends the assessment, which is why it is the keyed distractor in the chapter's own review item.

Topic: teaching and discipline for the child with cognitive impairment

Q: A nurse is teaching a 12-year-old with cognitive impairment to perform a simple dressing change. Which approach is most consistent with how this child learns?
A) Demonstrate the procedure one step at a time and reinforce each step as it is mastered
B) Explain the reason each step is performed so the child understands why the dressing matters
C) Give the full sequence of steps at the start so the child can see how they fit together
D) Provide written instructions the child can refer to between sessions
Answer: A
Rationale: The chapter names four learning deficits and their teaching implications: "demonstration is preferable to verbal explanation," learning should aim at "mastering a skill rather than understanding the scientific principles underlying a procedure," short-term memory limits mean the child needs "simple, one-step directions," and reinforcement is built into the task (p. 633).
Trap: (B) and (C) are each the direct opposite of a stated rule, and (B) pulls hardest because explaining the rationale is good teaching in nearly every other context; here the chapter says to teach the skill, not the reason.

Q: The parents of a 7-year-old with cognitive impairment whose mental age is about 4 years ask how to set limits. What should the nurse advise?
A) Make the limits simple and consistent and match them to the child's mental age
B) Match the limits to the child's chronologic age so expectations stay age appropriate
C) Explain after each incident why the behavior was wrong so the child learns from the mistake
D) Delay formal limit setting until the child's comprehension improves
Answer: A
Rationale: The chapter directs that limit-setting measures "need to be simple, consistently applied, and appropriate for the child's mental age," and adds that "Discipline must begin early" (p. 636).
Trap: (C) is the moral-reasoning distractor the chapter names explicitly — "Stressing moral lessons is of little value to a child who lacks the cognitive skills to learn from self-criticism" (p. 636) — and (B) swaps mental age for chronologic age, which is the single most likely discipline error in this chapter.

Q: Parents report that their child with cognitive impairment greets visitors politely at home but does not do so at the clinic or at school. What should the nurse explain?
A) There is no automatic transfer of learning from one situation to another, so the skill must be practiced in each setting
B) The behavior at home is likely prompted by the parents rather than genuinely learned
C) Inconsistent performance indicates the skill was taught before the child was developmentally ready
D) Social behaviors typically generalize over time and no additional teaching is needed
Answer: A
Rationale: The chapter instructs parents to "expose the child to strangers so that he or she can practice manners because there is no automatic transfer of learning from one situation to another" (p. 636).
Trap: (D) assumes generalization, which is exactly the assumption the chapter corrects; (C) reads inconsistency as a readiness problem and would delay the practice the chapter says is the intervention.

Q: A nurse is advising the parents of an 8-year-old with cognitive impairment about clothing and play. Which guidance is correct?
A) Choose clothing appropriate to the child's chronologic age and choose play activities suited to the developmental age
B) Choose both clothing and play activities suited to the child's developmental age
C) Choose both clothing and play activities appropriate to the child's chronologic age
D) Choose clothing suited to the developmental age and play activities appropriate to the chronologic age
Answer: A
Rationale: The chapter says clothes should be "clean, age appropriate, and well fitted with self-adhering fasteners and elastic openings to facilitate self-dressing" (p. 636), while play is "based on the child's developmental age" (p. 634).
Trap: (D) is the exact inversion and is the reason this item exists — dressing a child in clothing for their mental age undercuts the social acceptance the chapter says grooming is for, while matching play to chronologic age sets the child up to fail.

Topic: down syndrome associated conditions and surveillance

Q: A newborn has low-set ears, upward-slanting palpebral fissures, and marked hypotonia, and the pediatrician states that Down syndrome is very likely. The parents ask why blood is being drawn if the doctor is already sure. What should the nurse explain?
A) The diagnosis can usually be made from the clinical features alone, but chromosome analysis confirms it and identifies which cytogenetic form is present
B) Chromosome analysis is the only reliable means of diagnosis, because clinical features are unreliable in the newborn period
C) The chromosome study screens for the leukemia and thyroid disease associated with the condition
D) Confirmation is deferred until 6 months of age, when the characteristic features become fully apparent
Answer: A
Rationale: The chapter states that Down syndrome "can usually be diagnosed by the clinical manifestations alone, but a chromosome analysis should be done to confirm the genetic abnormality" (p. 637), and the karyotype also distinguishes trisomy 21 from translocation, which is the hereditary form.
Trap: (B) makes the karyotype diagnostic rather than confirmatory, and (C) misassigns its purpose — leukemia and thyroid dysfunction are real associations but are not what a karyotype detects; the reason the study matters to this family is what it says about recurrence.

Q: The parents of an 18-month-old with Down syndrome ask whether it is really necessary to treat every ear infection, since the child seems to recover without antibiotics. How should the nurse respond?
A) Treatment is needed to prevent auditory loss, which can in turn influence cognitive function
B) Treatment is needed because otitis media in Down syndrome progresses to mastoiditis more often than in other children
C) Treatment can be withheld for mild episodes, since recurrent otitis media is expected in this population
D) Treatment is needed primarily to prevent the child from developing chronic pain
Answer: A
Rationale: The chapter directs that "treatment of otitis media is required to prevent auditory loss, which can influence cognitive function" (p. 637), and lists evaluation of sight and hearing among the essential surveillance for these children.
Trap: (C) is plausible because recurrent otitis media genuinely is common here, and the chapter's answer is that its commonness is the reason to treat it rather than a reason to tolerate it; the chain the chapter gives runs infection to hearing loss to cognition, which is why the stakes are higher than the ear.

Q: A nurse is teaching new parents of an infant with Down syndrome about the risks of the first year. What should the nurse identify as the chief cause of death in this period?
A) Respiratory tract infection combined with congenital cardiac anomalies
B) Leukemia, which occurs with increased incidence in this population
C) Congenital hypothyroidism if it is not detected on newborn screening
D) Spinal cord compression from atlantoaxial instability
Answer: A
Rationale: The chapter states that respiratory tract infections "when combined with cardiac anomalies, are the chief causes of death, particularly during the first year of life," attributing the vulnerability to hypotonicity of the chest and abdominal muscles and immune dysfunction (p. 637).
Trap: (B) and (C) are both real associated conditions the chapter names, which is what makes them plausible, but neither is identified as a leading cause of death; the chapter names the combination and names the first year.

Q: The parents of an 8-year-old with Down syndrome are told by the school that a cervical spine x-ray is required before their daughter can compete in Special Olympics, but their pediatrician did not order one at the well visit. How should the nurse explain this?
A) The American Academy of Pediatrics no longer recommends screening asymptomatic children, but the Special Olympics still requires neck x-rays before sports participation
B) The pediatrician has made an error, since screening is recommended for all children with Down syndrome
C) The school has made an error, since screening is no longer recommended by any organization
D) Screening is required only for children who have already reported neck pain or weakness
Answer: A
Rationale: The chapter gives both positions in one paragraph — the Academy "no longer recommends screening asymptomatic children with Down syndrome for atlantoaxial instability with cervical spine x-rays," while "the Special Olympics continues to require that all athletes with Down syndrome receive neck x-rays before sports participation" — and adds that recommendations "remain varied" (p. 638).
Trap: (B) and (C) each take one authority's position and state it as the universal rule, which is exactly the error the chapter's two-sided paragraph guards against; about 15% of these children have the instability and almost all are asymptomatic, which is why the question is contested at all.

Q: A 10-year-old with Down syndrome is brought in with persistent neck pain, and his mother reports he has begun wetting himself after years of being continent. What is the nurse's priority action?
A) Report the findings immediately as possible spinal cord compression
B) Obtain a urine specimen to evaluate for urinary tract infection
C) Reassure the mother that regression is common during periods of stress
D) Schedule a routine follow-up appointment for a cervical spine x-ray
Answer: A
Rationale: The chapter's Nursing Alert directs the nurse to "Immediately report any child with the following signs of spinal cord compression": persistent neck pain, loss of established motor skills and bladder or bowel control, and changes in sensation (p. 638).
Trap: (B) and (C) each explain the incontinence away, and (C) is especially seductive because regression under stress is real elsewhere in pediatrics; the discriminator is that this is a *loss of an established* skill paired with neck pain, which is the exact pattern the alert exists for.

Topic: nursing care of the infant with down syndrome

Q: The mother of a newborn with Down syndrome says tearfully that her baby does not cuddle or mold to her the way her other children did, and she wonders whether the baby is rejecting her. What is the nurse's best response?
A) Explain that the lack of molding is a physical characteristic rather than a sign of detachment, and teach her to swaddle the infant snugly before picking him up
B) Reassure her that infants with Down syndrome form attachments more slowly and that molding will develop over the first year
C) Encourage more frequent skin-to-skin contact so the infant learns to mold to her body
D) Arrange a referral to a lactation consultant, since feeding contact is the usual route to attachment
Answer: A
Rationale: The chapter directs the nurse to emphasize "that the child's lack of clinging or molding is a physical characteristic and not a sign of detachment or rejection," and to "Encourage the parents to swaddle or wrap the infant snugly in a blanket before picking up the child" for security and warmth (p. 639).
Trap: (B) offers a false timeline for something hypotonia will not resolve, and (C) prescribes more of the very contact the hypotonia makes awkward without giving the mother the technique that solves it.

Q: A nurse is teaching the parents of an infant with Down syndrome how to reduce respiratory infections. Which measures should be included? Select all that apply.
A) Clear the nose with a bulb-type syringe
B) Rinse the mouth with water after feedings
C) Use a cool-mist vaporizer to keep the mucous membranes moist
D) Change the infant's position frequently
E) Limit fluid intake to reduce nasal secretions
Answer: A, B, C, D
Rationale: The chapter lists clearing the nose with a bulb syringe, rinsing the mouth after feedings, increasing fluid intake, using a cool-mist vaporizer, changing position frequently, good hand washing, and proper disposal of soiled articles (p. 639).
Trap: (E) reverses a listed measure — the chapter increases fluid intake to liquefy secretions — and it pulls because restricting fluids sounds like a reasonable way to dry up a runny nose; the underdeveloped nasal bone, not overproduction, is what causes the congestion.

Q: The father of a 9-month-old with Down syndrome says he has stopped offering solids because his son keeps pushing the food back out with his tongue. What should the nurse teach?
A) The tongue thrust is a physiologic response rather than refusal, and extruded food should be refed using a small, long, straight-handled spoon that places food toward the back and side of the mouth
B) The tongue thrust indicates the infant is not developmentally ready for solids, and they should be reintroduced in 2 to 3 months
C) Food should be placed on the tip of the tongue with a wide, shallow spoon so the infant can move it back independently
D) Solid food should be thinned to a liquid consistency and offered by bottle until the thrust resolves
Answer: A
Rationale: The chapter states that "the tongue thrust is not an indication of refusal to feed but a physiologic response," advises "a small but long, straight-handled spoon to push the food toward the back and side of the mouth," and adds that "If food is thrust out, it should be refed" (p. 639).
Trap: (B) reads a physical characteristic as a developmental signal and would delay feeding progression; (C) inverts both halves of the technique, since the tip of the tongue is where the thrust is strongest.

Q: A 3-year-old with Down syndrome plots below the fifth percentile on the standard growth chart, and the parents are worried about failure to thrive. What should the nurse know about growth monitoring in this child?
A) Revised growth charts compare the child with peers of the same age and sex who also have Down syndrome
B) Standard growth charts are the appropriate reference, and plotting below the fifth percentile indicates failure to thrive
C) Growth charts are not useful in Down syndrome, so weight alone should be followed
D) Height and weight should be measured only if growth appears severely delayed
Answer: A
Rationale: The chapter explains that new growth charts "were developed to provide indications of how growth of an individual child compares with peers of the same age and sex with Down syndrome," and directs that height and weight "should be obtained on a serial basis" (p. 639).
Trap: (B) is the error the revised charts exist to prevent; (D) contradicts the serial-measurement instruction, which matters because the same section warns about preventing obesity, so monitoring runs in both directions.

Topic: fragile X syndrome

Q: A woman is found to carry 120 base-pair repeats at the fragile X site. How should the nurse interpret this result?
A) She has a premutation and is a carrier
B) She has a full mutation and will show clinical manifestations
C) The result is within the normal range
D) The result is indeterminate and must be repeated
Answer: A
Rationale: The chapter gives three bands: normal is between 6 and 50 repeats, 50 to 200 repeats constitutes a premutation and carrier status, and expansion to 200 or more is a full mutation (p. 639).
Trap: (B) reaches for the full-mutation band, and the boundary is 200 rather than 100, which is where an unanchored memory tends to drift; note the chapter prints permutation for premutation at this point, which is a typographic defect in the source.

Q: A man who carries the fragile X premutation asks whether his daughters are at risk of having affected children. What should the nurse explain?
A) Expansion to a full mutation occurs when a carrier mother transmits the gene, not when a carrier father transmits it to his daughters
B) Expansion occurs regardless of which parent transmits the gene
C) His daughters cannot inherit the premutation from him because transmission is maternal
D) Expansion occurs only when a carrier father transmits the gene
Answer: A
Rationale: The chapter states that "This expansion occurs only when a carrier mother passes the mutation to her offspring; it does not occur when a carrier father passes the mutation to his daughters" (p. 639).
Trap: (D) is the direct reversal and (B) removes the asymmetry entirely; (C) overshoots by denying transmission altogether, when what the father does not transmit is the *expansion*, not the premutation.

Q: A nurse is preparing genetic counseling materials for a family with fragile X syndrome and wants to explain why the counseling differs from that for hemophilia. Which statement is accurate?
A) Fragile X is inherited as X-linked dominant with reduced penetrance, so carrier females may be affected, males may be carriers, and both sexes can transmit it
B) Fragile X follows the classic X-linked recessive pattern, so all carrier females are unaffected and no males are carriers
C) Fragile X is autosomal dominant, so each child of an affected parent has a 50% risk regardless of sex
D) Fragile X is autosomal recessive, so both parents must be carriers for a child to be affected
Answer: A
Rationale: The chapter names the pattern "X-linked dominant with reduced penetrance" and contrasts it with "the classic X-linked recessive pattern in which all carrier females are normal, all affected males have symptoms of the disorder, and no males are carriers," concluding that "Both affected sexes are capable of transmitting the fragile X disorder" (p. 640).
Trap: (B) applies hemophilia logic to fragile X, which is precisely the comparison the chapter draws to say the two are unlike; fragile X breaks all three clauses of the classic pattern, and that is why counseling is more complex.

Q: A nurse is evaluating a 5-year-old boy with unexplained developmental delay for possible fragile X syndrome. Which finding is most likely to suggest the diagnosis at this age?
A) Behavioral manifestations such as hyperactivity and rapid speech with word repetition
B) A long face with prominent jaw, large protruding ears, and large testes
C) Macroorchidism, which is present from birth in affected males
D) A shortened life expectancy compared with the general population
Answer: A
Rationale: The chapter states that in prepubertal children the classic physical findings "may be less obvious, and behavioral manifestations may initially suggest the diagnosis" (p. 640); the physical triad describes adult men, and Box 20.3 places enlarged testicles specifically in postpubertal males.
Trap: (B) and (C) screen a prepubertal boy on adult findings, which is the error the chapter names; (D) transfers the Down syndrome prognosis, since individuals with fragile X "are expected to live a normal life span" while Down syndrome life expectancy "remains lower than for the general population" (pp. 638, 640).

Topic: types and severity of childhood hearing loss

Q: A 4-year-old with a history of recurrent serous otitis media is found to have a hearing loss. Which type is most likely, and what does it do to sound?
A) Conductive loss, which interferes mainly with the loudness of sound
B) Sensorineural loss, which distorts sound and impairs discrimination
C) Central auditory imperception, which prevents the message from becoming meaningful communication
D) Mixed loss, which reflects damage confined to the auditory nerve
Answer: A
Rationale: Conductive loss "results from interference of transmission of sound to the middle ear," is "the most common of all types of hearing loss and most frequently a result of recurrent serous otitis media," and "involves mainly interference with loudness of sound" (p. 641); chronic otitis media is also named as the most common cause of impaired hearing overall (p. 644).
Trap: (D) misdescribes mixed loss, which involves the middle ear *and* the neural pathways; the practical consequence of keying conductive is that amplification helps, which is what the next item turns on.

Q: A 6-year-old with sensorineural hearing loss has been fitted with a conventional hearing aid but his parents report he still cannot make out what people say, even when the device is turned up. What explains this?
A) Sensorineural loss distorts sound and impairs discrimination, so amplifying sound into a damaged inner ear does not restore comprehension
B) The aid is improperly fitted, which is the usual reason amplification fails in sensorineural loss
C) Sensorineural loss affects loudness, so the aid should be set to a higher gain
D) Comprehension improves only after several months of adjustment to any hearing aid
Answer: A
Rationale: Sensorineural loss "results in distortion of sound and problems in discrimination," and the chapter explains that "conventional hearing aids only amplify sound that may not be processed by a damaged inner ear," so some children "require a referral for a cochlear implant" (pp. 641, 642).
Trap: (C) is the mirror error of the previous item, applying the conductive rule to a sensorineural loss; (B) names a real problem — improper ear mold fit causes acoustic feedback, the annoying whistling sound — but that is not what this scenario describes.

Q: A child can hear sound but cannot interpret what she hears, though she can express ideas normally and discriminate among similar sounds. Which term describes this?
A) Agnosia
B) Aphasia
C) Dysacusis
D) Conversion hysteria
Answer: A
Rationale: Under central auditory imperception of the organic type, the chapter defines agnosia as "the inability to interpret sound correctly," aphasia as "the inability to express ideas in any form, either written or verbal," and dysacusis as "difficulty in processing details or discriminating among sounds" (p. 641).
Trap: (B) and (C) are each excluded by a clause in the stem, which is how this three-term set has to be read — the discriminating word in each definition is the verb; (D) is the functional-type example, an unconscious withdrawal from hearing to block a traumatic memory.

Q: A 7-year-old with a severe hearing loss in the 71 to 90 decibel range pronounces the word spoon as "poon." What explains this pattern?
A) At this level the child can distinguish vowels but not most consonants, which are the higher-frequency sounds
B) At this level the child hears only loud sounds, so all speech is reproduced incompletely
C) The child has a conductive loss, which typically produces a loud, monotone voice
D) The pattern reflects a speech articulation disorder unrelated to the hearing level
Answer: A
Rationale: Table 20.2 states that a child in the severe band "Can distinguish vowels but not most consonants," and the chapter gives this exact example — "an inability to hear higher frequencies may result in the word spoon being pronounced" poon (pp. 641, 643).
Trap: (B) describes the profound band at 91 decibels and above; (C) assigns the wrong speech pattern, since a mild conductive loss produces speech that is "fairly clearly but in a loud, monotone voice" while a sensorineural defect produces the articulation difficulty described here.

Q: A school nurse is reviewing referrals for children whose teachers describe them as difficult. Which behaviors should raise suspicion of hearing impairment? Select all that apply.
A) Frequent stubbornness because of lack of comprehension
B) Shy, timid, and withdrawn behavior
C) Suspicious alertness alternating with cooperation
D) Greater response to facial expression and gestures than to verbal explanation
E) A newly acquired stutter that appears only when the child is anxious
Answer: A, B, C, D
Rationale: Box 20.4 lists all four among the manifestations of hearing impairment in children, alongside asking to have statements repeated, avoidance of social interaction, and appearing to be in a world of their own (p. 642).
Trap: (E) is the non-member, and the four keyed options are exactly the ones a reader attributes to temperament or misbehavior instead; the chapter warns that a milder loss often surfaces at school entry as difficulty learning, and that some of these children are "erroneously placed in special classes for students with learning disabilities or CI" (p. 642).

Topic: communication with the child who has hearing impairment

Q: A nurse is preparing to teach an 11-year-old who relies on lipreading. Which actions facilitate lipreading? Select all that apply.
A) Attract the child's attention before speaking, using light touch to signal your presence
B) Stand still and face the child directly or at a 45-degree angle
C) Ensure good lighting falls on the speaker's face
D) Rephrase the message if the child does not understand the words
E) Exaggerate pronunciation of key words so their shapes are clearer
Answer: A, B, C, D
Rationale: The Nursing Care Guidelines list attracting attention with light touch, standing still and facing directly or at a 45-degree angle, speaking at eye level with good lighting on the speaker's face, speaking clearly at a slow and even rate, keeping sentences short, and rephrasing when the child does not understand (p. 643).
Trap: (E) is the non-member and it is the most intuitive wrong action, because the chapter states that "Exaggerating pronunciation or speaking in an altered rhythm further lessens comprehension" (p. 643); lipreading yields only about 40% of the spoken word at best, and over-articulating lowers that.

Q: The parents of a 5-year-old who uses speech and lipreading ask about a method that would help him tell apart words such as mat and bat, which look identical on the lips. What should the nurse describe?
A) Cued speech, an adjunct to lipreading that uses hand signals to distinguish words that look alike when formed by the lips
B) American Sign Language, a visual-gestural language that replaces the need for lipreading
C) Speech-language therapy, which retrains the child to produce the distinguishing consonants
D) Finger spelling, in which words are spelled letter by letter into the child's hand
Answer: A
Rationale: Cued speech is "an adjunct to straight lipreading" that "uses hand signals to help the hearing-impaired child distinguish between words that look alike when formed by the lips (e.g., mat, bat)" and is "most commonly employed by hearing-impaired children who are using speech rather than those who are nonverbal" (p. 643).
Trap: (B) is the most confusable option because both involve the hands, but sign language is a language in its own right rather than an adjunct; (D) is the method taught for combined hearing and visual impairment, not for lipreading support.

Q: An 11-month-old with profound sensorineural hearing loss is being evaluated for a cochlear implant. Which teaching point should the nurse include?
A) The implant bypasses the damaged hair cells to stimulate surviving auditory nerve fibers, and the child must receive the pneumococcal polyvalent vaccine
B) The implant is a more powerful hearing aid that amplifies sound directly into the inner ear
C) Implantation is generally deferred until the child is at least 3 years old and can cooperate with programming
D) The main risk is device failure, so no additional immunization is required beyond the routine schedule
Answer: A
Rationale: The chapter states that "A cochlear implant bypasses the hair cells to directly stimulate surviving auditory nerve fibers," that the trend is toward implantation "usually by 12 months old," and that because pneumococcal meningitis has been reported, "all children receiving a cochlear implant must be vaccinated with the pneumococcal polyvalent vaccine" (p. 642).
Trap: (B) describes an amplifier rather than a bypass, which is the whole distinction between an aid and an implant; (C) inverts the timing trend, and (D) drops the single most actionable safety item in the hearing section.

Q: A nurse is caring for a hospitalized 6-year-old with a hearing impairment. Which action is correct?
A) Make certain the child can see the nurse before any procedure, including routine ones such as changing a diaper or adjusting an infusion
B) Reserve the requirement to be seen first for invasive or painful procedures, to avoid disrupting the child unnecessarily
C) Communicate procedures through the parents, who understand the child's speech best
D) Awaken the child at night by turning on the overhead light before approaching the bed
Answer: A
Rationale: The chapter directs the nurse to "always make certain that the child can see him or her before any procedures, even routine ones such as changing a diaper or regulating an infusion," because the child "may not be aware of the nurse's presence until alerted through visual or tactile cues" (p. 644).
Trap: (C) is the failure the chapter names outright — caregivers "communicate only with the parents" and "the child's needs and feelings remain unrecognized" (p. 644); (D) misses the specific technique, which is to gently shake the child or turn on the hearing aid before arousing them.

Topic: refractive errors, amblyopia, and eye injuries

Q: A school-age child has a corrected visual acuity of 20/100 in the better eye. How should the nurse classify this?
A) Partial sight, and the child can generally use normal-sized print because near vision usually exceeds distance vision
B) Legal blindness, which requires immediate registration for special educational services
C) Partial sight, which requires large-print materials as the first educational accommodation
D) Within normal limits for school-age children with corrective lenses
Answer: A
Rationale: Partial sight is "a visual acuity between 20/70 and 20/200," and the chapter notes that such a child "can generally use normal-sized print because near vision is almost always better than distance vision"; legal blindness begins at 20/200 or lower, or a visual field of 20 degrees or less in the better eye (p. 645).
Trap: (C) supplies large print reflexively for any visual impairment, which the chapter does not; note also that legal blindness "is not a medical diagnosis but a legal definition" used to determine tax status, school entrance, and financial aid.

Q: A 9-year-old cannot read the whiteboard from her seat but reads her book easily. Her eye examination shows the image focusing in front of the retina. What does the nurse expect?
A) Myopia, resulting from an eyeball that is too long, corrected with biconcave lenses
B) Hyperopia, resulting from an eyeball that is too short, corrected with convex lenses
C) Astigmatism, resulting from unequal curvature of the cornea, corrected with compensating lenses
D) Anisometropia, resulting from different refractive strength in each eye, corrected with contact lenses
Answer: A
Rationale: The chapter states that in refractive disorders light rays "fall in front of the retina (myopia) or beyond it (hyperopia)," that myopia "Results from eyeball that is too long," and that it is "Corrected with biconcave lenses that focus rays on retina" (p. 645).
Trap: (B) is the mirror condition with the mirror lens, and it is worth noting that hyperopia in a young child is usually normal — "Most children are normally hyperopic until about 7 years old" — and is treated only when required.

Q: A 4-year-old with untreated strabismus is at risk for amblyopia. Which statement about the mechanism and treatment is correct?
A) The brain suppresses the image from the weaker eye until the visual cortex stops responding, and treatment patches the stronger eye, preventing loss if begun before 6 years old
B) The weaker eye loses acuity from disuse of the eye muscles, and treatment patches the weaker eye to strengthen it
C) The retina of the weaker eye degenerates progressively, and treatment is surgical realignment alone
D) Vision loss is permanent once strabismus is detected, so treatment aims only at cosmetic alignment
Answer: A
Rationale: Box 20.5 describes the chain — one eye receives insufficient stimulation, each retina receives a different image, the "Brain accommodates by suppressing less intense image," and the "Visual cortex eventually does not respond" — and states amblyopia is "Preventable if treatment of primary visual defect, such as anisometropia or strabismus, begins before 6 years old"; treatment "May involve occlusion therapy (patching stronger eye)" (p. 646).
Trap: (B) patches the affected eye, which is the classic reversal and would accelerate the very suppression the therapy exists to interrupt; the loss in amblyopia is cortical rather than ocular, which is why (C) is wrong and why the developmental window in (A) matters.

Q: A 10-year-old is brought in after being struck in the eye with a baseball. The lid is swollen and bruised. What should the nurse do first?
A) Use a flashlight to check for gross hyphema before applying anything to the eye
B) Apply ice immediately to reduce the swelling, then assess the eye
C) Patch both eyes and refer the child to an ophthalmologist
D) Irrigate the eye with lukewarm tap water for 20 minutes
Answer: A
Rationale: For a hematoma the chapter directs the nurse to "Use a flashlight to check for gross hyphema," then "Apply ice for first 24 hours to reduce swelling if no hyphema is present," and "Refer to an ophthalmologist immediately if hyphema is present" (p. 646).
Trap: (B) reverses the order, and the order is what the item tests — ice is conditional on the absence of hyphema; (D) applies the chemical-burn rule to a blunt injury, and (C) applies the ultraviolet-burn rule.

Q: A child arrives with a pencil embedded in the left eye. Which actions should the nurse take? Select all that apply.
A) Leave the object in place and do not irrigate the eye
B) Apply a Fox shield rather than a regular eye patch
C) Patch the unaffected eye to prevent bilateral movement
D) Maintain bed rest with the child in a 30-degree Fowler position
E) Flush the eye copiously with lukewarm tap water for 20 minutes before transport
Answer: A, B, C, D
Rationale: The Emergency Treatment box for penetrating injuries directs the nurse never to remove a penetrating object, not to irrigate, to "Apply a Fox shield if available (not a regular eye patch) and apply patch over unaffected eye to prevent bilateral movement," and to "Maintain bed rest with child in a 30-degree Fowler position" (p. 646).
Trap: (E) is the chemical-burn action, and the two categories carry exactly opposite irrigation rules — copious tap water for 20 minutes for a chemical splash, never for a penetrating injury; (C) is the member most often dropped, since patching the uninjured eye seems pointless until you remember the eyes move together.

Topic: nursing care of the child with visual impairment

Q: The parents of an infant with severe permanent visual impairment tell the nurse the baby "does not seem to know when we come in." What should the nurse teach them?
A) To watch for alternative cues — whether the eyelids blink, whether activity level changes, whether breathing patterns change, and whether the infant makes throaty sounds when they speak
B) That infants with visual impairment bond more slowly and that responsiveness typically emerges after 6 months
C) To increase the volume of their voices so the infant can locate them more easily
D) That attachment behaviors in a visually impaired infant are best assessed by a specialist rather than by parents
Answer: A
Rationale: The chapter names four cues the nurse teaches parents to read — eyelid blinking, acceleration or slowing of activity level, change in respiratory pattern when the parents come near, and throaty sounds when the parents speak — and adds that "In time, parents learn that the infant has unique ways of relating to them" (p. 648).
Trap: (B) offers a timeline instead of a skill, and (D) removes the observation from the people best placed to make it; the keyed intervention here is teaching an assessment, which parallels the Down syndrome instruction that lack of molding is physical rather than rejection.

Q: A nurse is assigned to a 7-year-old admitted with bilateral eye patches after surgery. Which action is correct?
A) Identify herself as soon as she enters the room and before approaching the child
B) Provide a cane so the child can navigate to the bathroom independently
C) Rearrange the furniture each shift so the child learns to adapt to changes
D) Perform most of the child's routine care to keep the child safe
Answer: A
Rationale: The chapter directs that nurses "should always identify themselves as soon as they enter the room and before they approach the child," and that "Whenever possible, the same nurse should care for the child to ensure consistency in the approach" (pp. 648, 649).
Trap: (B) supplies a cane to a child who has had no training with one — the chapter states that "Unlike the child who is visually impaired, these children are not familiar with navigating with a cane"; (C) reverses the rule that furniture "is always placed in the same position to prevent collisions"; and (D) violates the stated objective to encourage independence.

Q: A child with a prolonged visual impairment is to be admitted for an extended stay. Which measure best supports a safe environment?
A) Keep the furniture in the same position at all times and remind cleaning personnel to keep the room in order
B) Keep the room lights on continuously so shadows do not confuse the child
C) Keep the side rails up and the bed in low position at all times to prevent the child from getting up
D) Provide loose slippers and a long robe so the child can dress without assistance
Answer: A
Rationale: The chapter directs that "The furniture is always placed in the same position to prevent collisions" and to "Remind cleaning personnel to keep the room in order," adding that a rope may be attached from the bed to the bathroom if the child has difficulty navigating by feeling the walls (p. 649).
Trap: (C) achieves safety by removing independence, which the chapter lists as a nursing objective in its own right; (D) inverts a specific instruction, since the chapter calls for "well-fitting slippers and robes that do not drag on the floor" to prevent tripping.

Q: Two days after surgery restores vision to a child who has been blind for years, the child is tearful and withdrawn. The parents are distressed that she is not happy. How should the nurse respond?
A) Explain that a period of depression can follow restored sight and must be respected and supported, and that the child should not be bombarded with visual stimuli
B) Explain that the mood change likely indicates a surgical complication requiring evaluation
C) Encourage the parents to increase visual stimulation to help the child appreciate her new abilities
D) Reassure the parents that the reaction will resolve once the child begins reading print instead of braille
Answer: A
Rationale: The chapter states that newly sighted children "may also go through a period of depression, which must be respected and supported," that "They should not be bombarded with visual stimuli," and that they often need "to talk about what they see and to compare the visual images with their mental ones" (p. 649).
Trap: (C) is the well-meaning opposite of the pacing rule; (D) pushes a transition the chapter explicitly allows the child to defer, since a newly sighted child "may prefer to use braille to read rather than learning a new visual approach because of familiarity with the touch system."

Topic: autism spectrum disorder

Q: The mother of a newly diagnosed 3-year-old asks tearfully whether her return to work when he was an infant caused his autism. What is the nurse's best response?
A) Explain from a biologic standpoint what is known about the disorder, including that it is not related to socioeconomic level, race, or parenting style
B) Reassure her that many parents feel guilty and that her feelings will lessen with time
C) Explain that the cause is unknown, so no parenting factor can be entirely excluded
D) Refer her to a counselor, since guilt of this kind is outside the nurse's scope
Answer: A
Rationale: The chapter states that autism spectrum disorder "is not related to socioeconomic level, race, or parenting style," that nurses can "alleviate the guilt and shame often associated with this disorder by stressing what is known from a biologic standpoint," and that "It is imperative to help parents understand that they are not the cause of the child's condition" (pp. 650, 653).
Trap: (C) is technically hedged and does real harm, because it leaves the parenting hypothesis alive when the chapter has already excluded it; (B) normalizes the guilt without supplying the information that dissolves it, which is the specific method the chapter names.

Q: A nurse is reviewing well-child visit schedules. At what ages does the American Academy of Pediatrics recommend screening for autism spectrum disorder, and what follows a concerning result?
A) At 18 and 24 months, with a concerning result leading to a comprehensive developmental evaluation by a specialist
B) At 18 and 24 months, with a concerning result establishing the diagnosis
C) At 12 and 18 months, with a concerning result leading to referral for speech therapy
D) At 24 and 36 months, with a concerning result leading to repeat screening in 6 months
Answer: A
Rationale: The chapter states that providers should "administer two ASD screenings at ages 18 and 24 months using a valid screening tool," names the Modified Checklist for Autism in Toddlers and its follow-up interview, and directs that children with concerning results "should receive a comprehensive developmental evaluation from either a developmental pediatrician, child neurologist, child psychiatrist, or child psychologist" (p. 651).
Trap: (B) collapses screening into diagnosis, which is the distinction the chapter draws explicitly; (D) delays, and delay is already the named problem — diagnosis "is often not made until 2 to 3 years after symptoms are first recognized."

Q: At a 16-month well visit, a mother reports her son babbles but has never used a single word, and adds that he had been saying "mama" at 12 months but stopped. What should the nurse recommend?
A) Immediate hearing and language evaluation, since single words are expected by 16 months and regression is a red-flag event
B) Referral to speech therapy alone, since the concern is expressive language
C) Reassessment at the 18-month visit, when autism screening is due
D) Reassurance that expressive language commonly fluctuates in the second year
Answer: A
Rationale: The chapter recommends "immediate hearing and language evaluation" for any child not displaying "babbling or gesturing by 12 months old, single words by 16 months old, and two-word phrases by 24 months old," and describes autism regression — developing normally and then regressing suddenly — as "a red-flag event that has been frequently displayed in expressive language" (p. 651).
Trap: (B) is the most common wrong pick because the presenting problem is speech, but the chapter pairs hearing with language every time, since hearing loss is the differential; (D) treats regression as normal variation, which this chapter never does.

Q: A 6-year-old with autism spectrum disorder is admitted for a scheduled procedure. Which measures should the nurse include in the plan of care? Select all that apply.
A) Provide a structured routine for the child to follow
B) Place the child in a private room and reduce extraneous auditory and visual distractions
C) Use minimal holding and eye contact to avoid behavioral outbursts
D) Encourage the parents to stay with the child as much as possible and to bring familiar possessions
E) Assign a roommate of similar age so the child has company in an unfamiliar setting
Answer: A, B, C, D
Rationale: The chapter names a structured routine as "a key in the management of ASD," directs decreasing stimulation "by using a private room, avoiding extraneous auditory and visual distractions, and encouraging the parents to bring in possessions the child is attached to," states that "minimal holding and eye contact may be necessary to avoid behavioral outbursts," and says the parents "ideally should stay with the child as much as possible" (p. 653).
Trap: (E) is the generic pediatric kindness this chapter specifically contradicts, and it is the distractor in the chapter's own review item; every wrong option in that item is an intervention that is correct in other contexts and wrong here, which is what makes this material worth drilling as a block.
