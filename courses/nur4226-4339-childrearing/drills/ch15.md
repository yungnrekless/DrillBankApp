# Chapter 15 drills — Health Promotion of the School-Age Child and Family

Authored from `sources/Ch15_Fact_Bank.md` (Hockenberry, Wilson & Rodgers, 11th ed.,
chapter author Cheryl C. Rodgers, printed pp. 460–494).

Blueprint weight is **6 questions of 202 (3.0%), depth `standard`** — 43 drills
across 10 topics.

This is the fourth and last rung of the developmental ladder in this course, and
the longest of the four chapters: school entry, peer culture, and a full
injury-prevention section on top of the usual developmental frame. Topic names are
school-age-qualified for the same reason the infant, toddler, and preschooler
topics are — Chapter 17 banks the same categories for the adolescent.

**Where the weight went.** Three areas the fact bank names. **Concrete operations**
in full, because conservation with its three justifications is the chapter's
signature cognitive content and the thing every school-age item set reaches for.
**The peer group and what goes wrong in it**, above all bullying, which the book
treats at length with its own definition, prevalence figures, gendered forms, and a
named list of what does and does not work. And **injury prevention**, because that
is where the numbers live — the motor-vehicle and bicycle-helmet statistics are the
most item-shaped facts in the chapter.

**Drilled from both ends:** conservation's three justifications against one
another, younger against older school-age moral reasoning (consequences versus
intentions), direct against indirect bullying, bullying interventions that work
against those that do not, family against peer values when they conflict, and the
physical-versus-emotional maturity mismatch in both directions.

**What thirty-four printed pages could not buy at 43 questions.** Left undrilled:
Table 15.1's full year-by-year grid beyond the findings drilled here; the
Coordinated School Health Program and school nursing services; gangs; hero worship;
sleep and sleepwalking; physical fitness, organized athletics, and exergaming;
dental health and flossing technique; obesity and school meal programs; television
and Internet effects beyond anticipatory socialization; Box 15.1's year-by-year
stress sources; and the ATV, skateboard, and firearm injury sections. The GAP
register flags no omission as likely-to-be-tested, so nothing required expanding
before this chapter was drilled.

Five questions are select-all, one for each list that genuinely sorts two ways:
bullying interventions that work against two the chapter names as ineffective,
stress signs warranting further exploration against a normal finding, latchkey
telephone and door rules against the one that endangers the child, guidelines for
helping a child in school against the one that undermines responsibility, and
injury epidemiology against a category that is actually lowest at this age.

Topic: school-age biologic development and prepubescence

Q: A 9-year-old boy has grown noticeably stronger over the past year and wants to begin an intensive weight-training program. Which consideration should the nurse raise?
A) By the end of this period both boys and girls double their strength and physical capabilities, but this increased strength is often misleading, because muscles are still functionally immature compared with those of the adolescent and are more readily injured by overuse
B) Strength gains at this age indicate muscular maturity equivalent to an adolescent's
C) Strength does not increase appreciably during the school-age years
D) Overuse injury is not a concern until after puberty
Answer: A
Rationale: "By the end of this age period, both boys and girls double their strength and physical capabilities." But "this increased strength is often misleading. Although strength increases, muscles are still functionally immature compared with those of the adolescent, and they are more readily injured by overuse." Bones also continue to ossify, and "mineralization is not completed until maturity."
Trap: (B) draws the natural inference from visible strength and is exactly the reasoning that produces overuse injuries in this age group. The fact that makes this item worth writing is that capability and tissue maturity diverge here, and only one of them is visible.

Q: A 7-year-old is the size of a typical 10-year-old, and his parents have begun expecting him to behave accordingly. Which principle should the nurse teach?
A) Physical maturity is not necessarily correlated with emotional and social maturity; a 7-year-old who looks 10 will think and act like a 7-year-old, and expecting otherwise can be detrimental to competence and self-esteem
B) Physical size is a reliable indicator of emotional readiness at this age
C) Children who are large for their age mature emotionally faster to match
D) Treating a 10-year-old as though they were 7 carries no developmental cost
Answer: A
Rationale: "Physical maturity is not necessarily correlated with emotional and social maturity. Seven-year-old children who look like 10-year-old children will think and act like 7-year-olds." Expecting 10-year-old behavior "is unrealistic and can be detrimental to their development of competence and self-esteem."
Trap: (D) is the other half of the same principle, and the chapter closes it off deliberately: "Conversely, treating 10-year-old children as though they were 7 years old is an equal disservice." The mismatch cuts both ways, and an item can test either direction.

Q: A parent asks when to expect pubertal changes in their 9-year-old daughter and 9-year-old son. Which response reflects the chapter?
A) Generally puberty begins at 10 years in girls and 12 years in boys, with onset in either sex after age 8 years considered normal; the average age is 12 years in girls and 14 years in boys, with approximately a 2-year difference between the sexes
B) Puberty begins at 12 years in girls and 10 years in boys
C) Onset before age 10 is abnormal in both sexes and requires evaluation
D) Boys and girls begin pubertal changes at the same average age
Answer: A
Rationale: "Generally, puberty begins at 10 years in girls and 12 years in boys, but its onset in either sex after age 8 years is considered normal. The average age of puberty is 12 years in girls and 14 years in boys." On average "there is a difference of approximately 2 years between girls and boys in the age of onset of pubescence," and first signs appear "at about 9 years (particularly in girls)."
Trap: (B) swaps the two sexes, which is the commonest inversion here because the numbers themselves are easy to hold and their owners are not. (C) would make normal development pathologic — the chapter sets the lower boundary of normal at 8 years, not 10.

Q: A 10-year-old girl has developed breasts well ahead of her classmates, and a 12-year-old boy in the same clinic has shown no pubertal changes. What does the chapter say about the psychosocial implications?
A) Early appearance of secondary sex characteristics in girls is often associated with dissatisfaction with physical appearance, greater general unhappiness, and lower self-esteem, while late-developing boys often have a negative self-concept; both have been linked to participation in risk-taking behaviors
B) Off-schedule development carries psychosocial risk only for girls
C) Early development in girls is consistently associated with higher self-esteem
D) Neither pattern has any documented psychosocial association
Answer: A
Rationale: "Early appearance of secondary sex characteristics in girls is often associated with dissatisfaction with physical appearance, greater general unhappiness, and lower self-esteem. Late-developing boys often have a negative self-concept." Both "have been linked to participation in risk-taking behaviors (e.g., early sexual activity, substance use, and reckless vehicle use)."
Trap: (C) assumes that developing early confers status, which is the intuition the data overturn for girls. Note the underlying driver the chapter names: preadolescent children "do not want to be different," and the variability at this age is striking in both directions.

Topic: industry versus inferiority in the school-age child

Q: An 8-year-old proudly shows the nurse a birdhouse he built and asks whether it is good enough to enter in a competition. Which psychosocial task does this reflect?
A) Initiative versus guilt
B) Industry versus inferiority, achieving a sense of personal and interpersonal competence through the acquisition of technologic and social skills
C) Identity versus role confusion
D) Autonomy versus shame and doubt
Answer: B
Rationale: "A sense of industry, or a stage of accomplishment, occurs somewhere between age 6 years and adolescence." The goal is "to achieve a sense of personal and interpersonal competence through the acquisition of technologic and social skills." School-age children "are eager to build skills and participate in meaningful and socially useful work," and they "want to engage in tasks that they can complete."
Trap: (A) belongs to the preschooler and (C) to the adolescent. The discriminator is what the child is seeking: the preschooler is testing whether it is permissible to try, while the school-age child is asking whether the finished product is good — accomplishment rather than permission.

Q: A 9-year-old who struggles in athletics tells the nurse she is "bad at everything." Which understanding should guide the nurse's response?
A) No child is able to do well in everything, and all children feel some degree of inferiority regarding a specific skill they cannot master; repeated failures can make a child reluctant to attempt any new task
B) A child who reports feeling inferior in one area has a pathologic self-concept requiring referral
C) Children who fail at one skill readily compensate through any other skill they possess
D) Feelings of inferiority arise only from the social environment, never from the child
Answer: A
Rationale: "No child is able to do well in everything… All children, even children who usually have positive attitudes toward work and their own capabilities, feel some degree of inferiority in regard to a specific skill that they cannot master." "Repeated failures can generate such strong feelings that eventually the child is reluctant to attempt any new task… Consequently, the child may no longer try."
Trap: (C) understates a real obstacle the chapter names: "society places a higher value on success in team sports than on success in repairing a bicycle," so compensating through a less valued skill "is difficult for children." (D) drops half the source — feelings of inferiority come "from children themselves or from the social environment."

Q: A 10-year-old consistently sets goals far beyond what he can achieve and is repeatedly disappointed. Which principle applies?
A) Children who aspire to more than they are capable of usually experience failure, whereas children who set their aspirations lower than their level of achievement are likely to experience success
B) Setting the highest possible goals always builds industry
C) Aspiration level has no relationship to experienced success at this age
D) The child should be told to stop setting goals until he is older
Answer: A
Rationale: "Children who aspire to more than they are capable of usually experience failure. In contrast, children who set their aspirations lower than their level of achievement are likely to experience success." The chapter also notes that at this age "Most accomplishments during the school years are very public. Family, teachers, and peers are all aware of success or failure in school."
Trap: (B) is the encouraging advice adults reach for, and in this stage it manufactures the repeated failure that leads to a child refusing to attempt anything new. What builds industry is completing tasks, which requires aspirations the child can actually reach.

Q: A slow-to-warm-up 8-year-old becomes tearful and complains of stomachaches before each new after-school activity. What should the nurse advise the parents?
A) Encourage the child to try new experiences but allow adaptation at the child's own speed; pressure to move quickly into new situations only strengthens the tendency to withdraw
B) Insist the child attend every session without exception, so avoidance is not reinforced
C) Withdraw the child from all new activities until adolescence
D) Treat the somatic complaints as a medical problem requiring workup before proceeding
Answer: A
Rationale: Slow-to-warm-up children "exhibit discomfort when introduced to new situations and need time to become accustomed to a new environment, authority figures, and expectations," and may respond "with tears, somatic complaints, or other maneuvers to avoid the event." The guidance is to "Encourage them to try new experiences but allow them to adapt to their surroundings at their own speed," because "Pressure to move quickly into new situations only strengthens the tendency to withdraw." Attending with a friend, or contracting for permission to withdraw after a specified number of tries, may help.
Trap: (B) is the firm-hand answer and it produces the opposite of the intended effect in this temperament. (C) abandons the encouragement half of the instruction — the child is meant to try new things, just not on someone else's schedule.

Topic: concrete operations and conservation in the school-age child

Q: A 9-year-old is shown two identical balls of clay, and one is flattened into a pancake. She says, "It's still the same amount — you could roll it back into a ball." Which concept is she using?
A) Identity
B) Reversibility
C) Reciprocity
D) Seriation
Answer: B
Rationale: Children justify conservation with one of three concepts. Reversibility is that "The clay can be reshaped into its original form (a ball)." Identity is that "Because nothing has been added and nothing has been taken away, the pancake is still the same clay. Nothing has changed but the shape." Reciprocity is that "Although the pancake appears larger in circumference, the ball is much thicker."
Trap: (A) is the neighbouring justification and the most commonly substituted one, because both amount to "it's still the same clay." The discriminator is the child's reasoning: identity appeals to nothing having been added or removed, while reversibility appeals to the transformation being undoable.

Q: A second child, shown the same flattened clay, says, "It's wider, but it's a lot thinner too." Which concept is this, and what ability does it demonstrate?
A) Identity, demonstrating that nothing was added or removed
B) Reciprocity, demonstrating the ability to deal with two dimensions at the same time and to comprehend that a change in one dimension compensates for a change in another
C) Reversibility, demonstrating that the action can be undone
D) Classification, demonstrating grouping by shared attributes
Answer: B
Rationale: Reciprocity is the justification that "Although the pancake appears larger in circumference, the ball is much thicker." The child "demonstrates the ability to deal with two dimensions at the same time and to comprehend that a change in one dimension compensates for a change in another." Once children can use all three concepts, "they can conserve along any physical dimension."
Trap: (C) is the mirror error of the previous item. Nothing in this child's reasoning refers to undoing the change; the reasoning tracks two dimensions simultaneously, which is the cognitive step reciprocity names.

Q: A 7-year-old can conserve number and liquid but insists that a ball of clay weighs more after being flattened. How should the nurse interpret this?
A) Expected; there is a developmental sequence to conservation — number at 5 to 6 years, liquids, mass, and length at about 6 to 7 years, weight at 9 to 10 years, and volume last at 9 to 12 years
B) Delayed; conservation of weight is expected by 6 years
C) Concerning; conservation is acquired all at once or not at all
D) Advanced; conservation of liquid is not expected until 10 years
Answer: A
Rationale: "There appears to be a developmental sequence in children's capacity to conserve matter": numbers at 5 to 6 years, "liquids, mass, and length" at about 6 to 7 years, weight at 9 to 10 years, and "volume or displacement" at 9 to 12 years.
Trap: (C) is the assumption that conservation is a single switch, and the staggered sequence is precisely what makes this child's answer normal. A 7-year-old who conserves liquid but not weight is exactly on schedule.

Q: An 8-year-old has begun amassing large collections of cards, rocks, and stuffed animals, and ranks her friends as "first best friend" and "second best friend." Which cognitive ability underlies both behaviors?
A) Classification — the ability to group objects according to attributes they have in common, holding a concept in mind while making decisions based on it
B) Conservation of volume
C) Egocentrism
D) Metalinguistic awareness
Answer: A
Rationale: Classification is "the ability to group objects according to the attributes that they have in common." School-age children "can place things in a sensible and logical order, group and sort, and hold a concept in their minds while they make decisions based on that concept." This is why they "become occupied with numerous and varied collections," and why "They even begin to order friends and relationships (e.g., first best friend, second best friend)."
Trap: (C) belongs to the preschooler and is what concrete operations is replacing: "Their rigid, egocentric outlook is replaced by thought processes that allow them to see things from another's point of view."

Q: A nurse is explaining what changes cognitively when a child enters concrete operations, between ages 7 and 11. Which statement is accurate?
A) Children progress from making judgments based on what they see, which is perceptual thinking, to judgments based on what they reason, which is conceptual thinking, and they can delay an action until they have evaluated alternative responses
B) Children in concrete operations can reason abstractly about hypothetical situations
C) Children in concrete operations must still carry out an action before they can anticipate its result
D) Egocentrism increases during this stage
Answer: A
Rationale: "The concrete operational stage occurs between ages 7 and 11 years." Children "progress from making judgments based on what they see (perceptual thinking) to making judgments based on what they reason (conceptual thinking)," and "are able to delay an action until they have evaluated alternative responses to situations." Their "steady reduction in egocentricity helps form the basis for logical thought and the development and maturation of morality."
Trap: (C) describes the preschooler and is what reversibility replaces: school-age children "have the ability to think through an action sequence, anticipate the consequences, and, if needed, return to the beginning and rethink the action in a different direction. They no longer need to experience an action before they can anticipate the results." (B) belongs to adolescence — abstract, principled reasoning is "not until adolescence or beyond."

Topic: school-age moral, spiritual, and language development

Q: A 6-year-old says that a child who accidentally broke five cups is naughtier than one who deliberately broke a single cup. A 10-year-old in the same family disagrees. What explains the difference?
A) Younger school-age children usually judge an act by its consequences, while older school-age children are able to judge an act by the intentions that prompted it rather than just by the consequences
B) The 6-year-old is showing delayed moral development
C) Younger children judge by intentions and older children by consequences
D) Moral reasoning does not change across the school-age years
Answer: A
Rationale: Younger school-age children, 6 to 7 years, "know the rules and behaviors expected of them, but they do not understand the reasons behind them" and "usually judge an act by its consequences. Rewards and punishment guide their judgment." Older school-age children are "able to judge an act by the intentions that prompted it rather than just by the consequences," and rules become "less absolute and authoritarian."
Trap: (C) is the straight inversion of the pair. (B) mistakes a normal stage for a delay — this is exactly what a 6-year-old's moral reasoning is expected to look like, and it is the reason the two children in this stem disagree.

Q: A hospitalized 8-year-old asks the nurse whether she got sick because she was mean to her brother last week. Which understanding should guide the response?
A) School-age children often view illness or injury as a punishment for a real or imagined misdeed, are developing a conscience, and want and expect to be punished for misbehavior
B) This reflects magical thinking that should have resolved by age 5 and warrants referral
C) Children this age do not connect behavior with illness
D) The child is attempting to avoid a procedure
Answer: A
Rationale: School-age children "are fascinated by heaven and hell and, with a developing conscience and concern about rules, they fear going to hell for misbehavior. School-age children want and expect to be punished for misbehavior and, if given the option, tend to choose a punishment that 'fits the crime.' Often they view illness or injury as a punishment for a real or imagined misdeed."
Trap: (B) mislabels a normal feature of this stage as a residue of preschool magical thinking. The mechanism differs: the preschooler believes thoughts cause events, while the school-age child believes misdeeds earn punishment — a moral framework rather than a magical one.

Q: A 10-year-old is delighted by puns and riddles and can explain what "A stitch in time saves nine" means. Which language development does this demonstrate?
A) Metalinguistic awareness — an ability to think about language and comment on its properties, which enables appreciation of jokes, riddles, and puns and understanding of metaphors and figurative statements
B) Telegraphic speech
C) The minimum-distance principle
D) Egocentric speech
Answer: A
Rationale: Metalinguistic awareness is "an ability to think about language and to comment on its properties." This "enables them to appreciate jokes, riddles, and puns that involve play on words, sounds, or double meanings," and to begin understanding "metaphors and figurative statements, such as 'A stitch in time saves nine.'" Children become "less likely to engage in literal interpretation of messages."
Trap: (C) names a real school-age language rule, but a different one — the minimum-distance principle is "the rule that the subject of a verb in an active sentence is the noun or pronoun that immediately precedes it," which is why a 6-year-old understands "Ask Mary her last name" but is confused by "Ask Mary what to bring to the party" until age 9 or 10.

Q: A nurse plans religious and spiritual support for a hospitalized 8-year-old. Which approach fits the chapter?
A) Present religious concepts in concrete terms, since children this age have difficulty understanding symbols, and recognize that the beliefs of family and religious personages are more influential than those of peers in matters of faith
B) Use abstract theological explanations, since concrete operations allow abstract reasoning
C) Rely on peer influence, which predominates over family in matters of faith at this age
D) Avoid religious ritual, which increases anxiety in hospitalized children
Answer: A
Rationale: "Religious concepts must be presented to them in concrete terms" because children this age "have difficulty understanding symbols." "The beliefs and ideals of family and religious personages are more influential than those of their peers in matters of faith," and religious rituals "can help children cope with threatening situations."
Trap: (C) generalizes the chapter's emphasis on peer influence into a domain where the chapter says the opposite. Peers dominate standards for dress, behavior, and acceptance; family and religious figures dominate faith.

Topic: school-age peer groups, clubs, and team play

Q: A 10-year-old belongs to a club with a secret code, a required style of dress, and elaborate rules for membership. The parents ask whether this rigidity is healthy. Which response reflects the chapter?
A) A prominent feature of middle childhood groups is the code of rigid rules imposed on members, and conforming to the rules provides children with feelings of security and relieves them of the responsibility of making decisions
B) Rigid group rules at this age indicate an unhealthy need for control
C) Conformity to peer rules replaces family values permanently
D) Formalized clubs are unusual in middle childhood and warrant assessment
Answer: A
Rationale: "One of the outstanding characteristics of middle childhood is the formation of formalized groups or clubs," and "A prominent feature of middle childhood groups is the code of rigid rules imposed on the members." "Conformity is the core of the group structure." Membership is marked by "secret codes, shared interests, special styles of dress, and special words," and "Conforming to the rules provides children with feelings of security and relieves them of the responsibility of making decisions."
Trap: (C) overstates what conformity displaces, and the chapter is explicit that children "substitute conformity to a peer-group pattern for conformity to a family pattern while they are still too insecure to function independently" — a substitution that is temporary and partial.

Q: A parent worries that their 11-year-old is adopting peer values that conflict with the family's. Which response reflects the chapter?
A) Although the peer group is highly influential and necessary to normal development, parents are the primary influence in shaping personality, setting standards, and establishing values, and family values usually predominate when parental and peer value systems come into conflict
B) Peer values reliably displace family values by this age
C) Children who test peer values have rejected family values permanently
D) Peer influence is not significant until adolescence
Answer: A
Rationale: "Although the peer group is highly influential and necessary to normal child development, parents are the primary influence in shaping children's personalities, setting standards for behavior, and establishing value systems. Family values usually predominate when parental and peer value systems come into conflict." Children "may appear to reject parental values while testing the new values of the peer group," but "ultimately they retain and incorporate many parental values into their own value systems."
Trap: (B) is the fear that brings parents to this conversation, and the chapter answers it directly. Note the related finding: children "need and want restrictions placed on their behavior" and "are uneasy if they succeed" in breaking down parental limits.

Q: A 10-year-old on a losing soccer team is distraught about having let the team down. What does the chapter say team play teaches, and what caution applies?
A) Team play teaches subordinating personal goals to group goals and the concept of interdependence, with each member's behavior open to public evaluation; however, winning is not universally given high value, and some cultures emphasize the game and consideration for one's companions rather than the outcome
B) Team play teaches that winning is the universal measure of success
C) Children this age are unaffected by team loss
D) Team play has no cognitive benefit at this age
Answer: A
Rationale: Each child on a team "is accountable to the other team members," each member's behavior "is open to public evaluation," and children "risk ostracism, ridicule, or scapegoating if they contribute to a team loss," learning "the concept of interdependence." Because "losing is often interpreted as failure," children go to great lengths to avoid public embarrassment — "However, winning is not universally given high value. Some cultures and subcultures emphasize the game and consideration for one's companions rather than the outcome."
Trap: (D) misses a named benefit: team play requires children "to learn many complex rules, make judgments about those rules, plan strategies, and assess the strengths and weaknesses" of both teams, which is cognitive growth as well as social.

Q: A nurse is explaining what children gain from peer interaction that they cannot gain from family. Which statement reflects the chapter?
A) Because age-mates are not authorities, other children have a significant influence on decreasing the egocentric outlook, and children learn to argue, persuade, bargain, cooperate, and compromise to maintain friendships
B) Peer interaction primarily reinforces what the family has already taught
C) Peer relationships at this age are interchangeable and carry no lasting significance
D) Best friendships are discouraged at this age in favor of larger group membership
Answer: A
Rationale: Through peers, children "discover that there are many occupations for fathers and mothers, more than one version of the same song, different rules for the same game." Because age-mates are not authorities, "other children have a significant influence on decreasing the egocentric outlook," and children "learn to argue, persuade, bargain, cooperate, and compromise to maintain friendships."
Trap: (C) dismisses what the chapter treats as formative: school age "is the time when children have 'best friends' with whom they share secrets, private jokes, and adventures," and these dyadic relationships "seem to be important as a foundation for relationships in adulthood." Because "neither child has authority over the other," the children must work through differences themselves.

Topic: bullying and cyberbullying

Q: A school nurse is asked to define bullying for a staff in-service. Which definition reflects the chapter?
A) Any recurring activity that intends to cause harm, distress, or control toward another individual where there is a perceived imbalance of power between the aggressor and the victim
B) Any single act of physical aggression between two children of equal status
C) Any conflict between children in which one child becomes upset
D) Physical aggression only, since verbal acts are classified separately
Answer: A
Rationale: Bullying is "any recurring activity that intends to cause harm, distress, or control toward another individual where there is a perceived imbalance of power between the aggressor(s) and the victim." It "can occur in varying degrees of severity in a physical, social, or emotional context."
Trap: (B) drops both defining elements — the recurrence and the power imbalance — which is what separates bullying from an ordinary conflict between peers. (C) drops them too and would classify every playground dispute as bullying, which makes the term useless for the interventions that follow from it.

Q: A school nurse is advising on where to concentrate supervision to reduce bullying. Which statement reflects the chapter?
A) Although bullying can occur in any setting, it usually takes place in school hallways or on the playground where supervision is minimal but peers are present to witness the attack
B) Bullying occurs most often in the classroom, under direct teacher observation
C) Bullying occurs mainly when no other children are present
D) Location has no bearing on where bullying occurs
Answer: A
Rationale: "Although bullying can occur in any setting, it usually takes place in school hallways or on the playground where supervision is minimal but peers are present to witness the attack."
Trap: (C) misses the audience element, which is part of why location matters: the attack requires witnesses to accomplish what it is for. That combination — minimal supervision plus present peers — is what makes hallways and playgrounds the characteristic setting, and it is why high levels of playground supervision is a named effective intervention.

Q: A nurse compares traditional bullying with cyberbullying for a parent group. Which statement is accurate?
A) Cyberbullying can be more harmful than traditional bullying because the attack can instantly reach a wider audience while allowing the bully to remain anonymous; traditional bullying risk is greater in elementary and middle school, while cyberbullying is more frequent in high school
B) Cyberbullying is less harmful than traditional bullying because there is no physical contact
C) Both forms peak in elementary school
D) Cyberbullying always identifies the perpetrator, making intervention easier
Answer: A
Rationale: Cyberbullying "involves an electronic medium to harm or bother another individual and can be more harmful than traditional bullying because the attack can instantly reach a wider audience, while allowing the bully to remain anonymous." Risk of traditional bullying is greater in elementary and middle school than high school, while cyberbullying is more frequent in high school. Up to 30% of school-age children engage in bullying, and 11% to 42% in cyberbullying as bully or victim.
Trap: (B) is the intuition that physical harm is the measure of severity, and the chapter names the two features that make the electronic form worse instead: reach and anonymity.

Q: A school nurse notes that boys and girls in the school bully differently. Which statement reflects the chapter?
A) Boys tend to use physical force, referred to as direct bullying, while girls usually use methods such as exclusion, gossip, or rumors, referred to as indirect bullying
B) Boys use indirect bullying and girls use direct bullying
C) Exclusion and gossip are not classified as bullying
D) Only physical force meets the definition of bullying
Answer: A
Rationale: Boys who bully "tend to use physical force, referred to as direct bullying," while girls usually use "bullying methods, such as exclusion, gossip, or rumors, which is referred to as indirect bullying."
Trap: (C) and (D) both exclude the indirect form from the definition, which would make most girls' bullying invisible to a school's response. Bullying occurs "in a physical, social, or emotional context," and social exclusion qualifies on the definition's own terms.

Q: A school is planning an antibullying program. Which approaches does the chapter identify as effective? Select all that apply.
A) High levels of playground supervision
B) Schoolwide rules related to bullying
C) Teacher training
D) Grouping students who bully together
E) Parent training activities and meetings
Answer: A, B, C, E
Rationale: Effective approaches are "high levels of playground supervision," "schoolwide rules related to bullying," "teacher training," and "parent training activities and meetings." Bullying "can be reduced or prevented through supportive relationships with family, intervention of school personnel, and involvement with positive peer groups."
Trap: (D) is one of two approaches the chapter names as ineffective, the other being "brief (e.g., 1-day) awareness campaigns." It pulls because grouping looks like targeted intervention; in practice it concentrates children who "have aggressive attitudes" and "a positive view of violence" into one room.

Topic: school-age self-concept, sexuality, and sex education

Q: A nurse is assessing an 11-year-old's self-concept. Which statement reflects the chapter's definitions?
A) Self-concept is a conscious awareness of a variety of self-perceptions including physical characteristics, abilities, values, and self-ideals and one's idea of self in relation to others, and it includes body image, sexuality, and self-esteem
B) Self-concept and self-esteem are interchangeable terms
C) Body image is determined solely by the opinions of significant others
D) Self-esteem consists only of positive qualities
Answer: A
Rationale: Self-concept is "a conscious awareness of a variety of self-perceptions, such as one's physical characteristics, abilities, values, and self-ideals, and one's idea of self in relation to others. It also includes one's body image, sexuality, and self-esteem." Children's self-concepts are "composed of their own critical self-assessments plus their interpretations of the opinions of others."
Trap: (C) drops the qualifier the chapter puts in the definition: body image is "what children think about their bodies and is influenced, but not solely determined, by significant others." (D) drops the other half of self-esteem, which "consists of both positive and negative qualities."

Q: Parents discover their 9-year-old engaging in sex play with a same-age friend and ask the nurse whether this indicates a problem. Which response reflects the chapter?
A) Many children experience some form of sex play during or before preadolescence as a response to normal curiosity, not as a result of love or sexual urge; it is incidental and transitory, and adverse emotional consequences depend largely on how the parents manage the behavior
B) Sex play at this age indicates prior sexual abuse in all cases
C) Expressions of anger or disgust are the most effective parental response
D) Sex play at this age reflects a precocious sexual urge requiring evaluation
Answer: A
Rationale: "Many children experience some form of sex play during or before preadolescence as a response to normal curiosity, not as a result of love or sexual urge. Children are experimentalists by nature, and this play is incidental and transitory." "Any adverse emotional consequences or guilt feelings depend on how the parents manage the behavior and whether children view their actions as wrong in the eyes of significant persons, particularly their parents."
Trap: (C) names a response the chapter specifically identifies as harmful: parents who discourage exploration through "expressions of anger or disgust" communicate that children "should not engage in such activities, discourage questions about sex, and limit the sources of information."

Q: A parent believes their 10-year-old has learned everything needed about sex from home. Which finding should the nurse share?
A) Sexual information received in childhood is often acquired almost entirely from peers, is transmitted in secret conversation, and contains misinformation; in one study most parents believed they were open about sex education, but only a few communicated direct information about safe sex practices
B) Children reliably obtain accurate sexual information from peers
C) Formal sex education is inappropriate during middle childhood
D) Parents are the sole source of sexual information for most school-age children
Answer: A
Rationale: "Sexual information received in childhood is often acquired almost entirely from peers." One study found that although most parents "believed they were open with sex education discussions… only a few parents communicated direct information about safe sex practices." Peer-sourced information "is often transmitted and exchanged in secret conversation and contains misinformation."
Trap: (C) contradicts the chapter's own judgment that middle childhood "is an ideal time for formal sex education," particularly when presented "from a life span perspective" and treated "as a normal part of growth and development," which makes the information "less likely to contain overtones of uncertainty, guilt, or embarrassment."

Q: A nurse is planning sex education sessions for a fifth-grade class. Which approach reflects the chapter?
A) School-age children may be more comfortable when boys and girls are segregated for discussions, but each group needs information about both sexes
B) Boys and girls should always be taught together, since separating them creates shame
C) Each group needs information only about its own sex
D) Preadolescents should be given general principles rather than concrete answers to specific questions
Answer: A
Rationale: "School-age children may be more comfortable when boys and girls are segregated for discussions; however, each group needs information about both sexes." The nurse should treat sex "as a normal part of growth and development" and answer questions "honestly, matter-of-factly, and at the child's level of understanding."
Trap: (C) takes the first half of the guidance and drops the second, which is the half that matters — segregating the audience is a comfort measure, not a restriction on content. (D) inverts what preadolescents need: they ask concrete questions such as "What if I start my period in the middle of class?" and should be told "what they want to know and what they can expect to happen as they mature sexually."

Topic: school experience, discipline, and dishonest behavior

Q: Parents ask the nurse how to support their child's success at school. Which guidelines does the chapter give? Select all that apply.
A) Demonstrate an interest in content and growth more than in grades
B) Provide a quiet, well-lit area for study without television or music
C) Make it clear to the child that schoolwork is between the child and the teacher
D) Complete difficult homework problems for the child to prevent frustration
E) Send the child to school every day, since other plans convey that school is unimportant
Answer: A, B, C, E
Rationale: The specific guidelines include "send the child to school every day" because other plans "convey the impression that school is unimportant"; "demonstrate an interest in content and growth more than in grades"; "make it clear to the child that schoolwork is between the child and the teacher" so the child feels "responsible for school successes and failures"; and "provide a quiet, well-lit area for study… do not allow television or music."
Trap: (D) is the one option from the wrong side of the sort, and the chapter draws the line precisely: "help with homework should focus on explaining the question, not giving the answer." Doing the work removes the responsibility the third guideline exists to establish.

Q: Parents of a 9-year-old ask whether spanking is an effective disciplinary measure. Which response reflects the chapter?
A) Parents should not use punitive actions or corporal punishment, because these methods are of limited value and are associated with poor internalizing behaviors such as depression and anxiety, poor externalizing behaviors such as aggression, slower cognitive development, and increased risk for physical abuse
B) Spanking is effective when used consistently and immediately
C) Corporal punishment is appropriate for school-age children but not for preschoolers
D) Discipline at this age should be limited to withholding affection
Answer: A
Rationale: "Parents should not use punitive actions or corporal punishment because these methods are of limited value and are associated with multiple negative consequences." Spanking is linked to "poor internalizing behaviors such as depression, anxiety, and hopelessness; poor externalizing behaviors such as aggression and violence; slower cognitive development; and increased risk for physical abuse." Reasoning "is an effective disciplinary technique for school-age children," with time-out to stop a behavior acutely, and withholding privileges, requiring recompense, imposing penalties, and contracting as cognitive skills advance.
Trap: (D) names a technique with its own documented harm and is not among the alternatives the chapter offers. The chapter's stated best approach is different again: "Problem solving is the best approach to limit setting, and children themselves can be included in the process of determining appropriate disciplinary measures."

Q: A 6-year-old repeatedly cheats at board games to win. What should the nurse tell the parents?
A) Cheating is most common in young children ages 5 to 6 years, who find it difficult to lose and have not yet acquired the full realization of the wrongfulness of the behavior; it usually disappears as they mature
B) Cheating at this age predicts dishonesty in adulthood and requires intervention
C) Cheating indicates the child does not understand the rules of the game
D) The child should be excluded from games until the behavior stops
Answer: A
Rationale: "Cheating is most common in young children, ages 5 to 6 years." They "find it difficult to lose at a game or contest, and they cheat to win. They have not yet acquired the full realization of the wrongfulness of this behavior and do it almost automatically. It usually disappears as they mature."
Trap: (B) treats a developmental behavior as a character trait. The chapter does name a real risk, but it lies with the adults: "when children observe parental behaviors such as boasting about cheating on income taxes, they assume this to be appropriate behavior."

Q: A 7-year-old has taken money from a parent's wallet. Which approach does the chapter recommend?
A) In most situations it is best not to attempt to find a hidden or deep meaning to the stealing; a reprimand together with an appropriate and reasonable punishment, such as having the older child pay back the money or return the stolen items, will ordinarily suffice
B) Ask the child directly whether they took the money, to establish honesty
C) Search the child's room and belongings without explanation
D) Assume the behavior signals serious emotional disturbance requiring referral
Answer: A
Rationale: "In most situations it is best not to attempt to find a hidden or deep meaning to the stealing. A reprimand, together with an appropriate and reasonable punishment, such as having the older child pay back the money or return the stolen items, will ordinarily take care of most cases." Between ages 5 and 8 years "children's sense of property rights is limited."
Trap: (B) is the parent's natural first move and the chapter advises against it: "It is seldom helpful to trap children into admission by asking directly if they did the offensive thing," since children "do not take on such responsibility until nearer the end of middle childhood." (C) undermines the very thing being taught — "If children's personal rights are respected, they are more likely to respect the rights of others."

Q: A 10-year-old has begun telling elaborate untrue stories about family vacations. Which understanding should guide the nurse's counseling?
A) School-age children can distinguish what is real from what is make-believe and often exaggerate a story as a means to impress family or friends; older children may also lie because of low self-esteem or to meet expectations they have been unable to meet
B) A school-age child who lies cannot yet distinguish fantasy from reality
C) Lying at this age always indicates an underlying conduct disorder
D) Severe punishment is the most effective deterrent to lying
Answer: A
Rationale: School-age children "still tell stories but can distinguish between what is real and what is make-believe." They often "exaggerate a story… as a means to impress their family or friends." Older children "may lie to meet expectations set by others to which they have been unable to measure up," "because of low self-esteem," or "as a means of getting ahead."
Trap: (B) describes the preschooler, who does "not have the cognitive capacity to deliberately mislead." (D) is contradicted directly: "Lying is more common in families in which punishment is severe," and "If parents lie, the children will emulate their behavior."

Topic: school-age stress, fears, and latchkey children

Q: A school nurse is teaching parents which changes in a school-age child warrant further exploration for stress. Which findings does the Nursing Alert name? Select all that apply.
A) Stomach pains or headache
B) Bedwetting, or regression to earlier behaviors such as thumb sucking
C) Trouble concentrating or changes in academic performance
D) Wanting to spend increasing time with same-sex friends
E) Aggressive or stubborn behavior, or withdrawal and reluctance to participate
Answer: A, B, C, E
Rationale: The Nursing Alert directs exploring further on observing "Stomach pains or headache; Sleep problems; Bedwetting; Changes in eating habits; Aggressive or stubborn behavior; Withdrawal or reluctance to participate; Regression to earlier behaviors (e.g., thumb sucking); Trouble concentrating or changes in academic performance."
Trap: (D) is the one option from the wrong side of the sort and describes normal school-age social development rather than distress. "Identification with peers is a strong influence in children's gaining independence from parents," and same-sex peer groups are characteristic of this stage.

Q: An 11-year-old tells the nurse privately that he is afraid of failing sixth grade but would never admit it to friends. Which understanding applies?
A) Most new fears troubling school-age children relate to school and family, and children are inclined to hide their fears to avoid being ridiculed or labeled; hiding fears does not end them, and children afraid to communicate them may develop displaced fears or phobias
B) School-age children readily disclose fears to peers
C) Fears at this age are the same as those of the preschool years
D) A child who hides a fear has resolved it
Answer: A
Rationale: "Most new fears that trouble school-age children are related to school and family" — "fear of failing, fear of teachers and bullies, or fear of something bad happening to their parents." School-age children "are inclined to hide their fears to avoid being ridiculed or labeled as a 'baby' or 'chicken'. Hiding fears does not end them, and children who are afraid to communicate their fears may develop displaced fears or phobias."
Trap: (C) misses a real shift: school-age children are "less fearful of body safety" and have "less fear of noises, darkness, storms, and dogs" than preschoolers, but they "fear death and are fascinated by all aspects of death and dying." The content of the fears changes even as the number does not.

Q: A nurse is teaching a 10-year-old to manage stress. Which approach reflects the chapter?
A) Teach the child to recognize signs of stress in themselves, such as a pounding heart, rapid breathing, or butterflies in the stomach, and teach deep breathing, progressive muscle relaxation, positive imagery, and physical activity
B) Shield the child from all sources of stress, since any stress is harmful at this age
C) Teach the child to suppress physical signs of stress so peers do not notice
D) Have the child list only the solutions that will definitely work, to avoid wasting effort
Answer: A
Rationale: Children "need to learn how to recognize signs of stress in themselves, such as a pounding heart, rapid breathing, or 'butterflies' in the stomach." Techniques include "deep-breathing exercises, progressive relaxation of muscle groups, and positive imagery," and "blow[ing] off steam' through physical activity."
Trap: (D) truncates the problem-solving sequence the chapter gives, which deliberately starts wide: list all possibilities "including those they know will not work," examine the consequences of each, then select the best option. Adaptive coping is described as problem solving, emotional regulation, and cognitive reappraisal; maladaptive coping shows as withdrawal and helplessness or as aggression and blaming others.

Q: A nurse counsels the parents of a 10-year-old who stays alone after school. Which instructions does the chapter give? Select all that apply.
A) Teach the child not to enter the house after school if the door is ajar, a window is open, or anything appears unusual
B) Teach the child to tell callers that parents are busy and cannot come to the phone right now
C) Teach the child to tell callers honestly that the parents are not at home
D) Teach the child not to display keys and to always lock doors
E) Be punctual in arriving home, since a child's anxiety accelerates when parents are not home when expected
Answer: A, B, D, E
Rationale: The Family-Centered Care guidance directs teaching the child "not to display keys and to always lock doors," and to not enter the house "if the door is ajar, a window is open, or anything appears unusual." For the telephone: "Teach the child to tell callers that parents are 'busy' and cannot come to the phone right now; instruct the child not to tell a caller that parents are not at home." On loneliness: "be punctual in arriving home. A child's anxiety level accelerates when parents are not home when expected."
Trap: (C) is the one option from the wrong side of the sort, and it is the instruction the chapter explicitly reverses. Honesty is the child's default and the reason the script has to be taught deliberately; the number of school-age children spending time alone has decreased to about 15%, but the ones who do are at greater risk for injury and delinquent behavior.

Topic: school-age injury prevention and bicycle safety

Q: A nurse compares injury patterns in school-age children with those of younger children. Which statements reflect the chapter? Select all that apply.
A) The incidence of unintentional injury is diminished in middle childhood compared with early childhood, because of more refined muscular coordination and control
B) The incidence of injury is significantly higher in school-age boys than girls, and boys' death rate is twice that of girls
C) The incidence of transportation-related injuries is higher in school-age children than in younger children
D) Injuries from burns and poisonings are at their peak during the school-age years
E) Most injuries occur in or near the home or school
Answer: A, B, C, E
Rationale: Because school-age children "have developed more refined muscular coordination and control," the incidence of unintentional injury "is diminished in middle childhood compared with early childhood," though they encounter more environments with less supervision. "The incidence of injury during middle childhood is significantly higher in school-age boys than in school-age girls, and their death rate is twice that of girls." "Most injuries occur in or near the home or school," and transportation-related injury incidence is higher than in younger children.
Trap: (D) is the one option from the wrong side of the sort and inverts the chapter's statement: "Injuries from burns and poisonings are lowest in school-age children." What is high instead is "childhood fractures, strains, and sprains," along with cuts and abrasions.

Q: A 7-year-old weighing 52 pounds and measuring 48 inches is being restrained with the vehicle's adult lap-shoulder belt alone. Which response is correct?
A) A booster seat should be used until the child is 57 inches tall, and the rear vehicle seat is the safest place for children younger than 13 years old
B) An adult belt is appropriate once a child reaches 7 years of age
C) The front seat is acceptable for children over 6 years old
D) Booster seats are discontinued at 40 pounds
Answer: A
Rationale: "Booster seats should be used until the child is 57 inches tall," and "The rear vehicle seat is the safest place for children younger than 13 years old." In 2014, 602 children ages 0 to 12 years died in motor vehicle accidents and 121,350 were injured, and "34% of those who died were not using a seat belt."
Trap: (B) uses age where the chapter uses height, which is the substitution that leaves children in adult belts too early. The chapter explains why belts fail children ages 5 to 9 specifically: the child's "center of gravity is located above the level of the lap belt," so body mass above the belt causes "jackknifing over the belt," and the child's "smaller and less developed iliac crests" cannot anchor an adult restraint.

Q: A parent asks why a bicycle helmet matters so much when their child only rides on quiet streets. Which response reflects the chapter?
A) Head injury is the major cause of bicycle-related fatalities, so the single most important aspect of bicycle safety is wearing a protective helmet; helmet use reduces head injury by 51%, serious head injury by 69%, and fatal head injury by 65%
B) Helmets primarily prevent facial injury rather than head injury
C) Helmets are needed only for riding in traffic
D) Helmet effectiveness has not been quantified
Answer: A
Rationale: "Because head injury is the major cause of bicycle-related fatalities, the single most important aspect of bicycle safety is to encourage the rider to wear a protective helmet." Helmet use reduces head injury by 51%, serious head injury by 69%, facial injury by 33%, and fatal head injury by 65%. Hard-shelled helmets lined with expanded polystyrene "provide the best head protection," should fit securely without limiting vision or hearing, should carry a Consumer Product Safety Commission seal, and "should be replaced after any damage or crash."
Trap: (B) names the smallest of the four reductions and misses the one that drives the recommendation. Note the fitting rule too: the helmet "should sit on top of the head in a level position and should not rock back and forth or from side to side," with the strap "fastened securely under the chin."

Q: Parents say their 9-year-old refuses to wear a bicycle helmet, and neither parent wears one. Which information is most useful?
A) Ninety percent of children reported they were likely to wear a helmet if their parent used a helmet, compared with only 38% of children reporting a likelihood to wear one if their parent did not
B) Helmet laws are more influential than parental behavior
C) Children's helmet use is unrelated to parental modeling
D) Children this age cannot be expected to wear helmets consistently
Answer: A
Rationale: "Ninety percent of children reported they were likely to wear a helmet if their parent used a helmet, compared with only 38% of children reporting a likelihood to wear a helmet if their parent did not use a helmet." Legislation has a smaller effect: "51% of children always wearing a helmet in states that have helmet laws and only 40% of children always wearing a helmet in states that do not."
Trap: (B) reverses the relative size of the two effects. Both matter, but the gap produced by parental modeling — 90% against 38% — is far larger than the gap produced by state law, which makes the parents in this stem the intervention rather than the audience.
