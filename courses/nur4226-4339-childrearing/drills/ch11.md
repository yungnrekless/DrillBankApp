# Chapter 11 drills — Health Problems of the Infant

Authored from `sources/Ch11_Fact_Bank.md` (Hockenberry, Wilson & Rodgers, 11th ed.,
printed pp. 372–392).

Blueprint weight is **7 questions of 202 (3.5%), depth `standard`** — 45 drills
across 10 topics, at the top of the 30–45 band.

This is the health-problems companion to Chapter 10, and the two are meant to be
studied together: Chapter 10 says what a normal infant does, this one says what
goes wrong. Where a fact here has a counterpart there — pacifiers, sleep position,
crying, solid-food timing — the two chapters' drills are deliberately consistent
rather than deduplicated.

**Where the weight went.** The fact bank names two dominant surfaces and both get
the most room: **SIDS**, split across two topics because epidemiology and safe-sleep
teaching are separate restudy units, and the **food-sensitivity cluster**, where the
discriminations between food allergy, cow's milk allergy, and lactose intolerance
are the whole item. Colic, sleep disturbances, and failure to thrive each carry a
distinct nursing rule and each earned a topic.

**Drilled from both ends:** kwashiorkor and marasmus, cow's milk allergy and
lactose intolerance, acute and chronic failure to thrive, trained and developmental
night crying, dyssomnias and parasomnias, room-sharing and bed-sharing. Each is a
distinction a learner can hold backwards, and in three of them the inversion
changes what the nurse does.

**What twenty printed pages could not buy at 45 questions.** Left undrilled: the
vitamin and mineral imbalance tables that open the chapter (hypervitaminosis A and
D, vitamin K, C, and B-complex deficiencies, iron and mineral imbalances), which
the fact bank itself flags as the first place to expand if a drill misses there;
Box 11.1's food-by-food list of hidden allergen sources; the ten-item colic
history; home apnea monitor operation and troubleshooting beyond the "look first
at the infant" rule; the ALTE diagnosis percentages; and the NICU education
before-and-after figures. Zinc deficiency survives only inside the kwashiorkor
select-all. The rule when cutting was to keep the fact that decides an action —
epinephrine thresholds, sleep position, the milk-free trial — over the fact that
decides a percentage.

Six questions are select-all, one for each list in the chapter that genuinely
sorts two ways: kwashiorkor's picture against marasmus's, epinephrine indications
against antihistamine territory, prevention measures that work against the one
that inverts the evidence, hospitalization criteria against normal toddler
behavior, SIDS risk factors against protective ones, and unsafe sleep-environment
findings against a protective one.

Topic: severe acute malnutrition and protein-energy deficiency

Q: A 3-year-old seen in a refugee clinic has thin, wasted extremities, a prominent abdomen, and scaly, dry, depigmented skin. The child was weaned from the breast when a younger sibling was born. Which form of severe acute malnutrition does this describe, and what is the deficit?
A) Kwashiorkor, primarily a deficiency of protein with an adequate supply of calories
B) Marasmus, general malnutrition of both calories and protein
C) Marasmic kwashiorkor, in which stunted growth occurs without edema
D) Kwashiorkor, a deficiency of both calories and protein
Answer: A
Rationale: Kwashiorkor is "primarily a deficiency of protein with an adequate supply of calories." It develops "in the first child, usually between 1 and 4 years of age, when weaned from the breast after the second child is born," and presents with "thin, wasted extremities and a prominent abdomen from edema (ascites)" and skin that is "scaly and dry" with depigmentation. The word itself means "the sickness the older child gets when the next baby is born."
Trap: (D) attaches the correct name to marasmus's deficit, which is the commonest error because both are called malnutrition and the calorie-versus-protein split is the only thing separating them. (C) names a real entity, but marasmic kwashiorkor requires edema, severe wasting, and stunted growth together.

Q: A 4-month-old in the same clinic shows gradual wasting and atrophy of body tissues, especially subcutaneous fat, with no edema, after breastfeeding was unsuccessful. Which form of severe acute malnutrition is this?
A) Kwashiorkor, from protein deficiency with adequate calories
B) Marasmus, from general malnutrition of both calories and protein
C) Secondary lactase deficiency from intestinal damage
D) Failure to thrive from defective utilization
Answer: B
Rationale: Marasmus "results from general malnutrition of both calories and protein" and is "characterized by gradual wasting and atrophy of body tissues, especially of subcutaneous fat." It may appear "in infants as young as 3 months of age if breastfeeding is unsuccessful," and it carries no edema.
Trap: (A) is the mirror error of the previous item. Absence of edema is the discriminating finding at the bedside, and the age here — months rather than years — points the same way.

Q: A child with kwashiorkor is admitted. Which findings should the nurse expect? Select all that apply.
A) Edema that masks severe muscular atrophy, making the child appear less debilitated than he or she actually is
B) Increased susceptibility to infection from protein deficiency
C) Blindness from severe lack of vitamin A
D) Absence of edema, with wasting of subcutaneous fat as the dominant finding
E) Anemia and fatty infiltration of the liver
Answer: A, B, C, E
Rationale: In kwashiorkor the edema "often masks severe muscular atrophy making the child appear less debilitated than he or she actually is." The chapter also lists blindness from severe lack of vitamin A, deficiencies of iron, calcium, and zinc, a blunted immune response, fatty liver infiltration, and anemia. "Protein deficiency increases the child's susceptibility to infection, which eventually results in death."
Trap: (D) is the one option from the wrong side of the sort, and it is marasmus's defining appearance. It pulls because the word malnutrition summons a wasted image, and kwashiorkor's edema hides exactly that — which is also why these children are undertriaged.

Q: A nurse is teaching about the causes of severe acute malnutrition worldwide. Which statement is accurate?
A) Lack of food is always the primary cause, so increasing food supply is the only meaningful intervention
B) Lack of food is not always the primary cause; in many developing and underdeveloped nations diarrhea is a major factor
C) Severe acute malnutrition does not occur in the United States, where food supplies are ample
D) Kwashiorkor in the United States is most often caused by excessive whole cow's milk intake
Answer: B
Rationale: "Lack of food is not always the primary cause of malnutrition. In many developing and underdeveloped nations, diarrhea (gastroenteritis) is a major factor." In the United States "SAM may occur despite ample food supplies," and the chapter names a rice beverage diet as a domestic cause of kwashiorkor, since that drink "contains only 0.13 g of protein per ounce (compared with the 0.5 g found in human milk and infant formulas)."
Trap: (C) is the reflex assumption that makes this chapter's United States cases invisible; the book lists cystic fibrosis, renal disease, cancer, bone marrow transplantation, HIV, inborn errors of metabolism, and prolonged untreated anorexia nervosa among domestic causes.

Topic: food allergy recognition and anaphylaxis management

Q: A parent asks why food allergies seem to appear in babies rather than later in childhood. Which explanation does the chapter give?
A) The immature intestinal tract is more permeable to proteins than the mature tract, increasing the likelihood of an immune response
B) Infants produce more immunoglobulin E than older children at every age
C) Infants lack the enzymes needed to digest most food proteins
D) Breast milk sensitizes the infant to common allergens during the first months of life
Answer: A
Rationale: Food allergies "are most common during infancy because the immature intestinal tract is more permeable to proteins than the mature intestinal tract thus increasing the likelihood of an immune response."
Trap: (C) substitutes an enzyme problem for an immune one, which is the same confusion that runs through the cow's milk allergy and lactose intolerance pair later in this chapter. (D) inverts the recommendation, which is "exclusive breastfeeding for at least 4 to 6 months."

Q: Both parents of a newborn have documented allergies. What risk should the nurse convey?
A) The infant's risk is the same as that of the general population
B) The infant has a 50% or greater risk, the same figure as with one affected parent
C) The infant has up to a 100% risk, and some atopic infants can be identified at birth from elevated immunoglobulin E in umbilical cord blood
D) The infant has roughly a 25% risk, following an autosomal recessive pattern
Answer: C
Rationale: "Children who have one parent with allergy have a 50% or greater risk"; "Children who have both parents with allergy have up to a 100% risk." Some atopic infants "can be identified at birth from elevated levels of IgE in umbilical cord blood." Atopy is defined as "allergy with a hereditary tendency."
Trap: (B) is the one-parent figure, and it is picked by anyone carrying a single number for family history. The chapter gives two, and the two-parent figure is double it.

Q: A 2-year-old with a known peanut allergy begins wheezing with decreased air movement and dyspnea about 10 minutes after a meal. There are no hives, rash, or flushing. What should the nurse conclude?
A) This is an asthma exacerbation, since anaphylaxis always begins with skin signs
B) This may be anaphylaxis; in children who died of food anaphylaxis the reactions often did not begin with skin signs but mimicked an acute asthma attack
C) This is too early to be anaphylaxis, which begins 2 to 48 hours after ingestion
D) This is oral allergy syndrome, which produces wheezing without skin involvement
Answer: B
Rationale: In the fatal cases the chapter describes, "Onset of the reactions occurred shortly after ingestion (5 to 30 minutes). In most of these children, the reactions did not begin with skin signs, such as hives, red rash, and flushing, but rather mimicked an acute asthma attack (e.g., wheezing, decreased air movement in airways, dyspnea)."
Trap: (A) is the assumption behind these deaths, and it is why the chapter reports them: waiting for hives before treating is what made the reactions fatal. (C) applies the delayed-allergy window of 2 to 48 hours to an immediate reaction. (D) misdescribes oral allergy syndrome, which is "edema and pruritus involving the lips, tongue, palate, and throat."

Q: A child with a known food allergy is being assessed after an accidental ingestion. Which findings indicate that intramuscular epinephrine should be given? Select all that apply.
A) Itching sensation or tightness in the throat, hoarseness, difficulty swallowing
B) A barky cough, wheezing, dyspnea, or cyanosis
C) Mild cardiac dysrhythmia or mild hypotension
D) An isolated area of localized itching where the food touched the skin
E) Severe bradycardia, hypotension, or loss of consciousness
Answer: A, B, C, E
Rationale: The Nursing Alert lists four indications for intramuscular epinephrine, any one of which qualifies: throat itching or tightness with hoarseness and difficulty swallowing; a barky cough, wheezing, dyspnea, cyanosis, or respiratory arrest; mild cardiac dysrhythmia or mild hypotension; and severe bradycardia, hypotension, cardiac arrest, or loss of consciousness.
Trap: (D) is the one option from the wrong side of the sort, and it is where antihistamines belong: diphenhydramine and cetirizine "are effect[ive] for cutaneous and nasal symptoms but not for airway symptoms." Note that even mild hypotension is on the epinephrine list, which is a lower threshold than most learners set.

Q: A 4-year-old treated with epinephrine for food anaphylaxis improves within minutes, and the parents ask to take the child home. What should the nurse explain?
A) A biphasic reaction — apparent recovery followed by acute recurrence of symptoms — is reported in as many as 35% of children, so continued close observation is needed
B) Discharge is appropriate once symptoms resolve, because epinephrine prevents recurrence
C) A dose of diphenhydramine at home will prevent any recurrence of airway symptoms
D) Recurrence can happen only if the child ingests the allergen again
Answer: A
Rationale: A biphasic reaction is "an immediate response to treatment, apparent recovery, and then acute recurrence of symptoms," reported "in as many as 35% of children." Children with severe food allergies should also "wear a medical identification bracelet and have an injectable epinephrine cartridge (EpiPen) readily available," plus a written emergency treatment plan.
Trap: (C) offers an antihistamine for exactly the symptoms it does not treat: diphenhydramine and cetirizine are effective for cutaneous and nasal symptoms "but not for airway symptoms."

Topic: food allergy diagnosis and prevention evidence

Q: A family asks which test will confirm their child's suspected food allergy. Which response is accurate?
A) The gold standard is the double-blind, placebo-controlled food challenge; skin prick and serum immunoglobulin E testing are adjuncts and singly should not be used for diagnosis
B) A serum immunoglobulin E level is definitive and is the recommended first test
C) The atopy patch test establishes the diagnosis when skin prick testing is equivocal
D) The intradermal test is the gold standard for food allergy in children
Answer: A
Rationale: "The gold standard is the double-blind, placebo-controlled food challenge." Skin prick and serum IgE "may be used as an adjunct... but singly should not be used for diagnosis," and "The atopy patch test, intradermal test, and serum IgE test are not recommended for establishing a diagnosis."
Trap: (B) is the reflex pick because a blood test feels more objective than a supervised feeding, and it is one of the three tests the chapter names explicitly as not recommended for establishing a diagnosis.

Q: An expectant mother with a strong family history of allergy asks what she can do to prevent food allergy in her baby. Which statements reflect the chapter's evidence? Select all that apply.
A) There is no evidence that maternal avoidance of common food allergens during pregnancy or lactation prevents food allergies in children
B) There is no evidence that soy formulas prevent allergic disease in infants and children
C) Delaying the introduction of highly allergenic foods past 4 to 6 months of age may not be as protective as previously believed
D) Prolonged or complete avoidance of foods is recommended, since lack of exposure to antigens is protective
E) Exclusive breastfeeding for at least 4 to 6 months is recommended
Answer: A, B, C, E
Rationale: The chapter states each of the three negatives directly and recommends "exclusive breastfeeding for at least 4 to 6 months." It adds that "The prolonged or complete avoidance of foods to prevent food allergy is not recommended, and there is some evidence that lack of exposure to antigens may be detrimental."
Trap: (D) is the one option from the wrong side of the sort, and it inverts the evidence precisely: lack of exposure may be detrimental rather than protective. That reversal is what most families, and much older teaching, still hold.

Q: A 3-year-old has been on a strict milk-elimination diet for a confirmed allergy. What ongoing care does the chapter direct?
A) An annual nutritional assessment, because dietary restriction of milk may lead to calcium, vitamin D, calorie, and protein deficiencies
B) No additional monitoring, since elimination diets are nutritionally complete by definition
C) Annual skin prick testing to confirm that the allergy persists
D) Permanent avoidance of the food in every form, since cooking does not change tolerance
Answer: A
Rationale: Children with food allergies "are at risk for inadequate nutrient intake and growth failure," and should have "an annual nutritional assessment." "Dietary restrictions of milk may lead to calcium, vitamin D, calorie, and protein deficiencies," and eliminating wheat "may result in inadequate intake of the B vitamins, iron, and calories."
Trap: (D) contradicts the chapter's note that children may tolerate previously allergenic foods "when these foods are extensively heated as in muffins or breads." It is the reflex answer because the word allergy reads as absolute.

Topic: cow's milk allergy versus lactose intolerance

Q: A 3-month-old formula-fed infant has diarrhea, bloody stools, eczema, and pallor, and is otherwise healthy. Which condition should the nurse suspect first?
A) Cow's milk allergy, which may be manifested within the first 4 months of life
B) Primary lactase deficiency, which usually manifests after 4 or 5 years of age
C) Congenital lactase deficiency, which is the most common lactose intolerance in infancy
D) Colic, which is defined by bloody stools and eczema
Answer: A
Rationale: Cow's milk allergy "may be manifested within the first 4 months of life," with signs appearing "within 45 minutes of milk ingestion or after several days." Box 11.2 lists diarrhea, bloody stools, and rectal bleeding among gastrointestinal signs, and eczema and "Pallor (from anemia secondary to chronic blood loss in gastrointestinal tract)" among the others. The presentation is protean: "colic, diarrhea, vomiting, GI bleeding, gastroesophageal reflux, chronic constipation, or sleeplessness in an otherwise healthy infant."
Trap: (C) mislabels congenital lactase deficiency, which is "extremely rare." Primary late-onset deficiency is the most common type and does not appear in infancy at all.

Q: An infant with confirmed cow's milk allergy needs a formula change. Which choice reflects the chapter's guidance, and what caution accompanies it?
A) A soy-based formula, which is safe because soy protein is unrelated to cow's milk protein
B) A casein hydrolysate formula such as Pregestimil, Nutramigen, or Alimentum, noting that approximately 50% of infants sensitive to cow's milk protein also react to soy-based formulas
C) A reduced-lactose formula, since lactose is the offending component
D) Whole cow's milk diluted with water, to reduce the protein load
Answer: B
Rationale: Treatment is a change to "a casein hydrolysate milk formula (Pregestimil, Nutramigen, or Alimentum) in which the protein has been broken down into its amino acids through enzymatic hydrolysis." The AAP recommends extensively hydrolyzed formulas, and "approximately 50% of infants who are sensitive to cow's milk protein will also demonstrate a sensitivity to soy-based formulas." Amino acid-based alternatives exist but cost is a major consideration.
Trap: (A) is the substitution practitioners often reach for first because of expense, and the chapter says so — but half of these infants react to soy as well, so calling it safe is the error. (C) treats a protein allergy as a sugar intolerance, which is the central confusion of this topic.

Q: A parent says their 6-year-old is "allergic to milk" because dairy causes bloating, flatulence, and diarrhea about an hour after eating it. Which statement distinguishes lactose intolerance from cow's milk allergy?
A) Lactose intolerance is an immune reaction to milk protein and can produce anaphylaxis
B) Lactose intolerance is a deficiency of the enzyme lactase needed to digest milk sugar, and it will not manifest as an anaphylactic-type reaction
C) Cow's milk allergy produces symptoms only after several days, so an hour is too soon for it
D) The two are the same condition described at different ages
Answer: B
Rationale: Lactose intolerance "encompasses at least four different conditions that involve a deficiency of the enzyme lactase, which is needed for the hydrolysis or digestion of lactose in the small intestine," with symptoms appearing "within 30 minutes to several hours of lactose consumption." The chapter names the key discrimination outright: "An important distinction between lactose intolerance and food allergy is that lactose intolerance will not manifest as an anaphylactic-type reaction."
Trap: (A) inverts the definition, and it is why this distinction earns a question: families use the word allergy for both conditions, and only one of them can kill. (C) misstates cow's milk allergy timing, which runs from 45 minutes to several days.

Q: A preterm infant born at 32 weeks of gestation has lactose malabsorption. Which type of lactase deficiency does this represent?
A) Congenital lactase deficiency, which requires a lifelong lactose-free diet
B) Primary, or late-onset, lactase deficiency
C) Developmental lactase deficiency, the relative deficiency observed in preterm infants of less than 34 weeks of gestation
D) Secondary lactase deficiency following intestinal damage
Answer: C
Rationale: Developmental lactase deficiency is "the relative lactase deficiency observed in preterm infants of less than 34 weeks of gestation." Congenital deficiency is extremely rare and requires "a lifelong lactose-free or extremely reduced lactose diet"; primary deficiency is the most common type and appears after 4 or 5 years; secondary deficiency follows intestinal damage from conditions such as celiac disease, kwashiorkor, giardiasis, HIV, or rotavirus.
Trap: (A) is chosen because present-from-birth sounds like congenital. Congenital lactase deficiency appears in a newborn "soon after birth when the newborn has consumed lactose-containing milk" and is permanent; prematurity has its own named category, and it is relative rather than absolute.

Q: A school-age child with lactose intolerance has been placed on a completely dairy-free diet by the family. What should the nurse advise?
A) Continue complete elimination, since any amount of dairy will produce symptoms
B) Decrease rather than eliminate dairy, because dairy avoidance contributes to reduced bone mineral density and osteoporosis later in life
C) Replace all dairy with soy formula, which supplies equivalent calcium
D) Add lactase tablets only if symptoms later develop on the dairy-free diet
Answer: B
Rationale: "Some advocate decreasing amounts of dairy products rather than total elimination, especially in small children," because "dairy avoidance in children and adolescents with lactose intolerance will contribute to reduced bone mineral density and osteoporosis later in life." The chapter adds that "dietary lactose enhances calcium absorption and... lactose-free diets may negatively affect bone mineralization." Calcium and vitamin D should be supplemented.
Trap: (A) is the intuitive management and is the one the chapter argues against. The harm from total avoidance is skeletal and appears years later, so it is invisible at the moment the decision is made.

Q: A family asks how to keep some dairy in their lactose-intolerant child's diet. Which strategy set does the chapter support?
A) Most people tolerate about 1 cup of milk per day, milk is better tolerated with meals than alone, and fresh plain yogurt is often tolerated because its inactive lactase is activated by the temperature and pH of the duodenum
B) Milk is best tolerated on an empty stomach, when intestinal transit is fastest
C) Frozen and flavored yogurts are tolerated better than fresh plain yogurt
D) Hard cheeses should be avoided, since they carry the highest lactose content
Answer: A
Rationale: "Most people can tolerate small amounts of lactose (approximately 1 cup of milk per day [12 g])." "Milk taken at meals may be better tolerated than when taken alone." Yogurt "contains inactive lactase enzyme which is activated by the temperature and pH of the duodenum," substituting for endogenous lactase, and "Fresh, plain yogurt may be tolerated better than frozen or flavored yogurt." Hard cheeses, lactase-treated dairy products, and lactase tablets are also useful.
Trap: (C) inverts the yogurt guidance, and it pulls because frozen yogurt feels like the more processed and therefore more treated product. The chapter says the opposite, and the mechanism explains why: freezing does not activate the enzyme, the duodenum does.

Topic: failure to thrive in the infant

Q: A 9-month-old's weight has fallen from the 50th to the 4th percentile over four months. Which statement supports a diagnosis of failure to thrive?
A) Growth parameters that drop more than 2 percentiles from baseline, or are persistently below the third to fifth percentiles, meet the diagnostic criteria
B) A single weight below the 50th percentile is sufficient for the diagnosis
C) Height for age is the most accurate indicator of undernutrition
D) The diagnosis requires a weight below the first percentile
Answer: A
Rationale: Failure to thrive is diagnosed on growth parameters that "(1) drop more than 2 percentiles from baseline, (2) are persistently below the third to fifth percentiles, or (3) are less than the 80th percentile of median weight for height." "Weight for length is reported to be a more accurate indicator of undernutrition," and it is "the finding of a pattern of persistent deviation from established growth parameters" that matters.
Trap: (C) names the wrong ratio — weight for length, not height for age, is the more accurate indicator. (B) mistakes a below-average measurement for a deviation, and no single measurement makes this diagnosis.

Q: A 2-year-old has a weight below the fifth percentile with length at the tenth percentile. A second 2-year-old has both weight and length below the fifth percentile. How should the nurse interpret these two patterns?
A) The first child has chronic failure to thrive and the second has acute failure to thrive
B) The first child has acute failure to thrive and the second has chronic failure to thrive, indicating ongoing malnutrition
C) Both patterns indicate acute failure to thrive
D) Neither pattern meets diagnostic criteria without an accompanying developmental delay
Answer: B
Rationale: "If FTT is acute, the weight, but not the length/height, is below accepted standards (usually the 5th percentile). If FTT is chronic, both weight and length/height are low, indicating ongoing malnutrition."
Trap: (A) is the mirror error, and it is easy to make because chronic intuitively sounds like the more selective finding. Chronicity actually shows up as the broader deficit, because linear growth takes far longer to fall away than weight does.

Q: A nurse reviews the causes of failure to thrive. Which statement reflects the chapter?
A) The four pathophysiologic categories are inadequate caloric intake, inadequate absorption, increased metabolism, and defective utilization, and the primary etiology is inadequate caloric intake regardless of the cause
B) The organic and nonorganic categories remain the preferred classification
C) Increased metabolism is the primary etiology in the majority of cases
D) Poverty is a minor contributor in developed countries
Answer: A
Rationale: The four pathophysiologic categories replaced the older organic and nonorganic split, now considered "too simplistic." "However, the primary etiology of FTT is inadequate caloric intake regardless of the cause." The chapter also states that "poverty is believed to be the greatest risk factor for FTT in developed and developing countries."
Trap: (D) is the reflex assumption that poverty belongs to the developing world, and the chapter names it as the greatest risk factor in both settings. (B) keeps a classification the chapter has explicitly retired.

Q: An 11-month-old admitted with failure to thrive is beginning nutritional rehabilitation. Which plan reflects the chapter's goals?
A) Standard 20 kcal/oz formula at maintenance volumes, with unrestricted juice to increase total intake
B) Higher-calorie formula at 24 kcal/oz, calories adequate for catch-up growth at 2 to 3 times the average rate of weight gain for corrected age, multivitamins including zinc and iron, and restricted juice until adequate weight gain is achieved
C) 30 kcal/oz formula, which is the standard infant density for catch-up growth
D) Rapid advancement without monitoring, since refeeding syndrome occurs only in adults
Answer: B
Rationale: Catch-up growth requires "2 to 3 times the average rate of weight gain for the child's corrected age," using "higher-calorie formulas (24 kcal/oz)" for infants, with multivitamin supplementation "including zinc and iron." "Fruit juice is restricted in children with FTT until adequate weight gain has been achieved," then limited to "no more than 4 oz/day."
Trap: (C) applies the 30-kcal/oz density meant for children 1 to 6 years to an infant, which is the commonest swap in this pair of numbers. (A) adds unrestricted juice, which the chapter names as a cause of inadequate caloric intake in the first place. (D) dismisses refeeding syndrome, which the chapter says to watch for when malnutrition is severe.

Q: A toddler with failure to thrive is being managed as an outpatient. Which findings indicate that hospitalization is warranted? Select all that apply.
A) Anthropometric evidence of severe acute malnutrition
B) Suspected child abuse or neglect
C) Significant dehydration
D) Occasional refusal of food at the dinner table
E) Failure of outpatient management to result in weight gain
Answer: A, B, C, E
Rationale: Hospitalization is indicated for "(1) evidence (anthropometric) of SAM, (2) child abuse or neglect, (3) significant dehydration, (4) caretaker substance abuse or psychosis, (5) serious concurrent infection, or (6) if outpatient management fails to result in weight gain."
Trap: (D) is the one option from the wrong side of the sort, and the chapter draws that line itself: feeding-time "war of wills" behaviors "are different from the occasional toddler behavior of food refusal, which is primarily developmental, not pathologic."

Topic: infantile colic

Q: A 6-week-old cries inconsolably for about four hours most evenings, five days a week, and has done so for a month. Weight gain has tracked steadily along the 50th percentile. How should the nurse interpret this?
A) Colic, characterized by crying greater than 3 hours a day, more than 3 days per week, for more than 3 weeks; despite the behavioral indications of pain the infant with colic gains weight and usually thrives
B) Failure to thrive, since prolonged crying implies inadequate intake
C) Cow's milk allergy, which the crying pattern alone confirms
D) Colic, which the normal weight gain excludes
Answer: A
Rationale: Colic "can be characterized by crying (1) greater than 3 hours a day, (2) for more than 3 days per week, and (3) for more than 3 weeks," and symptoms "may increase in the late afternoon or evening." "Despite the obvious behavioral indications of pain, the infant with colic gains weight and usually thrives." Colic is also "more common in younger infants under the age of 3 months."
Trap: (D) inverts the relationship: normal weight gain is characteristic of colic rather than against it, and it is the finding that separates colic from a problem causing failure to thrive. (B) is the reflex leap from crying to not eating, which this growth curve contradicts.

Q: Parents of a colicky 8-week-old ask how long this will last and whether it will harm their child. Which response is accurate?
A) Colic is self-limiting and usually resolves as the infant matures, generally around 12 to 16 weeks of age, with no evidence of a residual effect on older children
B) Colic typically persists until the first birthday and is associated with later behavioral disorders
C) Colic resolves only with dietary treatment and continues indefinitely without it
D) An organic cause is identified in most infants evaluated for excessive crying
Answer: A
Rationale: "Colic is self-limiting and usually resolves as the infant matures, generally around 12 to 16 weeks of age," and "There is no evidence of a residual effect of colic on older children, except perhaps a strained parent-child relationship." Colic affects "15% to 20% of all infants," yet "an organic cause may be identified in less than 5% of infants seen by practitioners for excessive crying."
Trap: (D) badly overstates how often a cause is found — under 5% — and it pulls because a symptom this dramatic feels as though it must have a findable explanation. That expectation is what drives families through repeated unproductive workups.

Q: Parents of a colicky infant are exhausted, and the mother says she must be doing something wrong. What is the most important nursing intervention?
A) Reassure the parents that they are not to blame for their infant's discomfort, and address coping with the crying
B) Begin simethicone, which resolves symptoms in most infants
C) Advise an immediate switch to soy formula, which eliminates colic
D) Instruct the parents to leave the infant to cry until the episode passes each evening
Answer: A
Rationale: "One important nursing intervention is to reassure parents they are not to blame for their infant's discomfort." The chapter goes further: "Helping caregivers understand the nature of the infant's crying and methods to effectively cope may be more important than finding a specific cause or treatment for colic." It also warns that "colicky infants may be at increased risk for being shaken by their caregivers and experiencing traumatic brain injury," and that excessive crying raises the risk of abusive head trauma and of maternal and paternal depression.
Trap: (B) overstates simethicone, which "may help relieve the symptoms" while "in most controlled studies no medications completely resolved the symptoms." The Nursing Alert is blunt: "there is no evidence to support one remedy that will relieve symptoms in every infant."

Q: Cow's milk sensitivity is suspected in a breastfed colicky infant. Which instruction is correct?
A) The mother should follow a milk-free diet for a minimum of 3 to 5 days, and should know that some nondairy creamers contain calcium caseinate, a cow's milk protein
B) The mother should wean to a soy formula, which carries no risk of sensitivity
C) The mother should eliminate milk for 24 hours, which is long enough to judge the response
D) No dietary trial is warranted, since colic and cow's milk allergy do not overlap
Answer: A
Rationale: If cow's milk sensitivity is suspected, "breastfeeding mothers should follow a milk-free diet for a minimum of 3 to 5 days," and "some nondairy creamers may contain calcium caseinate, a cow's milk protein." Soy formulas are often prescribed, but "10% to 14% of those infants will develop a sensitivity to soy protein as well."
Trap: (D) is contradicted by the overlap the chapter reports — "Infants with CMA symptoms have a high rate of colic (44%)" — which is exactly why a milk-free trial is a legitimate colic intervention. (B) treats soy as risk-free, which this chapter denies twice, here and under cow's milk allergy.

Topic: infant sleep disturbances and their management

Q: A parent describes a 4-year-old who sleepwalks and has episodes of sleep terrors, and a 9-month-old who cannot fall asleep without prolonged rocking. How does the chapter classify these two problems?
A) Both are dyssomnias
B) The 4-year-old's episodes are parasomnias, and the infant's difficulty falling asleep is a dyssomnia
C) The 4-year-old's episodes are dyssomnias, and the infant's difficulty is a parasomnia
D) Both are parasomnias, which occur in children 3 to 8 years old
Answer: B
Rationale: Dyssomnias are problems in which "the child has trouble either falling or staying asleep at night or has difficulty staying awake during the day." Parasomnias are "confusional arousals, sleepwalking, sleep terrors, nightmares, and rhythmic movement disorders," which "typically occur in children 3 to 8 years old."
Trap: (C) is the inversion, and the two words are close enough that the pair has to be held by content rather than by prefix: trouble sleeping is a dyssomnia, events during sleep are parasomnias.

Q: A 9-month-old who has slept through the night for months now wakes abruptly and cries. A second 9-month-old falls asleep in a rocking chair each evening, is carried to bed, and cries on waking until the rocking is repeated. How should the nurse advise each family?
A) Both families should let the child cry until falling asleep
B) The first is developmental night crying, so reassure the parents that the phase is temporary and keep checks brief; the second is trained night crying, so put the child in the bed awake and do not resume the usual routine
C) The first is trained night crying and the second is developmental night crying
D) Both children should be fed on waking to re-establish the sleep cycle
Answer: B
Rationale: Table 11.1 describes developmental night crying in a child "ages 6-12 months with undisturbed nighttime sleep" who "now awakens abruptly," managed by reassuring parents "that this phase is temporary," entering the room immediately but keeping reassurances brief. Trained night crying occurs when a child "typically falls asleep in place other than own bed... and is brought to own bed while asleep; on awakening, cries until usual routine is instituted," managed by putting the child "in own bed when awake" and checking at progressively longer intervals without resuming the routine.
Trap: (C) is the inversion of the pair. The discriminator is where the child fell asleep: a child moved after falling asleep elsewhere has learned an association, while a previously good sleeper who wakes abruptly has not. (A) applies advice the chapter names as "difficult to implement and inappropriate for certain conditions."

Q: A nurse is teaching new parents how to prevent sleep problems. Which advice does the chapter identify as most constructive?
A) Place infants in their crib while still awake, and use the bed for sleeping only, not for play
B) Rock the infant fully to sleep before transferring to the crib, so the transition is smooth
C) Keep the infant in the parents' bed for the first year to build security
D) Offer a bottle in bed so the infant learns to settle independently
Answer: A
Rationale: "The best way to prevent sleep problems is to encourage parents to establish bedtime routines that do not foster problematic patterns. One of the most constructive routines consists of placing infants in their crib while still awake." When infants fall asleep elsewhere and are transferred, "they awaken in unfamiliar surroundings and may be unable to fall back to sleep until the routine is repeated." The chapter adds that "the bed should be used for sleeping only, not for play."
Trap: (B) is precisely the pattern that produces trained night crying, and it is the reflex answer because a sleeping infant transfers more quietly tonight. (D) contradicts the management of nighttime feeding — "Offer no bottles in bed. Put to bed awake" — and creates caries risk on top of it.

Q: A 4-year-old resists bedtime and repeatedly comes out of the bedroom. Which assessment does the chapter direct the nurse to make first?
A) Whether the hour of sleep is too early, since a child may resist sleep if not tired
B) Whether an underlying parasomnia requires referral
C) Whether the parents should begin sleeping in the child's room
D) Whether a bottle at bedtime would settle the child
Answer: A
Rationale: For refusal to go to sleep, Table 11.1 directs the nurse to "Evaluate whether hour of sleep is too early (child may resist sleep if not tired)," then establish a consistent bedtime routine with consistent limits, close the door for progressively longer periods if the child persists in leaving the bedroom, and use a reward system. The same evaluation heads the management of nighttime fears, where the child "may fantasize when nothing to do but think in dark room."
Trap: (C) is the accommodation the chapter warns against under nighttime fears: "Avoid patterns that can lead to additional problems (e.g., sleeping with child or taking child to parents' room)." It pulls because it works tonight and builds the problem it is meant to solve.

Topic: sudden infant death syndrome epidemiology and risk

Q: A 3-month-old is found dead in the crib and sudden infant death syndrome is suspected. What should the nurse understand about the diagnosis?
A) It is the sudden death of an infant younger than 1 year that remains unexplained after a complete postmortem examination including a death-scene investigation and case-history review, and the autopsy typically cannot distinguish it from intentional suffocation
B) The autopsy alone establishes the diagnosis definitively
C) It can be diagnosed at the scene when no signs of trauma are present
D) It is defined only in infants younger than 6 months, which is when 95% of cases occur
Answer: A
Rationale: SIDS is "the sudden death of an infant younger than 1 year of age that remains unexplained after a complete postmortem examination (autopsy), including an investigation of the death scene and a review of the case history." The autopsy is essential to identify natural explanations "and to identify a death that was the result of child abuse," but "The autopsy typically cannot distinguish between SIDS and intentional suffocation."
Trap: (B) is the assumption that makes the scene investigation look optional, and the chapter is explicit that postmortem findings in SIDS and in accidental or intentional suffocation "are practically the same." (D) confuses the statistic that 95% of cases occur by 6 months with the age boundary of the definition, which is 1 year.

Q: A nurse preparing safe-sleep teaching wants to emphasize the window of highest risk. Which statement is accurate?
A) The peak age is 2 to 3 months, with 95% of cases occurring by 6 months
B) The peak age is 6 to 9 months, once the infant can roll independently
C) Risk is distributed evenly across the first year
D) The peak age is the first week of life
Answer: A
Rationale: Table 11.2 gives the peak age as "2-3 months; 95% occur by 6 months." SIDS is "the third leading cause of infant mortality in the United States, accounting for approximately 8% of all infant deaths and claiming the lives of 3500 US infants each year," and it is "the most common cause of postneonatal infant mortality, accounting for 40% to 50% of all deaths between 1 month and 1 year of age."
Trap: (B) ties the risk to the ability to roll into an unsafe position, which is a plausible mechanism and the wrong window; risk peaks well before rolling is established. (D) describes SUEND, which occurs in the first week of life and is a separate category from SUID.

Q: A pregnant woman who smokes asks what she can change to reduce her baby's risk of sudden infant death syndrome. Which response is accurate?
A) The incidence is approximately 3 times greater among infants whose mothers smoked during pregnancy, and the risk grows progressively as daily cigarette use increases
B) Only postnatal smoke exposure matters, so quitting after delivery is sufficient
C) Smoking affects birth weight but has no established relationship to sudden infant death syndrome
D) Sleeping with the infant on a sofa offsets the smoking risk by allowing closer monitoring
Answer: A
Rationale: "The incidence of SIDS is approximately 3 times greater among infants whose mothers smoked during pregnancy, and risk of death is progressively greater as daily cigarette use increases." A meta-analysis gives odds ratios of "2.25 for prenatal maternal smoking and 1.97 for postnatal maternal smoking," so both exposures carry risk and neither cancels the other.
Trap: (D) inverts one of the chapter's sharpest statements: sleeping on a sofa carries "a twofold increase in accidental suffocation or strangulation" compared with other locations, and cosleeping risk is higher still when the adult smokes. Two risk factors here compound rather than offset.

Q: A parent delays immunizations, believing that vaccines and sleep apnea both cause sudden infant death syndrome. Which response reflects the chapter?
A) Both beliefs are supported by current research
B) Sleep apnea is not the cause, no association exists between it and any childhood vaccine, and updated immunization status has been shown to be protective
C) Sleep apnea is the established cause, but vaccines are unrelated
D) Vaccines are associated with it, but only in preterm infants
Answer: B
Rationale: "Sleep apnea is not the cause of SIDS. The majority of infants with apnea do not die and only a minority of SIDS victims have documented apparent life-threatening events." Separately, "the findings of numerous studies indicate that no association exists between SIDS and any childhood vaccine." Table 11.2 records a "lower incidence in immunized infants," and the chapter states that "Updated childhood immunization status has also been shown to be protective against SIDS."
Trap: (C) is the half most clinicians still carry, because home apnea monitoring was built on it. The chapter rejects apnea as the cause and adds, separately, that "There is no evidence that home apnea monitoring prevents SIDS." The leading hypothesis instead involves "brainstem abnormalities in the neurologic regulation of cardiorespiratory control."

Q: A nurse reviews Table 11.2 to identify infants at increased risk for sudden infant death syndrome. Which factors are associated with increased risk? Select all that apply.
A) Prone sleeping position
B) Cosleeping with an adult, especially on a sofa or noninfant bed
C) Being put to sleep with a pacifier
D) Preterm birth, especially extremely and very low birth weight
E) Increasing birth order, so a subsequent sibling rather than a firstborn
Answer: A, B, D, E
Rationale: Table 11.2 gives "Highest risk associated with prone position," soft bedding, overheating from thermal stress, "cosleeping with adult, especially on sofa or noninfant bed," higher risk in preterm infants "especially infants of extremely and very low birth weight," and "Increasing birth order (subsequent siblings as opposed to firstborn child)."
Trap: (C) is the one option from the wrong side of the sort and belongs to the protective list: there is a "Lower incidence in infants put to sleep with pacifier," and the protection holds "even with dislodgment."

Topic: safe infant sleep teaching and care after a sids death

Q: A nurse is discharging a medically stable preterm infant and, separately, a term infant with gastroesophageal reflux. What sleep position should be taught for each?
A) Supine for both, unless a specific upper airway disorder places the infant at greater risk of death than the risk from sudden infant death syndrome
B) Supine for the term infant and prone for the preterm infant, to support respiratory effort
C) Side-lying for both, as a compromise between aspiration risk and sudden infant death syndrome risk
D) Prone for the infant with reflux, to reduce aspiration
Answer: A
Rationale: Supine sleep is recommended for all infants, including "medically stable preterm infants and infants diagnosed with gastroesophageal reflux (GER)... unless they have a specific upper airway disorder that places them at greater risk of death than the risk of death from SIDS." Most preterm infants at discharge "should be placed in a supine sleeping position unless special factors predispose them to airway obstruction."
Trap: (C) is the compromise that feels safest, and it is the one the chapter specifically retired: "the side-lying position is no longer recommended for infants sleeping at home, day care, or hospitals (unless medically indicated)." (D) applies adult reflux positioning to an infant, where the SIDS risk outweighs it.

Q: New parents ask where their newborn should sleep. Which recommendation reflects the chapter?
A) Share a bedroom but not the same sleeping surface, preferably until the infant is a year old but at least for the first 6 months; room-sharing decreases the risk by as much as 50%
B) Share a bed, since proximity lets the parent detect apnea
C) Place the infant in a separate room from birth, since sharing a room increases risk
D) Share a bedroom for the first 2 weeks only, then move the infant to a separate room
Answer: A
Rationale: Infants should "share a bedroom with parents, but not the same sleeping surface, preferably until the infant is a year old but at least for the first 6 months." "Room-sharing decreases the risk of SIDS by as much as 50%."
Trap: (B) conflates room-sharing with bed-sharing, which is the most consequential confusion in this teaching: one halves the risk and the other raises it. Bed-sharing risk is highest when the infant is younger than 11 weeks, which is also the peak age for SIDS itself.

Q: A nurse inspects the sleep environment of a 2-month-old. Which findings require teaching? Select all that apply.
A) A quilt and a sheepskin placed under the fitted sheet
B) Crib bumper pads lining the sides of the crib
C) A pacifier offered at sleep time
D) Stuffed animals and toys left in the crib
E) A blanket arranged so that it may cover the infant's head
Answer: A, B, D, E
Rationale: Soft bedding — "waterbeds, sheepskins, beanbags, pillows, and quilts" — should be avoided, stuffed animals and toys removed, and "crib bumper pads should not be used." "Head covering by a blanket has also been found to be a risk factor."
Trap: (C) is the one option from the correct side of the sort. It pulls because a pacifier reads as one more object in the bed and this list is otherwise about removing objects; pacifier use at sleep time is protective, and "there is increasing evidence that pacifier use even with dislodgment can increase the arousability of infants during sleep."

Q: A nurse counsels a new mother about modifiable factors that reduce the risk of sudden infant death syndrome. Which statement is accurate?
A) Rates may be reduced by 45% for infants who are breastfed, and the protective effect increases with exclusive breastfeeding
B) Breastfeeding has no established relationship to the risk
C) Pacifier use should be avoided in breastfed infants, since rigorous studies show it shortens breastfeeding duration
D) Home apnea monitoring is the most effective preventive measure available
Answer: A
Rationale: "SIDS rates may be reduced by 45% for infants who are breastfed and this protective effect increases with exclusive breastfeeding." The concern about pacifiers interfering with breastfeeding is unsupported: "rigorous studies have failed to show an association between pacifier use and breastfeeding duration."
Trap: (D) is the intervention families most often ask for, and the chapter refuses it twice: "No diagnostic tests exist to predict which infants... will die of SIDS," and "There is no evidence that home apnea monitoring prevents SIDS."

Q: Parents arrive at the emergency department after finding their infant dead at home. Which nursing action is appropriate?
A) Ask only factual questions, such as when they found the infant, how the infant looked, and whom they called for help
B) Ask why they did not check on the infant earlier, in order to establish the timeline
C) Explain that an autopsy will not be needed because the scene showed no signs of trauma
D) Answer the parents when they ask why this happened to their baby, so as to relieve their uncertainty
Answer: A
Rationale: Parents "should only be asked factual questions such as when they found the infant, how he or she looked, and whom they called for help." The nurse must avoid any remark suggesting responsibility. Parents should also be encouraged to hold their infant before leaving, and transport home arranged, since "the parents will be leaving the hospital without their infant."
Trap: (D) is the compassionate instinct the chapter forbids outright: "One question that the nurse will not be able to answer and therefore should never attempt is, 'Why did this happen to our baby?' or 'Who is responsible for this tragedy?'" (B) reproduces almost word for word an example the chapter gives of what never to say.

Topic: positional plagiocephaly and brief resolved unexplained events

Q: A 3-month-old has a flattened right occiput with frontal and parietal bossing on the same side, a prominent cheekbone, and an anteriorly displaced ear. Viewed from above, the head resembles a parallelogram. What is this, and how is it diagnosed?
A) Positional plagiocephaly, diagnosed by physical examination viewed frontally and from above; imaging is used only to rule out craniosynostosis or other cranial deformities
B) Craniosynostosis, requiring immediate surgical referral
C) Positional plagiocephaly, which requires computed tomography to confirm
D) A normal transient finding requiring no further assessment
Answer: A
Rationale: "The typical infant's head shape will resemble a parallelogram with unilateral flattening of the occiput, frontal and parietal bossing, a prominent cheekbone, and an anterior ear displacement." Diagnosis is by physical examination "viewed frontally and from above," and imaging is used "only to rule out craniosynostosis or other cranial deformities." Approximately 20% of infants have this deformity, "most prevalent between 2 and 4 months of age."
Trap: (C) is the reflex order because the deformity looks structural; imaging here rules out a different diagnosis rather than confirming this one. The nurse should also assess neck movement and range of motion, since "The sternocleidomastoid muscle may tighten on the preferential side resulting in a condition called torticollis."

Q: Parents of a 6-week-old ask how to prevent a flat spot on the back of the head. Which instructions are correct?
A) Place the infant prone for sleep several nights a week to relieve occipital pressure
B) Continue supine sleep with the head position alternated nightly, avoid prolonged placement in car safety seats and swings, and provide tummy time for at least 10 to 15 minutes three times a day while awake
C) Use a positioning pillow in the crib to keep pressure off the occiput
D) Withhold tummy time until head control is established at about 4 months
Answer: B
Rationale: Prevention begins shortly after birth with supine sleep and the head position "alternated nightly," avoiding prolonged placement in car safety seats and swings, and tummy time "for at least 10 to 15 minutes three times a day" while the infant is awake. Tummy time also builds shoulder girdle strength.
Trap: (A) trades a cosmetic problem for a fatal one, which is the error this entire section exists to prevent. (C) adds soft bedding to the crib, which is itself a named risk factor for sudden infant death syndrome.

Q: A parent of a 4-month-old with visible positional plagiocephaly says they have begun putting the baby down prone because the flat head is getting worse. What should the nurse say?
A) Prone sleep is acceptable once the infant can lift the head, and it will resolve the deformity
B) The supine sleeping position is still recommended, because it has led to a significant decrease in loss of infant lives from sudden infant death syndrome, and the bald spot over the flattened area is usually transient
C) Side-lying is the recommended compromise for infants with plagiocephaly
D) The deformity indicates craniosynostosis, so sleep position is no longer the relevant issue
Answer: B
Rationale: "Despite the perceived increase in incidence of positional plagiocephaly, the supine sleeping position is still recommended because it has led to a significant decrease in loss of infant lives from SIDS," and nurses "should continue to encourage parents to place the infant in a supine sleep position despite the development of plagiocephaly." The typical bald spot that develops over the flattened area "is usually transient."
Trap: (C) reaches for the position the chapter retired: side-lying "is no longer recommended for infants sleeping at home, day care, or hospitals." The plagiocephaly trade-off is real, and the answer is repositioning while awake, never a different sleep position.

Q: A 2-month-old had a 40-second episode of pallor, irregular breathing, and altered responsiveness that resolved completely, with no explanation found after a thorough history and physical examination. The parents ask whether this was a near-miss sudden infant death syndrome event. How should the nurse respond?
A) This meets the definition of a brief resolved unexplained event, and it is erroneous to characterize such an event as a near-miss incident
B) This confirms the infant will die of sudden infant death syndrome without home monitoring
C) This counts as an apparent life-threatening event only if resuscitation was performed, and it does confirm a near-miss
D) This cannot be a brief resolved unexplained event, since the episode lasted longer than 30 seconds
Answer: A
Rationale: A brief resolved unexplained event is "an event observed in infants younger than 1 year of age during which an observer reports a sudden, brief (less than 1 minute), but then resolved episode" including at least one of cyanosis or pallor; absent, decreased, or irregular breathing; marked change in muscle tone; or altered responsiveness — and it "is diagnosed only when there is no explanation for a qualifying event after completion of a thorough history and physical examination." The chapter is explicit that "It is erroneous to characterize ALTE as a near-miss SIDS incident."
Trap: (B) is the conclusion parents reach, and the reason the chapter states the negative so plainly: the CHIME study found apnea and bradycardia "in all groups of infants studied," including healthy term infants, and "Many infants experience apnea and bradycardia in each of these groups, yet do not die." (D) invents a 30-second cutoff; the stated criterion is less than 1 minute.
