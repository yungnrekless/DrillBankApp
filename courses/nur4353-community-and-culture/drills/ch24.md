# Chapter 24 drills — Rural Health and Migrant Health

Authored from `sources/Ch24_Fact_Bank.md` (Stanhope & Lancaster, 6th ed., printed pp. 420–439).
Blueprint-weighted heaviest chapter, so coverage leans into the flagged confusable pairs:
migrant vs. seasonal, the two "rural" definitions, frontier vs. rural, and the OSHA exemption.

---

Topic: rural-urban classification
Subtopic: population thresholds

Q: Using the chapter's own operating definition, which thresholds define "rural"?
A) Fewer than 50,000 residents or fewer than 10 persons per square mile
B) Fewer than 99 persons per square mile and communities of 20,000 or fewer inhabitants
C) Fewer than six persons per square mile
D) An urban core of at least 10,000 but fewer than 50,000 people
Answer: B
Rationale: The chapter's operating definition states rural refers to areas having fewer than 99 persons per square mile and communities having 20,000 or fewer inhabitants.
Trap: Box 24.1 phrases the same idea with "or" instead of "and." Both phrasings appear in the reading — the thresholds (20,000 and 99 per square mile) are what matter, not the conjunction. Don't get thrown by which connector a stem uses.

Q: A region has fewer than six persons per square mile and schools more than 100 miles apart. Which classification best fits?
A) Rural
B) Frontier
C) Micropolitan
D) Non-core
Answer: B
Rationale: Frontier regions have fewer than six persons per square mile — a stricter threshold than rural's fewer-than-99. A stem describing extreme remoteness like widely spaced schools is cuing frontier, not just rural.
Trap: Frontier is easy to confuse with rural because both describe sparse areas. Rural is fewer than 99 per square mile; frontier is fewer than six.

Q: A metropolitan area is defined by which population threshold for its core urban area?
A) 10,000 or more
B) 20,000 or more
C) 50,000 or more
D) 99 or more per square mile
Answer: C
Rationale: A metropolitan area contains a core urban area of 50,000 or more population.
Trap: The chapter also defines "urban" elsewhere as cities with at least 20,000 but less than 50,000 residents. These are two different classification systems on the same page — metro/micro uses 50,000 and 10,000; urban-vs-rural uses 20,000 and 99 per square mile. Match the numbers to which system the stem is asking about.

Q: Which population range defines a micropolitan area's urban core?
A) Fewer than 10,000
B) At least 10,000 but fewer than 50,000
C) At least 50,000
D) Exactly 20,000
Answer: B
Rationale: A micropolitan area contains an urban core of at least 10,000 but fewer than 50,000 people.
Trap: Non-core areas are defined as having no urban cluster of 10,000 or more — don't treat "non-core" as a general synonym for rural; it's a specific tier below micropolitan.

Q: Which statements correctly distinguish farm residency from nonfarm residency? Select all that apply.
A) Farm residency is outside the area zoned as "city limits"
B) Nonfarm residency is within the area zoned as "city limits"
C) Farm residency usually infers involvement in agriculture
D) Nonfarm residency is another term for frontier living
Answer: A, B, C
Rationale: Farm residency is residency outside the area zoned as city limits and usually implies involvement in agriculture; nonfarm residency is residence within that zoned area.

Subtopic: rural-urban continuum and demographics

Q: How does the chapter characterize the relationship between rural and urban lifestyles?
A) They are opposing lifestyles with a clear dividing line
B) A continuum, from remote farm through village, town, and large metro area
C) Defined entirely by income level
D) Defined entirely by racial composition
Answer: B
Rationale: Rural and urban are not opposing lifestyles but a continuum: remote farm to village/small town to larger town/city to large metro area with a core inner city.

Q: What is the "doughnut effect" as described in the chapter?
A) Rural residents moving into dense urban cores
B) A population shift from highly populated urban areas to outlying suburbs and less-populated regions
C) The concentration of health services in a metro area's center
D) A pattern of farm closures surrounding small towns
Answer: B
Rationale: The doughnut effect is the recent population shift from urban to less-populated regions, accelerated in 2020 as urban residents relocated during COVID-19.

Q: Which statements about rural population characteristics are accurate? Select all that apply.
A) Whites comprise about 80% of the rural population vs. 58% of urban
B) Rural communities have a higher proportion of residents younger than 18 and older than 65
C) Rural residents are more likely to have private insurance than urban residents
D) The "graying" of rural areas is partly due to retirees choosing rural areas
Answer: A, B, D
Rationale: Whites make up about 80% of the rural population versus 58% urban; rural communities skew younger-than-18 and older-than-65; retirees choosing rural areas for scenic beauty and pace of life contribute to the graying of rural populations.
Trap: Rural families are less likely to have private insurance and more likely to be uninsured or on public assistance — the opposite of option C.

Q: Which factors explain why "working poor" families are more common in rural settings? Select all that apply.
A) High proportion of self-employment in family businesses like ranching or farming
B) Employment in small enterprises where insurance is not an employee benefit
C) Part-time or seasonal work without health insurance benefits
D) Income levels that qualify the family for public insurance
Answer: A, B, C
Rationale: The working poor are employed but cannot afford private insurance, and their income disqualifies them from public insurance. Rural settings drive this through self-employment in family businesses, small enterprises, and part-time or seasonal work without insurance benefits.
Trap: Working poor families are disqualified from public insurance by their income, not qualified for it.

Q: According to the Rural Health Information Hub, which are named social determinants that are barriers to accessing rural care? Select all that apply.
A) Higher poverty rates
B) Low health literacy levels
C) Lower population densities limiting program economies of scale
D) Surplus of affordable, reliable transportation options
Answer: A, B, C
Rationale: The nine named barriers include higher poverty rates, low health literacy, linguistic and educational disparities, limited affordable or reliable transportation, unpredictable work hours, lower population densities, resource availability, and lack of access to healthy foods and physical activity.
Trap: Transportation is limited, not surplus, in rural areas — reverse of option D.

Q: Box 24.2 describes several characteristics of rural life. Which are among them? Select all that apply.
A) Lack of anonymity
B) Preference for interacting with locals (insiders)
C) Mistrust of newcomers (outsiders)
D) Predominance of large industries over small family enterprises
Answer: A, B, C
Rationale: Box 24.2 names lack of anonymity, preference for interacting with insiders, and mistrust of outsiders as characteristic of rural life, along with small family enterprises rather than large industries.

Topic: rural health status
Subtopic: health behaviors and outcomes

Q: Compared to urban adults, rural adults are more likely to do which of the following? Select all that apply.
A) Smoke
B) Report higher rates of alcohol use
C) Have more chronic health conditions
D) Wear seat belts
Answer: A, B, C
Rationale: Rural adults are more likely to smoke, report higher alcohol use, and have more chronic health conditions and mobility limitations.
Trap: Rural adults are less likely to wear seat belts, not more — that belongs on the "less likely" list along with physical activity, blood pressure checks, and cancer screenings.

Q: Which statement correctly describes usual source of care patterns?
A) Urban adults are more likely than rural adults to identify a particular provider as their usual source of care
B) Rural adults are more likely than urban adults to identify a particular provider as their usual source of care
C) Neither group shows a difference in usual source of care
D) Rural adults are more likely to see a medical specialist as their usual provider
Answer: B
Rationale: Rural adults are more likely than urban adults to identify a particular medical provider as their usual source of care, most often a general practitioner or APRN. Urban adults are more likely to see a medical specialist.

Q: Which figures correctly describe provider maldistribution between rural and urban areas?
A) Primary care physicians: 39.8 per 100,000 rural vs. 53.3 per 100,000 urban
B) Specialty physicians: 30 per 100,000 rural vs. 263 per 100,000 urban
C) Both A and B are accurate
D) Rural areas actually have more specialty physicians per capita
Answer: C
Rationale: Rural areas have 39.8 primary care physicians and 30 specialty physicians per 100,000, versus 53.3 and 263 respectively in urban areas — a much larger gap in specialty care than primary care.

Q: Which statements about injury and accident risk in rural areas are accurate? Select all that apply.
A) More than 50% of vehicle crash-related fatalities occur in rural areas
B) There is an additional 22% risk of injury-related deaths in rural areas
C) Guns may be more readily found in rural vs. urban homes
D) Rural areas have lower rates of unintentional injury deaths than fringe metro counties
Answer: A, B, C
Rationale: More than half of vehicle crash fatalities occur in rural areas, with an additional 22% risk of injury-related death, and guns are more commonly found in rural homes due to distance from town and animal-related danger.
Trap: Box 24.3 shows the most rural counties have the HIGHEST death rates for unintentional and motor vehicle traffic injuries — the opposite of option D, which describes fringe metro counties.

Q: Per Box 24.3, residents of the most rural counties (compared to fringe counties near large metro areas) have which outcomes? Select all that apply.
A) Highest death rates for children and young adults
B) Highest death rates among adults for ischemic heart disease and suicide
C) Fewest physician specialists and dentists per capita
D) Lowest percent without health insurance
Answer: A, B, C
Rationale: The most rural counties have the highest death rates for children/young adults and for ischemic heart disease/suicide among adults, and the fewest specialists and dentists per capita.
Trap: Fringe counties near large metro areas have the lowest percent without insurance — the most rural counties have the highest percent uninsured, not the lowest.

Q: Which populations are named as particularly at risk for higher infant and maternal morbidity in rural areas? Select all that apply.
A) Women who live on or near Indian reservations
B) Migrant workers
C) Women of African American descent
D) Women in fringe counties near large metro areas
Answer: A, B, C
Rationale: Rural populations overall have higher infant and maternal morbidity, especially in counties designated as HPSAs; particularly at-risk groups named are women near Indian reservations, migrant workers, and women of African American descent.

Q: Why is the incidence of rural sexual assault difficult to document, and what is the actual rate believed to be?
A) It's rarely reported because rates are genuinely lower in rural areas
B) Documentation is hard due to isolation and familiarity, but the rate is thought to be higher than in urban areas
C) It's well-documented and confirmed to be lower than urban rates
D) Reporting rates are identical to urban areas
Answer: B
Rationale: Incidence is hard to document because of rural isolation and the higher likelihood that the person assaulted knows people in the community; the rate is thought to be higher than in urban areas, not lower.
Trap: Under-documentation is the reason the rate looks lower on paper — it is not evidence that the actual rate is lower. Confidentiality concerns, familiarity with the person receiving the report, and family perpetrators all suppress reporting.

Subtopic: children, mental health, and COVID-19

Q: Which statement correctly compares usual providers for children in rural vs. urban settings?
A) Urban children are less likely to have a usual provider but more likely to see a pediatrician when ill
B) Rural children are less likely to have a usual provider
C) Urban children are more likely to see a general practitioner as their regular caregiver
D) There is no difference between rural and urban children's usual providers
Answer: A
Rationale: Urban children are less likely to have a usual provider but more likely to see a pediatrician when ill; rural adults and children are more likely to have a general practitioner as their regular caregiver.

Q: Why are school nurses scarce in frontier and rural areas? Select all that apply.
A) A shortage of health care professionals
B) Fewer taxpayers, meaning less income to support school nurses
C) State laws prohibiting school nursing positions in rural districts
D) Federal restrictions on rural school funding
Answer: A, B
Rationale: The two named reasons are a shortage of health care professionals and fewer taxpayers providing less income to support school nurse positions. Workarounds include counties sharing a "district" health nurse or contracting with an urban agency.

Q: Which statement best describes the pattern of mental health care-seeking among rural residents?
A) Rural residents seek care early, before symptoms escalate
B) Rural residents delay seeking care for emotional problems until an emergency or crisis arises
C) Rural residents seek mental health care at the same rate as urban residents
D) Rural residents primarily use telehealth for mental health concerns
Answer: B
Rationale: Rural residents delay seeking care for emotional problems until an emergency or crisis arises, even when services are available and accessible.

Q: What mechanism does the chapter describe for underreporting of domestic violence and substance abuse in small rural communities?
A) Formal reporting systems are unavailable
B) When people are related or know each other well, they are less likely to report, and destructive coping behaviors may become accepted as usual for a particular family
C) Rural law enforcement actively discourages reporting
D) Substance abuse is rare in rural communities, reducing the need to report
Answer: B
Rationale: Familiarity reduces reporting likelihood, and in small, tight-knit communities destructive coping behaviors can come to be accepted as usual occurrences for a particular family.

Q: Which factors are named as making rural Americans more vulnerable during COVID-19? Select all that apply.
A) Higher proportion of elderly residents
B) Higher smoking rates
C) Lower insurance coverage
D) Surplus hospital capacity and funding
Answer: A, B, C
Rationale: Rural Americans were more vulnerable due to a higher proportion of elderly residents, higher smoking rates, prevalence of certain chronic diseases, and lower insurance coverage. Nearly half of rural hospitals operate in a financial deficit, the opposite of surplus capacity.

Topic: occupational and environmental health
Subtopic: regulatory gaps and pesticide exposure

Q: Which four industries does OSHA identify as high-risk and predominantly rural?
A) Forestry, mining, marine-related fields, agriculture
B) Construction, manufacturing, transportation, agriculture
C) Forestry, retail, agriculture, hospitality
D) Mining, healthcare, agriculture, education
Answer: A
Rationale: The four OSHA-identified high-risk industries in predominantly rural environments are forestry, mining, marine-related fields, and agriculture.

Q: Which statement correctly describes the regulatory status of small farms and ranches?
A) They fall fully under OSHA guidelines like other workplaces
B) Small enterprises with few employees do not fall under OSHA guidelines, and are also not covered by workers' compensation
C) They are covered by workers' compensation but not OSHA
D) They are covered by OSHA but not workers' compensation
Answer: B
Rationale: Small enterprises with a small number of employees do not fall under OSHA guidelines, so safety standards are not enforceable on most farms and ranches, and small businesses such as farms are not covered under workers' compensation insurance.
Trap: This is counterintuitive and therefore testable — farms and ranches are among the most dangerous workplaces, yet they escape both OSHA coverage and workers' compensation. Any option implying standard occupational safety law protects farmworkers is wrong.

Q: Which are among the six most common health issues for farmworkers (NCFH, 2018b)? Select all that apply.
A) Pesticide exposure
B) Skin disorders
C) Musculoskeletal injuries
D) Cardiovascular disease
Answer: A, B, C
Rationale: The six most common health issues named are pesticide exposure, skin disorders, infectious diseases, musculoskeletal injuries, respiratory illnesses, and hearing and vision disorders. Cardiovascular disease is not among the six named.

Q: A farmworker's child develops pesticide-related symptoms despite never entering the fields. What is the most likely mechanism?
A) Airborne pesticide has no effect on children
B) The worker's clothing and dwelling can be a source of cross-contamination, along with drift and unwashed hands
C) Children are immune to organophosphate exposure
D) This can only occur through direct field exposure
Answer: B
Rationale: The farmworker's clothing and dwelling can be a major source of cross-contamination for both the worker and the family; entire families are at risk from drift, unwashed hands, and contaminated clothes brought home.

Q: What is the Agricultural Worker Protection Standard (WPS), and when were its most recent EPA revisions implemented?
A) 1962; established migrant health centers
B) January 2017; restrictions on age of workers handling pesticides, training, and protective practices
C) 1990; established OSHA field sanitation standards
D) 2012; ACOG recommendation on LARC use
Answer: B
Rationale: The Agricultural Worker Protection Standard's EPA revisions were implemented in January 2017, restricting the age of workers who handle pesticides and mandating worker training and other protective practices.

Q: Which are mild symptoms of pesticide poisoning, as distinguished from severe symptoms? Select all that apply.
A) Headache and dizziness
B) Eye irritation
C) Convulsions
D) Inability to breathe
Answer: A, B
Rationale: Mild symptoms include headache, fatigue, dizziness, nervousness, perspiration, loss of appetite, thirst, and eye/nose/throat irritation. Convulsions and inability to breathe are severe symptoms, along with fever, intense thirst, vomiting, muscle twitches, and unconsciousness.

Q: Why does heat stress compound pesticide risk for farmworkers?
A) Heat has no relationship to pesticide absorption
B) Pesticides are more readily absorbed through hot, sweaty skin than through cool skin
C) Heat exposure reduces the amount of pesticide applied
D) Sweating washes pesticide residue away before it can be absorbed
Answer: B
Rationale: Pesticides are more readily absorbed through hot, sweaty skin than through cool skin, adding danger on top of the heat exhaustion symptoms themselves (heavy sweating, cold/pale/clammy skin, fast weak pulse, nausea, fainting).

Subtopic: levels of prevention for pesticide exposure

Q: Per the textbook's Levels of Prevention box for rural health, which intervention represents secondary prevention for pesticide exposure?
A) Teaching workers how to reduce exposure
B) Conducting urine testing for pesticide exposure
C) Initiating treatment for nausea and skin irritation
D) Providing personal protective equipment during training
Answer: B
Rationale: The textbook specifically names urine testing as the secondary prevention intervention — screening to detect exposure that has already occurred, not yet symptomatic disease.
Trap: The textbook is unusually specific here — it names urine testing rather than blood work or general physical assessment. If an option offers a different screening modality, it does not match the source.

Q: A community health nurse initiates treatment for a farmworker's pesticide-related nausea and skin irritation. This represents which level of prevention?
A) Primary
B) Secondary
C) Tertiary
D) Quaternary
Answer: C
Rationale: Per the textbook's Levels of Prevention box, initiating treatment for symptoms of pesticide exposure is tertiary prevention — managing an already-occurring condition.

Topic: barriers to rural care
Subtopic: the four A's

Q: A rural county has no hospital and no physicians practicing within 60 miles. Which barrier to care does this best illustrate?
A) Acceptability
B) Affordability
C) Availability
D) Accessibility
Answer: C
Rationale: Availability means health services exist and have the necessary personnel; sparseness of population limits the number and array of services in a region, which is exactly what an absent hospital and no nearby physicians describes.

Q: A rural resident has a nearby clinic and can afford the visit, but lacks a car and there is no public transportation to reach it. Which barrier does this best illustrate?
A) Availability
B) Accessibility
C) Affordability
D) Acceptability
Answer: B
Rationale: Accessibility means a person has logistical access to needed services as well as the ability to purchase them; the described scenario is a logistical/transportation barrier.

Q: A clinic exists and is reachable, but its services are not offered in a manner congruent with the community's cultural values, partly due to the urban orientation of health professionals. Which barrier does this best illustrate?
A) Availability
B) Accessibility
C) Affordability
D) Acceptability
Answer: D
Rationale: Acceptability means a service is appropriate and offered in a manner congruent with the values of a target population, hampered by both patient cultural preference and the urban orientation of health professions.

Q: Which are named barriers to health care in rural areas per Box 24.4? Select all that apply.
A) Great distances to obtain services
B) Inequitable reimbursement policies for providers
C) Inadequate provider attitudes and understanding about rural populations
D) Surplus of publicly funded entitlement programs
Answer: A, B, C
Rationale: Box 24.4 names distance, transportation gaps, inequitable reimbursement, unpredictable weather, inability to pay, lack of know-how to procure entitlements, inadequate provider attitudes, and language and cultural barriers — not a surplus of entitlement programs.

Q: What does the chapter say results from a demeaning provider attitude or insensitivity about rural lifestyle?
A) It has no measurable effect on the patient relationship
B) It generates mistrust, causing rural patients to view professionals as outsiders to the community
C) It only affects patients who are new to the area
D) It improves compliance because patients respect authority
Answer: B
Rationale: A demeaning attitude or insensitivity about rural lifestyle generates mistrust, causing rural patients to view professionals as outsiders to the community. Conversely, some rural providers report professional isolation and lack of community acceptance.

Topic: migrant farmworkers
Subtopic: migrant vs. seasonal and demographics

Q: A farmworker travels to do agricultural work and cannot return to a permanent residence within the same day. How is this worker classified?
A) Seasonal farmworker
B) Migrant farmworker
C) Undocumented immigrant
D) Non-core resident
Answer: B
Rationale: A migrant farmworker is a seasonal farmworker who must travel to do farm work and is unable to return to a permanent residence within the same day.
Trap: This is the highest-probability confusable pair in the chapter. The discriminator is travel and same-day return, not the seasonality of the work — both migrant and seasonal workers do seasonal work. Migrant = travels, can't return home same day. Seasonal = returns to a permanent residence.

Q: A farmworker returns home each night to a permanent residence and works in agriculture only part of the year, never working year-round exclusively in agriculture. How is this worker classified?
A) Migrant farmworker
B) Seasonal farmworker
C) Frontier resident
D) Documented immigrant
Answer: B
Rationale: A seasonal farmworker returns to a permanent residence, works in agriculture seasonally, and does not work year-round exclusively in agriculture.

Q: Which statements about migrant and seasonal farmworker (MSFW) demographics are accurate? Select all that apply.
A) USDA estimated 1.0 to 2.7 million MSFWs in the United States
B) 70.7% of MSFWs are foreign born, most commonly from Mexico
C) The majority of MSFWs are hired through labor contractors rather than directly
D) Roughly 28% reported being unable to speak English at all
Answer: A, B, D
Rationale: USDA estimated 1.0–2.7 million MSFWs; 70.7% are foreign born with 64.1% born in Mexico; 28% reported being unable to speak English at all.
Trap: 85% of farmworkers are hired directly, and only 15% are contract workers — the reverse of option C.

Q: What are the three traditional migratory streams and their states of origin?
A) Eastern from New York, Midwestern from Illinois, Western from Oregon
B) Eastern from Florida, Midwestern from Texas, Western from California
C) Eastern from Georgia, Midwestern from Kansas, Western from Nevada
D) There are only two traditional streams, not three
Answer: B
Rationale: The three traditional migratory streams are Eastern originating in Florida, Midwestern originating in Texas, and Western originating in California. These streams are becoming less distinct as workers travel more widely.

Q: Which factors does the textbook name as making MSFWs a "unique vulnerable population"? Select all that apply.
A) Mobility
B) Physical demands of their work
C) Social and geographic isolation
D) High rates of home ownership
Answer: A, B, C
Rationale: The textbook's own list is mobility, the physical demands of their work, social and often geographic isolation, language differences, and high rates of financial impoverishment — not home ownership.

Subtopic: labor law, trafficking, and housing

Q: Under the Fair Labor Standards Act, which farms are exempt from minimum wage requirements?
A) Farms with more than seven full-time workers
B) Farms with fewer than seven workers in a calendar quarter
C) All farms regardless of size
D) No farms are exempt from FLSA minimum wage
Answer: B
Rationale: Farms with fewer than seven workers in a calendar quarter are exempt from minimum wage under the FLSA; farmworkers are also not included in overtime pay protections regardless of farm size.

Q: What does the Migrant and Seasonal Agricultural Worker Protection Act require of contractors and employers? Select all that apply.
A) Disclose employment terms
B) Post worker protection information at the worksite
C) Ensure housing complies with federal and state safety standards
D) Provide employer-sponsored health insurance regardless of hours worked
Answer: A, B, C
Rationale: The Act requires disclosure of employment terms, posted worker protection information, itemized pay statements, and housing that meets safety standards. It does not mandate employer health insurance; the chapter separately notes many workers are excluded from ACA coverage due to the small-farm exemption and the 120-day seasonal exclusion.

Q: Which signs suggest possible labor trafficking among farmworkers? Select all that apply.
A) Performing work duties in exchange for basic necessities rather than money
B) Identification documents held by the employer
C) Freedom to choose and change housing at will
D) Inability to freely choose where they live
Answer: A, B, D
Rationale: Signs include working for necessities instead of pay, an employer holding identification documents, and inability to freely choose housing — not freedom to change housing at will, which would be the opposite of a red flag.

Q: A nursing assessment identifies a minor as a likely victim of labor trafficking. What is the correct nursing action?
A) Obtain the minor's permission before contacting authorities
B) Notify child protective services and law enforcement; the minor should not be left alone until services arrive
C) Wait until the minor turns 18 to involve authorities
D) Refer the case to the employer for internal resolution
Answer: B
Rationale: If a minor is identified, the nurse should notify child protective services and law enforcement, and the minor should not be left alone until services arrive.
Trap: Obtaining permission before contacting authorities applies to adult victims, not minors — for a minor, reporting is not optional and does not require the minor's consent first.

Q: What is named as a common consequence of pesticide exposure combined with substandard migrant housing conditions? Select all that apply.
A) Tuberculosis
B) Gastroenteritis
C) Hepatitis
D) Improved sanitation compared to urban housing
Answer: A, B, C
Rationale: Poor and crowded residence contributes to tuberculosis, gastroenteritis, hepatitis, and lead exposure — housing is generally substandard, not improved compared to other settings.

Topic: migrant health issues
Subtopic: TB, access barriers, and health problems

Q: Why is tuberculosis treatment especially difficult to complete among migrant farmworkers?
A) TB medication is dispensed monthly, but adequate treatment requires 6 to 12 months, and workers who relocate must independently seek new services to continue treatment
B) Migrant workers are not susceptible to TB
C) TB medication does not require ongoing follow-up
D) Migrant health centers do not provide TB treatment
Answer: A
Rationale: Health departments dispense TB medications monthly, but adequate treatment requires 6 to 12 months; when workers move, they must independently seek out new health services, and incomplete treatment contributes to resistant TB.

Q: What is the purpose of the Migrant Clinicians Network (MCN) TB tracking program?
A) To deny services to workers with a TB history
B) To make previous provider information available to a farmworker's current provider, maintaining continuity of TB care
C) To replace the need for monthly medication dispensing
D) To track farmworkers for immigration enforcement purposes
Answer: B
Rationale: The MCN TB tracking program makes previous provider information available to a farmworker's current provider, maintaining continuity of TB care for a mobile population.

Q: Which factors limit adequate provision of health care services to migrant farmworkers? Select all that apply.
A) Health care records typically do not travel with families as they move
B) Services are often available only during work hours, meaning lost earnings to seek care
C) Undocumented workers are excluded from employer and individual insurance mandates
D) Medicaid eligibility follows workers seamlessly across state lines
Answer: A, B, C
Rationale: Records fragmenting, lost earnings from seeking care during work hours, and exclusion of undocumented workers from insurance mandates are all named barriers. Medicaid eligibility does NOT follow seamlessly — workers may lose benefits when relocating to a state with different eligibility standards.

Q: Which health problems are most often reported among migrant workers (NCFH, 2018a)? Select all that apply.
A) Overweight/obesity and hypertension
B) Diabetes mellitus
C) Otitis media and eustachian tube disorders
D) Osteoporosis
Answer: A, B, C
Rationale: Most often reported issues are overweight/obesity, hypertension, diabetes mellitus, otitis media and eustachian tube disorders, depression and other mood disorders, and substance abuse — osteoporosis is not named.

Q: What does the term "nervios" refer to in the context of migrant health?
A) A physical illness caused by pesticide exposure
B) A term used by some Western hemisphere Hispanics for increased susceptibility to mental stress and nervousness symptoms
C) A folk term for tuberculosis
D) A term describing chronic fatigue from fieldwork
Answer: B
Rationale: Nervios is a term used by some Western hemisphere Hispanics for increased susceptibility to mental stress and symptoms of nervousness; 22% of a California farmworker household survey reported it.

Q: Which statement about diabetes among the Latino farmworker population is accurate?
A) Prevalence is similar to the general US population
B) Prevalence is estimated three to five times greater than the general population, with higher rates of end-stage complications
C) Prevalence is lower than the general population due to diet
D) Diabetes is not tracked separately for this population
Answer: B
Rationale: Diabetes prevalence among the US Latino population is estimated three to five times greater than the general population, with CDC incidence of 8.4 per 1000 versus 5.7 per 1000 for non-Hispanic whites.

Q: Which HIV risk factors are named specifically for migrant farmworker populations? Select all that apply.
A) Sharing needles for common medications such as vitamins and antibiotics
B) Needle sharing through amateur tattooing
C) Isolation and separation from families
D) Routine access to comprehensive sex education
Answer: A, B, C
Rationale: Named risk factors include sharing needles for medications like vitamins and antibiotics, needle sharing through amateur tattooing, isolation from families, unprotected sexual activity, and limited education — not routine access to comprehensive sex education, which would be protective rather than a risk factor.

Subtopic: children of migrant workers

Q: A nurse performs nutrition education at a migrant camp. Per Box 24.5's worked example, this represents which level of prevention?
A) Primary
B) Secondary
C) Tertiary
D) None of these — nutrition education is not a prevention activity
Answer: A
Rationale: Box 24.5's worked example classifies nutrition education as primary prevention, diabetes screening as secondary prevention, and a self-care educational program for those already diagnosed as tertiary prevention.

Q: Which age ranges correctly match the two named programs for migrant children?
A) Migrant and Seasonal Head Start Program: 6 months to 5 years; Migrant Education Program: older than 5 years, a summer school program
B) Migrant and Seasonal Head Start Program: older than 5 years; Migrant Education Program: 6 months to 5 years
C) Both programs serve children 6 months to 5 years
D) Both programs serve children older than 5 years
Answer: A
Rationale: The Migrant and Seasonal Head Start Program serves children 6 months to 5 years, while the Migrant Education Program is a summer school program for children older than 5, currently offered in only 38 states.
Trap: The age ranges are easy to invert. Head Start = younger (6 months–5 years); Migrant Education = older (over 5, summer school).

Q: Under FLSA child labor rules in agriculture, at what age may a child work any farm job at any time, including hazardous work with no restriction on hours?
A) 12 and older
B) 14 or 15
C) 16 and older
D) Federal law never permits hazardous agricultural work by minors
Answer: C
Rationale: Workers 16 and older may work any farm job at any time, including hazardous work, with no restrictions on hours. Federal law does not protect children from overworking or limit hours worked outside school in agriculture, unlike other industries.

Q: What educational consequences are named for children of migrant farmworkers? Select all that apply.

Answer: A, B, C

Topic: rural nursing practice
Subtopic: lack of anonymity and professional challenges

Q: A rural nurse says, "I never really feel like I am off duty because everybody in the county knows me through my work." This best illustrates which defining feature of rural nursing practice?
A) Professional isolation
B) Lack of anonymity
C) Restricted social life
D) Heavy workload
Answer: B
Rationale: Lack of anonymity is the defining feature of rural practice — boundaries between home and work roles blur, and patients are known as neighbors, friends, or extended family.

Q: Which are named challenges of rural nursing? Select all that apply.
A) Professional isolation
B) Limited opportunities for continuing education
C) The need to function well across several clinical areas
D) Excessive anonymity that prevents community connection
Answer: A, B, C
Rationale: Named challenges include professional isolation, limited continuing education, lack of other professionals to interact with, heavy workloads, needing to function across several clinical areas, lack of anonymity (not excess of it), and for some, a restricted social life.

Q: Which are named rewards of rural nursing practice? Select all that apply.
A) Close relationships with patients and coworkers
B) Greater autonomy
C) Caring for patients across several generations in some cases
D) Reduced diversity of clinical experience
Answer: A, B, C
Rationale: Named rewards include close relationships, diverse clinical experiences, caring for patients across generations, professional development opportunities, greater autonomy, and the pleasures of rural living — not reduced diversity of clinical experience, which is the opposite of what's described.

Q: What is the correct order of the four steps of community-oriented primary health care (COPHC)?
A) Identify problems, define the community, develop services, monitor outcomes
B) Define and characterize the community, identify health problems, develop or modify services, monitor and evaluate outcomes
C) Develop services, define the community, monitor outcomes, identify problems
D) Monitor outcomes, define the community, identify problems, develop services
Answer: B
Rationale: The four COPHC steps in order are: define and characterize the community, identify the community's health problems, develop or modify health care services in response to identified needs, and monitor and evaluate program process and patient outcomes.

Q: What two models does the chapter name as effective for addressing rural health disparities?
A) Case management and COPHC
B) The socioecological model and levels of prevention
C) Telehealth and mobile clinics
D) Migrant health centers and school nursing
Answer: A
Rationale: Case management and community-oriented primary health care (COPHC) are the two effective models named for addressing rural health disparities.

Q: What preconditions does the chapter name for telehealth use in rural areas, and what does this reveal?
A) Only a landline telephone is required, making telehealth universally accessible
B) Patients need a cell phone and know how to text, and a computer with email skills — itself a rural access barrier
C) Telehealth requires no technology literacy at all
D) Telehealth was not reimbursed by insurers during COVID
Answer: B
Rationale: The chapter names having a cell phone with texting ability and a computer with email skills as preconditions for telehealth — and explicitly frames this precondition itself as a rural access barrier. During COVID, many electronic appointments were reimbursed by insurers.
