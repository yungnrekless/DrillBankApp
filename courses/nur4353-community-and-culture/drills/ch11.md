# Chapter 11 drills — Infectious Disease Prevention and Control

Authored from `sources/Ch11_Fact_Bank.md` (Stanhope & Lancaster, 6th ed.,
printed pp. 194–216). Every item is anchored to that file; where the fact bank
flags a trap or an internal inconsistency, the trap line says so explicitly.

Blueprint weight is 4 questions, but the immunity-type pairings, the six
agent characteristics, transmission modes, and levels of prevention are all
heavily testable, so this file goes deeper than the raw blueprint weight.

Topic: infectious disease
Subtopic: immunity types

Q: A nurse administers a live attenuated vaccine to a healthy toddler. Which type of immunity is this intervention producing?
A) Natural immunity
B) Passive immunization
C) Active immunization
D) Herd immunity
Answer: C
Rationale: Active immunization is the administration of an antigen (infectious agent or vaccine), characterized by an antibody produced by the individual host, as in vaccinating children. It is slower to develop but long lasting.
Trap: Calling a vaccine "passive" is the classic distractor — passive immunity is a transferred, ready-made antibody, not a host-generated one.

Q: A patient is bitten by a stray dog and, along with the rabies vaccine series, receives human rabies immunoglobulin. What type of immunity does the immunoglobulin provide?
A) Active immunization
B) Passive immunization
C) Natural immunity
D) Herd immunity
Answer: B
Rationale: Passive immunization is the transfer of a specific antibody from an immunized individual or source (immunoglobulin/antiserum). It works almost immediately but is short lived, serving as a stopgap until active immunity develops.
Trap: This is the rabies postexposure prophylaxis scenario, which uses both passive (immunoglobulin, immediate) and active (vaccine, durable) immunity at once — do not assume only one type is present.

Q: Rabies postexposure prophylaxis after a bite from a possibly rabid animal typically includes human rabies immunoglobulin plus the rabies vaccine series. What does this combination illustrate?
A) Two forms of active immunization given together for redundancy
B) Passive immunization (immediate) paired with active immunization (durable)
C) Natural immunity boosted by herd immunity
D) Acquired immunity substituting for active immunization
Answer: B
Rationale: This is the textbook example of passive plus active immunization used together: the immunoglobulin provides an immediate, transferred antibody while the vaccine series stimulates the host to build its own longer-lasting antibody response.

Q: Which statement correctly distinguishes active from passive immunization?
A) Active immunization is immediate but short lived; passive immunization is slow but durable
B) Active immunization involves a host-produced antibody after antigen exposure and is slow but long lasting; passive immunization involves a transferred antibody and is immediate but short lived
C) Both produce antibody responses of similar speed and duration
D) Passive immunization always precedes active immunization in a vaccine schedule
Answer: B
Rationale: Active immunization means the host makes its own antibody after receiving an antigen (agent or vaccine); onset is slower but protection is long lasting. Passive immunization transfers a ready-made antibody; protection is almost immediate but short lived, often used as a stopgap.
Trap: Option A reverses the two — this exact reversal is the single most likely EAQ distractor pattern for this topic.

Q: An opossum rarely contracts rabies because of species-determined, innate resistance to the virus. Which type of immunity does this describe?
A) Acquired immunity
B) Active immunization
C) Natural immunity
D) Herd immunity
Answer: C
Rationale: Natural immunity is species-determined, innate resistance to an infectious agent — the opossum-and-rabies example given in the source.
Trap: Do not confuse natural immunity (innate, species-level, present from birth) with "naturally acquired active immunity" from having had a disease — that is acquired immunity, not natural immunity.

Q: A woman who had measles as a child is now protected against future measles infection. What type of immunity does this represent?
A) Natural immunity
B) Acquired immunity
C) Passive immunization
D) Herd immunity
Answer: B
Rationale: Acquired immunity is resistance a host acquires as a result of previous natural exposure to an infectious agent (having measles once protects against future infection). It may be induced by either active or passive immunization.

Q: What is the basis of herd immunity, and what is it the rationale for?
A) An individual's own antibody response after natural infection; the rationale for isolation precautions
B) A high proportion of individuals in a group being immune, which resists invasion and spread of an agent; the rationale for increasing immunization coverage
C) Passive transfer of maternal antibody across a whole community; the rationale for prenatal screening
D) Species-level innate resistance shared across a population; the rationale for vector control
Answer: B
Rationale: Herd immunity is the immunity of a group or community — resistance to invasion and spread of an infectious agent based on a high proportion of individuals being immune. It is the basis for programs that increase immunization coverage.

Q: How does "resistance," as the chapter uses the term, relate to natural and acquired immunity?
A) Resistance is a synonym for acquired immunity only
B) Resistance is the host's ability to withstand infection, made up of both natural and acquired immunity
C) Resistance is a property of the infectious agent, not the host
D) Resistance refers only to herd-level protection
Answer: B
Rationale: Resistance is the ability of the host to withstand infection and may involve natural or acquired immunity.

Q: Which statements about immunity types are accurate? Select all that apply.
A) Active immunization produces an antibody made by the host and tends to be long lasting
B) Passive immunization transfers a ready-made antibody and tends to be short lived
C) Acquired immunity can be induced by either active or passive immunization
D) Natural immunity is disease-specific resistance built through past exposure
E) Herd immunity depends on a high proportion of a population being immune
Answer: A, B, C, E
Rationale: Active immunization is host-produced and durable; passive immunization is transferred and short lived; acquired immunity may follow either active or passive immunization; herd immunity depends on high individual immunization coverage across a group.
Trap: Natural immunity is innate and species-determined, not built from past exposure to a specific disease — that description belongs to acquired immunity.

Subtopic: agent, host, environment (epidemiologic triangle)

Q: A nurse is reviewing the six characteristics of an infectious agent from Box 11.1. Which characteristic refers to the ability to produce a severe pathologic reaction?
A) Infectivity
B) Pathogenicity
C) Virulence
D) Toxicity
Answer: C
Rationale: Virulence is the ability to produce a severe pathologic reaction, distinct from pathogenicity (the ability to produce any specific clinical reaction at all) and from toxicity (the ability to produce a poisonous reaction).
Trap: Pathogenicity and virulence are frequently confused. Pathogenicity asks whether disease results at all; virulence asks how severe that disease is.

Q: Which agent characteristic describes the ability to penetrate and spread throughout a tissue?
A) Antigenicity
B) Invasiveness
C) Infectivity
D) Toxicity
Answer: B
Rationale: Invasiveness is the ability of an infectious agent to penetrate and spread throughout a tissue.

Q: Which agent characteristic describes the ability to stimulate an immunologic response in the host?
A) Antigenicity
B) Pathogenicity
C) Infectivity
D) Virulence
Answer: A
Rationale: Antigenicity is the ability of an infectious agent to stimulate an immunologic response.

Q: Which of the six characteristics of an infectious agent are named in Box 11.1? Select all that apply.
A) Infectivity
B) Pathogenicity
C) Virulence
D) Toxicity
E) Invasiveness
F) Antigenicity
Answer: A, B, C, D, E, F
Rationale: The six characteristics are infectivity (ability to enter and multiply in the host), pathogenicity (ability to produce a specific clinical reaction), virulence (ability to produce a severe pathologic reaction), toxicity (ability to produce a poisonous reaction), invasiveness (ability to penetrate and spread through tissue), and antigenicity (ability to stimulate an immunologic response).

Q: Measles is described as extremely infectious. Which term is this describing?
A) Virulence — the severity of the resulting disease
B) Infectiousness — the ease with which the agent is transmitted from an infected host to other hosts
C) Toxicity — the poisonous reaction it produces
D) Antigenicity — the strength of the immune response it stimulates
Answer: B
Rationale: Infectiousness is a measure of the potential ability of an infected host to transmit the infection to other hosts, reflecting the ease with which the agent is transmitted; measles is given as an example of an extremely infectious disease.
Trap: Infectiousness (a host-transmission property) is easy to mix up with infectivity (the agent's ability to enter and multiply in a host) — they sound alike but are defined differently in the chapter.

Q: Which of the following are the four categories of infectious agents named in the chapter?
A) Bacteria, fungi, parasites, viruses
B) Bacteria, viruses, prions, parasites
C) Bacteria, fungi, viruses, toxins
D) Viruses, bacteria, rickettsiae, parasites
Answer: A
Rationale: The chapter names four categories of infectious agents: bacteria, fungi, parasites, and viruses.

Subtopic: infection, disease, and the disease timeline

Q: A patient is HIV-positive but has no signs or symptoms of illness. How should this be classified?
A) Infected and diseased
B) Infected but not diseased
C) Diseased but not infected
D) Neither infected nor diseased
Answer: B
Rationale: Infection is the entry, development, and multiplication of the infectious agent in the host; disease is a possible outcome of infection, involving physiologic dysfunction or a pathologic reaction. An asymptomatic HIV-positive person is infected but not diseased; once clinical AIDS develops, the person is both infected and diseased.
Trap: Infection does not automatically equal disease — this exact HIV framing appears in the source and is a likely stem.

Q: Whether an exposure results in infection depends on which factors, according to the chapter? Select all that apply.
A) The infective dose
B) The infectivity of the agent
C) The immunocompetence of the host
D) The season of the year
Answer: A, B, C
Rationale: Whether infection occurs depends on the infective dose, the infectivity of the agent, and the immunocompetence of the host.

Q: Why does the chapter emphasize subclinical (inapparent) cases along the disease spectrum?
A) They are the most severe cases and drive mortality statistics
B) They are a source of infection for others but often go undetected and untreated, making them a public health priority
C) They always progress to a carrier state
D) They are excluded from surveillance reporting
Answer: B
Rationale: The disease spectrum runs from subclinical/inapparent through clinical disease to an outcome (recovery, death, or an in-between state such as a carrier state, complications, or disability). Subclinical cases matter most in public health because they can transmit infection while often remaining outside of care, making them a priority for early diagnosis and treatment.

Q: A patient with hepatitis B is infectious for weeks before symptoms appear and remains infective through the acute phase and possibly for life as a chronic carrier. What does this illustrate about incubation and communicable periods?
A) They are synonymous and always overlap exactly
B) The incubation period always outlasts the communicable period
C) They are not synonymous and can diverge, as HBV shows by being communicable well before and long after the incubation period ends
D) The communicable period only begins after the incubation period is complete
Answer: C
Rationale: The incubation period is the time from invasion by an infectious agent to first appearance of signs and symptoms; the communicable period is the interval during which the agent may be transferred to others. They are not synonymous. HBV-infected persons are infectious many weeks before symptoms and remain infective through the acute phase and possibly for life as chronic carriers.
Trap: Any option treating incubation period and communicable period as interchangeable is wrong — the chapter explicitly flags them as distinct concepts that can diverge.

Q: A patient with influenza is communicable for approximately how long after clinical onset, according to the chapter's example?
A) 3 to 5 days
B) 2 to 3 weeks
C) Several months
D) For life, as a chronic carrier
Answer: A
Rationale: Influenza is given as an example where the communicable period is roughly 3 to 5 days after clinical onset, contrasted with HBV, which is communicable for weeks before symptoms and potentially for life.

Subtopic: modes of transmission

Q: A pregnant woman with untreated syphilis transmits the infection to her fetus transplacentally. What type of transmission is this?
A) Horizontal transmission via common vehicle
B) Vertical transmission
C) Horizontal transmission via vector-borne route
D) Horizontal transmission via direct contact
Answer: B
Rationale: Vertical transmission is infection passed from parent to offspring via sperm, placenta, milk, or contact in the vaginal canal at birth; transplacental HIV and syphilis are the chapter's named examples.
Trap: Mislabeling transplacental spread as horizontal transmission is the source's flagged distractor — vertical transmission is specifically parent-to-offspring.

Q: Which four routes make up horizontal transmission, according to the chapter?
A) Vertical, direct contact, airborne, vector-borne
B) Direct or indirect contact, common vehicle, airborne, vector-borne
C) Common vehicle, droplet, fecal-oral, transplacental
D) Direct contact, indirect contact, fomite, congenital
Answer: B
Rationale: Horizontal transmission is person-to-person spread through one or more of four routes: direct or indirect contact, common vehicle, airborne, or vector-borne. Most STDs are transmitted by direct sexual contact.

Q: Hepatitis A is transmitted via contaminated food or water, and hepatitis B via contaminated blood. Which transmission route do both illustrate?
A) Vector-borne
B) Airborne
C) Common vehicle
D) Vertical transmission
Answer: C
Rationale: Common vehicle transmission is the transportation of an infectious agent from an infected host to a susceptible host via food, water, milk, blood, serum, saliva, or plasma — illustrated by hepatitis A (food/water) and hepatitis B (blood).

Q: A mosquito transmits Zika virus by biting a susceptible host after feeding on an infected one. Which transmission route does this describe, and what distinguishes it as biologic rather than mechanical?
A) Vector-borne; biologic because the vector is necessary for the agent to develop before transmission can occur
B) Common vehicle; mechanical because the mosquito simply carries the virus on its body
C) Airborne; biologic because the virus travels on respiratory droplets
D) Direct contact; mechanical because no intermediate organism is involved
Answer: A
Rationale: Vectors are arthropods or invertebrates that transmit an infectious agent by biting or depositing infective material near the host. Biologic transmission means the vector is necessary for the agent to develop, as with mosquitoes and Zika or malaria, or fleas and plague; mechanical transmission means the vector merely carries the agent without it developing further.

Subtopic: prevention, eradication, and elimination

Q: A community health department runs a campaign teaching families safe food-handling practices before any outbreak occurs. Which level of prevention does this illustrate?
A) Primary prevention
B) Secondary prevention
C) Tertiary prevention
D) Herd-level prevention
Answer: A
Rationale: Primary prevention aims to prevent occurrence; the chapter's own example is educating about safe food-handling in the home. Other primary examples include immunization, "no shots, no school" laws, and sanitation.

Q: When a foodborne outbreak is suspected, the health department immediately evaluates the possible source. Which level of prevention does this illustrate?
A) Primary prevention
B) Secondary prevention
C) Tertiary prevention
D) Eradication
Answer: B
Rationale: Secondary prevention aims to prevent spread; the chapter's example is immediately evaluating the possible source of any foodborne outbreak. Screening (TB, HIV, STDs) and contact investigation are also secondary prevention.

Q: A patient with a confirmed foodborne infection is treated promptly to prevent complications. Which level of prevention does this illustrate?
A) Primary prevention
B) Secondary prevention
C) Tertiary prevention
D) Elimination
Answer: C
Rationale: Tertiary prevention aims to reduce complications and disability; the chapter's example is immediately treating any foodborne infection, alongside broader treatment and rehabilitation.

Q: Which of the following best distinguishes elimination from eradication of a disease?
A) Elimination removes a disease worldwide; eradication removes it from a large geographic area
B) Elimination removes a disease from a large geographic area, such as a country or region; eradication removes it worldwide by ending all transmission
C) The two terms are interchangeable in public health usage
D) Elimination applies only to vaccine-preventable diseases; eradication applies to all infectious diseases
Answer: B
Rationale: Elimination means removing a disease from a large geographic area such as a country or region of the world. Eradication means removing it worldwide by ending all transmission through complete extermination of the agent.
Trap: Reversing the two definitions is the frequent trap the source explicitly flags.

Q: Which disease has been eradicated worldwide, according to the chapter?
A) Polio
B) Measles
C) Smallpox
D) Tuberculosis
Answer: C
Rationale: Smallpox is the only disease that has been eradicated worldwide; the WHO declared eradication on May 8, 1980.
Trap: Any answer option describing another disease (such as polio or measles) as "eradicated" is wrong — those are, at most, eliminated from certain regions, not eradicated worldwide.

Q: Which are named as goals of the multisystem approach to communicable disease control? Select all that apply.
A) Improve host resistance
B) Improve the safety of the environment
C) Improve public health systems
D) Facilitate social and political change
E) Eliminate all infectious agents from the environment
Answer: A, B, C, D
Rationale: The four goals are improving host resistance (hygiene, immunization, nutrition), improving environmental safety (sanitation, clean water and air, vector control), improving public health systems (access to care, surveillance, reporting), and facilitating social and political change (individual, group, and community action and legislation).

Subtopic: surveillance and reportable disease

Q: Which statement correctly describes the legal basis for reportable disease surveillance in the United States?
A) Reporting is mandated by federal law and is identical across all states
B) Reporting is mandated by state law, and states voluntarily report selected diseases to the CDC through the NNDSS
C) Reporting is entirely voluntary at both the state and federal level
D) The CDC directly mandates which diseases each state must report
Answer: B
Rationale: Reporting of notifiable diseases is mandated by state law, not federal law, and varies slightly by state. States voluntarily report selected diseases to the CDC through the National Notifiable Diseases Surveillance System (NNDSS).
Trap: A stem implying federal law requires disease reporting is the distractor — reporting is state-mandated, and reporting up to the CDC is voluntary.

Q: Approximately how many National Notifiable Conditions were there in 2019?
A) 42
B) 68
C) 127
D) 210
Answer: C
Rationale: In 2019 there were 127 National Notifiable Conditions; the list is revised as diseases emerge or decline.

Subtopic: bioterrorism agents

Q: What are the two CDC criteria that place an agent among the highest-concern bioterrorism agents?
A) High mortality alone
B) Easy dissemination alone
C) Potential for high mortality and the ability to be easily disseminated
D) Prior use in a documented bioterrorism attack
Answer: C
Rationale: The CDC's highest-concern bioterrorism agents are those with the potential for high mortality AND the ability to be easily disseminated, producing major public panic and social disruption.
Trap: An option citing only one of the two criteria is incomplete — both must be present.

Q: Which organisms/agents are named among the CDC's highest-concern bioterrorism agents? Select all that apply.
A) Bacillus anthracis (anthrax)
B) Yersinia pestis (plague)
C) Variola major (smallpox)
D) Clostridium botulinum (botulism)
E) Francisella tularensis (tularemia)
F) Selected hemorrhagic viruses (Filoviridae, Arenaviridae)
Answer: A, B, C, D, E, F
Rationale: The named highest-concern agents are anthrax (Bacillus anthracis), plague (Yersinia pestis), smallpox (Variola major), botulism (Clostridium botulinum), tularemia (Francisella tularensis), and selected hemorrhagic viruses (Filoviridae and Arenaviridae).

Q: Regarding smallpox, which statements are accurate? Select all that apply.
A) It has been eradicated worldwide since 1979–1980
B) The US stopped routine immunization against it in 1982
C) Susceptibility is 100% among the unvaccinated
D) The vaccinia vaccine can protect even after exposure
E) Case fatality is under 5%
Answer: A, B, C, D
Rationale: Smallpox was eradicated worldwide (last natural death in Somalia 1977, WHO declaration 1980); the US stopped routine immunization in 1982; susceptibility is 100% in the unvaccinated; estimated fatality is 20% to 40% or higher; and the vaccinia vaccine can protect even after exposure.
Trap: Case fatality is high (20% to 40% or higher), not under 5%.

Subtopic: influenza, antigenic drift and shift

Q: Which influenza virus type is responsible for large epidemics and is the only type capable of antigenic shift?
A) Type A
B) Type B
C) Type C
D) Type D
Answer: A
Rationale: Influenza type A causes large epidemics and is the only type in which antigenic shift occurs. Type B tends to be more regional, type C is sporadic and mild, and type D infects cattle rather than people.
Trap: Attaching antigenic shift to type B or type C is the flagged trap — shift occurs only with type A.

Q: How do antigenic drift and antigenic shift differ?
A) Drift is a major change producing new subtypes; shift is a minor yearly change
B) Drift is a minor antigenic change causing yearly epidemics; shift is a major change producing new subtypes with pandemic potential, occurring only in type A
C) Drift and shift both occur equally across types A, B, and C
D) Drift causes pandemics; shift causes only regional outbreaks
Answer: B
Rationale: Antigenic drift refers to minor antigenic changes that drive yearly epidemics and regional outbreaks. Antigenic shift refers to major changes producing new subtypes, which occur only with type A; shift plus drift together drive epidemics every few years and pandemics every 10 to 40 years, such as novel influenza A H1N1 in 2009.
Trap: Reversing "minor/yearly" and "major/pandemic potential" between drift and shift is the classic inversion distractor.

Subtopic: vector-borne and zoonotic disease

Q: A patient develops symptomatic rabies several days after being bitten by a bat and not seeking care. What is the appropriate expectation regarding treatment at this point?
A) Aggressive antiviral treatment reliably cures symptomatic rabies
B) There is no successful treatment once symptoms appear; the effective intervention window was postexposure prophylaxis before symptom onset
C) Supportive care alone reliably reverses symptomatic rabies within days
D) The rabies vaccine given now will still prevent progression
Answer: B
Rationale: Rabies has no successful treatment once symptoms appear. Postexposure prophylaxis — human rabies immunoglobulin plus the rabies vaccine — can prevent disease only if given promptly before symptom onset.
Trap: Any option offering "treatment after symptom onset" for rabies is wrong; the only effective window is before symptoms appear.

Q: What distinguishes biologic transmission from mechanical transmission by a vector?
A) Biologic transmission requires no vector at all; mechanical transmission always requires a vector
B) In biologic transmission the vector is necessary for the agent to develop, as with mosquitoes and malaria; in mechanical transmission the vector simply carries the agent
C) Biologic transmission only applies to fungi; mechanical transmission only applies to bacteria
D) The two terms are interchangeable
Answer: B
Rationale: Biologic transmission means the vector is necessary for the infectious agent to develop, as with mosquitoes transmitting Zika and malaria, or fleas transmitting plague. Mechanical transmission means the vector simply carries the agent without it developing further.

Q: Why can early Lyme disease (stage I) be harder to diagnose serologically than later stages?
A) Serologic tests are more accurate in stages II and III than in stage I
B) Serology is never useful for Lyme disease at any stage
C) Erythema migrans only appears in stage III
D) Stage I Lyme disease has no serologic markers whatsoever
Answer: A
Rationale: The distinctive erythema migrans skin lesion makes early diagnosis straightforward when present; without it, diagnosis is harder because serologic tests are more accurate in stages II and III than in stage I.

Q: What defines a zoonosis?
A) An infection that can only be transmitted from human to human
B) An infection transmitted from a vertebrate animal to a human under natural conditions, where the agent does not need humans to maintain its life cycle
C) An infection transmitted exclusively by insect vectors
D) An infection that requires a common vehicle such as food or water
Answer: B
Rationale: A zoonosis is an infection transmitted from a vertebrate animal to a human under natural conditions, caused by viruses, bacteria, parasites, or fungi; the agents do not need humans to maintain their life cycles.
