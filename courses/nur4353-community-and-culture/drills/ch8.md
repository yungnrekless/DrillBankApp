# Chapter 8 drills — Environmental Health

Authored from `sources/Ch8_Fact_Bank.md` (Stanhope & Lancaster, 6th ed.,
printed pp. 137–155, Section 2, author Barbara Sattler). Every item is
anchored to that file; where the fact bank flags a trap or a source
conflict, the trap line says so explicitly.

Topics track the fact bank's heaviest testable clusters: the five-part
exposure pathway, children's vulnerability and the lead worked example,
I PREPARE, the regulatory chain, point vs. nonpoint sources, persistent

Topic: exposure pathway
Subtopic: the five-element chain

Q: For an environmental exposure to result in harm, which five elements must all be in place and connected?
A) Agent, host, environment, dose, and route
B) Source, environmental medium, receptor population, route of exposure, and dose
C) Toxicology, epidemiology, risk assessment, risk communication, and monitoring
D) Point source, nonpoint source, medium, host, and standard
Answer: B
Rationale: The five-element exposure pathway is a source of harm, an environmental medium for transport, a receptor population, a route of exposure, and an adequate dose. All five must be present and connected for harm to occur.
Trap: Option A recycles the epidemiological triangle (agent, host, environment) plus two extra terms — a plausible-sounding but different list from the exposure pathway.

Q: A community health nurse wants to interrupt an ongoing environmental exposure. According to the chapter, what is required?
A) All five links in the exposure pathway must be addressed simultaneously
B) Breaking any single link in the chain interrupts the harm
C) Only the source and the dose need to be addressed; the other three are administrative
D) The receptor population must be relocated before any other action is effective
Answer: B
Rationale: Breaking any one of the five links — removing the source, blocking the medium, protecting the receptor, cutting off the route, or reducing the dose — interrupts the harm. This is the conceptual basis for nursing intervention.
Trap: An option implying all five links must be addressed at once misses the point of the chain concept.

Q: Which of the following are recognized environmental media for transporting a hazard along the exposure pathway? Select all that apply.
A) Air
B) Surface water or groundwater
C) Soil
D) Genetic material
E) Electromagnetic fields
Answer: A, B, C
Rationale: The chapter names air, water (surface or groundwater), and soil as the environmental media through which a source of harm is transported to a receptor population.

Subtopic: routes of exposure

Q: According to the chapter, humans can be exposed to an environmental hazard through which routes?
A) Inhalation, ingestion, and dermal absorption
B) Inhalation, ingestion, dermal absorption, and genetic transmission
C) Ingestion and inhalation only
D) Inhalation, injection, and ingestion
Answer: A
Rationale: The three routes of exposure for humans are inhalation, ingestion, and skin (dermal) absorption. This is the single most likely fact to be tested from the exposure pathway section.
Trap: Any fourth "route" — such as genetic transmission or injection — is a distractor; the chapter names only three.

Q: A nurse is teaching a community group about how chemical exposures reach the body. Which statement is accurate?
A) There are four recognized routes of exposure: inhalation, ingestion, dermal absorption, and inhalation of dust specifically
B) There are three recognized routes of exposure: inhalation, ingestion, and dermal absorption
C) Dermal absorption is not considered a true route of exposure because skin is a protective barrier
D) Ingestion is the only route relevant to children
Answer: B
Rationale: Only three routes of exposure are recognized: inhalation, ingestion, and dermal absorption.

Topic: children's vulnerability
Subtopic: physiologic mechanisms

Q: Which physiologic factors does the chapter cite for children's heightened vulnerability to environmental exposures? Select all that apply.
A) Breathing more rapidly than adults, increasing proportionate exposure to air pollutants
B) A lower breathing zone due to shorter stature, closer to floors and ground-level contaminants
C) An immature blood-brain barrier
D) Kidneys that filter less effectively, increasing the dose retained
E) A stronger innate immune response than adults
Answer: A, B, C, D
Rationale: Children breathe more rapidly, have a lower breathing zone from shorter stature, an immature blood-brain barrier, and kidneys that filter less effectively — all increasing exposure or retained dose relative to adults.
Trap: The chapter does not describe children as having a stronger immune response; their vulnerability comes from immature protective mechanisms, not enhanced ones.

Q: A nurse explains why toddlers are at greater risk for lead dust ingestion than older children. What behavior is responsible?
A) Increased outdoor play time
B) Mouthing behavior — putting objects and hands in their mouths
C) Higher caloric intake per kilogram of body weight
D) Frequent hand-washing that redistributes dust
Answer: B
Rationale: Mouthing behavior — putting things in the mouth — increases ingestion, notably of lead dust.

Q: Why are fetal periods described as "windows of heightened sensitivity" in the chapter?
A) Because the placenta filters out all but the largest particles
B) Because during specific periods of fetal development, even extraordinarily small exposures can permanently alter normal development
C) Because maternal blood pressure changes reduce nutrient transfer during those periods
D) Because fetal kidneys are more efficient than a child's kidneys at filtering toxins
Answer: B
Rationale: During fetal development there are periods of heightened sensitivity when even extraordinarily small exposures can permanently alter normal development.
Trap: An option attributing children's or fetal risk solely to behavior ("carelessness") ignores that vulnerability here is physiologic, not behavioral.

Q: At approximately what age do children's lungs reach full function, according to the chapter, though they continue changing afterward?
A) Age 2
B) Age 6
C) Age 12
D) Age 18
Answer: B
Rationale: Lungs reach full function around age 6 and continue changing through adolescence.

Subtopic: lead as the worked example

Q: A nurse is assessing a home built in 1965 with old, well-maintained, fully intact lead-based paint on the walls. What is the most accurate statement about the risk?
A) Intact lead paint is a serious hazard regardless of condition and must be removed immediately
B) Intact lead poses no risk; the danger arises when it flakes or turns to dust
C) There is no risk because the home predates 1978
D) The risk is limited to drinking water in homes of this age
Answer: B
Rationale: Intact lead poses no risk; the risk comes when it flakes or turns to dust on surfaces or soil. Homes built before 1978 (when lead was banned in household paint) are the flag for risk, particularly when the paint is disturbed, as during renovation.
Trap: A scenario describing well-maintained, non-deteriorated lead paint is a lower-risk scenario, not an automatic hazard — disturbance or deterioration is what creates the danger.

Q: Lead-based paint in residential housing is most likely to be found in homes built in what time frame?
A) After 2000
B) Before 1978
C) Between 1980 and 1990
D) Only in homes built before 1950
Answer: B
Rationale: Lead was banned in household paint in 1978, so homes built before that year are most likely to contain lead-based paint.

Q: According to the EPA, as cited in the chapter, what proportion of lead poisoning is preventable?
A) 25%
B) 50%
C) 75%
D) 100%
Answer: D
Rationale: The EPA states that lead poisoning is 100% preventable.

Q: A child is found to have fetal-origin effects consistent with lead exposure. Which outcomes are named in the chapter? Select all that apply.
A) Premature birth
B) Low birth weight
C) Brain and nerve damage
D) Congenital heart defects
Answer: A, B, C
Rationale: Fetal effects of lead exposure named in the chapter are premature birth, low birth weight, and brain and nerve damage. Congenital heart defects are not listed among the chapter's lead effects.

Subtopic: lead screening and the nurse's role

Q: A community health nurse wants to address lead exposure risk at the population level rather than case by case. What is the nurse's best first action, per the chapter?
A) Order a blood-lead test for every patient seen in clinic that week
B) Set up a blood-lead screening program through the local health department
C) Refer all patients to a tertiary care center for lead testing
D) Wait for the state department of environmental protection to initiate screening
Answer: B
Rationale: The chapter names setting up a blood-lead screening program through the local health department as a specific nursing action, alongside educating local providers to systematically test children and working with local landlords to improve housing stock.
Trap: A stem asking for the nurse's best action on a community lead risk wants the population-level screening/education answer, not a single-patient clinic order.

Q: Which actions does the chapter specifically name as part of the nurse's role in addressing lead exposure? Select all that apply.
A) Set up a blood-lead screening program through the local health department
B) Educate local health providers to systematically test children for lead poisoning
C) Work with local landlords to improve the condition of their housing stock
D) Personally perform lead paint abatement in homes
E) Prescribe chelation therapy without physician oversight
Answer: A, B, C
Rationale: The three nursing actions the chapter names are setting up a screening program through the health department, educating providers to test children systematically, and working with landlords on housing conditions. Abatement and chelation are outside the nurse's named scope here.

Q: Which professionals may be part of the multidisciplinary lead team described in the chapter? Select all that apply.
A) A housing inspector or sanitarian
B) Clinical specialists
C) Laboratory workers who assess lead levels in blood, paint, house dust, and drinking water
D) Lead-based paint remediation specialists
E) A criminal justice liaison
Answer: A, B, C, D
Rationale: The multidisciplinary lead team may include a housing inspector or sanitarian, clinical specialists, laboratory workers (assessing blood, paint, house dust, and water), and lead-based paint remediation specialists, potentially involving the local health department, state department of environmental protection, housing department, and a tertiary care center.

Q: Which five factors does the chapter credit for reducing lead levels in the United States? Select all that apply.
A) Elimination of lead in paint
B) Reduction of lead in gasoline
C) Reduction of lead solder in food/drink cans and household plumbing
D) Lead screening laws
E) Universal municipal water fluoridation
Answer: A, B, C, D
Rationale: The five named factors are elimination of lead in paint, reduction of lead in gasoline, reduction of lead solder in cans and plumbing components, lead screening laws, and lead paint abatement programs in communities. Water fluoridation is unrelated to lead reduction.
Trap: There are five factors named in total; a list showing only four is missing "lead paint abatement programs in communities."

Subtopic: levels of prevention for lead exposure

Q: A public health campaign promotes using only non-lead-based paint in new construction. What level of prevention does this represent?
A) Primary
B) Secondary
C) Tertiary
D) Quaternary
Answer: A
Rationale: The Levels of Prevention box for lead exposure lists using only non-lead-based paint as primary prevention.

Q: A home inspection finds lead paint on a windowsill, and the paint is removed and replaced with non-lead paint. What level of prevention does this represent?
A) Primary
B) Secondary
C) Tertiary
D) This cannot be classified as a level of prevention
Answer: B
Rationale: If lead is found in paint, removing it and replacing it with non-lead paint is the chapter's secondary prevention example.

Q: A child with early symptoms of lead exposure receives interventions aimed at reducing blood lead levels. What level of prevention does this represent?
A) Primary
B) Secondary
C) Tertiary
D) Screening
Answer: C
Rationale: Taking steps to reduce blood lead levels at the first sign of symptoms is the chapter's tertiary prevention example for lead exposure.
Trap: Screening for lead (detecting existing but asymptomatic exposure) is secondary prevention; responding to symptoms already present is tertiary.

Topic: assessment tools
Subtopic: windshield survey and I PREPARE

Q: What role does the windshield survey play in environmental health assessment?
A) It is a definitive, complete exposure assessment
B) It is a helpful first step to understanding potential environmental health risks in a community
C) It replaces the need for an exposure history
D) It is used only for indoor air quality assessment
Answer: B
Rationale: The windshield survey is a helpful first step to understanding potential environmental health risks in a community, used to determine whether an exposure is in the air, water, soil, or food.
Trap: Treating the windshield survey as a complete or definitive assessment is the distractor; it is only a first step.

Topic: air and water quality
Subtopic: point vs. nonpoint sources

Q: A factory smokestack releasing emissions from a single identifiable location is an example of what kind of pollution source?
A) A point source
B) A nonpoint source
C) A fugitive source
D) A secondary source
Answer: A
Rationale: Point sources are identifiable sites of pollution, such as smokestacks, traceable to a specific origin.

Q: Vehicle emissions from cars, trucks, and buses are best classified as what kind of pollution source?
A) A point source, because each vehicle has an identifiable tailpipe
B) A nonpoint source, because they are diffuse and harder to trace to a single origin
C) Neither a point nor a nonpoint source, since motor vehicles are unregulated
D) A point source, because vehicles are registered with the state
Answer: B
Rationale: Nonpoint sources are diffuse pollution sources such as vehicles, harder to trace to a single origin. Motor vehicles are the greatest single source of air pollution.
Trap: Calling vehicle emissions a "point source" is the chapter's named trap — each vehicle may be identifiable, but as a pollution source category, vehicles are diffuse and nonpoint.

Q: Which statement about air pollution regulation is accurate?
A) The Clean Air Act regulates only point sources
B) The Clean Air Act regulates only nonpoint sources
C) The Clean Air Act regulates air pollution from both point and nonpoint sources
D) Point and nonpoint sources fall under separate, unrelated federal statutes
Answer: C
Rationale: The Clean Air Act regulates air pollution from both point sources (such as smokestacks) and nonpoint sources (such as vehicles).

Q: What is the consumer confidence report (CCR)?
A) A survey of consumer satisfaction with municipal water utilities
B) The annual drinking-water quality report, part of the public's right to know
C) A quarterly air quality index report
D) A federal registry of permitted polluters
Answer: B
Rationale: The consumer confidence report is the annual water-quality report, part of the public's right to know about drinking water.

Topic: government regulation
Subtopic: the regulatory chain

Q: Place the government's regulatory steps for controlling pollution in the correct order.
A) Standards, permitting, monitoring, compliance, enforcement
B) Permitting, standards, compliance, enforcement, monitoring
C) Compliance, enforcement, permitting, standards, monitoring
D) Monitoring, permitting, standards, enforcement, compliance
Answer: B
Rationale: The regulatory chain runs permitting, then standards, then compliance, then enforcement, then monitoring.

Q: Which definition correctly matches "compliance" as used in the chapter?
A) The government actions that follow to control pollution once requirements are not met
B) The processes for ensuring that permit and standard requirements are met
C) A legally binding document required for stormwater, construction, or operation activities
D) Ongoing measurement confirming that standards continue to be met
Answer: B
Rationale: Compliance is defined as the processes for ensuring that permit and standard requirements are met.
Trap: Option A describes enforcement, the step that follows compliance. Swapping the definitions of compliance and enforcement is the chapter's named trap.

Q: Which definition correctly matches "enforcement" as used in the chapter?
A) The processes for ensuring that permit and standard requirements are met
B) The government actions, paired with compliance, that are the next steps for controlling pollution
C) A permitted level of emissions or a maximum contaminant level allowed
D) A legally binding document such as a stormwater or construction permit
Answer: B
Rationale: Enforcement refers to the government actions, paired with compliance, that are the next steps for controlling pollution — the development and enforcement of standards and regulations that limit pollution.
Trap: Option A is the definition of compliance, not enforcement — the same swap risk as the companion question.

Q: An environmental standard may be expressed in which of the following ways? Select all that apply.
A) A permitted level of emissions
B) A maximum contaminant level allowed
C) An action level for cleanup
D) A risk-based standard
E) A blood-lead screening threshold specific to landlords
Answer: A, B, C, D
Rationale: Environmental standards may be expressed as a permitted emission level, a maximum contaminant level, an action level for cleanup, or a risk-based standard.

Q: A permit, as defined in the chapter, is best described as what?
A) An informal agreement between industry and a regulatory agency
B) A legally binding document; multiple permits (e.g., stormwater, construction, operation) may be required
C) A voluntary certification with no legal weight
D) A one-time approval that never requires renewal
Answer: B
Rationale: A permit is a legally binding document, and a range of permits may be required, such as stormwater control, construction, or operation permits.

Topic: persistent toxins and bioaccumulation
Subtopic: PBTs, POPs, and methylmercury

Q: What defines persistent bioaccumulative toxins (PBTs) and persistent organic pollutants (POPs)?
A) They break down rapidly once released into air, water, or soil
B) They do not break down in air, water, or soil, or in plant, animal, and human bodies
C) They are only found in industrial wastewater
D) They dissipate before reaching the food chain
Answer: B
Rationale: PBTs and POPs are chemicals that do not break down in air, water, or soil, or in the plant, animal, and human bodies to which they may be passed.
Trap: An option describing PBTs/POPs as "rapidly degrading" is wrong — persistence, not degradation, is the defining feature.

Q: What happens to the body burden of methylmercury as it moves up the food chain, according to the chapter's worked example?
A) It decreases at higher trophic levels because larger fish metabolize it more efficiently
B) It increases significantly as larger fish eat smaller fish
C) It remains constant regardless of trophic level
D) It is diluted as fish populations grow
Answer: B
Rationale: Methylmercury is bioaccumulated in fish, and as larger fish eat smaller fish, the body burden increases significantly up the food chain.
Trap: Any option stating that concentration decreases at higher trophic levels inverts the actual bioaccumulation pattern.

Q: Why does mercury persist indefinitely in waterways and oceans, according to the chapter?
A) Because it is manufactured continuously and never removed from production
B) Because, like lead, it is an element
C) Because it dissolves fully and becomes biologically inert
D) Because ocean currents prevent its breakdown
Answer: B
Rationale: Mercury, like lead, is an element and therefore persists indefinitely in waterways and oceans.

Topic: environmental justice
Subtopic: disparities

Q: How does the chapter define environmental justice (environmental equity)?
A) The principle that all populations are exposed to environmental hazards equally
B) The framework addressing the fact that certain environmental health risks disproportionately affect poor people and people of color
C) A legal requirement that all industrial permits be reviewed by a community board
D) A federal program that relocates residents away from hazardous waste sites
Answer: B
Rationale: Environmental justice, also called environmental equity, addresses the fact that certain environmental health risks disproportionately affect poor people and people of color. It is grounded in the Environmental Justice Act (1993) and Executive Order 12898 (1994).
Trap: Framing environmental justice as "everyone is exposed equally" directly contradicts the chapter's point about disproportionate burden.

Q: Which statement reflects the chapter's description of environmental health disparities?
A) A poor person of color is more likely to live near a hazardous waste site or incinerator and to have children exposed to lead
B) Environmental hazards are distributed randomly across income and racial groups
C) Wealthier communities bear a greater share of hazardous waste site proximity
D) Environmental health disparities are declining and are no longer a significant concern
Answer: A
Rationale: The chapter states that a poor person of color is more likely to live near a hazardous waste site or incinerator and to have children exposed to lead, reflecting disproportionate burden on poor people and people of color.
