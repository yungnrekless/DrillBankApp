# Chapter 14 drills — Health Education in the Community

Authored from `sources/Ch14_Fact_Bank.md` (Stanhope & Lancaster, 6th ed.,
printed pp. 251–270). Ch 14 is a narrow, 2-question blueprint chapter with a
short list of named focus points, so this deck stays tight and hits each one:
the three domains of learning, characteristics of effective educators,
barriers to learning (low health literacy, lack of motivation), the Health
Belief Model, and levels of trust among group participants.

---

Topic: health education in the community
Subtopic: domains of learning

Q: A nurse teaches a newly diagnosed diabetic what diabetes is and the facts of self-care. Which domain of learning does this activity target?
A) Cognitive
B) Affective
C) Psychomotor
D) Andragogical
Answer: A
Rationale: The cognitive domain involves thinking — memory, understanding, reasoning, and problem solving. Teaching the facts of a disease and self-care is the textbook cognitive example.
Trap: "Andragogical" describes an approach to teaching adults, not a domain of learning — it is a category-error distractor.

Q: A nurse teaches a patient how to self-administer an injection, then has the patient perform a return demonstration. Which domain of learning does this target?
A) Cognitive
B) Affective
C) Psychomotor
D) Cognitive and affective equally
Answer: C
Rationale: The psychomotor domain covers performance of motor skills requiring neuromuscular coordination. Self-injection is the classic psychomotor teaching example.

Q: A nurse spends extra time helping a newly diagnosed patient come to value and accept the importance of managing a chronic condition, addressing the patient's feelings and beliefs about the diagnosis. Which domain of learning is this, and what is notable about it?
A) Cognitive; it is the easiest domain to change
B) Affective; it is the hardest domain to change
C) Psychomotor; it requires the most practice opportunities
D) Affective; it is unrelated to attitudes or values
Answer: B
Rationale: The affective domain involves feeling — changes in attitudes and values — and is the hardest domain to change, requiring support and encouragement.
Trap: Any option that separates the affective domain from attitudes/values, or claims cognitive is hardest to change, inverts the fact bank.

Q: Which teaching activities belong in the cognitive domain? Select all that apply.
A) Explaining the pathophysiology of a new diagnosis
B) Helping a patient reason through treatment options
C) Teaching crutch-walking
D) Reviewing memory-based facts about a medication regimen
E) Fostering a patient's willingness to change a health behavior
Answer: A, B, D
Rationale: The cognitive domain covers thinking — memory, understanding, reasoning, and problem solving. Crutch-walking is a psychomotor (motor skill) activity, and fostering willingness/attitude change is affective.

Q: What three conditions must be present for psychomotor learning to occur? Select all that apply.
A) The learner must have the necessary ability (cognitive and physical)
B) The learner must have a sensory image of how to perform the skill
C) The learner must have opportunities to practice
D) The learner must first master the affective domain
E) The learner must complete a written test
Answer: A, B, C
Rationale: Psychomotor learning requires ability (cognitive plus physical), a sensory image of how to do the skill, and opportunities to practice.

Q: What is the correct teaching sequence for a psychomotor skill such as dressing changes?
A) Return demonstration first, then a live demonstration by the nurse
B) The nurse demonstrates the skill first (picture, model, video, or live), then the learner performs a return demonstration
C) Written instructions only, followed by independent practice at home
D) The learner practices independently before any demonstration is given
Answer: B
Rationale: The nurse first demonstrates the skill, then has the learner perform a return/repeat demonstration to validate learning.

Subtopic: characteristics of effective educators

Q: Place the instructional sequence for effective teaching in the correct order.
A) Gain attention → state objectives → recall prior knowledge → present material → apply → demonstrate → feedback
B) State objectives → present material → gain attention → apply → feedback → demonstrate
C) Present material → gain attention → demonstrate → recall prior knowledge → apply → feedback
D) Demonstrate → gain attention → present material → state objectives → apply → feedback
Answer: A
Rationale: The instructional sequence is: gain attention, state objectives, recall prior knowledge, present essential material, help learners apply it, have learners demonstrate what they learned, and provide feedback.

Q: A nurse skips straight from presenting material to having learners demonstrate the skill, without first linking the new material to what learners already know or helping them apply it. What is the most likely result?
A) No effect, since recall of prior knowledge and application are optional steps
B) Superficial, fragmented learning
C) Improved retention, since fewer steps means less cognitive load
D) A shift from cognitive to psychomotor learning
Answer: B
Rationale: Omitting steps in the instructional sequence leads to superficial, fragmented learning.

Q: Which practices characterize an effective educator? Select all that apply.
A) Using an active, lively format rather than a passive one
B) Making the point quickly, given short attention spans
C) Using teach-back to confirm understanding
D) Communicating in a culturally responsive way
E) Apologizing at the outset for limited experience with the topic
Answer: A, B, C, D
Rationale: Effective educators keep teaching active, are concise, use teach-back, and communicate in a culturally responsive way. A lack-of-credibility barrier is fixed by preparing thoroughly and not apologizing, not by opening with an apology.

Subtopic: barriers to learning

Q: What are the two most important learner-related barriers to learning identified in the chapter?
A) Lack of time and lack of transportation
B) Low health literacy and lack of motivation
C) Language barriers and low income
D) Fear of public speaking and poor timing
Answer: B
Rationale: The two most important learner-related barriers are low literacy/low health literacy and lack of motivation.
Trap: Fear of public speaking and poor timing are educator-related barriers, not learner-related barriers.

Q: How does the IOM define health literacy?
A) The ability to read at or above a sixth-grade level
B) The degree to which individuals have the capacity to obtain, process, and understand basic health information and services needed to make appropriate health decisions
C) Completion of the National Assessment of Adult Literacy at the proficient level
D) The ability to self-manage a chronic disease without professional assistance
Answer: B
Rationale: The IOM defines health literacy as the degree to which individuals have the capacity to obtain, process, and understand basic health information and services needed to make appropriate health decisions.

Q: Which groups are described as being at higher risk for low health literacy? Select all that apply.
A) Older adults (65+)
B) Those below the poverty line
C) Medically underserved people
D) Recent refugees and immigrants
E) College-educated adults under 40
Answer: A, B, C, D
Rationale: Groups more likely to have low health literacy include older adults (65+), those below the poverty line, medically underserved people, recent refugees/immigrants, and nonnative English speakers.

Q: A patient with low health literacy nods along during teaching, says "I understand," and asks no questions. What should the nurse recognize?
A) The patient has demonstrated adequate understanding and no further action is needed
B) Patients with low literacy may be embarrassed, may pretend to understand, and may not ask questions, so understanding should be confirmed with teach-back
C) The absence of questions confirms the patient is highly health literate
D) The nurse should proceed to written materials as the primary teaching method
Answer: B
Rationale: Patients with low literacy may be embarrassed, pretend to understand, and not ask questions. The nurse should use plain language and confirm understanding with teach-back rather than assuming comprehension.

Subtopic: motivation and the Health Belief Model

Q: A patient says, "Even if I did get lung disease, it wouldn't be that bad — I'd just deal with it." Which Health Belief Model component is this patient expressing low perceived level of?
A) Perceived susceptibility
B) Perceived severity
C) Perceived benefits
D) Self-efficacy
Answer: B
Rationale: Perceived severity asks "if it happens, is it a big problem?" This patient is minimizing how serious the consequence would be.
Trap: Perceived susceptibility is about whether the event will happen at all ("will something happen to me?"), not how bad it would be if it did — the two are easy to swap.

Q: A patient says, "I don't think quitting smoking will actually make a difference for someone like me at this point." Which Health Belief Model component does this reflect?
A) Perceived susceptibility
B) Perceived barriers
C) Perceived benefits
D) Cues to action
Answer: C
Rationale: Perceived benefits addresses "will doing this really help?" The patient is doubting that the behavior change will actually help.
Trap: Perceived barriers concerns obstacles to acting (cost, unpleasantness), not doubt about whether the action would work if performed.

Q: Which are the six components of the Health Belief Model? Select all that apply.
A) Perceived susceptibility
B) Perceived severity
C) Perceived benefits
D) Perceived barriers
E) Cues to action
F) Self-efficacy
G) Stages of change
Answer: A, B, C, D, E, F
Rationale: The HBM's six components are perceived susceptibility, perceived severity, perceived benefits, perceived barriers, cues to action, and self-efficacy. Stages of change belongs to the Transtheoretical Model, a different model entirely.
Trap: Stages of change is the hallmark of the Transtheoretical Model (TTM), not the HBM — a common cross-model mix-up.

Q: A nurse is planning an intervention for a patient who understands the facts about her condition but has not taken any steps toward behavior change. Which model is most appropriate to guide planning, and why?
A) The Transtheoretical Model, because the patient already has the necessary knowledge
B) The Health Belief Model, because the concern is what motivates the patient to act
C) Pedagogy, because the patient needs to relearn the basic facts
D) Andragogy, because the patient is self-directed
Answer: B
Rationale: The HBM should be used when learner motivation is the concern — it addresses what motivates a person to act, distinct from simply possessing knowledge.

Subtopic: groups and levels of trust

Q: A community health nurse is forming a support group focused on helping individual members change a personal health behavior. What group size best supports this goal, and why?
A) 8–12 members, because that size supports the intimacy needed for individual behavior change
B) Up to 25 members, because larger groups generate more diverse input
C) 3–5 members, because smaller is always better for trust
D) Group size does not affect the group's function
Answer: A
Rationale: A group size of 8–12 members works best for individual health change; groups of up to 25 can work when the focus is community needs, and large groups should split into subgroups.
Trap: Up to 25 members is appropriate when the focus is community needs, not individual behavior change — the two group purposes call for different sizes.

Q: Match each type of group norm to its focus. Which pairing is correct?
A) Task norms — getting the work done; maintenance norms — keeping relationships healthy; reality norms — a shared view of what is real
B) Task norms — keeping relationships healthy; maintenance norms — getting the work done; reality norms — resolving conflict
C) Reality norms — getting the work done; task norms — a shared view of what is real; maintenance norms — resolving conflict
D) Maintenance norms — a shared view of what is real; task norms — resolving conflict; reality norms — keeping relationships healthy
Answer: A
Rationale: Task norms guide getting the work done, maintenance norms guide keeping relationships healthy, and reality norms guide a shared view of what is real.

Q: Members of a newly formed support group begin to disagree openly about how the group should proceed. The facilitator is considering whether to redirect the group away from the disagreement. What does the chapter say about this situation?
A) Conflict should be suppressed quickly to preserve group cohesion
B) Conflict is normal and useful, signaling that differing viewpoints must be considered; a group that repeatedly avoids conflict becomes fragile
C) Conflict indicates the group is too large and should be split into subgroups
D) Conflict is a sign that trust has already failed to develop
Answer: B
Rationale: Conflict is normal and useful — it signals that differing viewpoints must be considered. A group that repeatedly avoids conflict becomes fragile and unable to adapt. Effective facilitation respects diverse opinions and promotes their expression.
Trap: Suppressing conflict is the intuitive but wrong move — the fact bank is explicit that suppression damages the group, while trust is built progressively through cohesion and working through conflict, not by avoiding it.
