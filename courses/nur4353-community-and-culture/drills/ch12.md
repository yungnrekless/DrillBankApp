# Chapter 12 drills — Communicable and Infectious Disease Risks

Authored from `sources/Ch12_Fact_Bank.md` (Stanhope & Lancaster, 6th ed.,
printed pp. 217–236). Every item is anchored to that file; where the fact bank
flags a trap or an internal inconsistency (notably the OCR-corrupted AIDS CD4
count), the trap line says so explicitly.

Topics follow the chapter's heaviest testable clusters: HIV natural history
and testing, the STD/PID pair, syphilis staging, the hepatitis A/B/C split,
TB screening and DOT, and levels of prevention with partner notification.

---

Topic: HIV
Subtopic: natural history and window period

Q: A patient contracted HIV three weeks ago and now reports fever, sore throat, night sweats, and swollen lymph nodes. An antibody test is drawn today. What is the most likely result and why?
A) Positive, because antibodies form within days of infection
B) Negative, because antibodies have not yet developed even though the virus is present in large amounts
C) Positive, because the mononucleosis-like syndrome always follows seroconversion
D) Indeterminate, requiring a Western blot regardless of timing
Answer: B
Rationale: This is the primary infection stage — a mononucleosis-like syndrome within a month of infection when the virus is most plentiful. An antibody test at this stage is usually negative because antibodies have not yet formed.
Trap: The symptoms are dramatic and the viral burden is high, which tempts a "positive" answer, but antibody tests detect antibodies, not the virus itself.

Q: Place the three stages of HIV natural history in order.
A) Clinical latency, primary infection, symptomatic disease/AIDS
B) Primary infection, clinical latency, symptomatic disease/AIDS
C) Symptomatic disease/AIDS, primary infection, clinical latency
D) Primary infection, symptomatic disease/AIDS, clinical latency
Answer: B
Rationale: The sequence is primary infection (mononucleosis-like syndrome), clinical latency (asymptomatic period ending in seroconversion), then symptomatic disease progressing to AIDS.

Q: What is the HIV "window period," and what is its clinical significance?
A) The 20 minutes required for a rapid oral test to return a result
B) The 6 weeks to 3 months after infection before antibodies appear, during which an antibody test can give a false negative
C) The interval between a positive EIA and a confirmatory Western blot
D) The period after AIDS diagnosis during which opportunistic infections are most likely
Answer: B
Rationale: The window period is the 6-weeks-to-3-months interval after infection but before antibodies are produced, during which the antibody test can give a false negative.
Trap: An early negative test during this window is not definitive proof of no infection.

Q: A patient tests negative for HIV antibodies 5 weeks after a high-risk exposure and asks if this rules out infection. What is the best response?
A) Yes, a negative antibody test at any point rules out infection
B) No, this falls within the window period, and repeat testing is recommended because antibodies may not yet be detectable
C) Yes, because the mononucleosis-like syndrome would already have resolved by 5 weeks
D) No, because antibody tests are never accurate for HIV
Answer: B
Rationale: Seroconversion occurs 6 weeks to 3 months after infection. A negative test at 5 weeks falls inside the window period and does not rule out infection; repeat testing is warranted.

Q: Which of the following is an accurate statement about AIDS diagnostic criteria?
A) AIDS is diagnosed when the CD4 count drops below 200 cells/mm³ or the person develops an opportunistic infection
B) AIDS is diagnosed only when the CD4 count drops below 22 cells/mm³
C) AIDS is diagnosed solely on the basis of a positive Western blot
D) AIDS is diagnosed when antibodies first appear in the blood
Answer: A
Rationale: AIDS, the advanced stage of HIV infection, is diagnosed when the CD4 cell count drops below 200 cells/mm³ or when the person develops opportunistic infections.
Trap: The source PDF's two-column scan corrupts this number to "22 cells/mm" because an unrelated clause bled into the sentence. The correct, standard threshold is 200; treat "22" as a scan artifact, never as the tested value.

Q: What role do HIV antibodies play once they appear in the blood?
A) They neutralize the virus and halt disease progression
B) They provide protective immunity against reinfection
C) They do not serve a protective role, but their presence allows the infection to be detected
D) They convert the infection from chronic to self-limited
Answer: C
Rationale: HIV antibodies do not serve a protective role, but their presence allows detection — this is the basis of antibody testing.

Subtopic: transmission and testing sequence

Q: Which is the most common route of HIV transmission in the US?
A) Sharing needles or drug-preparation equipment
B) Anal or vaginal sex with an HIV-positive person not using a condom or HIV medicines
C) Accidental needlestick injury
D) Organ transplantation
Answer: B
Rationale: The first most common US transmission route is anal or vaginal sex with an HIV-positive person not using a condom or HIV medicines. Sharing needles/syringes is second most common; needlestick and organ transplant are rare routes.

Q: A screening blood donation returns a positive result on the initial HIV test. What should happen next, and why?
A) The result should be reported as confirmed positive immediately, since screening tests are highly specific
B) A Western blot should be performed to confirm the result and minimize false positives
C) No further testing is needed until symptoms develop
D) The EIA should simply be repeated on the same sample with no other test
Answer: B
Rationale: EIA is the most commonly used screening test. A positive EIA is confirmed with a Western blot, which minimizes false positives.
Trap: Running the Western blot first, before an EIA screen, reverses the correct sequence and is the classic distractor.

Q: Which statement correctly describes the HIV antibody test?
A) It detects and isolates the virus itself
B) It detects symptomatic AIDS directly
C) It detects the antibody to HIV, not symptoms and not the virus itself
D) It is diagnostic and requires no confirmatory testing
Answer: C
Rationale: The HIV antibody test is the most commonly used screening test; it detects the antibody to HIV, not symptoms and not the virus itself.
Trap: A stem claiming the antibody test "isolates the virus" or "diagnoses symptomatic AIDS" is testing this exact misconception.

Q: A patient wants immediate results and chooses the rapid HIV antibody test. What should the nurse know about this test?
A) It requires a blood draw and 24-hour turnaround
B) It uses oral fluid, is about 99.5% accurate, and gives results in about 20 minutes
C) It is a confirmatory test used after a positive EIA
D) It is only available through hospital laboratories, not for self-testing
Answer: B
Rationale: The rapid HIV antibody test (oral fluid, e.g., OraQuick) is about 99.5% accurate with results in 20 minutes, and is the only FDA-approved rapid self-test.

Q: Regarding HIV testing policy, which statement is accurate?
A) Patients cannot decline HIV testing once it is ordered
B) Patients can opt out of HIV testing, and annual testing is recommended for those with high-risk behavior
C) HIV testing is mandatory for all patients admitted to a hospital
D) Annual testing is recommended for all patients regardless of risk
Answer: B
Rationale: Patients can opt out of HIV testing; annual testing is recommended specifically for those with high-risk behavior.

Q: Which statements about HIV transmission are accurate? Select all that apply.
A) Transmission requires contact with blood, semen, rectal or vaginal fluids, or breast milk reaching a mucous membrane or damaged tissue, or direct injection into the bloodstream
B) HIV can live in a used needle for up to 42 days
C) Casual contact such as sharing utensils is a common transmission route
D) Transplanted organs are a possible, though rare, transmission route
E) Sharing needles or drug-preparation equipment is the most common US transmission route
Answer: A, B, D
Rationale: Transmission requires the named fluids to contact a mucous membrane, damaged tissue, or be injected directly into the bloodstream; HIV can survive in a used needle up to 42 days; transplanted organs are a rare route. Sexual transmission, not needle-sharing, is the most common route, and casual contact is not a transmission route.

Q: According to Box 12.3, which are stated responsibilities of a person who is HIV infected? Select all that apply.
A) Have regular medical evaluations and follow-ups
B) Donate blood or organs only if currently asymptomatic
C) Inform sexual or injection-drug-using partners of potential exposure, or arrange notification through the health department
D) Take precautions against exchanging body fluids during sexual activity
E) Inform health care providers of the HIV infection
Answer: A, C, D, E
Rationale: Box 12.3 lists regular medical evaluation and follow-up; not donating blood, plasma, organs, tissue, or sperm under any circumstance; taking precautions against exchanging body fluids during sexual activity; informing partners or arranging notification through the health department; informing health care providers; and considering the risk of perinatal transmission with follow-up contraception.
Trap: There is no exception for being asymptomatic — an HIV-infected person should not donate blood, plasma, organs, tissue, or sperm at all.

Topic: STDs
Subtopic: chlamydia, gonorrhea, and PID

Q: Which infectious disease is the most commonly reported in the United States?
A) Gonorrhea
B) Chlamydia
C) Syphilis
D) Genital herpes
Answer: B
Rationale: Chlamydia is the most commonly reported infectious disease in the US; gonorrhea is second most common.
Trap: Chlamydia and gonorrhea are easy to invert. Chlamydia is first, gonorrhea is second.

Q: Which infectious disease is the second most commonly reported notifiable disease in the United States?
A) Chlamydia
B) Gonorrhea
C) Genital warts
D) Genital herpes
Answer: B
Rationale: Gonorrhea is the second most commonly reported notifiable disease in the US, after chlamydia.

Q: A woman with untreated, asymptomatic chlamydia is at greatest risk for which complication, and why is she particularly vulnerable?
A) Pelvic inflammatory disease, because remaining asymptomatic and not seeking treatment is the key risk factor
B) Pelvic inflammatory disease, because symptomatic infection is the key risk factor
C) Genital warts, because HPV commonly co-occurs with chlamydia
D) Tertiary syphilis, because untreated STDs progress to syphilis
Answer: A
Rationale: PID is a serious complication and a particular risk for women who remain asymptomatic and do not seek treatment; it can follow untreated chlamydia or gonorrhea and threatens fertility.
Trap: The danger is the silent, unsought case — not the symptomatic infection that prompts treatment.

Q: Which women are recommended for chlamydia/gonorrhea screening according to the chapter?
A) All sexually active women regardless of age
B) All sexually active women younger than 25, and women 25 and older at increased risk (new or multiple partners)
C) Only women who report symptoms
D) Only pregnant women
Answer: B
Rationale: Screening is recommended for all sexually active women younger than 25, and for women 25 or older who are at increased risk due to new or multiple partners.

Q: Which statements about gonorrhea treatment are accurate? Select all that apply.
A) Treatment has become simpler over time as new antibiotic options emerged
B) Treatment has become more complex because of drug resistance
C) The current regimen combines injected ceftriaxone with oral azithromycin
D) In 2005 there were five treatment options; today there is essentially one regimen
Answer: B, C, D
Rationale: Gonorrhea treatment is becoming more complex due to drug resistance; the current regimen is injected ceftriaxone plus oral azithromycin, down from five treatment options in 2005 to essentially one.

Subtopic: syphilis staging

Q: Place the four stages of syphilis in correct order.
A) Secondary, primary, latent, tertiary
B) Primary, latent, secondary, tertiary
C) Primary, secondary, latent, tertiary
D) Latent, primary, secondary, tertiary
Answer: C
Rationale: Syphilis, caused by Treponema pallidum, progresses through primary, secondary, latent, and tertiary stages in that order.

Q: A patient presents with a chancre — a macule that progressed to a papule and then ulcerated — at the site of entry. Which stage of syphilis does this describe?
A) Primary
B) Secondary
C) Latent
D) Tertiary
Answer: A
Rationale: Primary syphilis is marked by a chancre at the site of entry, beginning as a macule, progressing to a papule, then ulcerating.

Q: In which stage of syphilis does the organism enter the lymph system and spread throughout the body, producing rash and lymphadenopathy?
A) Primary
B) Secondary
C) Latent
D) Tertiary
Answer: B
Rationale: In the secondary stage, Treponema pallidum enters the lymph system and spreads throughout the body, producing rash and lymphadenopathy.

Q: Which statement correctly describes tertiary syphilis?
A) It is an asymptomatic period with no visible signs
B) It occurs within weeks of initial infection and is common in the US
C) It occurs several years after initial infection and is rare in the US, though a major problem in developing countries
D) It is the stage in which the chancre first appears
Answer: C
Rationale: Tertiary syphilis occurs several years after initial infection; it is rare in the US but a major problem in developing countries.
Trap: The asymptomatic, no-visible-signs description belongs to the latent stage, not tertiary.

Topic: viral hepatitis
Subtopic: HAV vs HBV vs HCV

Q: Which hepatitis virus is transmitted fecal-orally and does not lead to chronic infection?
A) Hepatitis A
B) Hepatitis B
C) Hepatitis C
D) Both hepatitis B and C
Answer: A
Rationale: HAV is a self-limited disease that does not lead to chronic infection; it is transmitted fecal-orally or via contaminated food/water and is vaccine-preventable.
Trap: An option calling HAV "blood-borne" or "chronic" is the classic distractor — HAV is the outlier that is neither.

Q: Which statement about hepatitis B is accurate?
A) It is transmitted fecal-orally like hepatitis A
B) It is spread through blood and body fluids, with the same transmission properties as HIV, and can become chronic
C) It is always self-limited and never becomes chronic
D) It is the most common blood-borne pathogen in the US
Answer: B
Rationale: HBV is spread through blood and body fluids — a blood-borne pathogen with the same transmission properties as HIV — and can become chronic. It is vaccine-preventable, and pregnant women are screened for HBsAg.
Trap: HCV, not HBV, is described as the most common blood-borne pathogen in the US.

Q: Which hepatitis virus is described as the most common blood-borne pathogen in the US, and what proportion of infected persons develop chronic infection?
A) Hepatitis A; less than 10%
B) Hepatitis B; about 50%
C) Hepatitis C; about 75% to 85%
D) Hepatitis C; about 15% to 25%
Answer: C
Rationale: HCV is the most common blood-borne pathogen in the US, and about 75% to 85% of those infected develop chronic/lifelong infection; 15% to 25% clear it without treatment.
Trap: The 15%-to-25% figure describes the minority who clear HCV, not the majority who become chronic — do not swap these figures.

Q: A nurse is teaching a patient the difference between hepatitis A, B, and C. Which statement is correct?
A) HAV is fecal-oral and self-limited; HBV and HCV are blood-borne and can become chronic
B) All three types are blood-borne and can become chronic
C) HAV can become chronic, but HBV and HCV are always self-limited
D) HBV is fecal-oral, while HAV and HCV are blood-borne
Answer: A
Rationale: HAV is fecal-oral and self-limited with no chronic carrier state; HBV and HCV are blood-borne and can become chronic, with HCV most often becoming chronic.

Q: Which statements about hepatitis A, B, and C are accurate? Select all that apply.
A) HAV is vaccine-preventable
B) HBV is vaccine-preventable
C) HCV is vaccine-preventable
D) Injection drug use is a major transmission route for HIV, HBV, and HCV
E) Children under 6 with HAV are often asymptomatic
Answer: A, B, D, E
Rationale: HAV and HBV are vaccine-preventable; HCV is not. Injection drug use is a major transmission route shared by HIV, HBV, and HCV. Children under 6 infected with HAV are often asymptomatic.

Topic: tuberculosis
Subtopic: screening, latent vs active, and DOT

Q: A patient has a positive Mantoux (PPD) skin test result. What should happen next?
A) The patient should be diagnosed with active TB and started on treatment
B) The patient should undergo chest radiography, since the skin test is a screening test, not a diagnostic one
C) No further workup is needed since the skin test is diagnostic
D) The patient should be placed in isolation as infectious immediately
Answer: B
Rationale: The Mantoux/PPD test is used for initial screening. A positive skin reaction plus pulmonary symptoms is followed by chest radiography for confirmation.
Trap: Treating a positive TST as a TB diagnosis, rather than as a trigger for chest x-ray follow-up, is the classic screening-vs-diagnosis trap.

Q: A patient has latent TB infection. Which statement is correct?
A) The patient is infected and can transmit the disease to others
B) The patient is infected but not ill and cannot transmit the disease
C) The patient has active pulmonary symptoms but is not infectious
D) The patient no longer carries the TB organism
Answer: B
Rationale: In latent TB, the person is infected but not ill and cannot transmit the disease. WHO estimates about one-fourth of the world's population has latent TB.
Trap: Any option suggesting a latent-TB patient can spread the disease is the distractor — infectiousness requires active disease.

Q: What is transmitted by airborne droplets from persons with pulmonary TB who talk, cough, or sneeze?
A) Only active TB, not latent TB
B) Latent TB only
C) Both latent and active TB equally
D) Neither, since TB is a blood-borne pathogen
Answer: A
Rationale: TB is spread by airborne droplets from persons with pulmonary TB who talk, cough, or sneeze; this transmissibility applies to active disease, since latent TB is not infectious.

Q: What is the primary stated purpose of directly observed therapy (DOT) for TB?
A) To provide convenience for individual patients who forget their medication schedule
B) To ensure adherence and prevent drug resistance, functioning as a population-level intervention
C) To reduce the cost of TB medications for the health department
D) To replace the need for chest radiography follow-up
Answer: B
Rationale: DOT programs have nurses observe and document patients taking their TB drugs to ensure adherence and prevent drug resistance; premature discontinuation risks resistance that can affect an entire susceptible community, making DOT a population-level intervention.
Trap: Framing DOT as merely individual convenience misses the point — its purpose is adherence and prevention of resistance at the population level.

Q: What distinguishes XDR-TB from MDR-TB?
A) XDR-TB is resistant to fewer drugs than MDR-TB
B) XDR-TB is MDR-TB plus resistance to injected drugs
C) MDR-TB is airborne while XDR-TB is blood-borne
D) XDR-TB refers only to latent infection, while MDR-TB refers to active disease
Answer: B
Rationale: MDR-TB is multidrug-resistant TB; XDR-TB is MDR-TB plus resistance to injected drugs. Drug-resistant TB is a particular concern for immunocompromised people such as those with HIV.

Q: Which populations are named as being at higher risk for TB? Select all that apply.
A) Immunosuppressed persons
B) Undernourished persons
C) Persons with diabetes
D) Persons with silicosis or a history of gastrectomy
E) Persons with well-controlled hypertension
Answer: A, B, C, D
Rationale: Higher-risk groups named include immunosuppressed persons, undernourished persons, and persons with diabetes, silicosis, or gastrectomies.

Q: Nurses involved in TB care are described as participating in which activity beyond medication observation?
A) Contact investigations
B) Prescribing antitubercular regimens independently
C) Performing bronchoscopy
D) Manufacturing PPD antigen
Answer: A
Rationale: Nurses conducting DOT are also involved in contact investigations as part of population-level TB control.

Topic: prevention and partner notification
Subtopic: levels of prevention and contact tracing

Q: According to the chapter, what is the best way to prevent STDs, and what is emphasized when that approach is unrealistic?
A) Vaccination is the best method; abstinence is a secondary consideration
B) Sexual abstinence is the best way to prevent STDs, but teaching safer sexual behavior is critical when abstinence is unrealistic
C) Partner notification is the best method of primary prevention
D) Screening is the best method of primary prevention
Answer: B
Rationale: The chapter states sexual abstinence is the best way to prevent STDs, but when abstinence is unrealistic, teaching safer sexual behavior is critical. Primary prevention also includes HPV and HBV vaccination.

Q: A nurse follows up with the contacts of a patient recently diagnosed with a reportable STD to prevent further spread. Which level of prevention does this represent?
A) Primary
B) Secondary
C) Tertiary
D) Quaternary
Answer: B
Rationale: Secondary prevention includes screening for early identification and treatment, and follow-up with contacts to prevent further spread.

Q: Which level of prevention applies to chronic viral STDs such as genital herpes and genital warts, as well as TB, with a focus on managing symptoms and psychosocial support?
A) Primary
B) Secondary
C) Tertiary
D) None — chronic conditions fall outside the prevention framework
Answer: C
Rationale: Tertiary prevention applies to chronic viral STDs (genital herpes, genital warts) and TB, focusing on managing symptoms and maintaining psychosocial support.

Q: What is the relationship between partner notification and contact tracing?
A) They are unrelated interventions applied to different diseases
B) They are synonyms for the same population-level, confidential intervention carried out by health departments
C) Partner notification applies only to STDs, while contact tracing applies only to TB
D) Contact tracing is a voluntary patient-led activity, while partner notification is mandatory
Answer: B
Rationale: Partner notification and contact tracing are the same thing — a population-level control intervention carried out confidentially by health departments, usually alongside reportable-disease requirements, to identify and notify exposed individuals.
Trap: Do not treat partner notification and contact tracing as two different interventions; the chapter uses them interchangeably.

Q: A patient newly diagnosed with a reportable STD is unwilling to notify past partners. What is the nurse's role?
A) The nurse has no further role once the patient declines
B) The nurse carries out partner notification through the health department
C) The nurse must obtain a court order before proceeding
D) The nurse should only document the refusal and take no action
Answer: B
Rationale: If a patient is unwilling to notify past partners, the nurse carries out partner notification, which is a confidential, population-level intervention typically coordinated through health departments.

Q: Which statements accurately describe the three levels of prevention as applied to STDs and TB? Select all that apply.
A) Primary prevention includes safer sex education and HPV/HBV vaccination
B) Secondary prevention includes screening and contact follow-up
C) Tertiary prevention includes managing symptoms and psychosocial support for chronic conditions
D) Primary prevention includes chest radiography follow-up on a positive skin test
E) Contact tracing is carried out confidentially and is a population-level intervention
Answer: A, B, C, E
Rationale: Primary prevention is risk assessment, education, safer sex, and HPV/HBV vaccination; secondary is screening and contact follow-up; tertiary is symptom management and psychosocial support for chronic viral STDs and TB; contact tracing/partner notification is confidential and population-level.
Trap: Chest radiography follow-up on a positive skin test is part of the TB screening sequence, not a level-of-prevention category by itself.
