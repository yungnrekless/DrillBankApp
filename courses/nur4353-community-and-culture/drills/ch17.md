# Chapter 17 drills — Public Health Surveillance and Outbreak Investigation

Authored from `sources/Ch17_Fact_Bank.md` (Stanhope & Lancaster, 6th ed.,
printed pp. 305–316).

The fact bank flags that seven of the eight topics listed in this chapter's
blueprint row (types of immunity, disease transmission, food safety specifics,
at-risk populations, emerging/reemerging disease, confidentiality, named
diseases like West Nile/Lyme/TB/HIV) actually belong to Chapters 11 and 12.
Those topics are deliberately NOT drilled here — they are unsourced from this
chapter's own text and are already covered by the Ch11/Ch12 drill banks. This
file covers only what Chapter 17 itself contains: disease surveillance
definitions, process/outcome data, notifiable disease reporting, case
definitions, the four surveillance system types, the outbreak/endemic
vocabulary, outbreak patterns, agent classification, bioterrorism clues, the
six-step investigation process, and the foodborne outbreak definition.

Topic: disease surveillance
Subtopic: definitions and purpose

Q: Which four activities are named in the textbook's definition of disease surveillance?
A) Screening, diagnosis, treatment, and follow-up
B) Collection, analysis, interpretation, and dissemination of specific health data
C) Notification, investigation, quarantine, and reporting
D) Detection, confirmation, control, and evaluation
Answer: B
Rationale: Disease surveillance is defined as "the ongoing systematic collection, analysis, interpretation, and dissemination of specific health data for use in public health."
Trap: Notification/investigation/quarantine/reporting describe outbreak response activities, not the definition of surveillance itself.

Q: According to the CDC, disease surveillance serves as which of the following?
A) An optional adjunct to clinical practice
B) The foundation of public health practice
C) A function reserved for federal agencies only
D) A substitute for epidemiologic investigation
Answer: B
Rationale: The CDC indicates that public health surveillance is the foundation of public health practice.

Subtopic: process vs. outcome data

Q: A health department tracks the proportion of the eligible population vaccinated against influenza in a given year. What type of data is this?
A) Outcome data
B) Process data
C) Sentinel data
D) Syndromic data
Answer: B
Rationale: Process data focus on what is done — services provided or protocols for care delivery. The proportion of the eligible population vaccinated is the textbook's own example of process data.

Q: A health department tracks the incidence rate of new influenza cases in that same population over the same year. What type of data is this?
A) Process data
B) Outcome data
C) Registry data
D) Algorithmic data
Answer: B
Rationale: Outcome data focus on changes in health status; incidence rates of influenza are the textbook's own example of outcome data.
Trap: This is the mirror image of the vaccination-proportion item. Process = what is done (an action/service); outcome = what happened to health status as a result.

Topic: notifiable disease reporting
Subtopic: legal basis and national/state structure

Q: A nurse assumes that because a disease is on the CDC's nationally notifiable disease list, every state legally requires it to be reported. Is this assumption correct?
A) Yes, national notifiable status automatically creates a legal reporting mandate in every state
B) No — reporting requirements are mandated by state law or regulation, and states differ in their lists, so not all nationally notifiable diseases are legally mandated for reporting in a given state
C) Yes, but only for diseases added to the list after 1997
D) No — only CDC WONDER-listed diseases carry any legal mandate
Answer: B
Rationale: Requirements for reporting diseases are mandated by law or regulation at the state level. Each state and Washington, DC, differs in its list of reportable diseases, so not all nationally notifiable diseases are legally mandated for reporting in a given state.
Trap: The national list (NNDSS) is a CDC/CSTE reporting arrangement between states and the CDC, not itself a law compelling reporting within a state — that authority is state law.

Q: A nurse wants to know exactly which diseases are legally reportable in her own state. Where does the chapter direct her to look?
A) The CDC WONDER database
B) The Morbidity and Mortality Weekly Report
C) Her state health department's website
D) The National Electronic Disease Surveillance System
Answer: C
Rationale: The chapter states, "To determine which of the national notifiable diseases are reportable in your state, go to your state health department website."

Q: Weekly national notifiable disease data from the 50 states, US territories, New York City, and DC are published in which source?
A) CDC WONDER
B) The Morbidity and Mortality Weekly Report (MMWR)
C) The National Vital Statistics System
D) The National Program of Cancer Registries
Answer: B
Rationale: Data are published weekly in the MMWR; annual updated final reports are published in CDC WONDER.
Trap: CDC WONDER holds the annual, finalized data — MMWR is the weekly publication. Swapping "weekly" and "annual" between the two is the standard distractor.

Subtopic: case definitions

Q: A case definition may be built from which types of criteria?
A) Clinical symptoms only
B) Laboratory values only
C) Clinical symptoms, laboratory values, and epidemiologic criteria
D) Epidemiologic criteria only, per CDC/CSTE policy
Answer: C
Rationale: Case definitions may include clinical symptoms, laboratory values, and epidemiologic criteria, such as exposure to a known or suspected case.

Q: What are the three classifications used to describe the strength of evidence for a case?
A) Confirmed, notifiable, and reportable
B) Suspected, probable, and confirmed
C) Sporadic, endemic, and epidemic
D) Primary, secondary, and tertiary
Answer: B
Rationale: Cases are classified by strength of evidence as suspected, probable, or confirmed.

Q: A local health department identifies a possible case that does not yet meet the formal case definition for a reportable disease because lab confirmation is pending. What is the correct action?
A) Wait until the case definition is formally met before taking any public health action
B) Take action to control the disease as soon as the problem is identified, even without enough information to meet the case definition
C) Refer the decision to the CDC before acting
D) Take no action unless the patient develops severe symptoms
Answer: B
Rationale: The chapter states plainly that action to control a disease should be taken as soon as a problem is identified, although there may not be enough information to meet the case definition. The case definition should not be used as the only criterion for taking public health action.
Trap: Waiting for laboratory confirmation before acting is the classic wrong answer — the case definition is a reporting/monitoring tool, not a gate on control action.

Topic: surveillance systems
Subtopic: the four types

Q: A physician's office sends a case report to the local health department after diagnosing a reportable illness. Which type of surveillance system does this describe?
A) Active
B) Passive
C) Sentinel
D) Special
Answer: B
Rationale: In a passive system, case reports are sent to local health departments by health care providers, or laboratory reports are sent to the local health department. Reports come in to the health department.

Q: Several schoolchildren become ill after eating lunch in the cafeteria. The health department nurse begins contacting local providers, names the suspected illness, and actively gathers data on existing cases to determine the scope of the problem. Which type of surveillance system does this describe?
A) Passive
B) Active
C) Sentinel
D) Syndromic
Answer: B
Rationale: This is the chapter's own example of an active surveillance system: the nurse, as a health department employee, begins a search for cases through contacts with local providers and agencies, names the disease, and gathers data to determine the magnitude of the problem.
Trap: A distractor may frame active surveillance as merely "faster" or "automated" reporting. Its defining feature is who initiates the search — the health department goes out looking, rather than waiting for reports to come in.

Q: Which statement correctly describes a sentinel surveillance system?
A) It only ever monitors a single named disease
B) A disease or an event may be the sentinel, or a population may be the sentinel
C) It is always passive in direction of effort
D) It requires automated real-time data systems
Answer: B
Rationale: The chapter states explicitly that a disease or event may be the sentinel, or a population may be the sentinel; a sample of health care providers or agencies is asked to report the problem.
Trap: Distractors typically offer only the disease half of this definition and omit the population half.

Q: A special surveillance system, such as a syndromic surveillance system, is best described as which of the following?
A) A system limited strictly to passive case reporting
B) A system developed for collecting particular types of data, which may combine active, passive, and/or sentinel elements
C) A system used only during declared bioterrorism events
D) A system that reports exclusively to WHO
Answer: B
Rationale: Special surveillance systems are developed for collecting particular types of data and may be a combination of active, passive, and/or sentinel systems; syndromic surveillance systems are the given example.

Q: Although all surveillance systems are important, which two is the public health nurse most likely to use?
A) Sentinel and special
B) Active and passive
C) Syndromic and sentinel
D) Special and active only
Answer: B
Rationale: The chapter states, "Although all of the systems are important, the public health nurse is most likely to use the active or passive systems."

Q: What is a stated weakness of a passive surveillance system?
A) It cannot be used for chronic disease
B) A disease outbreak may already be occurring before all reports are received by the state health department
C) It requires more staff than an active system
D) It cannot generate weekly reports
Answer: B
Rationale: Because a passive system has limits, a disease outbreak may be occurring before all reports are received by the state health department.

Topic: outbreak and endemic vocabulary
Subtopic: magnitude terms

Q: Which term describes the persistent, usual presence of a disease at a low to moderate number of cases, and provides the baseline against which increases are measured?
A) Sporadic
B) Endemic
C) Hyperendemic
D) Epidemic
Answer: B
Rationale: Endemic is a persistent (usual) presence with a low to moderate number of cases of the disease or event in a population, and provides the baseline.

Q: A disease is highly prevalent and commonly acquired early in life. Counterintuitively, its prevalence decreases as age increases. Which term describes this pattern?
A) Hyperendemic
B) Sporadic
C) Holoendemic
D) Epidemic
Answer: C
Rationale: Holoendemic describes a highly prevalent problem commonly acquired early in life, in which prevalence decreases as age increases — the reverse of what most conditions do.
Trap: This is stated as counterintuitive precisely because most health problems become more prevalent with age; holoendemic is the exception, not the rule.

Q: A population has a persistently high number of cholera cases well above the low-to-moderate baseline typical elsewhere. Which term applies?
A) Endemic
B) Hyperendemic
C) Holoendemic
D) Pandemic
Answer: B
Rationale: Hyperendemic describes a persistently (usually) high number of cases; the chapter's example is the high cholera incidence rate among Asian and Pacific Islanders.

Q: Occasional cases of a disease occur at irregular intervals with no consistent pattern. Which term applies?
A) Endemic
B) Epidemic
C) Sporadic
D) Mixed
Answer: C
Rationale: Sporadic describes an irregular pattern with occasional cases found at irregular intervals.

Q: The occurrence of a disease within an area clearly in excess of expected (endemic) levels for a given time period is often called which of the following?
A) The pandemic
B) The outbreak
C) The sentinel event
D) The cluster
Answer: B
Rationale: An epidemic is the occurrence of a disease clearly in excess of expected (endemic) levels for a given time period; this is often called the outbreak. A pandemic is an epidemic spread over several countries or continents.

Subtopic: outbreak patterns

Q: In an outbreak, all persons exposed become ill at the same time, during one incubation period. Which outbreak pattern does this describe?
A) Propagated
B) Point source
C) Intermittent source
D) Mixed
Answer: B
Rationale: A point source outbreak is one in which all persons exposed become ill at the same time, during one incubation period.
Trap: The discriminator to memorize is incubation periods, not severity or case count: point source = one incubation period, everyone at once; propagated = more than one incubation period, no common source.

Q: An outbreak has no common source and spreads gradually from person to person over more than one incubation period, as with tuberculosis. Which outbreak pattern does this describe?
A) Point source
B) Common source
C) Propagated
D) Intermittent or continuous source
Answer: C
Rationale: A propagated outbreak does not have a common source and spreads gradually from person to person over more than one incubation period; tuberculosis spreading from one person to another is the textbook example.

Q: A group is exposed to a single common noxious influence, such as the release of a toxic gas into a subway or water system. Which outbreak pattern does this describe?
A) Common source
B) Mixed
C) Propagated
D) Sentinel
Answer: A
Rationale: A common source outbreak is one in which a group is exposed to a common noxious influence; the textbook example is the release of ricin into a subway system and into a US water system.

Q: A restaurant chain's food poisoning cases appear over several days to weeks, traced to a shipment of contaminated green onions purchased at different times. Which outbreak pattern does this describe?
A) Point source
B) Intermittent or continuous source
C) Propagated
D) Mixed
Answer: B
Rationale: An intermittent or continuous source outbreak involves cases exposed over a period of days or weeks; the textbook example is food poisonings at a restaurant chain nationwide from contaminated green onions.

Q: A person becomes ill after a common source exposure, then has person-to-person contact with others and spreads the disease further, as occurs with influenza. Which outbreak pattern does this describe?
A) Propagated
B) Mixed
C) Point source
D) Common source
Answer: B
Rationale: A mixed outbreak occurs when a victim of a common source epidemic has person-to-person contact with others and spreads the disease, further propagating the health problem; influenza is the given example.
Trap: Mixed starts as common source, then becomes person-to-person — it is not the same as a purely propagated outbreak, which never has a common source at all.

Topic: agent classification
Subtopic: infectivity, pathogenicity, virulence

Q: Of all the people who become infected with an agent, the proportion who go on to develop clinical disease describes which concept?
A) Infectivity
B) Pathogenicity
C) Virulence
D) Communicability
Answer: B
Rationale: Pathogenicity is the proportion of infected people who develop the disease.
Trap: Swapping pathogenicity and virulence is the standard error — pathogenicity's denominator is "infected," virulence's denominator is "clinically ill."

Q: Of all the people with clinical disease, the proportion who become severely ill or die describes which concept?
A) Infectivity
B) Pathogenicity
C) Virulence
D) Incidence
Answer: C
Rationale: Virulence is the proportion of people with clinical disease who become severely ill or die.

Q: The capacity of an agent to enter a susceptible host and produce infection or disease describes which concept?
A) Infectivity
B) Pathogenicity
C) Virulence
D) Endemicity
Answer: A
Rationale: Infectivity is the capacity of an agent to enter a susceptible host and produce infection or disease — the first step in the ladder of enter, become sick, become severely sick.

Topic: bioterrorism
Subtopic: epidemiologic clues

Q: Which finding is a named epidemiologic clue that may signal a covert bioterrorism attack?
A) Death or illness among animals that precedes or accompanies illness or death in humans
B) A gradual seasonal rise in a common respiratory illness
C) A steady, predictable incidence of a well-known endemic disease
D) Illness limited to a single household during a normal flu season
Answer: A
Rationale: Death or illness among animals preceding or accompanying human illness or death is a named epidemiologic clue for covert bioterrorism.

Q: An investigation finds no illness among people who were not exposed to a building's shared ventilation system, but illness among those in proximity to the system. What does this pattern suggest?
A) A propagated outbreak of a common respiratory virus
B) A possible bioterrorism attack — this pattern is a named epidemiologic clue
C) A sentinel surveillance artifact with no clinical significance
D) A holoendemic disease pattern
Answer: B
Rationale: "No illness in people not exposed to common ventilation systems, but illness among those in proximity to the systems" is a named epidemiologic clue that may signal a covert bioterrorism attack.

Q: Which of the following are named epidemiologic clues that may signal a covert bioterrorism attack? Select all that apply.
A) Failure of a common disease to respond to usual therapy
B) A single case of disease caused by an uncommon agent
C) Simultaneous clusters of similar illness in noncontiguous areas
D) An endemic disease with unexplained increase in incidence
E) A predictable seasonal rise matching prior years' patterns
Answer: A, B, C, D
Rationale: Named clues include failure of a common disease to respond to usual therapy, a single case caused by an uncommon agent, simultaneous clusters of similar illness in noncontiguous areas, and an endemic disease with an unexplained increase in incidence.
Trap: A predictable pattern matching prior years is the opposite of a bioterrorism clue — the chapter notes that naturally occurring outbreaks tend to be predictable, while purposeful introduction of an agent tends to break that predictability.

Topic: outbreak investigation
Subtopic: the six-step process and foodborne definition

Q: Which of the following is the FIRST of the CDC's six steps for conducting an outbreak investigation?
A) Develop and evaluate a hypothesis
B) Confirm the existence of an outbreak
C) Estimate the number of cases
D) Institute control measures and communicate findings
Answer: B
Rationale: The six CDC steps, in order, are: confirm the existence of an outbreak; verify the diagnosis and/or define a case; estimate the number of cases; orient the data collected to person, place, and time; develop and evaluate a hypothesis; and institute control measures and communicate findings.

Q: Orienting collected outbreak data to person, place, and time occurs at which point in the CDC's six-step investigation process?
A) Before confirming the existence of an outbreak
B) After estimating the number of cases and before developing a hypothesis
C) After instituting control measures
D) It is not part of the six-step process
Answer: B
Rationale: The six-step order is: confirm the outbreak; verify the diagnosis/define a case; estimate the number of cases; orient data to person, place, and time; develop and evaluate a hypothesis; institute control measures and communicate findings.

Q: According to the CDC, how many people must become ill from the same contaminated food or drink for the event to be defined as a foodborne disease outbreak?
A) One or more
B) Two or more
C) Five or more
D) Ten or more, per state notifiable disease thresholds
Answer: B
Rationale: The CDC defines a foodborne disease outbreak as occurring when two or more people get the same illness after ingesting the same contaminated food or drink.

Q: A community health nurse is deciding whether an unusual cluster of gastrointestinal illness warrants investigation. According to the chapter, when should an event be investigated?
A) Only after laboratory confirmation of the causative agent
B) Any unusual increase in disease incidence or an unusual event in the community should be investigated
C) Only if the local health department receives five or more independent case reports
D) Only after the state epidemiologist authorizes the investigation
Answer: B
Rationale: The chapter states that any unusual increase in disease incidence (new cases) or an unusual event in the community should be investigated.
