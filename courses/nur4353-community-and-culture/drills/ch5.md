# Chapter 5 drills — Economics of US Health Care Delivery

Authored from `sources/Ch5_Fact_Bank.md` (Stanhope & Lancaster, 6th ed.,
printed pp. 78–102). Ch 5's own blueprint line is only three questions (WHO/
USDHHS/CDC roles, nurse practice acts — cross-referenced to Ch3/Ch4), but this
chapter is the definitional home for the payment-mechanism vocabulary
(managed care, HMO, PPO, Medicare, Medicaid, CHIP, prospective payment) that
the blueprint lists under the Ch3/Ch4 line. Coverage here is weighted toward
that vocabulary accordingly.

---

Topic: payment mechanisms
Subtopic: retrospective vs. prospective reimbursement (organizations)

Q: A home health agency is paid based on its cost per patient-day, with the rate determined only after services have already been delivered. Which reimbursement method is this?
A) Prospective payment system
B) Retrospective reimbursement
C) Capitation
D) Preferred provider arrangement
Answer: B
Rationale: Retrospective reimbursement is the traditional method of paying organizations: fees are set after services are delivered, based on cost or charges. It encourages cost shifting and shows little regard for cost.
Trap: The "per patient-day" detail sounds like a fixed rate, but what makes it retrospective is that the rate is not fixed until after the service is rendered.

Q: What is the central risk created by retrospective reimbursement of organizations?
A) Under-serving patients to stay within a fixed budget
B) Cost shifting — charging one payer more to cover losses incurred elsewhere
C) Denying care to patients who cannot pay a capitated fee
D) Restricting referrals through a gatekeeper
Answer: B
Rationale: Because retrospective reimbursement pays organizations on cost or charges after the fact, it encourages inflating prices and cost shifting rather than cost control.
Trap: Under-serving to stay in budget is the risk of prospective payment, the opposite method — an easy inversion.

Q: A hospital is paid a fixed amount per diagnosis category, determined in advance, regardless of the hospital's actual cost of care. Which method is this, and what risk does it carry?
A) Retrospective reimbursement; risk of cost shifting
B) Prospective payment system; risk of under-serving and compromised quality
C) Fee-for-service; risk of inflated billing
D) Capitation; risk of denied referrals
Answer: B
Rationale: Prospective payment systems set the reimbursement rate before care is delivered. Medicare's hospital PPS pays a fixed amount per diagnosis category (DRG) regardless of actual cost, which encourages staying within budget but risks under-serving patients and compromising quality.

Q: Which classification system is the basis of Medicare's prospective payment system for hospitals, and when was it established?
A) RBRVS, established 1991
B) DRGs, established 1983
C) CPT codes, established 1965
D) UCR schedules, established 1997
Answer: B
Rationale: Diagnosis-related groups (DRGs) classify hospital payment into set amounts per diagnosis category, established by the amended Social Security Act (PL 98-21) in 1983 — Medicare's prospective payment method for hospitals.
Trap: RBRVS is a real Medicare payment reform, but it applies to physician reimbursement (1989/1991), not to the hospital PPS.

Subtopic: paying practitioners (fee-for-service, preferred provider, capitation)

Q: Under fee-for-service payment, when is the rate for the service determined, and on what basis?
A) Before care is delivered, based on a negotiated discount
B) After the practitioner delivers the service, based on usual, customary, and reasonable (UCR) charges the practitioner sets
C) Before care is delivered, based on a preset per-member fee
D) After care is delivered, based on a diagnosis-related group
Answer: B
Rationale: Fee-for-service is the traditional method of paying practitioners: the practitioner sets the cost, delivers the service, and then bills a third-party payer based on UCR charges. It is retrospective in nature.

Q: A managed care organization pays a primary care practitioner a fixed amount per enrolled member each month, regardless of how many services that member actually uses. Which payment method is this?
A) Fee-for-service
B) Preferred provider arrangement
C) Capitation
D) Retrospective reimbursement
Answer: C
Rationale: Capitation pays a practitioner a preset, negotiated fee per member (per member per month), agreed before services are delivered, regardless of the volume of care used. It is typically used by managed care organizations.

Q: In a preferred provider arrangement, when is the practitioner's reimbursement rate set relative to care delivery?
A) After care is delivered, based on the practitioner's own charges
B) Before care is delivered, negotiated by the third-party payer, with reimbursement capped per patient
C) Before care is delivered, but only as a per-member-per-month fee
D) There is no set rate; payment is fully at the payer's discretion
Answer: B
Rationale: Preferred provider payment, like prospective payment, has the third-party payer negotiate the rate before care is delivered and cap reimbursement per patient.

Q: Which statements correctly pair a practitioner payment method with when its rate is set? Select all that apply.
A) Fee-for-service — rate set after the service, like retrospective reimbursement
B) Preferred provider — rate set before the service, like prospective reimbursement
C) Capitation — rate set before the service is delivered
D) Capitation — rate set after utilization is tallied for the month
E) Fee-for-service — rate negotiated in advance by the payer
Answer: A, B, C
Rationale: Fee-for-service parallels retrospective reimbursement (rate set after care). Preferred provider and capitation both parallel prospective reimbursement (rate set before care) — preferred provider as a negotiated, capped rate, and capitation as a preset per-member-per-month fee paid regardless of use.
Trap: The single most testable contrast in this chapter is the "when is the rate set" axis: after = fee-for-service/retrospective (incentive to do more); before = preferred provider/capitation/prospective (incentive to do less, stay in budget).

Q: The resource-based relative value scale (RBRVS) was established by which legislation, and when did Medicare adopt it?
A) The Balanced Budget Act of 1997; adopted 1998
B) The Omnibus Reconciliation Act of 1989; adopted by Medicare in 1991
C) The Social Security Act of 1965; adopted 1966
D) The amended Social Security Act (PL 98-21); adopted 1983
Answer: B
Rationale: RBRVS was established by the Omnibus Reconciliation Act of 1989 and adopted by Medicare in 1991; it reimburses physicians based on the service provided and the resources required.
Trap: PL 98-21 and 1983 belong to DRGs, not RBRVS — the two prospective-payment reforms are easy to cross-assign.

Q: The Balanced Budget Act of 1997 changed reimbursement for nurse practitioners and clinical nurse specialists in what way?
A) It granted them full parity with physician rates under Medicare Part A
B) It granted them third-party reimbursement under Medicare Part B at 85% of physician rates for the same service
C) It capitated all NP/CNS services under Medicaid
D) It excluded NPs and CNSs from Medicare reimbursement entirely
Answer: B
Rationale: Effective January 1, 1998, the Balanced Budget Act of 1997 granted NPs and CNSs third-party reimbursement for Medicare Part B services at 85% of physician rates for the same service.
Trap: Part B (medical/provider services), not Part A (hospital insurance), is where this reimbursement applies.

Topic: public insurance programs
Subtopic: Medicare vs. Medicaid

Q: A 68-year-old with no history of disability is asking who pays for her hospitalization. Which federal program covers her, and under what title of the Social Security Act?
A) Medicaid, Title XIX
B) Medicare, Title XVIII
C) CHIP, Balanced Budget Act of 1997
D) Medicaid, Title XVIII
Answer: B
Rationale: Medicare (Title XVIII, Social Security Act of 1965) is federal insurance for people 65 and older, the permanently disabled, and those with ALS or end-stage renal disease (ESRD). Age alone, without a means test, qualifies her.

Q: A low-income family with dependent children needs coverage but no member is 65 or disabled. Which program applies, and what determines eligibility?
A) Medicare, based on age
B) Medicaid, based on means testing (income and assets)
C) CHIP, based on Social Security Act Title XVIII
D) Medicare Part C, based on income
Answer: B
Rationale: Medicaid (Title XIX, Social Security Act of 1965) is joint federal/state social insurance for the poor, aged, blind, disabled, and families with dependent children, with eligibility determined by means testing.
Trap: The chapter's own trap: if the stem says "65-year-old," think Medicare; if it says "low-income family" or "needy," think Medicaid. Age/disability-based and means-tested are not interchangeable qualifying criteria.

Q: Which statements correctly describe Medicare? Select all that apply.
A) It is federal insurance administered by CMS
B) Eligibility is based on age or disability, not income
C) It is a joint federal/state program requiring matching state funds
D) Part A is financed through the Medicare Trust Fund and a dedicated payroll tax
E) Part B is financed through beneficiary premiums and general revenue
Answer: A, B, D, E
Rationale: Medicare is a federal program (not joint federal/state) administered by CMS, based on age/disability rather than income. Part A draws on the Medicare Trust Fund and dedicated payroll tax; Part B draws on beneficiary premiums plus general revenue.
Trap: "Joint federal/state" and "matching funds" describe Medicaid, not Medicare — swapping this one feature is the most common way this pair gets inverted.

Q: Which statements correctly describe Medicaid? Select all that apply.
A) It is administered jointly by the federal government and the states, with matching funds
B) Eligibility is means-tested (income/assets), not based on age
C) It is classified as insurance rather than social insurance
D) It pays for more than half of all long-term care spending in the US
E) It is financed by federal and state general revenues
Answer: A, B, D, E
Rationale: Medicaid is a joint federal/state social insurance program financed by federal and state general revenues, with means-tested eligibility, and it pays for more than half of all long-term care spending.
Trap: Medicaid is classified as social insurance (assistance), not straight "insurance" — that classification belongs to Medicare.

Subtopic: Medicare's four parts

Q: Which part of Medicare covers inpatient hospital stays, home health, hospice, and limited skilled nursing?
A) Part A
B) Part B
C) Part C
D) Part D
Answer: A
Rationale: Medicare Part A is hospital insurance, covering inpatient hospital care, home health, hospice, and limited skilled nursing.

Q: Which part of Medicare covers "medically necessary" outpatient provider services, diagnostics, and physiotherapy?
A) Part A
B) Part B
C) Part C
D) Part D
Answer: B
Rationale: Medicare Part B is medical insurance, covering medically necessary provider services, outpatient care, home health, diagnostics, and physiotherapy.

Q: Match each Medicare part to what it covers. Select all correct pairings.
A) Part A — hospital insurance (inpatient, home health, hospice, limited skilled nursing)
B) Part B — medical insurance (outpatient/provider services, diagnostics)
C) Part C — Medicare Advantage plans, since 1999
D) Part D — prescription drug coverage, added 2006
E) Part D — hospital insurance financed by payroll tax
Answer: A, B, C, D
Rationale: Part A is hospital insurance; Part B is medical insurance; Part C is Medicare Advantage (since 1999); Part D is prescription drug coverage (2006). Option E misassigns hospital insurance and payroll-tax financing, which belong to Part A.

Subtopic: CHIP

Q: The Children's Health Insurance Program (CHIP) was created by which legislation, and whom does it cover?
A) The Social Security Act of 1965; children who qualify for Medicaid
B) The Balanced Budget Act of 1997; children in low-income families who earn too much for Medicaid but cannot afford private insurance
C) The Affordable Care Act of 2010; all uninsured children regardless of income
D) The Omnibus Reconciliation Act of 1989; children of Medicare beneficiaries
Answer: B
Rationale: CHIP was created by the Balanced Budget Act of 1997 to cover children in low-income families who earn too much to qualify for Medicaid but cannot afford private insurance.

Q: A nursing student sees "CHIP" referenced in both a community health economics context and an earlier chapter on the community-as-client model. Which statement correctly distinguishes the two uses?
A) They refer to the same program under two different names
B) In this chapter, CHIP is the Children's Health Insurance Program created by the Balanced Budget Act of 1997; elsewhere, CHIP refers to the Community Health Improvement Process, an unrelated planning framework
C) CHIP is always an abbreviation for the Community Health Improvement Process, and "Children's Health Insurance Program" is the outdated name
D) The Children's Health Insurance Program was renamed the Community Health Improvement Process under the ACA
Answer: B
Rationale: This chapter's CHIP is the Children's Health Insurance Program (Balanced Budget Act of 1997), covering children whose families earn too much for Medicaid but not enough to afford private insurance. This is a distinct term from the Community Health Improvement Process (Ch1), which is a planning framework, not an insurance program.
Trap: The identical acronym across chapters is a deliberate trap — do not assume shared meaning just because the letters match.

Topic: managed care
Subtopic: HMO vs. PPO

Q: A managed care enrollee must select a primary care provider who authorizes referrals to specialists, and the practice is paid a preset amount per enrolled member. Which model is this?
A) Preferred provider organization (PPO)
B) Health maintenance organization (HMO)
C) Fee-for-service indemnity plan
D) Prospective payment system
Answer: B
Rationale: HMOs are prepaid plans in which enrollees use a defined network and typically need a primary care gatekeeper for referrals; they emphasize prevention and are often paid via capitation.

Q: An enrollee can see any provider in a network of "preferred providers" who accept negotiated, discounted rates, and can go out-of-network at a higher cost, without needing a referral. Which model is this?
A) Health maintenance organization (HMO)
B) Preferred provider organization (PPO)
C) Capitation-only plan
D) Diagnosis-related group plan
Answer: B
Rationale: A PPO is a network of preferred providers who accept negotiated discounted rates; enrollees may go out of network at higher cost, and there is no strict gatekeeper.

Q: Which of the following correctly distinguishes HMOs from PPOs? Select all that apply.
A) HMOs typically require a primary care gatekeeper for referrals; PPOs do not
B) HMOs are commonly paid via capitation; PPOs rely on negotiated discounted rates
C) PPOs offer more provider choice and flexibility than HMOs, generally at higher cost
D) HMOs allow unrestricted out-of-network care at the same cost as in-network care
E) Both are forms of managed care
Answer: A, B, C, E
Rationale: HMOs use a gatekeeper model with capitated payment and the tightest network; PPOs offer looser networks with negotiated discounts, more choice, and higher cost, but no strict gatekeeper. Both fall under managed care.
Trap: Unrestricted out-of-network access at no added cost describes neither model — even PPOs charge more for out-of-network care.

Q: Managed care is built on the concept of "managed competition" and negotiates with providers on behalf of which unit of enrollees?
A) Diagnosis-related groups
B) Covered lives
C) Capitated households
D) Preferred beneficiaries
Answer: B
Rationale: Managed care integrates the financing and delivery of care and negotiates with hospitals and providers for care to its enrollees, referred to as "covered lives," based on managed competition (introduced in the late 1980s–early 1990s).

Topic: cost drivers
Subtopic: factors driving health care costs

Q: Which factors does the chapter name as drivers of rising health care costs? Select all that apply.
A) Demographics, including the aging population
B) Technology and the intensity of services provided per case
C) Chronic illness
D) Prescription drugs and inflation
E) Declining rates of health insurance coverage
Answer: A, B, C, D
Rationale: The named cost drivers are demographics (population growth and the aging population), technology and intensity, chronic illness, and prescription drugs/inflation.
Trap: Declining coverage rates are a disparities/access issue, not one of the chapter's named cost drivers.

Q: The chapter specifically calls out which demographic trend, tied to a surge in Medicare enrollment, as a cost driver?
A) Declining birth rates
B) The aging population (baby boomers)
C) Rural-to-urban migration
D) Increasing life expectancy among the uninsured
Answer: B
Rationale: The blueprint calls out population growth and the aging population specifically — baby boomers aging into eligibility drive surging Medicare enrollment and cost.

Topic: access and disparities
Subtopic: the uninsured and the working poor

Q: How did the number of uninsured Americans trend between 2013 and 2016, and to what was the change attributed?
A) It rose from about 28 million to about 44 million due to rising premiums
B) It fell from about 44–48 million to about 27.6–28.1 million, following the ACA and Medicaid expansion
C) It remained flat at approximately 30 million throughout the period
D) It fell to near zero following the Balanced Budget Act of 1997
Answer: B
Rationale: The uninsured population fell from roughly 44–48 million in 2013 to roughly 27.6–28.1 million in 2016, after the ACA (PL 111-148, 2010) and Medicaid expansion.

Q: More than 80% of the uninsured belong to which group, and why do they remain uninsured?
A) Retired seniors who are not yet enrolled in Medicare
B) Working families — the "working poor" — who earn too much for Medicaid but cannot afford private coverage
C) Undocumented immigrants ineligible for any federal program
D) College students who opted out of parental coverage
Answer: B
Rationale: More than 80% of the uninsured were in working families (income below 400% of poverty), most with one or more full-time workers — the "working poor," who earn too much to qualify for Medicaid but cannot afford private insurance.
Trap: This is the same earn-too-much-for-Medicaid gap that CHIP is designed to close for children — the adult working-poor population has no equivalent program named in this chapter.
