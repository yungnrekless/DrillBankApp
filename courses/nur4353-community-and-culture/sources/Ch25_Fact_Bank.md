## §1 Poverty: Definition and Description (p. 441–443)

**The two federal terms, which is the single most testable pair in this section:**

| | Issued by | Used for |
|---|---|---|
| **Poverty Thresholds** | US Bureau of the Census | Primarily **statistical purposes** |
| **Federal Poverty Guidelines** | USDHHS, annually | Determining whether a person or family is **financially eligible** for assistance: marketplace insurance savings, Medicaid, CHIP |

Guidelines are updated annually against the **CPI**. Census also reports the **Supplemental Poverty Measure (SPM)**, whose thresholds vary by geographic area and homeownership status and can be higher than the official measure.

**Table 25.1 — 2020 Poverty Guidelines, 48 contiguous states + DC:**

| Family size | Guideline |
|---|---|
| 1 | $12,760 |
| 2 | $17,240 |
| 3 | $21,720 |
| 4 | $26,200 |
| 5 | $30,680 |
| 6 | $35,160 |
| 7 | $39,640 |
| 8 | $44,120 |
| 8 or more | Add **$5,600** per person |

> ⚠️ **Internal inconsistency in the textbook.** The table's own increments are **$4,480** per person at every step from 1 through 8. The "$5,600 per person" line applies only to units of 8 or more and appears to be an error carried from the Alaska schedule. **Do not state "$5,600 per additional person" as a general rule** (guide v1 does this). Safest phrasing: "$12,760 for 1 person, $26,200 for a family of 4; the guideline is higher for Alaska and Hawaii."

**Poverty statistics:**
- **2018 official poverty rate: 11.8%.** The **fourth consecutive annual decline**. Expected to rise beginning 2020 due to COVID-19.
- Children under 18: **16.2%**. Nearly **1 in 6** children lived in poverty in 2018, approximately **11.9 million** children. The youngest children are the poorest. Nearly **73% of poor children in America are children of color.**
- Adults 65+: **9.7%**. Under the official measure in 2017, 4.7 million adults 65+ lived in poverty. Rate **increases with age** and is higher among women, African Americans, and Hispanics.
- Women 2018: **12.9%** (down from 13.6% in 2017) vs. men **10.6%**. Full-time year-round employed women earn **$0.82** per $1.00 a man earns.
- Median 2018 earnings, full-time: men $55,291, women $45,097.
- More than 12.5 million children lived in food-insecure households in 2017; Black and Hispanic households with food-insecure children nearly **twice** the rate of White households.
- Children are the poorest age group in America (Children's Defense Fund).

**Named causes of the growing number of poor persons:** decreased earnings; increased unemployment; changes in retirement benefits when companies move, close, or file bankruptcy; changes in the labor force; increase in female-headed households; inadequate education and job skills; inadequate antipoverty programs and welfare benefits; weak enforcement of child support statutes; dwindling Social Security payments to children; increased numbers of children born to single women; outsourcing of jobs and COVID-19 furloughs, reduced salaries, and job loss; trade deficits, debt, and involvement in wars.

**Types of poverty.** The chapter states: "The terms **persistent poverty** and **neighborhood poverty** are used to describe types of poverty," then adds **multigenerational poverty**. **Crisis poverty** appears only in the homelessness section as a synonym for transitional homelessness. Guide v1 presents all four as a "four poverty types" set, which is a defensible synthesis but is not how the book frames it. If a question asks what terms the book uses for types of poverty, the anchored answer is persistent and neighborhood, plus multigenerational.

**Poverty and Health: Effects Across the Life Span (p. 443).** Poverty directly results in: higher rates of chronic illness; higher infant morbidity and mortality; shorter life expectancy; more complex health problems; more significant complications and physical limitations from higher incidence of chronic disease (asthma, diabetes, hypertension).

**Box 25.1 — Effects of Poverty on the Health of Children:** higher rates of prematurity, low birth weight, and birth defects; higher infant mortality; increased chronic disease; increased traumatic death and injury; nutritional deficits; growth restriction and developmental delays; iron-deficiency anemia; elevated blood lead levels; infections; increased risk for homelessness; decreased opportunities for education, income, and occupation.

## §2 Homelessness (p. 444–448)

**HUD definition:** a homeless person is "a person who lacks a fixed, regular, and adequate nighttime residence."

**Three categories:**
| Type | Definition |
|---|---|
| **Chronic** | Has a disability AND homeless at least 4 times in 3 years (12 months minimum) OR continuously homeless for 1 year. |
| **Transitional** | Also called **crisis poverty**. Typically caused by a catastrophic event leading to a short shelter stay, eventually transitioning to permanent housing. **Accounts for the majority of homelessness over time.** |
| **Episodic** | Moves in and out of homelessness. Marked by hardship and struggle including chronic unemployment. Younger, often with mental health and substance abuse problems. |

**Point-in-time statistics (single night, 2019):**
- Approximately **568,000** people experienced homelessness in the US.
- **63% sheltered** (emergency shelters or transitional housing), **37% unsheltered** (street, abandoned buildings, other places not suitable for human habitation).
- **27% were under age 25**; **8% were veterans**.
- African Americans **remain consistently overrepresented**.
- Homelessness increased 3% in West Coast states 2018–2019, likely due to moderate climate.
- In several states (Maine, Massachusetts, Nebraska, New York, North Dakota) at least 95% of the homeless population was sheltered.

**Why counts undercount (National Coalition for the Homeless, 2018):** HUD's definition excludes families doubled up with friends or family; others live in motels, vehicles, or hidden locations and never interact with service agencies or volunteers conducting counts; some refuse to be interviewed. Rural homelessness is less apparent because people are hidden from sight, sometimes in the woods in tents or campers, in barns or ice sheds, or on a friend's couch.

**Effects of Homelessness on Health (p. 446) — not in guide v1.** Named health problems with their stated causes:
- Hypothermia and heat-related illness (living conditions)
- Infestations and poor skin integrity
- Respiratory conditions or **TB due to crowded sleeping conditions**
- Peripheral vascular disease and hypertension **because they may be on their feet for long hours daily**
- Diabetes and nutritional deficits **because shelter and fast food is high in sodium**
- HIV/AIDS
- Trauma: gunshot or stab wounds, head trauma, suicide attempts, fractures, plus minor trauma (bruises, abrasions, concussions, sprains, puncture wounds, eye injuries, cellulitis)
- Conditions needing regular uninterrupted treatment (TB, HIV/AIDS) are extremely difficult to treat or control without adequate housing.

**Barriers to care named:** not knowing where to get treated; lack of transportation; lack of identification; embarrassment; nervousness or inability to fill out forms and answer questions; self-consciousness about appearance and hygiene. Care is usually crisis-oriented and sought in emergency departments. **Health promotion is a luxury, not part of daily life.**

**Homelessness and At-Risk Populations (p. 447) — not in guide v1:**
- **Youth:** an estimated **40% of unaccompanied homeless youth are LGBTQ**. Homeless adolescents show earlier onset of sexual activity, poorer health status, decreased access to care, and high risk for AIDS and hepatitis B. Many exchange sex for food, clothing, and shelter, putting girls at high risk for unintended pregnancy. Histories of runaway behavior, physical abuse, and sexual abuse are common.
- **Pregnant women:** at high risk for complex health problems. Younger, unmarried, uninsured, less educated, less likely to initiate and sustain breastfeeding, fewer prenatal and well-child visits. Higher rates of STIs, addiction, poorer nutritional status, more kidney and bladder infections, poorer birth outcomes (lower birth weight, preterm labor).
- **Veterans:** on a single night in January 2019, **37,085 veterans** were homeless; only 2% were part of a family; 90.3% men, 8.9% women. Numbers **dropped nearly 50% since 2009**, credited to HUD-VASH and SSVF and coordinated VA/HUD effort. **About 50% of homeless veterans have serious mental illness and 70% have substance use issues.** Majority are unmarried and may be socially isolated.
- **Older adults:** the most vulnerable of the impoverished older-adult population. Long-standing poverty, fewer supportive relationships, often homeless as a result of catastrophic events.
- **One in five homeless persons has a severe mental illness**, with similar rates of chronic substance abuse. Both are often associated with chronic homelessness.

## §3 Teen Pregnancy (p. 448–455) — thin in guide v1

**Four social determinants of health that influence teen pregnancy rates (Danawi et al., 2016):** (1) income, (2) education, (3) social support networks, (4) living environment.

**Framing:** teens who become pregnant are often caught in a cycle of poverty, school failure, and limited life options. There is neither a uniform reason teens become pregnant nor a universally acceptable solution. Even under ideal circumstances, a teen mother must circumvent her own developmental tasks to raise her child.

**Statistics:**
- 2019 YRBS: **27.4%** of surveyed high school students were sexually active.
- **77% of females and 91% of males** reported contraceptive use at first intercourse (Guttmacher, 2019).
- The **Youth Risk Behavior Surveillance System (YRBS)** monitors six categories of health-risk behaviors, one of which is sexual behaviors related to unintended pregnancy and STDs including HIV.

**Contraception:**
- **Dual protection** = hormonal contraceptive, preferably **LARC**, plus a **condom**. Current recommendation for greatest protection against pregnancy and STI.
- **ACOG (2012)** strongly recommended encouraging adolescents to use LARC methods (IUDs and contraceptive implants), which have the highest rates of continuation and prevention of pregnancy, rapid repeat pregnancy, and abortion.

**Risk factors named:** history of sexual victimization; family dysfunction; substance use; failure to use birth control; peer pressure; belief that a baby will bring joy or provide love the family has not provided.

**Special Issues in Caring for the Pregnant Teen (p. 452):**
- **"Pregnant teenagers are considered high-risk obstetrical patients."** Complications can result from poverty, late entry into prenatal care, sporadic prenatal care, and limited self-care knowledge.
- Named issue areas: **violence, initiation of prenatal care, low-birth-weight infants and preterm delivery, nutrition, infant care, repeat pregnancy, and schooling/educational needs.**
- **Violence:** teens are more likely than adult women to experience violence during pregnancy. Perpetrators include partners, family members, and acquaintances. Associated with increased substance abuse risk, poor prenatal compliance, and poor birth outcome. **The nurse must ask about violence at each visit**; frequent routine assessment is more revealing than a single inquiry at the first prenatal visit. Violence beginning in pregnancy may continue for several years with increasing severity.
- **Prenatal care:** teens are more likely to get no prenatal care or to begin later than older counterparts. Misunderstandings often stem from lack of basic anatomy and physiology knowledge (worked example: telling a teen near term that the baby's head is down alarms a young woman who imagines the baby could pop out at any time).
- **Nutrition:** the growing teen must increase caloric nutrients to meet her own growth needs as well as the pregnancy.
- Nurse should know about abortion and adoption services and refer appropriately. Pregnancy counseling should explore strengths and weaknesses, education and career plans, family finances, and personal values. A **follow-up visit is usually more productive** than counseling at the time of initial pregnancy testing results.

## §4 Mental Illness (p. 455–459) — thin in guide v1

**Mental health and illness are a continuum.**
- **Mental health:** being able to engage in productive activities, have fulfilling relationships, adapt to change, and cope with adversity. An integral part of personal well-being, family and interpersonal relationships, and contribution to community.
- **Mental disorders:** conditions characterized by alterations in thinking, mood, or behavior associated with distress or impaired functioning.
- **Mental illness:** collectively, all diagnosable mental disorders.
- **Healthy People 2030:** about **half of all people in the US will be diagnosed with a mental illness at some point in their lifetime.**

**AMI vs. SMI — the distinction guide v1 named but did not define:**

| | Definition | 2018 count |
|---|---|---|
| **AMI** | Any mental, behavioral, or emotional disorder in the past year meeting **DSM-5** criteria | 47.6 million adults 18+ |
| **SMI** | Over 18, within the last year, a diagnosable mental/behavioral/emotional disorder causing **serious functional impairment that substantially interferes with or limits one or more major life activities** | 11.4 million adults |
| **Serious emotional disturbance** | The under-18 parallel to SMI; limitation is ability to function in family, school, or community activities | — |

Formal diagnosis depends on a **reduction in the person's ability to function** as a result of the disorder.

**Co-occurrence (2018, SAMHSA):** 9.2 million adults 18+ had both AMI and at least one SUD; 3.2 million had co-occurring SMI and SUD; approximately 358,000 adolescents had a SUD and a major depressive episode.

**Global:** WHO reports the combined global burden of mental health, substance abuse, and neurological disease at **14%**. WHO launched **mhGAP** (mental health global action program). WHO poster: "No Health Without Mental Health."

**Deinstitutionalization (p. 456):**
- **What it was:** moving many people from state psychiatric hospitals to communities.
- **Main reason:** the **cost** of institutional care. Other influences: discovery of **psychotropic medications** and **civil rights activism**.
- **Goal:** improve quality of life by providing services in the communities where people lived rather than in large institutions.
- **The failure mode, stated directly:** community-based services were **not always in place** when people were released, and continuity of care became a problem. Families were not prepared for the treatment responsibilities they assumed, and few mental health systems offered them education and support programs. Many were discharged to family, nursing homes, apartments, rooming houses, single-room-occupancy hotels with little or no supervision; **some were placed in jails and prisons.** Staff in nursing homes and personal care settings often lacked the skills to treat persons with mental disorders and education programs were seldom available.
- **Quotable line:** "Deinstitutionalization was noble in conception yet bankrupt in implementation."
- **Community mental health centers** were the intended destination for this care.
- **ADA (1990)** mandated that individuals with mental and physical disabilities not be discriminated against and be brought into the mainstream through access to employment and public services.

**At-Risk Populations (p. 456–459):**
- **Children and adolescents:** at risk for disruption of normal development by biological, environmental, and psychosocial factors. Environmental factors named: crowded living conditions, violence, separation from parents, lack of consistent caregivers. Exposure to community violence relates to significant stress and depression. Depression, anxiety, and attention deficit disorders are often diagnosed; intellectual disabilities, Down syndrome, and autism are examples of chronic disorders. Community assessment should cover schools, homes, day-care centers, churches, and organizations running age-specific programs.
- **Suicide** was the **second leading cause of death for youth ages 10 to 24**. Males die by suicide about four times as often as females, yet females are more likely to have suicidal thoughts. HP2030 has objectives on reducing adolescent suicide attempts and screening for depression.
- **Low-income and minority groups** face increased risk largely because of lack of access to services.
- **Stigma:** people with mental illness are often identified by the illness ("a schizophrenic") rather than as a person with the illness. Onset of schizophrenia often coincides with finishing schooling and starting a career, driving many into underemployment, poverty, and lack of access to health services, housing, and social supports.

## §5 Levels of Prevention and the Nurse (p. 459–460)

**The chapter states plainly** that it is difficult to separate services for these high-risk groups into three levels, because interventions can be assigned to more than one. **Affordable housing** may qualify as primary prevention but could also be an important secondary or tertiary intervention. Expect a question that tests this ambiguity rather than clean sorting.

**Primary:** affordable housing, housing subsidies, effective job-training programs, employer incentives, preventive health care services, multisystem case management, birth control services, safe-sex education, needle exchange programs, parent education, counseling programs. Worked example: providing education about **stress reduction techniques to seniors attending a health fair** (possibly virtual).

**Secondary:** aimed at reducing the prevalence or pathological nature of a condition. Involves early diagnosis, prompt treatment, and limitation of disability. Worked example: **targeting persons on the verge of becoming high risk because of the threat of homelessness, as well as those who are newly homeless.**

**Tertiary:** reduces the disability that has already resulted, restoring function where possible. Worked example: **helping enhance self-esteem in those already affected.**

**LEVELS OF PREVENTION box — Related to Community Mental Health:**
- Primary: educate populations about mental health issues; teach stress reduction techniques; support and provide prenatal education; provide support to caregivers.

## Source conflicts and traps

- ⚠️ **The $5,600 figure.** See Table 25.1 above. The book's own increments are $4,480; $5,600 applies only at 8 or more. Guide v1 generalizes this incorrectly.
- ⚠️ **Child poverty rate vs. Chapter 23.** Ch. 25 says 16.2% for children under 18 in 2018. Ch. 23 says 17% of children 0–17 in 2017. Different years; both appear in the assigned reading.
- **Crisis poverty is not framed as a "type of poverty" in the book.** It appears only as a synonym for transitional homelessness. The book's named types of poverty are persistent, neighborhood, and multigenerational.
- **Multigenerational poverty has three named populations**, not two: African Americans in the South, Hispanics along the Mexico border, **and American Indians in states with multiple reservations.** Guide v1's Fig. 4 lists only the first two while its drill page lists all three.
- **Chronic homelessness requires BOTH a disability AND the time criterion.** A scenario with the time criterion but no disability is the distractor.
- **Transitional accounts for the majority of homelessness**, not chronic. Easy to invert.
- **Threshold vs. guideline:** if the question involves qualifying for Medicaid or CHIP, the answer is the guideline (USDHHS). If it involves statistics, the answer is the threshold (Census).
