## §1 Definitions and the three core functions (p. 3)

**Public health** — IOM (1988): *"what we, as a society, do collectively to assure the conditions in which people can be healthy."* The **mission**: *"to generate organized community efforts to address the public interest in health by applying scientific and technical knowledge to prevent disease and promote health."*

**The three core public health functions (IOM, 1988) — the single most testable triad in the chapter:**

| Function | What it means |
|---|---|
| **Assessment** | Systematically collecting data on the population, monitoring the population's health status, making information available about the community's health. |
| **Policy development** | Providing leadership in developing policies that support population health, using the scientific knowledge base to make policy decisions. |
| **Assurance** | Ensuring essential community-oriented health services are available (including personal health services for those who otherwise wouldn't get them); ensuring a competent public health and health care workforce. |

> **Memory hook:** *Assess → Plan (policy) → Assure.* These three functions map onto the nursing process and recur across the whole book. Assessment = collect/monitor data. Policy development = lead/plan. Assurance = make sure services and workforce exist.

## §2 The Ten Essential Public Health Services (p. 3–5)

The essential services implement the three core functions. The "How To" box lists how a public health nurse participates in each:

1. **Monitor** health status to identify community health problems.
2. **Diagnose and investigate** health problems and health hazards.
3. **Inform, educate, and empower** people about health issues.
4. **Mobilize** community partnerships to identify and solve health problems.
5. **Develop policies** and plans that support individual and community health efforts.
6. **Enforce laws and regulations** that protect health and ensure safety.
7. **Link people** to needed personal health services and assure care that is otherwise unavailable.
8. **Assure a competent** public health and personal health care workforce.
9. **Evaluate** effectiveness, accessibility, and quality of health services.
10. **Research** for new insights and innovative solutions to health problems.

> **Which service maps to which function?** Assessment ≈ services 1, 2, 10. Policy development ≈ 3, 4, 5. Assurance ≈ 6, 7, 8, 9.

**Ten Great Public Health Achievements of the 20th Century (CDC, 1999):** immunizations, motor-vehicle safety, workplace safety, control of infectious diseases, safer/healthier foods, healthier mothers and babies, family planning, drinking-water fluoridation, tobacco recognized as a health hazard, and declines in death from heart disease and stroke.

## §3 Levels of prevention — with the book's public-health-nursing examples (p. 10)

This table is the highest-yield item in the chapter for the blueprint. **Learn the examples, because EAQ prevention questions pin each distractor to a different level.**

| Level | Definition (as given) | Book's example |
|---|---|---|
| **Primary prevention** | General and specific measures in a population to **promote health and prevent the development of disease** (reduces **incidence**); includes specific measures to prevent disease in those predisposed. | The PHN develops a health-education program for school-age children about the **effects of smoking**. |
| **Secondary prevention** | Stopping the progress of disease by **early detection and treatment**, reducing **prevalence and chronicity**. | The PHN develops **toxin screenings for migrant workers** exposed to pesticides and refers those who screen positive for treatment. |
| **Tertiary prevention** | Stopping deterioration, relapse, disability, or dependency through **anticipatory nursing and medical care** (rehabilitation). | The PHN mobilizes a **community coalition to develop a Health Maintenance and Promotion Center** in a neighborhood with high chronic illness. |

> **Trap to watch:** *Screening* is the classic **secondary** cue (early detection). *Education / immunization / promotion before disease* is **primary**. *Rehabilitation / preventing relapse / limiting disability* is **tertiary**. The migrant-worker screening example is secondary even though it happens in a population — the level is set by what the nurse is doing (early detection), not the setting.

## §4 Population health and the community (p. 7–9)

- **Population** = a collection of individuals sharing one or more personal or environmental characteristics.
- **Aggregate / subpopulation** = a subgroup with a common characteristic. A whole community is made up of multiple, overlapping subpopulations (e.g., adolescents at risk for unplanned pregnancy overlap with females 15–24).
- **Social determinants of health** and public policy shape population health. The IOM's "shorter lives, poorer health" report attributed the US health disadvantage to inadequate health care, unhealthy behaviors, adverse economic and social conditions, environmental factors, and public policies/social values.
- Historically, the largest gains in population health came from **public health efforts** (sanitation, safe water, sewage, food safety), not medical care. Average US life span rose from **47 (1900) to 78.6 (2017)**, roughly a 60% increase, driven by sanitation, clean water, safer workplaces, food/drug safety, childhood immunization, and better nutrition/hygiene/housing.

## §5 Community-based vs. community-oriented practice (Box 1.5, p. 13) — a confusable pair

| | Community-**oriented** nursing practice | Community-**based** nursing practice |
|---|---|---|
| **Unit of focus** | The **community / population** | **Individuals and families** where they live, work, attend school |
| **Goal** | Prevent disease/disability, promote and protect health, create conditions for health via community diagnosis, surveillance, and monitoring | Acute and chronic care; comprehensive, coordinated, continuous services |
| **Who does it** | Generalist or specialist public health / community health nurse | Generalist or specialist (maternal/infant, pediatric, adult, psych/mental health) |

> **The distinction that gets tested:** *oriented* = population as the unit of care (population-focused); *based* = a **setting** in the community where individual/family care happens. "Based" is about **where**; "oriented" is about **whom** (the whole population).

## §6 Eight principles of public health nursing (p. 12) — testable list

1. The **unit of care is the population** (not the individual).
2. The primary obligation is to achieve the **greatest good for the greatest number** (utilitarian framing).
3. PHNs work **with the population as an equal partner**.
4. **Primary prevention is the priority** in selecting activities.
5. Select strategies that create healthy **environmental, social, and economic** conditions.
6. There is an obligation to **actively reach out** to all who might benefit.
7. **Optimal use of available resources** to assure the best overall improvement.
8. **Collaboration** with a variety of professions, organizations, and entities.

> Items 1, 2, and 4 are the ones most likely to anchor a question. If a stem asks "what is the priority intervention for a public health nurse," the answer defaults to the **primary-prevention** option unless the scenario clearly describes an existing disease.
