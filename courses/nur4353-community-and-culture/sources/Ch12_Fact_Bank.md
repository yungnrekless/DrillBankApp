## §1 HIV natural history (p. 218) — three stages and the window period

**HIV is a virus that attacks the immune system.** No cure, but controllable with proper care. Three stages:

1. **Primary infection** (within a month of contracting the virus): a **mononucleosis-like syndrome** lasting a few weeks; CD4 count drops briefly when the virus is most plentiful; often unrecognized. Flu-like symptoms (fever, chills, rash, night sweats, muscle aches, sore throat, fatigue, swollen lymph nodes, mouth ulcers) may appear 2 to 4 weeks after infection. **An antibody test at this stage is usually negative.**
2. **Clinical latency:** a period with no symptoms. After **6 weeks to 3 months**, HIV antibodies appear in the blood (seroconversion). HIV antibodies do **not** serve a protective role, but their presence allows detection.
3. **Symptomatic disease → AIDS:** during the prolonged incubation period the immune system gradually deteriorates and the person can transmit the virus. **AIDS is diagnosed when the CD4 count drops below 200 cells/mm³ or the person develops opportunistic infections.**

- **HAART/ART** has greatly increased survival.
- **Window period = 6 weeks to 3 months:** the interval after infection but before antibodies are produced, during which the antibody test can give a **false negative.**

## §2 HIV transmission and testing (p. 219) — the EIA-then-Western-blot sequence

**Transmission fluids:** blood, semen, pre-seminal fluid, rectal fluids, vaginal fluids, and breast milk; must contact a mucous membrane or damaged tissue, or be directly injected into the bloodstream. Also transplanted organs.
- **First most common US route:** anal or vaginal sex with an HIV-positive person not using a condom or HIV medicines.
- **Second most common:** sharing needles/syringes or drug-preparation equipment. **HIV can live in a used needle for up to 42 days.**
- Rare routes: accidental needlestick, organ transplants, blood transfusions.

**Testing:**
- **HIV antibody test** = the common screening test; detects the antibody, not symptoms or the virus.
- **EIA (enzyme-linked immunosorbent assay)** = the most commonly used form; screens blood and donor products.
- **Western blot** = the **confirmatory** test, used to verify a positive EIA and **minimize false positives.**
- **Rapid HIV antibody test** (oral fluid, e.g. OraQuick) = 99.5% accurate, results in 20 minutes; the only FDA-approved rapid self-test.
- Patients can **opt out** of HIV testing; annual testing is recommended for those with high-risk behavior.

## §3 Chlamydia, gonorrhea, and PID (p. 221–225)

- **Chlamydia = most commonly reported infectious disease; gonorrhea = second most common.** Both are frequently asymptomatic, which is why screening matters.
- **Screening recommendation:** all sexually active women younger than 25, and women 25+ at increased risk (new or multiple partners).
- **PID** is a serious complication and a particular risk for **women who stay asymptomatic and never seek treatment.** It can follow untreated chlamydia or gonorrhea and threatens fertility.
- **Gonorrhea treatment is becoming more complex** because of drug resistance; the current regimen is **injected ceftriaxone combined with oral azithromycin** (down from five treatment options in 2005 to essentially one).

## §4 Syphilis stages (p. 224) — the classic sequence

Caused by the spirochete ***Treponema pallidum***. Know the four stages in order:

| Stage | Hallmark |
|---|---|
| **Primary** | A **chancre** at the site of entry: begins as a macule, progresses to a papule, then ulcerates |
| **Secondary** | The organism enters the **lymph system and spreads throughout the body**; rash, lymphadenopathy |
| **Latent** | Asymptomatic period with no visible signs |
| **Tertiary** | Occurs **several years** after initial infection; **rare in the US**, a major problem in developing countries |

- **Congenital syphilis (CS):** rises whenever primary and secondary rates rise; increasing each year since 2012.

## §5 Viral hepatitis (p. 226–229) — the A / B / C split is the whole point

| | Route | Chronic? | Key detail |
|---|---|---|---|
| **HAV** | **Fecal-oral**, contaminated food/water | **No, self-limited** | Children under 6 often asymptomatic; vaccine-preventable; adults in high-prevalence areas often immune from childhood |
| **HBV** | **Blood and body fluids** (blood-borne) | Can become chronic | Same transmission properties as HIV; vaccine-preventable; pregnant women screened for HBsAg |
| **HCV** | **Blood/body fluids** (often shared needles) | **75% to 85% develop chronic/lifelong infection** | The **most common blood-borne pathogen in the US**; chronic HCV can lead to cirrhosis, liver cancer, and death; 15% to 25% clear it without treatment |

- **The trap lives here:** HAV is fecal-oral and self-limited (no chronic carrier state); HBV and HCV are blood-borne and can become chronic. HCV is the one that most often becomes chronic.

## §6 Tuberculosis (p. 229–230) — screening sequence and DOT

- **Transmission:** airborne droplets from persons with pulmonary TB who talk, cough, or sneeze.
- **Latent vs. active TB:** in **latent** TB the person is infected but **not ill and cannot transmit** the disease. WHO estimates about **one-fourth of the world's population has latent TB.** Active disease produces pulmonary symptoms and is transmissible.
- **Screening test:** the most effective tuberculin skin test (TST) is the **Mantoux test**, previously referred to as the **purified protein derivative (PPD)** test; used for **initial screening**. A **positive skin reaction plus pulmonary symptoms** is followed by **chest radiography.**
- **DOT (directly observed therapy):** nurses observe and document individual patients taking their TB medications. **Premature discontinuation risks drug resistance**, which can affect an entire susceptible community. DOT is a **population-level** intervention to prevent antibiotic resistance and ensure effective treatment; nurses are also involved in **contact investigations.**
- **Drug-resistant TB:** MDR-TB (multidrug-resistant); **XDR-TB = MDR-TB plus resistance to injected drugs.** Drug-resistant TB is an especially significant concern for immunocompromised people such as those with HIV.
- Higher risk: immunosuppressed persons, undernourished persons, and persons with diabetes, silicosis, or gastrectomies.

## §7 Levels of prevention and partner notification (p. 233–234)

**Primary:** keep people healthy and avoid onset. Assess for risk behavior and educate on avoiding infection; **safer sex**; **HPV and HBV vaccines.** The book states **sexual abstinence is the best way to prevent STDs**, but when abstinence is unrealistic, teaching safer sexual behavior is critical.

**Secondary:** **screening** for early identification and treatment, and **follow-up with contacts** to prevent further spread. If a patient is unwilling to notify past partners, the nurse carries out partner notification.

**Tertiary:** applies to chronic viral STDs (genital herpes, genital warts) and TB; focuses on **managing symptoms and maintaining psychosocial support.**

**Partner notification / contact tracing:** the two terms are synonyms. It is a **population-level** control intervention, carried out **confidentially** by health departments, usually alongside reportable-disease requirements. It identifies and notifies exposed individuals of persons found to have reportable diseases.

**BOX 12.3 — Responsibilities of persons who are HIV infected:** have regular medical evaluations and follow-ups; do **not** donate blood, plasma, organs, tissues, or sperm; take precautions against exchanging body fluids during sexual activity; inform sexual or injection-drug-using partners of potential exposure (or arrange notification through the health department); inform health care providers of the HIV infection; consider the risk of perinatal transmission and follow up with contraception.

## Source conflicts and traps

- ⚠️ **The AIDS-defining CD4 count is OCR-corrupted.** The scan renders it as "CD4 cell count... drops below **22** cells/mm" because an unrelated clause (needlestick injury, organ transplants, blood transfusions) bled into the middle of the sentence and swallowed a digit. The correct, standard AIDS-defining threshold is **CD4 < 200 cells/mm³ (or the presence of an opportunistic infection).** This file uses 200; verify against your physical book if a question hinges on the exact number, but 200 is the universally correct value and the "22" is a scan artifact, not the book's claim.
- **HIV antibody test detects the antibody, not the virus or symptoms.** A stem claiming the antibody test "isolates the virus" or "diagnoses symptomatic AIDS" is the distractor. The virus-antibody detection is why the **window period** exists.
- **Window period = false-negative window.** Testing during the 6-weeks-to-3-months window can miss a true infection. Any option treating an early negative test as definitive is wrong.
- **EIA screens, Western blot confirms.** The confirmatory test reduces false positives. Reversing the order (Western blot first) is the trap.
- **HAV vs. HBV/HCV is the hepatitis trap.** HAV = fecal-oral, self-limited, no chronic state. HBV and HCV = blood-borne, can become chronic. HCV becomes chronic in 75% to 85% of cases and is the most common US blood-borne pathogen. An option calling HAV "blood-borne" or "chronic" is wrong.
- **Latent TB cannot transmit.** A patient with latent TB is infected but not infectious and not ill. Any option saying a latent-TB patient can spread the disease is the distractor.
- **Mantoux/PPD is a screening test, not diagnostic.** A positive TST is followed by chest radiography for confirmation. Treating a positive skin test as a TB diagnosis is the trap (mirrors the Ch. 10 screening-vs-diagnosis rule).
- **DOT's purpose is adherence to prevent resistance**, and it operates at the population level. An option framing DOT as merely individual convenience misses the point.
- **Partner notification and contact tracing are the same thing** in this chapter; do not treat them as different interventions.
- **Chlamydia is most reported, gonorrhea second.** Inverting them is an easy factual distractor.
- **PID's risk factor is being asymptomatic and untreated**, not symptomatic infection; the danger is the silent, unsought case.
