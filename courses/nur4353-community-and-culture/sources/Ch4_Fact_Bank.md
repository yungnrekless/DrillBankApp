## §1 The three branches of government — with their health-policy roles (highest yield)

| Branch | Who | Role in health policy |
|---|---|---|
| **Executive** | President / governor / mayor + cabinet, administrative and regulatory departments and agencies (e.g., **USDHHS**) | **Suggests, administers, and regulates** policy. |
| **Legislative** | Congress (Senate + House) at federal level; elected by geographic area | **Identifies problems; proposes, debates, passes, and modifies laws.** |
| **Judicial** | Federal, state, and local courts, guided by the Supreme Court | **Interprets laws** and their meaning (e.g., states' rights, reproductive health access). |

> **The trap:** EAQ pins each distractor to a different branch. *Makes/passes laws* → **legislative.** *Administers/enforces/regulates* → **executive.** *Interprets laws / rules on constitutionality* → **judicial.** A Board of Nursing is part of the **executive** branch (an administrative/regulatory agency), even though it's *created by* a legislative statute.

Congress's health-care authority rests mainly on **Article I, Section 8** of the Constitution: provide for the general welfare, regulate interstate commerce, raise funds for the military, and spending power. Landmark case: **Stewart Machine Co. v. Davis (1937)** upheld federal authority (unemployment/old-age benefits) as promoting the general welfare.

## §2 The three types of law that matter to nursing

| Type | Source | Key idea |
|---|---|---|
| **Constitutional law** | Federal and state constitutions | Overall guidance; basis for state powers like quarantine. **Police power** lives here — the state's right to protect public health, even overriding individual rights when the public is threatened. |
| **Legislation / statute law** | Legislative branch | Coded statutes (e.g., a nurse practice act is statute law). |
| **Judicial / common law** | Courts | **Judicial law** = case law from court decisions. **Common law** rests on **precedent** (judges bound by prior rulings unless *distinguished*). |

> **Quarantine/isolation test point:** the legal basis is **constitutional law → police power.** When there is a genuine threat to the public's health, **the community's rights outweigh the individual's rights,** provided the action is necessary and done reasonably. This connects to communicable disease reporting (Ch 12, 17).

## §3 Scope of practice and the nurse practice act

**Scope of practice** = defining nursing, setting its credentials, and distinguishing nurses from physicians and other providers. Especially important for community nurses, who traditionally practice with high autonomy.

**Every state has a nurse practice act**, the most important statutory law affecting nurses. Each act accomplishes at least **four functions:**
1. **Defines** the practice of professional nursing.
2. **Identifies the scope** of nursing practice.
3. Sets **educational qualifications and other requirements for licensure.**
4. Determines the **legal titles** nurses may use.

Defining scope of practice forces clarification of **independent, interdependent, and dependent** nursing functions. Practitioners are subject to the laws of the **state in which they practice** and can practice **only with a license.**

## §4 Boards of Nursing — responsibilities (explicit blueprint item)

Every state has a **Board of Nursing**, created by the state's nurse practice act and made up of **nurses and consumers**. It may sit in the department of licensing boards or in an administrative agency of the governor's office. Its functions (from the nurse practice act) generally include:

- **Licensing and examination** of RNs and LPNs.
- **Licensing and/or certification of advanced practice nurses.**
- **Approval of schools of nursing** in the state.
- **Revocation, suspension, or denial of licenses.**
- **Writing regulations** about nursing practice and education.

> The Board **operationalizes, implements, and enforces** the statutory law (the nurse practice act) by writing explicit rules. In branch terms: the legislature *passes* the nurse practice act; the Board (executive/administrative) *writes the regulations and enforces* it.

## §5 Professional negligence / malpractice (explicit blueprint item)

**Professional negligence (malpractice)** = an act, or failure to act, that leads to injury of a patient. To recover damages, the patient must prove **all four** elements:

1. **Duty** — the nurse owed a duty to the patient / was responsible for their care.
2. **Breach** — the nurse failed to act as a **reasonable, prudent nurse** would in the same circumstances.
3. **Causation** — that failure led to the alleged injuries.
4. **Damages** — the injuries provide the basis for a monetary claim.

> **Memory hook — the 4 D's:** Duty, Dereliction (breach), Direct cause, Damages. All four must be present; missing any one defeats the claim.

**Respondeat superior** — when a nurse is employed and acting within the scope of employment, the **employer is responsible** for the nurse's negligent actions (along with the individual nurse), because the employer directed the function and is usually better able to pay. Reported negligence cases involving population-centered nurses are **rare.**

## §6 Nursing advocacy and the policy process (blueprint: "nursing's role")

Nurses influence policy through **legislative action** (communicating with policymakers, testifying, lobbying) and **regulatory action** (participating in the rule-making process after a law passes). The chapter frames the nurse as an **agent of change** and advocate for population health — the professional heritage that moved nursing into the policy and political arenas. **Healthy People 2030** is presented as the leading example of national health-policy guidance.
