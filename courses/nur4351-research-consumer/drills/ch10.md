# Chapter 10 drills — Measurement and Data Collection

Authored from `sources/Ch10_Fact_Bank.md` (Grove & Gray, 7th ed.).
Chapter 10 owns its own line on the final ("Statistics: data collection and
measurement", 10 of 75 questions), so the file is weighted toward the three
densest clusters the fact bank names: the four levels of measurement, the
reliability/validity split, and sensitivity versus specificity. Every
easily-inverted distinction is asked from both directions in adjacent items,
and the levels-of-measurement items ask you to classify a described variable
rather than recite a definition.

---

Topic: directness of measurement
Subtopic: direct versus indirect measures

Q: A study's variables include waist circumference, resting heart rate, and days of hospitalization for one aim, and perceived coping and depression for a second aim. Which classification of these variables is correct?
A) Both sets are direct measures, because both are recorded with standardized procedures
B) The first set require indirect measures; the second set are direct measures
C) Both sets are indirect measures, because some error is present in every measurement strategy
D) The first set are direct measures; the second set require indirect measures
Answer: D
Rationale: Direct measures determine the value of concrete factors such as waist circumference and heart rate, along with demographic variables such as days of hospitalization; abstract concepts such as coping and depression require indirect measures, which capture only some of the elements of the concept.
Trap: (C) mistakes the universality of measurement error for a statement about directness. Even direct measures contain error, and that does not make them indirect.

Q: A researcher notes that the pain instrument chosen for a study might also be measuring aspects of anxiety and fear. Which characteristic of indirect measures does this illustrate?
A) Indirect measures produce systematic rather than random error
B) Indirect measures cannot be evaluated for reliability in the study sample
C) Indirect measures may capture elements that are not part of the concept being measured
D) Indirect measures are appropriate only when the concept can be measured concretely
Answer: C
Rationale: With indirect measures, the methods used may capture elements that are not part of the concept; the chapter's example is that pain measures might be measuring aspects of anxiety and fear in addition to pain.
Trap: (D) inverts the definition. Concrete factors are exactly what direct measures handle; indirect measures exist because abstract concepts cannot be measured concretely.

Subtopic: multiple measures for abstract concepts

Q: A researcher measures children's postoperative pain with the FACES Pain Rating Scale, structured observation, and physiological measures. Which statements reflect the chapter's reasoning for this approach? Select all that apply.
A) Using multiple measurement methods decreases measurement error
B) Using multiple measurement methods increases understanding of the concept
C) Physiological measures such as pulse, blood pressure, and respiration provide a more objective measurement
D) Observation of pain includes rubbing or guarding the area, facial grimacing, and crying
E) Using all three methods together measures the entire concept of pain
Answer: A, B, C, D
Rationale: Rarely, if ever, can a single measurement strategy measure all aspects of an abstract concept, so multiple methods are needed; this decreases the measurement error and increases the understanding of the concept. Physiological measures give a more objective measurement, and observation covers guarding, grimacing, and crying.
Trap: (E) is the reflex that more methods equals full coverage. The chapter states that even with all three, the entire concept of pain is still not completely measured.

Q: A study protocol specifies how blood pressure will be obtained so the recorded value is as accurate and precise as possible. What does the chapter describe as the usual practice?
A) A single reading taken by the same nurse at every visit
B) Three BP measurements are taken and averaged
C) Two readings compared between two observers for interrater agreement
D) Readings repeated until two consecutive values match
Answer: B
Rationale: For blood pressure, three measurements are usually taken and averaged to determine the most accurate and precise reading.
Trap: (C) is a real chapter concept but belongs to observational data requiring judgments by two or more data gatherers, not to averaging out random error in a physiological reading.

Topic: levels of measurement
Subtopic: nominal level and its rules

Q: A researcher codes each participant's admitting diagnosis as kidney stone, gastric ulcer, or cholecystitis and enters the codes 1, 2, and 3 into the dataset. What level of measurement is this variable?
A) Nominal
B) Ordinal
C) Interval
D) Ratio
Answer: A
Rationale: Medical diagnoses are nominal: the categories are exclusive and exhaustive but cannot be ordered, because a kidney stone cannot be rated higher than a gastric ulcer. The categories differ in quality but not in quantity.
Trap: The numeric codes are what pull students upward to ordinal or interval. Codes assigned to categories are labels, not quantities, and nothing about 1, 2, 3 makes the diagnoses rankable.

Q: A demographic form offers Single, Married, Divorced, and Widowed. A participant who was widowed and has since remarried cannot decide which box to mark. Which nominal rule is violated, and what fix does the chapter give?
A) Exhaustiveness; add an "Other" category
B) Exhaustiveness; redefine the variable as Current Marital Status
C) Exclusiveness; redefine the variable as Current Marital Status
D) Orderability; rank the categories by duration of the current status
Answer: C
Rationale: The exclusive rule requires that each datum fit into only one category, and the chapter's remedy for this exact example is to redefine the variable as Current Marital Status.
Trap: (B) pairs the correct fix with the wrong rule. Two categories a person could legitimately claim is an exclusivity failure; a person for whom no category exists is an exhaustiveness failure.

Q: A "Cardiac Medical Diagnoses" item lists only Hypertension, Heart Failure, and Myocardial Infarction, leaving a participant with cardiomyopathy nowhere to be recorded. Which statement about adding an "Other" category is accurate?
A) It makes the categories exclusive and improves the analysis
B) It raises the variable from the nominal to the ordinal level
C) It has no effect unless the categories are also rank ordered
D) It makes the categories exhaustive, but the resulting data are difficult to analyze and interpret
Answer: D
Rationale: Adding "Other" makes the categories exhaustive so that all data fit somewhere, but the chapter notes that these data are difficult to analyze and interpret.
Trap: Adding "Other" reads like a free fix. It is a trade-off, and an option presenting it as pure improvement overstates what the chapter actually says.

Q: Which statements about nominal-level measurement are accurate? Select all that apply.
A) The categories must be exclusive, so each datum fits into only one category
B) The categories must be exhaustive, so all data fit somewhere
C) The categories can be rank ordered from lowest to highest
D) The categories differ in quality but not in quantity
E) Gender, race and ethnicity, marital status, and medical diagnoses are examples
Answer: A, B, D, E
Rationale: Nominal categories must be exclusive and exhaustive and are not orderable; they differ in quality but not quantity. The chapter's examples are gender, race and ethnicity, marital status, and medical diagnoses.
Trap: (C) imports the ordinal rule. Ranking is precisely what nominal data cannot support, and it is the single rule separating nominal from ordinal.

Subtopic: ordinal versus interval

Q: Participants rate their shortness of breath on a 0-to-4 dyspnea scale. What level of measurement does this produce, and why?
A) Nominal, because the numbers function only as labels for categories
B) Ordinal, because the ranks are ordered but the intervals between them are unequal
C) Interval, because the numerals 0 through 4 are equally spaced on the form
D) Ratio, because 0 on the scale represents the absence of dyspnea
Answer: B
Rationale: The 0-to-4 dyspnea scale is ordinal: the ranks are ordered, but a smaller difference may exist between the ranks of 1 and 2 than between the ranks of 3 and 4. The same logic applies to pain ranked excruciating, severe, moderate, mild, none, where a greater difference may exist between mild and moderate than between excruciating and severe.
Trap: (C) is the reflex because the printed numerals look evenly spaced. Equal spacing of the numerals is not equal spacing of the underlying attribute, and that is the entire ordinal-versus-interval distinction.

Q: A study records each participant's body temperature in degrees Fahrenheit. What level of measurement is this, and why?
A) Ordinal, because temperatures can be ranked from lowest to highest
B) Ratio, because a temperature of 0 degrees Fahrenheit indicates the absence of temperature
C) Interval, because the intervals are equal but there is no absolute zero
D) Interval, because temperature values can be multiplied and divided
Answer: C
Rationale: The difference between 70 and 80 degrees Fahrenheit is the same as the difference between 30 and 40 degrees, so the intervals are equal; but 0 degrees Fahrenheit does not indicate the absence of temperature, so there is no absolute zero.
Trap: (D) attaches the wrong permission to the right level. The chapter's level table gives interval data addition and subtraction, and reserves multiplication and division for ratio data.

Subtopic: interval versus ratio

Q: A researcher reports that "Medication container A holds twice as much as medication container B." What property of the measurement level justifies that statement?
A) Equal intervals between values, which is the defining addition of interval data
B) Exclusive and exhaustive categories, which nominal data require
C) Ranked ordering of values, which ordinal data provide
D) An absolute zero point, which ratio data have
Answer: D
Rationale: Ratio-level data have an absolute zero, and that is what permits ratio statements such as one container holding twice as much as another; weight, length, and volume are the chapter's ratio examples.
Trap: (A) is the near-miss. Equal intervals let you say participant A weighs 25 more pounds than participant B; only an absolute zero lets you say A is twice B.

Q: Which statements about interval-level measurement are accurate? Select all that apply.
A) The intervals between values are equal
B) There is an absolute zero point
C) Values can be added and subtracted
D) Temperature in degrees Fahrenheit is the chapter's example
E) Interval data can be analyzed with statistical techniques of greater strength than ordinal data
Answer: A, C, D, E
Rationale: Interval measurement adds equal intervals to the ordinal rules but has no absolute zero, and temperature in degrees Fahrenheit is the chapter's standing example. Interval and ratio data support statistical techniques of greater strength for determining significant relationships and differences.
Trap: (B) is the ratio rule, and the absence of an absolute zero is the only thing keeping interval data from being ratio. Note the chapter's own internal tension here: its level table gives interval data only addition and subtraction, while its summary sentence says interval- and ratio-level data alike can be added, subtracted, multiplied, and divided. Answer from the table when a stem asks about one level in isolation.

Q: A researcher redesigns a study so that a variable previously collected as ordinal ranks will now be collected at the interval level. According to the chapter, what does this change buy her?
A) The data can be analyzed with statistical techniques of greater strength to determine significant relationships and differences
B) The instrument no longer requires reliability testing in this sample
C) The data will no longer contain measurement error
D) The results can be reported as percentages rather than as frequencies
Answer: A
Rationale: Interval- and ratio-level data can be added, subtracted, multiplied, and divided, so they can be analyzed with statistical techniques of greater strength to determine significant relationships and differences.
Trap: (C) is the reflex that a higher level means a better measurement. Level of measurement governs which analyses are permitted; some error is always present regardless of the level.

Q: A research team is assigning levels of measurement to its study variables. Which pairings are correct? Select all that apply.
A) Weight in pounds — ratio
B) Level of mobility, ranked from bedbound to independent — ordinal
C) Race and ethnicity — nominal
D) Temperature in degrees Fahrenheit — ratio
E) Pain rated as mild, moderate, severe, or excruciating — ordinal
Answer: A, B, C, E
Rationale: Weight is a ratio variable because it has an absolute zero; levels of mobility and pain categories are the chapter's ordinal examples; race and ethnicity is nominal.
Trap: (D) is the most repeated misclassification in this chapter. Temperature in degrees Fahrenheit is interval precisely because zero does not mean the absence of temperature.

Topic: measurement error
Subtopic: random versus systematic error

Q: During data entry a research assistant occasionally transposes digits, and a few participants mark the wrong column on the answer sheet. The resulting values fall above and below the true values with no pattern. What type of error is this, and what does the chapter recommend?
A) Systematic error; recalibrate the measurement instrument
B) Random error; average multiple values, because a mean is a closer estimate of the true measurement
C) Systematic error; average multiple values to cancel out the bias
D) Random error; discard the affected cases before analysis
Answer: B
Rationale: In random measurement error the difference between the measured value and the true value is without pattern or direction; an average value such as the mean is a closer estimate of the true measurement.
Trap: (C) pairs the right remedy with the wrong error. Averaging works only because random errors fall in both directions and cancel one another.

Q: A digital scale used in a weight-loss study reads 0.5 lb high for every participant at every visit. How should this error be classified?
A) Random error, because the deviation is small relative to body weight
B) Random error, because weight is a direct measure and direct measures are precise
C) Systematic error, because the variation from the calculated average is primarily in the same direction
D) Systematic error, because the sample was too small to detect the difference
Answer: C
Rationale: Systematic measurement error is variation in measurement values from the calculated average that is primarily in the same direction, and a consistent flaw in the instrument or process produces it.
Trap: Taking and averaging more readings will not help here. Every reading carries the same 0.5 lb bias, so the mean is biased by the same amount. This is the mirror image of the random-error item, where averaging is the fix.

Q: A reviewer argues that a study's physiological measures cannot contain measurement error because they are direct measures. How does the chapter answer this?
A) Direct measures are error-free once the equipment has been calibrated before data collection
B) Direct measures contain systematic error only, never random error
C) Direct measures contain error only when more than one data collector is used
D) Some error is always present in any measurement strategy, including direct measures
Answer: D
Rationale: Some error is always present in any measurement strategy; the chapter's own examples include a scale inaccurate by 0.5 lb, calibrated BP equipment that loses accuracy with use, and a tape measure held at different tensions.
Trap: (A) is the reflex, but the chapter specifically names calibrated BP equipment losing accuracy with use as an error source, which is the counterexample to calibration as a guarantee.

Q: Which statements about measurement error are accurate? Select all that apply.
A) Measurement error is the difference between the true measure and what is actually measured
B) As the number of random errors increases, the precision of the estimate decreases
C) In random error, the difference between the measured and the true value is without pattern or direction
D) Systematic error is corrected by averaging repeated measurements
E) Even direct measures such as weight and blood pressure are subject to error
Answer: A, B, C, E
Rationale: Measurement error is the difference between the true measure and what is actually measured; random error has no pattern or direction, and as random errors accumulate the precision of the estimate decreases. Some error is present in every measurement strategy.
Trap: (D) borrows the random-error remedy. Averaging cannot remove a bias that pushes every value in the same direction, which parallels the rule that a larger sample fixes random but not systematic variation.

Topic: reliability
Subtopic: stability and test-retest

Q: A researcher administers the CES-D to the same participants on two occasions 4 weeks apart and correlates the two sets of scores. Which reliability is being examined?
A) Stability, determined by test-retest
B) Equivalence, determined by alternate forms
C) Equivalence, determined by interrater comparison
D) Internal consistency, determined by the Cronbach alpha
Answer: A
Rationale: Stability reliability is concerned with the reproducibility of scores with repeated measures of the same concept over time and is determined by test-retest; the CES-D shows r = 0.51 to 0.67 at 2- to 8-week intervals, which the chapter calls very solid.
Trap: Nothing in the stem involves two versions or two raters. Repeat administration to the same people over time is the signature of stability, not equivalence.

Q: What assumption underlies test-retest reliability testing?
A) The two administrations use parallel versions of the instrument
B) The attribute remains the same at the two testing times, and any change in the score is a consequence of random error
C) The instrument yields interval- or ratio-level data
D) The interval between administrations is long enough for the attribute to change
Answer: B
Rationale: Test-retest assumes that the attribute to be measured remains the same at the two testing times and that any change in the value or score is a consequence of random error.
Trap: (C) is the constraint on the Cronbach alpha, not on test-retest. (D) inverts the assumption outright, since real change would confound the estimate.

Subtopic: equivalence

Q: Two trained observers independently rate the same infant feeding sessions and their ratings are compared. Which reliability is this, and what benchmark does the chapter give?
A) Alternate forms reliability; values below 0.80 are a serious concern
B) Internal consistency; 0.70 is acceptable for a relatively new tool
C) Interrater reliability; values below 0.80 are a serious concern and reliability is best at 0.90 or higher
D) Interrater reliability; 0.70 is acceptable for a relatively new observation tool
Answer: C
Rationale: Comparison of two observers or two judges is interrater reliability, one of the two forms of equivalence; the chapter states that a value below 0.80 is a serious concern and that reliability is best at 0.90, or 90 percent, or higher.
Trap: (D) borrows the Cronbach alpha leniency for new scales. The 0.70 standard belongs to internal consistency of a relatively new scale, never to interrater agreement.

Q: A researcher compares participants' scores on two paper-and-pencil versions of the same anxiety instrument. Which reliability is being tested?
A) Interrater reliability
B) Stability reliability
C) Internal consistency
D) Alternate (parallel) forms reliability
Answer: D
Rationale: Equivalence is the comparison of two versions of the same paper and pencil instrument or of two observers measuring the same event; comparing two paper and pencil instruments or scales is alternate, or parallel, forms reliability.
Trap: This is the mirror of the interrater item and both live under equivalence. Two observers gives interrater reliability; two instruments gives alternate forms.

Subtopic: internal consistency

Q: A newly developed 15-item scale yields a Cronbach alpha of 0.74 in the study sample. How should this value be interpreted?
A) Acceptable, because 0.70 is acceptable for a relatively new scale
B) Unacceptable, because an alpha of at least 0.80 is required of every scale
C) Unacceptable, because values below 0.80 are a serious concern for interrater reliability
D) Perfect internal consistency, because 1.00 indicates none and 0.00 indicates perfect
Answer: A
Rationale: A Cronbach alpha of 1.00 indicates perfect reliability and 0.00 indicates none; 0.80 is strong for an established scale, and 0.70 is acceptable for a relatively new scale.
Trap: (B) applies the established-scale standard to a new one. The benchmark shifts with the maturity of the scale, and (C) drags an interrater benchmark into an internal consistency question.

Q: A researcher wants to estimate internal consistency for a screening tool whose items are all answered yes or no. Which statistic applies?
A) The Cronbach alpha, since it is the most commonly used measure of internal reliability for multi-item scales
B) The Kuder-Richardson formula (K-R 20)
C) A test-retest correlation over a 2-week interval
D) An interrater reliability coefficient between two scorers
Answer: B
Rationale: The Cronbach alpha can only be calculated for interval- and ratio-level data; for dichotomous yes/no data the Kuder-Richardson formula (K-R 20) is used.
Trap: (A) is the reflex precisely because the alpha is the most commonly used measure for multi-item scales. Dichotomous items disqualify it no matter how common it is.

Subtopic: reliability as a property of the use

Q: A published report states, "The instrument has established reliability, with a Cronbach alpha of 0.89 in prior research," and reports no reliability testing in the current sample. Which statements apply? Select all that apply.
A) Estimates of reliability are specific to the sample being tested
B) High reliability values reported for an established instrument do not guarantee satisfactory reliability in another sample or population
C) Researchers need to perform reliability testing on each instrument used in a study
D) A previously published alpha of 0.89 is sufficient evidence of reliability for this study
E) Reliability exists in degrees and is usually expressed as a correlation coefficient
Answer: A, B, C, E
Rationale: Estimates of reliability are specific to the sample being tested, and high values reported for an established instrument do not guarantee that reliability will be satisfactory in another sample or with a different population, so researchers must perform reliability testing on each instrument used in a study.
Trap: (D) is the reflex a published alpha invites. Reliability is a property of the use of the instrument in this sample, not a permanent attribute of the instrument.

Q: Which statements about reliability testing are accurate? Select all that apply.
A) Internal consistency testing is used primarily with multi-item scales, in which each item is correlated with all other items
B) Correlation coefficients closer to 1.0 indicate less random error and a more reliable scale
C) Studies collecting observational data or requiring judgments by two or more data gatherers must report interrater reliability
D) There is an absolute value below which interrater reliability is always unacceptable
E) Reliability testing determines the measurement error in an instrument or scale used in a study
Answer: A, B, C, E
Rationale: Internal consistency correlates each item with all other items on a multi-item scale; stronger coefficients closer to 1.0 indicate less random error and a more reliable scale; observational studies and those requiring judgments by two or more data gatherers must report interrater reliability; and reliability testing determines the measurement error in an instrument.
Trap: (D) hardens a guideline into a rule. The chapter gives 0.80 and 0.90 as benchmarks but states explicitly that there is no absolute value below which interrater reliability is unacceptable.

Topic: validity
Subtopic: content validity

Q: Before using a newly written adherence scale, a team compares the items against how the concept is described in the literature, asks content experts to rate the relevance of each item, and reviews study participants' responses to the items. Which statements about this validity work are accurate? Select all that apply.
A) All three activities are sources of evidence for content validity
B) Comparing items against the description of the concept in the literature is face validity
C) The content experts' evaluation may be reported as an index
D) Face validity is a fourth type of validity, separate from content validity, in this chapter
E) Content validity examines whether the scale includes all the major elements relevant to the construct
Answer: A, B, C, E
Rationale: Content validity examines the extent to which the measurement method or scale includes all the major elements or items relevant to the construct, and its evidence comes from three sources: how well items reflect the description of the concept in the literature, which is face validity; content experts' evaluation of item relevance, which may be reported as an index; and study participants' responses to the items.
Trap: (D) is imported from other texts. In this chapter face validity is one evidence source inside content validity, not a separate type of validity.

Subtopic: construct validity

Q: A researcher administers a new depression scale and the existing Hamilton Rating Scale to the same participants at the same time and obtains correlations of 0.40 to 0.80. Which evidence is this?
A) Predictive validity
B) Divergent validity
C) Convergent validity
D) Content validity from participant responses
Answer: C
Rationale: Convergence involves administering a new instrument and an existing one that measures the same construct at the same time, with a strong positive correlation expected; the CES-D correlated 0.40 to 0.80 with the Hamilton Rating Scale.
Trap: Simultaneous administration also describes concurrent validity, so timing alone will not sort these. Convergence compares two measures of the same construct; concurrent validity estimates present performance on a different criterion.

Q: A hope scale correlates -0.6 with a hopelessness scale administered at the same time. How should this result be interpreted?
A) The hope scale lacks reliability, so the correlation cannot be interpreted
B) Validity is not supported, because validity evidence requires positive correlations
C) Convergent validity is supported, because the magnitude exceeds 0.4
D) Divergent validity is supported, because instruments measuring opposite concepts are expected to correlate negatively
Answer: D
Rationale: Divergence involves administering instruments that measure opposite concepts, such as hope versus hopelessness or despair, and expecting a negative correlation, for example in the range of -0.4 to -0.8.
Trap: (B) is the reflex that a minus sign means failure. This is the mirror of the convergence item: the same construct gives a positive correlation, opposite constructs give a negative one, and both support construct validity.

Q: Investigators administer the CES-D to a group of people diagnosed with depression and a group without that diagnosis, predicting the diagnosed group will score higher. Which evidence is this?
A) Construct validity from contrasting groups
B) Criterion-related validity
C) Content validity from expert review
D) Stability reliability
Answer: A
Rationale: Contrasting-groups evidence compares groups expected or known to differ; on the CES-D, the group diagnosed with depression is expected to score higher.
Trap: (B) is tempting because a clinical diagnosis sounds like a criterion. Criterion-related validity uses a participant's score to infer performance on another variable, not to separate two already-known groups.

Subtopic: criterion-related validity

Q: Scores on a health-promotion-behaviors scale collected at enrollment are used to forecast participants' blood pressure, BMI, and minutes of exercise one year later. Which validity is being examined?
A) Concurrent validity
B) Predictive validity
C) Convergent validity
D) Content validity
Answer: B
Rationale: Predictive validity is the extent to which an individual's score on an instrument can be used to predict future performance or behavior on a criterion; the chapter's example is a health-promotion-behaviors scale predicting future BP, BMI, and minutes of exercise.
Trap: The distinguishing feature is timing alone. A criterion measured in the future makes it predictive no matter how strong or weak the resulting correlation is.

Q: A researcher uses participants' current self-esteem scores to estimate their present ability to cope with illness. Which validity is this, and what distinguishes it from its counterpart?
A) Predictive validity; the two are distinguished by the strength of the correlation
B) Concurrent validity; the two are distinguished by whether a criterion standard was used
C) Concurrent validity; the two are distinguished by the timing of the measurement of the other criterion
D) Convergent validity; the two are distinguished by whether both instruments measure the same construct
Answer: C
Rationale: Concurrent validity is the extent to which an individual's score can be used to estimate her or his present or concurrent performance on another variable; the difference between concurrent and predictive validity is the timing of the measurement of the other criterion.
Trap: (A) invokes strength. Strength never separates these two, and this item is the mirror of the predictive-validity item: present versus future is the whole distinction.

Subtopic: the reliability dependency

Q: An observation instrument used in a study achieves an interrater reliability of 0.55. What does the chapter conclude about that instrument's validity in this study?
A) Validity is unaffected, because reliability and validity are independent properties
B) Validity can still be established by adding more content experts to the review panel
C) Only stability is compromised; criterion-related validity may still be acceptable
D) If a measurement method does not have acceptable reliability, then it is not valid
Answer: D
Rationale: You cannot consider validity apart from reliability, and if a measurement method does not have acceptable reliability, then it is not valid.
Trap: The dependency runs one direction only. A reliable instrument is not automatically valid, but an unreliable one cannot be valid, and that one-way relationship is the testable point.

Q: A team plans to use the CES-D, which was developed in mental health settings, with patients receiving cancer treatment. Which statements are accurate? Select all that apply.
A) Validity testing evaluates the use of an instrument for a specific group or purpose rather than the instrument itself
B) An instrument may be valid in one situation but not in another
C) Whether the CES-D is valid for this population should be established by pilot-testing in the new population
D) Validity is an all-or-nothing property that the CES-D already possesses
E) Readability is essential if an instrument is to be considered valid and reliable for a sample
Answer: A, B, C, E
Rationale: Validity varies from one sample to another and one situation to another, so validity testing evaluates the use of an instrument for a specific group or purpose; the chapter's own case is that the CES-D's validity for cancer patients must be established by pilot-testing in the new population. Readability is essential if an instrument is to be considered valid and reliable for a sample.
Trap: (D) inverts the chapter's statement that validity, like reliability, is not an all-or-nothing phenomenon but is measured on a continuum, and that no instrument is completely valid.

Topic: diagnostic test accuracy
Subtopic: sensitivity versus specificity

Q: A screening tool detects 97.6 percent of adolescents who are truly at risk according to the criterion standard. Which property is being described, and what follows from a high value?
A) Sensitivity; the test has a low percentage of false negatives
B) Sensitivity; the test has a low percentage of false positives
C) Specificity; the test has a low percentage of false negatives
D) Specificity; the test has a low percentage of false positives
Answer: A
Rationale: Sensitivity is the proportion of patients with the disease who have a positive test result, the true-positive rate; a highly sensitive test is very good at identifying the disease in a patient and has a low percentage of false negatives.
Trap: (B) pairs the right term with the wrong error. Sensitivity is computed inside the diseased group, so the only mistake it can minimize is the false negative.

Q: In the ASQ implementation study, only 43.3 percent of patients who were not at risk on the criterion standard screened negative. Which property is low, and what is the consequence?
A) Sensitivity is low, so a high percentage of true cases are missed
B) Specificity is low, so the test produces a high percentage of false positives
C) Specificity is low, so the test produces a high percentage of false negatives
D) Negative predictive value is low, so a negative screen cannot be trusted
Answer: B
Rationale: Specificity is the proportion of patients without the disease who have a negative test result, the true-negative rate; a very specific test has a low percentage of false positives, so a specificity of 43.3 percent means many false positives.
Trap: (C) swaps the error type. Specificity is computed inside the disease-free group, where the only possible mistake is a false positive. This is the mirror of the sensitivity item.

Q: Developers revise a screening instrument so that it captures a larger proportion of true cases. According to the chapter, what usually happens to its other property?
A) Specificity usually increases in parallel
B) Specificity is unaffected, because the two are computed on different groups
C) The test usually becomes less specific
D) The negative predictive value usually falls below 0.1
Answer: C
Rationale: As a test becomes more sensitive, it usually becomes less specific.
Trap: (D) misuses the likelihood ratio cutoffs. A likelihood ratio greater than 10 rules the disease in and one less than 0.1 almost rules it out; 0.1 has nothing to do with predictive values.

Subtopic: predictive values and likelihood ratios

Q: Which statements about the ASQ (Ask Suicide Screening Questions) as reported in the chapter are accurate? Select all that apply.
A) The ASQ has 4 items, and a "yes" to any item counts as a positive screen
B) In the development study against the SIQ criterion standard, sensitivity was 97.6 percent and specificity was 65.6 percent
C) In the implementation study, sensitivity was 93.3 percent and specificity was 43.3 percent
D) A high negative predictive value means the patient probably does not have the disease, which reduces the number of uncomfortable, costly treatments
E) In the implementation study, 53 percent of patients who screened positive presented with suicide-related complaints
Answer: A, B, C, D
Rationale: The ASQ is a 4-item screen used in a pediatric emergency department with patients aged 10 to 21, where a yes to any item is a positive screen; the development study reported sensitivity 97.6 percent, specificity 65.6 percent, and NPV 96.9 percent, while the implementation study reported 93.3 percent, 43.3 percent, and an NPV calculated at 98.98 percent.
Trap: (E) reverses the finding. It was 53 percent of patients who screened positive who did NOT present with suicide-related complaints, and they were more likely to be male, African American, and carrying externalizing behavior diagnoses.

Q: Which statements about diagnostic test accuracy are accurate? Select all that apply.
A) A highly sensitive test is very good at identifying the disease in a patient
B) A highly specific test is very good at identifying the patients without a disease
C) Specificity is the true-positive rate
D) A likelihood ratio greater than 10 rules in the disease
E) A likelihood ratio less than 0.1 almost rules out the disease
Answer: A, B, D, E
Rationale: A highly sensitive test is very good at identifying the disease and a highly specific test is very good at identifying patients without it; sensitivity is the true-positive rate and specificity is the true-negative rate. A likelihood ratio above 10 rules the disease in and one below 0.1 almost rules it out.
Trap: (C) is the most common inversion in this section. Specificity is the true-NEGATIVE rate; the positive likelihood ratio is the ratio of true-positive to false-positive results and the negative likelihood ratio is the ratio of true-negative to false-negative results.

Q: Using a CES-D cutoff score of 15 or higher, which values does the chapter report for older adults?
A) Sensitivity 89 percent, specificity 70 percent
B) Sensitivity 87 percent, specificity 92 percent
C) Sensitivity 70 percent, specificity 89 percent
D) Sensitivity 92 percent, specificity 87 percent
Answer: D
Rationale: At a score of 15 or higher, CES-D sensitivity is 89 percent in adults and 92 percent in older adults, and specificity is 70 percent in adults and 87 percent in older adults.
Trap: (A) gives the adult figures and (B) transposes the two properties, so match both the population and the direction. Note also that this benchmark cutoff of 15 is not the same number as the score of 16 or higher that the chapter names as the extensively used cutoff for depression.

Topic: measurement strategies and scales
Subtopic: rating and Likert scales

Q: A researcher plans to have participants select from an ordered series of categories of a variable that are assumed to be based on an underlying continuum. How does the chapter characterize this strategy?
A) It is the crudest form of measurement involving scaling techniques
B) It is the most commonly used scaling technique
C) It measures the strength, magnitude, or intensity of subjective feelings
D) It requires that interrater reliability be reported
Answer: A
Rationale: A rating scale lists an ordered series of categories of a variable that are assumed to be based on an underlying continuum, and the chapter calls it the crudest form of measurement involving scaling techniques.
Trap: (B) belongs to the Likert scale and (C) to the visual analog scale. Rating scales are also easy to develop, but the chapter warns against end statements so extreme that no participant will select them.

Q: A 15-item Likert scale is scored by summing the item values into a single total. What does the chapter say about the level of measurement here?
A) Each item and the summed score are both ordinal
B) Each item is technically ordinal-level data, but the summed score is often analyzed as interval-level data
C) Each item is interval-level data, but the summed score must be treated as ordinal
D) Both the item and the summed score are ratio, because zero is a possible value
Answer: B
Rationale: Although the values of each item are technically ordinal-level data, the summed score is often analyzed as interval-level data.
Trap: Both halves of that sentence are true, and an exam item can test either half on its own. Do not resolve the tension by assigning one level to everything.

Q: A Likert scale is written with six response options and no midpoint. What is this version called, and what is the argument against including a midpoint?
A) A parallel form; a midpoint lowers internal consistency
B) An evaluation-type scale; a midpoint biases responses toward agreement
C) A forced choice version; a middle "uncertain" option allows the participant to avoid making a clear choice
D) A forced choice version; a midpoint converts ordinal data into nominal data
Answer: C
Rationale: An odd number of options puts uncertain or neutral in the middle, which is controversial because it allows the participant to avoid making a clear choice; using four or six options with no midpoint is a forced choice version.
Trap: (A) borrows a reliability term. Parallel forms are two versions of an instrument used to test equivalence, not a response-option layout.

Subtopic: visual analog scales and the CES-D

Q: A researcher wants to measure the intensity of patients' dyspnea with a visual analog scale. Which construction matches the chapter's description?
A) An ordered series of five declarative statements, each followed by a response scale
B) A 100-mm line whose anchors are set at the most commonly reported values to improve discrimination
C) A numeric rating scale from 1 to 10 read aloud to the patient
D) A line usually 100 mm long with right-angle stops at either end and bipolar anchors that span the entire range possible
Answer: D
Rationale: The VAS is a line usually 100 mm long with right-angle stops at either end, presented horizontally or vertically, with bipolar anchors beyond each end that must span the entire range possible, such as no pain and unbearable pain; participants place a mark through the line.
Trap: (A) describes a Likert scale, since declarative statements each followed by a scale is the Likert signature. (B) violates the rule that anchors must span the entire range possible.

Q: Which statements about the CES-D as described in the chapter are accurate? Select all that apply.
A) It has four response options scored 0 through 3
B) The response "Occasionally or a moderate amount of the time" corresponds to 3 to 4 days
C) Total scores range from 0 to 60
D) Higher scores indicate more depressive symptoms
E) A score of 25 or higher is the extensively used cutoff for depression
Answer: A, B, C, D
Rationale: The CES-D's four options are rarely or none, less than 1 day, scored 0; some or a little, 1 to 2 days, scored 1; occasionally or a moderate amount, 3 to 4 days, scored 2; and most or all, 5 to 7 days, scored 3. Total scores range from 0 to 60, with higher scores indicating more depressive symptoms.
Trap: (E) inflates the cutoff. A score of 16 or higher is the extensively used cutoff for depression, while 15 appears only as the cutoff used for the sensitivity and specificity benchmarks. Two different numbers, easy to merge into one.
