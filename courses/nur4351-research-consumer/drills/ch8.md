# Chapter 8 drills — Clarifying Quantitative Research Designs

Authored from `sources/Ch8_Fact_Bank.md` (Grove & Gray, 7th ed.).
Topics are the four types of design validity plus the design families, because
Table 8.1 is the structure this chapter is actually tested on and design-validity
language returns in Chapter 12's critical appraisal. Stems lead with a described
study and ask for the classification, since that is the applied skill; the
history/maturation and Type I/Type II pairs are written from both directions.

---

Topic: research design fundamentals
Subtopic: concepts underlying design

Q: A reviewer reads this purpose statement: "to determine the effect of a nurse-led sleep hygiene protocol on the incidence of ICU delirium." Using the chapter's appraisal question, how should the reviewer classify what this study is doing?
A) Describing variables as they exist
B) Examining relationships among variables
C) Examining causality
D) Testing a proposed model of relationships
Answer: C
Rationale: In critical appraisal, determine whether the study's purpose is to examine causality, examine relationships, or describe variables; the purpose statement and the framework's propositions usually reveal which. Determining the effect of an intervention on an outcome is causality.
Trap: (B) is the reflex whenever two variables share a sentence. A relationship purpose asks whether variables vary together; a causal purpose asks whether one produces the other.

Q: Which statement reflects this chapter's position on causality in nursing phenomena?
A) A well-developed theory identifies every element involved in causing a particular outcome
B) A multicausal perspective includes fewer variables so the true cause can be isolated
C) Absolute causality is the standard that quantitative designs are built to achieve
D) Very few phenomena in nursing can be clearly linked to a single cause and a single effect
Answer: D
Rationale: The chapter states that very few phenomena in nursing can be clearly linked to a single cause and a single effect, and that studies taking a multicausal perspective include more variables.
Trap: (B) inverts the text, since multicausality adds variables rather than stripping them out. (C) ignores probability, which addresses relative rather than absolute causality: a cause probably will result in a specific effect.

Q: Beyond calling a research design a blueprint, the chapter's fuller definition specifies that the design does what?
A) Establishes the conceptual definitions of the study variables
B) Maximizes control over factors that could interfere with the validity of the findings
C) Guarantees that the findings can be generalized to the target population
D) Determines which statistical tests will reach significance
Answer: B
Rationale: A research design is a blueprint for conducting a quantitative study that maximizes control over factors that could interfere with the validity of the findings.
Trap: (C) overstates. Generalization is external validity, and it is usually narrower for a single study than for multiple replications using different samples and settings.

Subtopic: bias

Q: Which statements about bias reflect what this chapter says? Select all that apply.
A) Bias is a slant or deviation from the true or expected
B) A study that used random assignment can be appraised as free of bias
C) The composition of the sample and the groups that are formed are potential sources of bias
D) Not all biases can be identified from the published study report
E) The environment and the data collection process are potential sources of bias
Answer: A, C, D, E
Rationale: Bias is a slant or deviation from the true or expected that distorts findings from what they would have been without it. Named sources include researcher attitudes or motivations, the environment, selection of participants, sample composition, the groups formed, measurement methods, the data collection process, and statistical analyses.
Trap: (B) is the overreach the chapter warns against directly. The article may not provide sufficient detail about methods to detect possible biases, so no appraisal can declare a study bias-free.

Topic: quantitative design types
Subtopic: descriptive and correlational designs

Q: A researcher measures fall rates on three medical-surgical units over one quarter and reports how the units differ. No intervention is delivered and no relationships are tested. Which design is this?
A) Simple descriptive
B) Comparative descriptive
C) Descriptive correlational
D) Predictive correlational
Answer: B
Rationale: A comparative descriptive design describes and compares variables across groups; a simple descriptive design only describes variables as they exist.
Trap: (A) is chosen because nothing was manipulated, but the absence of an intervention is not what separates these two. The comparison across the three units is what moves it from simple to comparative descriptive.

Q: A researcher measures resilience and burnout scores in staff nurses at one time point and reports how strongly the two are related. There is no attempt to forecast one score from the other. Which design is this?
A) Descriptive correlational
B) Predictive correlational
C) Model testing
D) Comparative descriptive
Answer: A
Rationale: A descriptive correlational design describes relationships among variables, whereas a predictive correlational design measures independent variables in order to predict a dependent variable.
Trap: (B) is the reflex whenever a correlation coefficient appears. Prediction requires that independent variables be measured to predict a dependent variable, not merely that a relationship be described.

Q: Which statements about the chapter's design families are accurate? Select all that apply.
A) A predictive correlational design measures independent variables to predict a dependent variable
B) A model testing design tests a proposed model of relationships
C) A descriptive correlational design tests the effect of an intervention
D) A simple descriptive design describes variables as they exist
E) A comparative descriptive design describes and compares variables across groups
Answer: A, B, D, E
Rationale: The chapter's families are simple descriptive, comparative descriptive, descriptive correlational, predictive correlational, model testing, and quasi-experimental or experimental designs, which test the effect of an intervention.
Trap: (C) hands the intervention-testing job to a correlational design. Only quasi-experimental and experimental designs test the effect of an intervention, because only they involve manipulation.

Subtopic: designs examining causality

Q: Postoperative patients are assigned to receive either guided imagery or usual care, and pain scores are compared. Which element distinguishes this family of designs from descriptive and correlational designs?
A) Prospective data collection
B) Use of more than one measurement method
C) A large sample size
D) Manipulation of the independent variable
Answer: D
Rationale: Manipulation is a form of control generally used in quasi-experimental and experimental studies, which are the designs that test the effect of an intervention.
Trap: (A) is true of many experiments but does not define them. Descriptive and correlational studies can also collect data looking forward, so time orientation never settles the design family.

Q: Which statements about designs that examine causality are accurate? Select all that apply.
A) Descriptive and correlational designs also test the effect of an intervention
B) Manipulation is a form of control generally used in quasi-experimental and experimental studies
C) A randomized controlled trial is a type of experimental design often considered one of the strongest designs to examine cause and effect
D) Internal validity is a concern in all studies but is a major focus in studies examining causality
E) Random assignment decreases the selection threat
Answer: B, C, D, E
Rationale: Manipulation is generally used in quasi-experimental and experimental studies; the RCT is an experimental design often considered one of the strongest for examining cause and effect; internal validity is a concern in all studies but a major focus in causal studies; and random assignment decreases the selection threat.
Trap: (D) looks too generous to be keyed, which is why students drop it. Any option restricting internal validity to experimental designs is too narrow, and so is any answer key that leaves this statement out.

Subtopic: time orientation

Q: One researcher enrolls patients at the time of catheter insertion and follows them forward for 30 days to record infections. A second researcher pulls last year's charts to identify who developed an infection after insertion. These two approaches are, respectively:
A) Prospective and retrospective
B) Retrospective and prospective
C) Experimental and quasi-experimental
D) Descriptive and correlational
Answer: A
Rationale: Prospective means looking forward; retrospective means looking backward, usually in relation to time.
Trap: The data source does not decide this. A chart review can serve either direction, so what settles it is whether the look runs forward from enrollment or backward from the outcome.

Topic: construct validity
Subtopic: mono-operation bias

Q: A researcher studying postoperative pain uses a 0-to-10 numeric rating scale as the only measure of pain. Which threat to construct validity does this illustrate?
A) Inadequate definition of the construct
B) Unreliable measurement method
C) Mono-operation bias
D) Experimenter expectancy
Answer: C
Rationale: Mono-operation bias occurs when only one method of measurement is used to assess a construct, so fewer dimensions of the construct are measured; construct validity greatly improves with more than one instrument.
Trap: (B) belongs to statistical conclusion validity and concerns whether a measure gives the same result each time. A single measure can be perfectly reliable and still produce mono-operation bias, because the problem is the count of methods, not their consistency.

Q: Following the chapter's own pain example, which measures would strengthen construct validity when pain is the dependent variable? Select all that apply.
A) A pain rating scale
B) Verbal reports from the patient
C) Physical responses such as increased pulse, blood pressure, and respirations
D) Observed behaviors such as crying, grimacing, guarding, and pulling away
E) The number of persons approached who declined to participate
Answer: A, B, C, D
Rationale: The chapter's mono-operation example names a pain rating scale, verbal reports, physical responses including increased pulse, blood pressure, and respirations, and observed behaviors including crying, grimacing, guarding, and pulling away.
Trap: (E) is the refusal rate. It must be reported, but it belongs to external validity as part of the interaction of selection and intervention, not to measuring the construct.

Subtopic: experimenter expectancy

Q: The principal investigator of a new mobility protocol believes strongly in the protocol and personally collects all outcome data. Which threat is this, and what is the chapter's fix?
A) An internal validity threat (selection); randomly assign participants to groups
B) A statistical conclusion validity threat (low power); increase the sample size
C) An external validity threat (interaction of setting and intervention); add more study sites
D) A construct validity threat (experimenter expectancy); blind the data collectors to group assignment
Answer: D
Rationale: Experimenter expectancy, the Rosenthal effect, means the expectancies of the researcher can bias the data. Using nonbiased data collectors or those blinded to group assignment increases construct design validity, and some researchers choose not to be involved in data collection at all.
Trap: (A) is the common misfile. This text classifies the Rosenthal effect as a construct validity threat, even though it sounds like a rival explanation for the finding and therefore feels like internal validity.

Topic: internal validity
Subtopic: selection and attrition

Q: Day-shift nurses receive a new handoff protocol and night-shift nurses serve as the comparison group, because randomizing individual nurses was not feasible. Which internal validity threat is most prominent?
A) History
B) Maturation
C) Selection
D) Mono-operation bias
Answer: C
Rationale: Selection is the process whereby participants are chosen to take part and how they are grouped within a study. It is more likely to occur in studies in which randomization is not possible, and random assignment decreases the threat.
Trap: (A) requires an unrelated event occurring during the study. Here the problem exists before the first data point, in how the two groups were formed.

Q: Which statements about attrition as a threat to internal validity are accurate? Select all that apply.
A) Attrition is participants dropping out of a study before it is completed
B) Attrition is harmless as long as the number of dropouts is the same in each group
C) It is a threat when the dropouts differ in type from those who remain
D) It is a threat when there is a difference in the number and type of dropouts between groups
E) Attrition greater than 25% could affect the accuracy of the results
Answer: A, C, D, E
Rationale: Attrition is participants dropping out before completion. It threatens internal validity when dropouts differ in type from those who remain, or when the number and type of dropouts differ between groups, and attrition greater than 25% could affect the accuracy of results.
Trap: (B) is the count-only reflex. Equal numbers do not settle it, because attrition is a threat mainly on account of who leaves; equal counts of very different people still bias the comparison.

Subtopic: history and maturation

Q: During a 12-month cardiac rehabilitation support study, several of the nurses who deliver the program quit. Which internal validity threat does this illustrate?
A) Maturation
B) History
C) Attrition
D) Selection
Answer: B
Rationale: History is an event that is not related to the planned study but occurs during the time of the study; the chapter's own example is several nurses quitting during a cardiac rehabilitation support study.
Trap: (C) is the reflex because someone left. Attrition refers to participants dropping out, not staff departing, and staff leaving is an external event acting on the study.

Q: Over an 18-month longitudinal study with repeated measures, participants become stronger and more experienced at the study tasks in ways the researchers neither planned nor noticed. Which threat is this, and where is it most likely?
A) History; most likely in cross-sectional studies
B) Selection; most likely when randomization is used
C) Attrition; most likely when the study period is short
D) Maturation; most likely in longitudinal studies with repeated measures
Answer: D
Rationale: Maturation is growing older, wiser, stronger, hungrier, more tired, or more experienced during the study; the changes are unplanned and unrecognized, and maturation is more likely in longitudinal studies with repeated measures.
Trap: History and maturation are routinely swapped. History is an external event that happens to the study; maturation is change occurring inside the participants themselves.

Topic: external validity
Subtopic: interaction of selection and intervention

Q: A published study reports that 39% of those approached declined to participate. Which threat does this raise, and to which type of design validity?
A) Interaction of selection and intervention; external validity
B) Selection; internal validity
C) Attrition; internal validity
D) Interaction of history and intervention; external validity
Answer: A
Rationale: A high refusal rate limits the sample in non-obvious ways. With 39% declining, those enrolled may be volunteers, do-gooders, or those with nothing better to do, so generalizing to all nurses or patients is not justified, and researchers must report the refusal rate.
Trap: (B) wins on the shared word. The plain selection threat concerns how enrolled participants were chosen and grouped and is an internal validity problem; the refusal rate concerns who never entered at all and is an external validity problem.

Subtopic: limits of generalization

Q: Which statements about external validity are accurate? Select all that apply.
A) With the most serious threat, the findings would be meaningful only for the group studied
B) Generalization is usually narrower for a single study than for multiple replications using different samples, populations, and settings
C) Hospitals seeking Magnet designation may welcome nursing studies while others resist, so the two types of setting may differ in important ways
D) Replicating a study during various time periods allows the findings to be generalized to the future
E) Choosing instruments that are valid and reliable but have fewer items decreases participant burden and helps reduce the selection threat
Answer: A, B, C, E
Rationale: With the most serious threat, findings would be meaningful only for the group studied; generalization is narrower for a single study than for multiple replications; the Magnet example illustrates interaction of setting and intervention; and shorter valid and reliable instruments reduce participant burden and help reduce the selection threat.
Trap: (D) overshoots the chapter's own caution. Logically one can never generalize to the future; replicating during various time periods strengthens the usefulness of findings over time but licenses no claim about it.

Q: Three patients die from dialysis equipment failure during a study of a support intervention for patients receiving dialysis. Which external validity threat does this create for the published findings?
A) Interaction of selection and intervention
B) Interaction of setting and intervention
C) Interaction of history and intervention
D) Maturation
Answer: C
Rationale: Interaction of history and intervention means the circumstances at the time the study was conducted affect generalization; the chapter's example is exactly this dialysis equipment failure during a dialysis support study.
Trap: History appears in both validity lists, so read what the item is asking. As a plain internal validity threat, history asks whether the event explains the result; as an interaction with intervention, it asks whether those circumstances make the findings ungeneralizable to other times.

Topic: statistical conclusion validity
Subtopic: statistical power and Type II error

Q: A study with 18 participants per group finds no significant difference between the intervention and comparison groups. Which threat is most likely, and which error does it produce?
A) Low statistical power; Type II error
B) Low statistical power; Type I error
C) Unreliable measurement methods; Type I error
D) Extraneous variances in the setting; Type I error
Answer: A
Rationale: Low statistical power increases the probability of concluding there is no significant relationship or difference when actually there is one, which is a Type II error. It is most likely when the sample size is small or the power of the statistical test is low.
Trap: (B) keeps the right threat and swaps the error. Low power means missing a real effect, which is Type II; nothing about a small sample manufactures a false positive.

Q: Which of the following is a Type II error as this chapter defines it?
A) Concluding there is a significant difference when in fact there is not one
B) Concluding there is no significant relationship or difference when actually there is one
C) Using only one method of measurement to assess a construct
D) Generalizing findings beyond the sample that was studied
Answer: B
Rationale: A Type II error is concluding there is no significant relationship or difference when actually there is one, and low statistical power increases its probability.
Trap: (A) is the mirror-image error and reads as the more familiar mistake. Sort it by what was missed: a stem describing a false alarm points the other way, while a stem describing a small sample and a null result points to Type II.

Subtopic: measurement and intervention fidelity

Q: A physiological instrument gives the same reading each time the same variable is measured under the same conditions. Which appraisal statement is accurate?
A) The measure is valid, because consistency establishes accuracy
B) The measure demonstrates construct validity, because it matches the conceptual definition
C) The measure demonstrates intervention fidelity
D) The measure is reliable, and a physiological measure that measures consistently is described as precise
Answer: D
Rationale: A measure is reliable if it gives the same result each time the same situation or variable is measured, and physiological measures that measure consistently are precise, as with a thermometer showing the same reading on repeat testing.
Trap: (A) equates consistency with accuracy, which is the single most common inversion here. A consistently wrong scale is still reliable; reliability is sameness of result, not truth of result.

Q: Which of the following are threats to statistical conclusion validity as this chapter lists them? Select all that apply.
A) Low statistical power
B) Unreliable measurement methods
C) Interaction of setting and intervention
D) Limited intervention fidelity
E) Extraneous variances in the setting
Answer: A, B, D, E
Rationale: The chapter lists low statistical power, unreliable measurement methods, limited intervention fidelity, and extraneous variances in the setting. Limited fidelity matters because when administration varies from one person to another, the chance of detecting a true difference decreases, so data collectors must be trained for consistency.
Trap: (C) trades on the shared word setting. Extraneous variance in the setting muddies the analysis and is a statistical conclusion threat; interaction of setting and intervention limits who the findings apply to and is an external validity threat.
