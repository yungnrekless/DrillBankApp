# Chapter 9 drills — Populations and Samples

Authored from `sources/Ch9_Fact_Bank.md` (Grove & Gray, 7th ed.).
Chapter 9 carries its own exam line worth 10 of the final's 75 questions, so the
weight here sits on the two things that line will test: naming a sampling method
from a described scenario, and keeping the population vocabulary straight in both
directions. Topics follow the chapter's own structure — the population funnel,
sampling criteria, representativeness, the four calculable rates, probability
methods, nonprobability methods, sample size and power, and qualitative sampling.

---

Topic: population and sampling terminology
Subtopic: the population funnel

Q: A researcher is studying adult males 18 years and older who have type 2 diabetes and have been hospitalized with a lower-extremity infection. Every adult male anywhere who fits that description belongs to which group?
A) The accessible population
B) The target population
C) The sample
D) The sampling frame
Answer: B
Rationale: The target population is the entire set of individuals or elements who meet the sampling criteria, regardless of whether the researcher can reach them.
Trap: (A) is the reflex answer because the description sounds like the group being studied. The accessible population is only the portion the researcher can reasonably reach.

Q: That same researcher can realistically recruit only from patients with type 2 diabetes admitted to one acute care hospital in Dallas, Texas. Those Dallas patients constitute which group?
A) The target population
B) The element
C) The accessible population
D) The stratum
Answer: C
Rationale: The accessible population is the portion of the target population to which the researcher has reasonable access.
Trap: This is the mirror image of the previous item. Sort it by the question the stem is asking: everyone who meets criteria is the target; whoever the researcher can actually get to is the accessible population.

Q: A study of central line care treats each individual intravenous catheter as one unit of study. What is each catheter called?
A) An element
B) A cluster
C) A stratum
D) A sampling frame
Answer: A
Rationale: An element is a person, event, object, or any other single unit of study. When elements are persons they are called participants or subjects, and participant is the term commonly used in nursing research.

Q: From which group is a study's sample actually obtained, and in what direction does generalization travel?
A) From the target population; findings generalize from the sample to the sampling frame
B) From the accessible population; findings generalize from the accessible population back to the sample
C) From the sampling frame; findings generalize from the target population to the accessible population
D) From the accessible population; findings generalize from the sample to the accessible population and then, more abstractly, to the target population
Answer: D
Rationale: The sample is obtained from the accessible population using a sampling method, and generalization flows back up the funnel: sample to accessible population, then more abstractly to the target population.
Trap: (B) reverses the direction. Generalization always moves from the smaller, studied group outward, never from a population down onto the sample.

Subtopic: generalization

Q: A study of males with type 2 diabetes in a Dallas hospital found a benefit from a nursing intervention. What determines how far the researcher may extend those findings?
A) The size of the accessible population
B) Whether participants were randomly assigned to groups
C) The quality of the study and the consistency of its findings with previous research
D) Whether the refusal rate was below 5%
Answer: C
Rationale: How far findings can be generalized depends on the quality of the study and the consistency of the study's findings with previous research. High quality plus agreement with prior work permits generalization to, for example, males with type 2 diabetes in Texas urban hospitals or urban hospitals in the southern United States.
Trap: (B) is the reflex because random assignment feels like the thing that makes results trustworthy. Random assignment is a design strategy about group equivalence, not about the reach of the findings.

Subtopic: sampling frame

Q: Which statements about a sampling frame are accurate? Select all that apply.
A) It is a list of every member of the population
B) The sampling criteria are used to define who is eligible to appear on the list
C) It is the portion of the target population to which the researcher has reasonable access
D) Probability sampling methods require one
E) HIPAA requirements have increased the difficulty of obtaining a complete list
Answer: A, B, D, E
Rationale: A sampling frame is a list of every member of the population, with the sampling criteria defining eligibility for the list. Probability sampling requires a sampling frame, and HIPAA's requirements to protect health information have made complete frames harder to obtain.
Trap: (C) is the definition of the accessible population, not the sampling frame. A frame is a list of names; an accessible population is a group of people.

Topic: sampling criteria
Subtopic: inclusion versus exclusion criteria

Q: In the chapter's study of preoperative teaching about early ambulation in older adults undergoing knee replacement, which were inclusion sampling criteria? Select all that apply.
A) History of previous joint replacement surgery
B) Adults 60 years of age or older
C) Diagnosis of dementia
D) Able to speak and read English
E) Undergoing surgical replacement of one knee joint
Answer: B, D, E
Rationale: The inclusion criteria were adults 60 years or older, able to speak and read English, undergoing surgical replacement of one knee joint.
Trap: (A) and (C) are the exclusion criteria from the same study, along with debilitating chronic muscle disease. Both lists come from one worked example, which is exactly why they get blended together.

Q: A 68-year-old who speaks and reads English is scheduled for replacement of one knee joint but carries a diagnosis of dementia. How is this person best described for that study?
A) A member of the accessible but not the target population
B) Someone who meets the inclusion criteria but is removed by an exclusion criterion
C) Someone who fails to meet the inclusion criteria
D) An eligible participant, because dementia is only a demographic variable
Answer: B
Rationale: Exclusion criteria are the characteristics that cause a person who meets the inclusion criteria to be excluded or removed from the target population. This person satisfies all three inclusion criteria and is then removed by one exclusion criterion.
Trap: (C) is the common misread. Failing an inclusion criterion and being caught by an exclusion criterion are two different events, and this person fails no inclusion criterion.

Q: Which statement correctly defines exclusion sampling criteria?
A) Any characteristic not named among the inclusion criteria
B) The characteristics that define the accessible population
C) The characteristics an element must possess to belong to the target population
D) Characteristics that can cause a person who meets the inclusion criteria to be excluded or removed from the target population
Answer: D
Rationale: Exclusion criteria act only on people who already meet the inclusion criteria, removing them from the target population.
Trap: (A) is the classic inversion — simply not meeting an inclusion criterion is not an exclusion criterion. (C) is the definition of inclusion criteria, which is the other half of the pair students flip.

Q: A researcher writes very narrow and restrictive sampling criteria. What is the stated consequence?
A) Difficulty obtaining an adequately sized sample, which can limit the generalization of the findings
B) A more heterogeneous sample with a broader range of values
C) Elimination of systematic variation in the sample
D) A guaranteed increase in the acceptance rate
Answer: A
Rationale: Criteria should produce a sample that is heterogeneous with a broad range of values; if the criteria are too narrow and restrictive, researchers may have difficulty obtaining an adequately sized sample, which can limit generalization.
Trap: (B) inverts the trade-off. Narrowing criteria makes a sample more homogeneous, not more heterogeneous.

Q: A study enrolled only English speakers because the teaching materials and the instruments were written in English, yet the discussion recommends the intervention for all postoperative patients. What is the appraisal problem?
A) The exclusion criteria were too broad
B) The accessible population exceeded the target population
C) The researchers generalized beyond their sampling criteria
D) The sampling frame was incomplete
Answer: C
Rationale: The chapter cautions that researchers sometimes generalize beyond their sampling criteria; non-English-speaking persons come from another culture and may not respond to the teaching in the same way. Examine the inclusion and exclusion criteria to judge whether a generalization is appropriate.
Trap: (A) is tempting because an English-language requirement does restrict the sample, but the criterion itself was justified by the study materials. The error is in the discussion, not the criteria.

Topic: sample representativeness
Subtopic: random versus systematic variation

Q: Every participant in a knowledge study has an IQ above 120, while the population they came from has a mean IQ near 100. Their test scores run consistently higher than the population mean. What is this?
A) Random variation
B) Systematic variation
C) Sampling error created by proportional allocation
D) Attrition bias
Answer: B
Rationale: Systematic variation, or systematic bias, is a consequence of selecting participants whose measurement values differ from those of the population in some specific way, with variation from the mean mostly in the same direction.

Q: A reviewer suggests that a study with a biased recruitment process fix the problem by enrolling twice as many participants. What is the best response?
A) Increasing sample size decreases random variation but has no effect on systematic variation
B) Increasing sample size corrects both random and systematic variation
C) Increasing sample size converts a convenience sample into a probability sample
D) Random assignment to groups would remove the systematic variation instead
Answer: A
Rationale: Increasing the sample size decreases random variation and improves representativeness, but increasing the sample size has no effect on systematic variation.
Trap: (B) is the reflex answer because a bigger sample feels like it fixes everything. This is the chapter's sharpest point: a biased selection process is not diluted by adding more people selected the same biased way.

Q: In another study, participants' scores fall above and below the mean in both directions by the amount expected among different people from the same sample. What is this, and what does a larger sample do to it?
A) Systematic bias; a larger sample will not change it
B) Sampling error; a larger sample has no effect
C) Systematic variation; a larger sample increases it
D) Random variation; a larger sample decreases it, placing more values near the mean and improving representativeness
Answer: D
Rationale: Random variation is the expected difference in values that occurs when different participants from the same sample are examined, scattering both above and below the mean. Increasing sample size decreases it and improves representativeness.
Trap: This is the mirror of the previous item. Read the direction word in the stem: scattered both ways means random; mostly one direction means systematic.

Q: Why is systematic bias especially dangerous in quasi-experimental and experimental studies?
A) It lowers the acceptance rate below the acceptable range
B) It makes the attrition rate impossible to calculate
C) It can lead the researcher to conclude that the treatment made a difference when the values would have been different even without the treatment
D) It drives power above 0.8 and inflates the Type I error rate
Answer: C
Rationale: When systematic bias occurs in quasi-experimental or experimental studies, it can lead the researcher to conclude that the treatment has made a difference when in actuality the values would have been different even without the treatment.

Subtopic: evaluating representativeness

Q: Which statements about evaluating the representativeness of a sample are accurate? Select all that apply.
A) Representativeness is evaluated in the setting, the characteristics of the participants, and the distribution of values on the measured variables
B) Studies drawing on large databases produce less representative samples than single-site studies
C) Private hospitals are less likely to recruit participants with low incomes
D) People who do not have access to care are usually excluded from studies
E) Researchers who gather data across a variety of settings have a more representative sample than those using a single setting
Answer: A, C, D, E
Rationale: Representativeness must be evaluated in three areas — setting, participant characteristics, and distribution of values. Private hospitals recruit fewer low-income participants, people without access to care are usually excluded, and multiple settings yield a more representative sample. Sample characteristics come from the demographic or attribute variables and must reasonably represent the population; when a study has groups, those groups must have comparable demographics.
Trap: (B) is the inversion — large databases produce more representative samples, not less.

Topic: sampling rates and attrition
Subtopic: calculating the four rates

Q: Investigators approached 80 eligible patients about a preoperative teaching study and 4 declined to participate. What is the refusal rate?
A) 4%
B) 5%
C) 8%
D) 20%
Answer: B
Rationale: Refusal rate equals the number refusing divided by the number approached, times 100 — 4 divided by 80 equals 5%.

Q: In that same study, 76 of the 80 patients approached consented. What is the acceptance rate, and how should it be appraised?
A) 76%, a study weakness
B) 5%, a study strength
C) 95%, but it cannot be appraised without the retention rate
D) 95%, a study strength
Answer: D
Rationale: Acceptance rate equals the number accepting divided by the number approached — 76 divided by 80 equals 95%, which the chapter identifies as a study strength. Acceptance rate also equals 100% minus the refusal rate.
Trap: (A) uses the raw count of acceptors as if it were a percentage. The denominator is always the number approached, not 100.

Q: That study began with a sample of 76 participants, and 31 of them withdrew before completion. What is the attrition rate?
A) 31%
B) 39%
C) 41%
D) 59%
Answer: C
Rationale: Attrition rate equals the number withdrawing divided by the sample size, times 100 — 31 divided by 76 equals 41%.
Trap: (A) treats the raw number withdrawing as the percentage, and (D) is the retention rate. Attrition and retention are complements, so having one does not mean you may report the other figure as the answer.

Q: Of the 76 participants who entered that study, 45 completed it. What is the retention rate?
A) 59%
B) 41%
C) 45%
D) 76%
Answer: A
Rationale: Retention rate equals the number completing divided by the sample size — 45 divided by 76 equals 59%, which is also 100% minus the 41% attrition rate.

Subtopic: interpreting attrition

Q: A study reports 41% attrition overall, with 12 of 38 (32%) withdrawing from the treatment group and 19 of 38 (50%) withdrawing from the comparison group. Which conclusions are supported? Select all that apply.
A) The study has strong potential for systematic variation
B) Systematic variation is greatest when a large number withdraws, or when a large number withdraws from one group but not the others
C) Attrition is never higher in a treatment group than in a comparison group
D) Comparison group participants may be more likely to withdraw because they receive no treatment
E) A higher retention rate would make the results more likely to be an accurate reflection of reality
Answer: A, B, D, E
Rationale: This study has strong potential for systematic variation because attrition was 41% and the comparison group's 50% exceeded the treatment group's 32%. The higher the retention rate, the more representative the sample and the more likely the results reflect reality.
Trap: (C) overstates the rule. The chapter says attrition can be higher in the treatment group when the intervention is complex or time-consuming, so the direction of differential attrition is not fixed.

Q: Which statements about calculating and reporting the sampling rates are accurate? Select all that apply.
A) A high refusal rate with a low acceptance rate indicates less chance for systematic variation
B) Refusal and acceptance rates use the number of people approached as the denominator
C) Attrition and retention rates use the sample size as the denominator
D) Researchers usually report either the refusal rate or the acceptance rate, but not both
E) Best practice is to report the number withdrawing, the attrition rate, and the rationales for withdrawal
Answer: B, C, D, E
Rationale: The two recruitment rates are figured on the number approached and the two completion rates on the sample size; researchers report one of each pair, since each is the complement of the other.
Trap: (A) inverts the interpretation rule. It is a high acceptance rate with a low refusal rate that means less chance for systematic variation and a sample more likely to be representative.

Topic: probability sampling methods
Subtopic: systematic sampling

Q: A researcher has an ordered list of 1200 clinic patients and wants a sample of 100. She uses a random procedure to choose the starting name and then takes every 12th name thereafter. Which sampling method is this, and where did the 12 come from?
A) Simple random sampling; 12 was chosen arbitrarily
B) Systematic sampling; k equals the population size divided by the desired sample size
C) Cluster sampling; 12 is the number of clusters
D) Stratified random sampling; 12 is the number of strata
Answer: B
Rationale: Systematic sampling selects every kth individual on a list from a randomly selected starting point, with k equal to population size divided by desired sample size — 1200 divided by 100 equals 12.

Q: Which statement about systematic sampling is accurate?
A) It gives every element an equal and random chance of inclusion, making it equivalent to simple random sampling
B) It can be carried out without a sampling frame
C) It is a nonprobability method under all conditions
D) If the initial starting point is not random, the sample is a nonprobability sample
Answer: D
Rationale: Systematic sampling qualifies as probability sampling only when the starting point is randomly selected, and it provides a random but not equal chance for inclusion.
Trap: (A) is the reflex because the word random appears in the method's description. A random but not equal chance is not the same as simple random sampling's equal chance.

Subtopic: stratified random sampling

Q: A researcher knows that ethnicity is a variable critical to achieving representativeness for her outcome and wants every level of it adequately represented in the sample. Which method fits?
A) Cluster sampling
B) Quota sampling
C) Stratified random sampling
D) Convenience sampling
Answer: C
Rationale: Stratified random sampling is used when the researcher knows some of the variables in the population that are critical for achieving representativeness; it ensures all levels of the variable are adequately represented and achieves that representativeness with a smaller sample than simple random sampling would need.
Trap: (B) is the nonprobability lookalike. Quota sampling also fills subgroups, but its resemblance to stratified random sampling is in the stratification, not in the randomness.

Q: A researcher stratifies by four age bands and enrolls exactly 25 participants in each, although the bands are very different sizes in the population. What is the consequence?
A) The smaller groups are overrepresented, which can create sampling error
B) The larger groups are overrepresented
C) Representativeness is guaranteed because all levels are present
D) The sample becomes proportional to the population
Answer: A
Rationale: With equal numbers of study participants in each group, the smaller groups are overrepresented, which can create sampling error. Proportional allocation instead matches each stratum's share of the population.
Trap: (C) is the reflex because equal groups feel fair. Equal allocation is one of two options, and it is the one that distorts the population's proportions.

Subtopic: cluster sampling

Q: A researcher needs a nationally dispersed sample of hospital nurses, travel to individual sites would be prohibitively expensive, and no list of all eligible nurses can be obtained. Which method fits, and why?
A) Simple random sampling, because it is the most basic probability plan
B) Cluster sampling, because a geographically dispersed sample is needed at prohibitive travel cost and individual elements cannot be listed
C) Cluster sampling, because it produces a more representative sample than any other probability method
D) Systematic sampling, because it does not require a sampling frame
Answer: B
Rationale: Cluster sampling is used for two stated reasons: a geographically dispersed sample is needed but travel cost is prohibitive, or individual elements cannot be listed so no sampling frame exists. It builds a frame of states, cities, institutions, or clinicians and samples those randomly, with several stages called multistage sampling.
Trap: (C) is the reflex answer because cluster sampling is a probability method and probability methods improve representativeness. Choosing it purely for representativeness misses both of its actual rationales.

Subtopic: comparing probability methods

Q: Which statements about simple random sampling are accurate? Select all that apply.
A) It is the most basic of the probability sampling plans
B) Elements may be selected using a computer program, which is the most common technique
C) A table of random numbers is useful only when the population number is less than 100
D) The researcher must identify strata before selecting elements
E) Drawing names from a container is one acceptable technique
Answer: A, B, C, E
Rationale: Simple random sampling randomly selects elements from the sampling frame using names in a container, a computer program, or a table of random numbers, with the computer program most common and the random numbers table useful only for populations under 100.
Trap: (D) imports stratified random sampling's requirement. Simple random sampling does not define strata.

Q: Which statements about probability sampling are accurate? Select all that apply.
A) Probability sampling methods are designed to increase representativeness and decrease systematic bias
B) A sampling frame is unnecessary when probability sampling is used
C) Each person or element in the population has an opportunity to be selected
D) Without random selection, researchers might consciously or unconsciously select individuals whose conditions or behaviors are consistent with the study hypotheses
E) Random sampling increases the validity of the study findings
Answer: A, C, D, E
Rationale: Random selection leaves the choice of participants to chance rather than to a researcher who might exclude people for being too sick, not sick enough, coping too well, uncooperative, or noncompliant, thereby increasing the validity of the findings.
Trap: (B) is the inversion — a sampling frame is required for probability sampling, and HIPAA has made complete frames harder to obtain.

Topic: nonprobability sampling methods
Subtopic: convenience sampling

Q: A nurse researcher enrolls every patient who happens to be admitted to her unit during her scheduled shifts, continuing until she reaches her desired sample size of 60. Which sampling method is this?
A) Systematic sampling
B) Convenience (accidental) sampling
C) Quota sampling
D) Purposeful sampling
Answer: B
Rationale: In convenience sampling, also called accidental sampling, participants are included merely because they happen to be in the right place at the right time, and enrollment continues until the desired sample size is reached.
Trap: (A) is tempting because enrolling in admission order sounds orderly and systematic. Systematic sampling requires an existing list, a computed k, and a random starting point.

Q: Which statements about convenience sampling are accurate? Select all that apply.
A) Any study using a convenience sample is invalid and should not inform practice
B) It provides little opportunity to control for biases and is the weakest sampling approach
C) Multiple biases may exist, some of which may be subtle and unrecognized
D) Serious biases are not always present in convenience samples
E) A convenience sample is acceptable when it is used with reasonable knowledge and care
Answer: B, C, D, E
Rationale: The chapter gives a balanced view — convenience sampling is the weakest approach and carries subtle unrecognized biases, but serious biases are not always present and it is acceptable when used with reasonable knowledge and care. It is inexpensive, accessible, less time-consuming, and lets researchers study nursing interventions when probability sampling is not possible.
Trap: (A) overstates the text. Many quasi-experimental studies and randomized controlled trials in nursing and medicine use convenience sampling; calling all of them invalid is a stronger claim than the chapter makes.

Q: A convenience sample is randomly assigned to a treatment group and a comparison group. What does the random assignment accomplish?
A) It makes the convenience sample representative of the target population
B) It converts the convenience sample into a probability sample
C) It strengthens the equivalence of the study groups but does not alter the risk of biases resulting from convenience sampling
D) It removes the need for a power analysis
Answer: C
Rationale: Random assignment to groups is not a sampling method but a design strategy; it does not alter the risk of biases resulting from convenience sampling but does strengthen the equivalence of the study groups.
Trap: (A) and (B) are the same reflex in two costumes — hearing the word random and crediting it to the sampling plan. Assignment happens after sampling and cannot repair how the sample was obtained.

Q: A team wants to strengthen the representativeness of a convenience sample. What does the chapter direct them to do?
A) Increase the sample size
B) Randomly assign participants to groups
C) Add more exclusion criteria
D) Report the effect size along with the findings
Answer: A
Rationale: To strengthen representativeness in a convenience-sampled study, researchers increase the sample size.
Trap: (C) moves in the wrong direction entirely — more restrictive criteria shrink the pool and can limit generalization.

Subtopic: quota sampling

Q: A researcher continues to enroll available patients but adds a strategy that fills a preset number of slots for adults over 65, chosen to replicate their proportion in the target population. Which sampling method is this, and how is it classified?
A) Stratified random sampling, a probability method
B) Cluster sampling, a probability method
C) Network sampling, a nonprobability method
D) Quota sampling, a nonprobability method
Answer: D
Rationale: Quota sampling uses a convenience sampling technique with an added feature — a strategy to ensure the inclusion of participant types likely to be underrepresented, such as females, minority groups, older adults, and the poor, rich, and undereducated — with the goal of replicating subgroup proportions in the target population. It offers an improvement in representativeness over convenience sampling.
Trap: (A) is the trap the chapter sets up by saying quota sampling is similar to stratified random sampling. The similarity is in identifying subgroups and proportions; the underlying selection is still convenience, so it never becomes a probability method.

Topic: sample size and power
Subtopic: power analysis

Q: Which statements about power in quantitative studies are accurate? Select all that apply.
A) Power is the ability of a study to detect differences or relationships that actually exist in the population
B) Power is the ability to reject a null hypothesis correctly
C) Alpha, the level of significance, is frequently set at 0.8 in nursing studies
D) The minimum acceptable level of power is 0.8
E) Power at that minimum level leaves a 20% chance of a Type II error
Answer: A, B, D, E
Rationale: Power is the ability to detect differences or relationships that actually exist, which is the ability to reject a null hypothesis correctly. The minimum acceptable power is 0.8, which results in a 20% chance of a Type II error.
Trap: (C) swaps the chapter's two numbers. Alpha is frequently set at 0.05; 0.8 is the power standard. Attaching the 20% risk to Type I error instead of Type II is the other half of this swap.

Q: A study reports no significant difference between its groups and includes no power analysis. What appraisal question does the chapter direct you to ask?
A) Whether alpha should have been set at 0.01
B) Whether there is really no difference, or whether an actual difference was not found because of inadequacies in the research methods such as small sample size
C) Whether a Type I error occurred
D) Whether the effect size was too large for the design
Answer: B
Rationale: Researchers must perform a power analysis to evaluate sample adequacy for all nonsignificant findings and report it in the discussion section, while the study's planned power analysis belongs in the sample section. Low-powered studies increase the risk of a Type II error.
Trap: (C) names the wrong error. A nonsignificant result that may be wrong is a missed real effect, which is Type II, not Type I.

Q: In a review of 103 randomized controlled trials, power analyses were reported for 81 of them (79%). What did the review find about those reporting studies?
A) All 81 enrolled the number of participants their power analysis required
B) 81% of them had power below 0.8
C) 24 of them (30%) did not include the required number of participants indicated by their own power calculations
D) 30 of them (24%) failed to state an alpha level
Answer: C
Rationale: Of the 81 studies reporting power analyses, 24 (30%) did not include the required number of participants indicated by their own power calculations.
Trap: (D) reverses the count and the percentage. The figure is 24 studies representing 30% of the 81, not 30 studies representing 24%.

Subtopic: factors influencing sample size

Q: A researcher expects a large difference between the treatment and comparison groups on the outcome. What is the effect on the sample size needed?
A) A larger sample is needed
B) Sample size is unaffected by the expected difference
C) Power must be reduced to compensate
D) A smaller sample is needed
Answer: D
Rationale: Effect size and sample size are inversely related: the larger the effect size, the smaller the sample needed. Effect size is the extent to which the null hypothesis is false — the statistical test tells you whether a difference exists, and the effect size tells you how big it is.
Trap: (A) is the reflex because bigger usually means more. Here it is the opposite: a large, obvious effect is easy to detect with few participants.

Q: Which of these studies is likely to require the largest sample?
A) A descriptive survey
B) A quasi-experimental study of a nursing intervention
C) An experimental study using matched pairs
D) A tightly controlled experimental study
Answer: A
Rationale: Descriptive studies, especially surveys, and correlational studies need very large samples, while quasi-experimental and experimental studies use smaller samples — as control increases, the sample size can be decreased. Matched pairs increase power and further decrease the sample size needed.
Trap: This is counterintuitive, which is why it is tested. Students assume an experiment, being the more demanding design, must need more people; the control built into the design is what lets the sample shrink.

Q: Which statements about the factors influencing needed sample size are accurate? Select all that apply.
A) Studies analyzing more variables require larger samples
B) Demographic variables used only to describe the sample create a power problem
C) More precise and sensitive instruments allow a smaller sample
D) Analyses at the interval and ratio levels are more powerful than those at the nominal and ordinal levels
E) For t-tests and analysis of variance, equal group sizes increase power
Answer: A, C, D, E
Rationale: More variables, including multiple dependent variables, increase the needed sample size; less-developed tools produce more variance from the true score, and as variance in instrument scores increases, the sample size needed to obtain significance increases. Larger samples are needed when the power of the planned statistical analysis is weak.
Trap: (B) misses the chapter's explicit exception — using demographic variables only to describe the sample does not cause a power problem. Only variables entered into the analyses to answer questions or test hypotheses drive the required sample size up.

Topic: qualitative sampling
Subtopic: matching method to purpose

Q: A qualitative researcher wants in-depth understanding of a complex experience in a new area and deliberately selects the participants she judges most able to speak to it. Which sampling method is this?
A) Purposeful (purposive) sampling
B) Quota sampling
C) Theoretical sampling
D) Convenience sampling
Answer: A
Rationale: In purposeful sampling, also called purposive, judgmental, or selective sampling, the researcher consciously selects certain participants, elements, events, or incidents, with the ultimate goal of selecting information-rich cases. It is criticized because the accuracy or relevance of the researcher's judgment is difficult to evaluate, so researchers must report the characteristics they desired and their rationale for selecting them.
Trap: (D) is the reflex because both methods lack randomness. Convenience takes whoever is available; purposeful takes whoever the researcher judges most informative.

Q: A researcher studying adults with a history of criminal conviction asks each enrolled participant to help identify others who might take part. Which sampling method is this?
A) Purposeful sampling
B) Theoretical sampling
C) Quota sampling
D) Network (snowball) sampling
Answer: D
Rationale: Network sampling takes advantage of social networks and the fact that friends tend to have characteristics in common, and is used for participants who are difficult or impossible to obtain in other ways.
Trap: (A) is close, since researchers often begin with purposeful or convenience sampling and then expand using network sampling. The defining feature here is that existing participants locate the next ones.

Q: A grounded theory researcher continues recruiting anyone who can provide relevant, varied, rich information and stops only when the codes are saturated. Which method is this, and what does saturation mean?
A) Purposeful sampling; saturation means the planned sample size has been reached
B) Theoretical sampling; a code is saturated when it is complete and the researcher can see how it fits in the theory
C) Network sampling; saturation means no further participants can be located
D) Maximum variation sampling; saturation means the cases are maximally heterogeneous
Answer: B
Rationale: Theoretical sampling is used in qualitative research to develop a selected theory or model and is most frequent in grounded theory; diversity in the sample is encouraged so the theory covers a wide range of behaviors in varied situations. When a code or concept is unclear, the researcher continues to seek participants and gather data.
Trap: (D) borrows a real term from the chapter — in maximum variation the researcher selects a small number of cases with maximum variation relevant to the research question — but attaches the wrong definition of saturation to it.

Q: Which statements about network sampling are accurate? Select all that apply.
A) It is also called snowball, chain, or nominated sampling
B) It takes advantage of social networks and the fact that friends tend to have characteristics in common
C) It is useful for reaching socially devalued populations such as people with alcohol dependence or a history of child abuse
D) It is a probability method because participants are nominated at random
E) It reaches groups outside the healthcare system, such as widows, grieving siblings, and people successful at lifestyle changes
Answer: A, B, C, E
Rationale: Network sampling is used for participants difficult or impossible to obtain in other ways, including socially devalued populations such as those involving alcohol dependence, child abuse, sexual offenses, drug addiction, and criminal acts, and groups outside the healthcare system.

Subtopic: sample size in qualitative studies

Q: Which statements about sample size in qualitative studies are accurate? Select all that apply.
A) A study with a broader scope requires more participants
B) A topic that is difficult to define and awkward for people to discuss requires more participants
C) Qualitative studies always require small samples regardless of scope or topic
D) Richer, higher quality data reduce the number of participants needed
E) The design of the study influences the number of participants needed
Answer: A, B, D, E
Rationale: Four factors govern qualitative sample size — scope of the study, nature of the topic, quality of the data, and design of the study. A study of living with chronic illness in older adulthood would need a large sample, while the lived experience of adults over 60 with Parkinson disease could give credible findings with a smaller one. The sample is adequate when saturation is reached and themes emerge.
Trap: (C) is the assumption most students carry into this section. Sensitive topics named in the chapter — medication administration errors, gender identity, loss of a child, history of child sexual abuse — are exactly the ones that require more participants, not fewer.
