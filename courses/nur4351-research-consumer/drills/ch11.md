# Chapter 11 drills — Understanding Statistics in Research

Authored from `sources/Ch11_Fact_Bank.md` (Grove & Gray, 7th ed.).
Topics follow the chapter's own structure: the significance machinery (alpha, p,
decision theory), the two errors and the power that controls one of them, the
normal curve, descriptive statistics, correlation, and the test-selection
algorithm. Because Type I/Type II and the parametric/nonparametric and
t-test/ANOVA pairs all invert easily, each is asked from both directions.

---

Topic: probability and statistical significance
Subtopic: decision theory

Q: A researcher comparing an intervention group and a control group begins from the assumption that both groups are components of the same population relative to the study variables. Which framework is being applied, and how is that assumption stated?
A) Probability theory, stated as the effect size
B) Decision theory, stated as the null hypothesis
C) Probability theory, stated as the research hypothesis
D) Decision theory, stated as the confidence interval
Answer: B
Rationale: Decision theory assumes that all of the groups in a study are components of the same population relative to the variables under study, an assumption expressed as the null hypothesis, which states there is no difference between or among the groups.
Trap: Probability theory is the other named framework, but it is used to explain the extent of a relationship or the probability that an event will occur or be accurately predicted. It is not the same-population assumption, and it is up to the researcher to provide evidence for a genuine difference.

Subtopic: alpha and p values

Q: An analysis reports p = 0.23. What does that value mean?
A) The two groups differ by 23 percentage points
B) 23% of participants fell outside the confidence interval
C) There is a 23% probability that the outcome occurs
D) The result is significant at the 0.23 level
Answer: C
Rationale: Probability ranges from 0 to 1 and can be expressed as a decimal or a percentage; p = 0.23 means a 23% probability that the outcome occurs.

Q: In a published study, which value reveals the risk of a Type I error in that particular study?
A) The effect size
B) The power level
C) Beta
D) The probability (p) value
Answer: D
Rationale: The chapter's summary is explicit: the probability (p) value reveals the risk of a Type I error in a particular study.
Trap: Students routinely attach p to Type II risk. Type II risk is the province of power and beta; p speaks only to the chance of calling something significant when it is not.

Q: Which value, established before the study begins, reveals whether the probability value met the cutoff point?
A) The alpha (level of statistical significance)
B) The probability (p) value
C) The coefficient of determination
D) The standard deviation
Answer: A
Rationale: The alpha value, set before the study, reveals whether the probability value met the cutoff point; alpha is the probability level at which results are judged to indicate a statistically significant difference between groups.
Trap: This is the mirror of the previous item. Sort it by timing: alpha is chosen in advance and is a threshold; p comes out of the analysis and is a risk estimate.

Q: Which statements about the alpha level in a nursing study are accurate? Select all that apply.
A) It is set before data collection begins
B) The level of significance selected for most nursing studies is 0.05
C) It is calculated from the sample data once the analysis is complete
D) If p is less than or equal to alpha, the groups are considered significantly different, that is, members of different populations
E) It reveals whether the probability value met the cutoff point
Answer: A, B, D, E
Rationale: Alpha is set before data collection, is 0.05 for most nursing studies, and functions as the cutoff: p at or below it means the groups are considered significantly different and therefore members of different populations.
Trap: (C) inverts the whole logic. Nothing about alpha is derived from the data; a cutoff chosen after seeing the results is not a cutoff.

Subtopic: degrees of significance

Q: A researcher set alpha at 0.05 and obtained p = 0.051. The report states the findings "approached significance." How should a consumer of research evaluate this statement?
A) Acceptable, because 0.051 rounds to 0.05
B) Acceptable, provided the sample was large enough for adequate power
C) Inappropriate; decision theory requires an absolute cutoff, so the groups are not significantly different
D) Inappropriate; the researcher should reset alpha to 0.10 and reanalyze
Answer: C
Rationale: Decision theory requires that the cutoff point selected for a study be absolute, and the chapter calls it inappropriate to state that findings approached significance at p = 0.051 when alpha was 0.05. Under decision theory that result means the groups are not significantly different.
Trap: "Approached significance" sounds like careful hedging, which is exactly why it survives in published abstracts. The chapter names this phrase specifically as the error.

Q: A study set alpha at 0.05. One comparison yields p = 0.001 and another yields p = 0.05. Which statements are accurate? Select all that apply.
A) Both comparisons are statistically significant
B) The p = 0.001 comparison is more significant than the p = 0.05 comparison
C) The level of significance is dichotomous, so there are no degrees of significance
D) From a probability standpoint, the chance the groups are from the same population is 1 in 1000 at p = 0.001 and 5 in 100 at p = 0.05
E) The p = 0.05 comparison should be reported as approaching significance
Answer: A, C, D
Rationale: Once alpha is set at 0.05, a result of 0.001 is not considered more significant than 0.05 — the level of significance is dichotomous, with no degrees of significance. From a probability-theory perspective the two still differ in Type I error risk: 1 in 1000 versus 5 in 100.
Trap: (B) is the reflex answer because the two p values genuinely do differ in Type I risk. The distinction the chapter draws is that they differ in risk, not in significance. And p = 0.05 with alpha at 0.05 is significant, not "approaching" anything.

Topic: type I and type II error
Subtopic: identifying the error

Q: A study concludes that a fall-prevention bundle significantly reduced falls. In fact the null hypothesis was true and there is no difference between the groups. What has occurred?
A) A Type I error
B) A Type II error
C) A correct decision, because the p value met alpha
D) A power failure, because power fell below 0.80
Answer: A
Rationale: A Type I error occurs when the null hypothesis is rejected when it is true — saying something is significant when it is not.
Trap: The tell is which hypothesis was true and what the researcher did with it. Rejecting a true null is Type I; the significance of the p value is irrelevant to naming the error.

Q: A study reports no significant difference in pressure injury rates between two turning schedules. A later, larger trial establishes that a real difference does exist. Which error did the first study make, and by which symbol is it represented?
A) Type I error, symbolized by alpha
B) Type I error, symbolized by beta
C) Type II error, symbolized by alpha
D) Type II error, symbolized by beta
Answer: D
Rationale: A Type II error occurs when the null hypothesis is regarded as true but is in fact false — saying something is not significant when it is. It is symbolized by beta.
Trap: This is the mirror of the previous item. Alpha belongs to Type I, beta to Type II; pairing the right error name with the wrong Greek letter is the standard half-credit trap.

Q: In 5% of statistical tests, the value of one of the groups can be expected to fall outside the 95% range and yet still belong to the same population. What is this situation called?
A) A nondirectional result
B) A Type I error
C) A Type II error
D) An effect size of zero
Answer: B
Rationale: If groups come from the same population their values fall within the 95% range; a value outside it leads to concluding they belong to different populations. In 5% of tests that conclusion is wrong while the groups still belong to the same population, which is a Type I error.
Trap: The 5% here is the same 5% as alpha = 0.05. That is why the level of significance is what indicates Type I risk.

Subtopic: alpha and the error tradeoff

Q: A researcher changes the level of significance from 0.05 to 0.01 to be more conservative. What is the consequence?
A) Both Type I and Type II risk decrease
B) Both Type I and Type II risk increase
C) Type I risk decreases and Type II risk increases
D) Type I risk increases and Type II risk decreases
Answer: C
Rationale: The risk of a Type I error is greater at 0.05 (5 in 100) than at 0.01 (1 in 100), while the risk of a Type II error is greater at 0.01 than at 0.05. Tightening alpha trades one error for the other.
Trap: (A) is the reflex answer because "more conservative" sounds like it should be safer in every direction. Any option claiming a stricter alpha reduces both errors is wrong.

Q: Compared with an alpha of 0.01, an alpha of 0.05 carries which risk profile?
A) Less Type I risk and more Type II risk
B) More Type I risk, at 5 in 100 rather than 1 in 100, and less Type II risk
C) More Type I risk and more Type II risk
D) Identical risk, because significance is dichotomous
Answer: B
Rationale: At alpha = 0.05 the chance of a Type I error is 5 in 100, versus 1 in 100 at 0.01; Type II risk moves in the opposite direction and is greater at 0.01.
Trap: This is the same tradeoff run backward from the previous item. (D) misapplies a true statement — significance is dichotomous, but that says nothing about error risk across different alpha levels.

Subtopic: type II risk in nursing research

Q: The chapter concludes that the risk of a Type II error is often high in nursing studies. Which reasons does it give? Select all that apply.
A) Many nursing studies are conducted with small samples
B) Instruments frequently do not accurately and precisely measure the variables
C) Multiple variables interact, and examining only a few can let small differences between groups be overlooked
D) Type II errors are often caused by flaws in the research methods
E) Nursing studies conventionally set alpha at 0.001 rather than 0.05
Answer: A, B, C, D
Rationale: The chapter attributes Type II errors to flaws in research methods, and names small samples, imprecise instruments, and multiple interacting variables of which only a few are examined as the reasons Type II risk is often high in nursing studies.
Trap: (E) is false on its own terms — 0.05 is the conventional nursing alpha — and it also reverses the tradeoff, since a stricter alpha would raise Type II risk rather than being an argument against it.

Topic: power and effect size
Subtopic: power analysis

Q: Which elements are the four parameters of a power analysis, any three of which allow the fourth to be calculated? Select all that apply.
A) Power
B) Level of significance
C) Effect size
D) Sample size
E) Level of measurement
Answer: A, B, C, D
Rationale: Cohen's four parameters of a power analysis are power, level of significance, effect size, and sample size; if three of the four are known, the fourth can be calculated.
Trap: (E) is a real and heavily tested concept in this chapter, but it belongs to test selection, not power analysis.

Q: Which statement defines power?
A) The probability level at which results are judged to indicate a statistically significant difference
B) The probability that a statistical test will detect a significant difference that exists
C) The degree to which the phenomenon is present in the population
D) The probability that the null hypothesis is rejected when it is true
Answer: B
Rationale: Power is the probability that a statistical test will detect a significant difference that exists.
Trap: Every distractor is a real chapter definition attached to the wrong term — (A) is alpha, (C) is effect size, (D) is a Type I error.

Subtopic: effect size and sample size

Q: A researcher planning a nursing intervention study can expect only a small effect size. What sample size does the chapter indicate is often needed to detect a significant difference?
A) 30 or more
B) 50 or more
C) 100 or more
D) 200 or more
Answer: D
Rationale: In many nursing studies only small effect sizes can be expected, and with a small effect a sample of 200 or more is often needed to detect a significant difference.
Trap: Small effect sizes themselves arise from small samples, weak study designs, and instruments that measure only large changes — so the fix is not simply to accept the small effect and proceed.

Subtopic: interpreting nonsignificant findings

Q: A study fails to reject the null hypothesis and reports a power level of 0.65. How should a consumer of research respond?
A) Question the validity of the nonsignificant findings, because power is below 0.80
B) Accept the nonsignificant findings, because power above 0.50 is adequate
C) Conclude that a Type I error occurred
D) Conclude that alpha was set too low
Answer: A
Rationale: The power level should be discussed in studies that fail to reject the null hypothesis, and if power is below 0.80 you need to question the validity of the nonsignificant findings. The minimum acceptable power is 0.80.
Trap: (C) inverts the error. A nonsignificant finding that may be wrong is a candidate Type II error, not a Type I error.

Topic: the normal curve and tailedness
Subtopic: standard deviation landmarks

Q: A student claims that the height data collected from one nursing class "fits the normal curve exactly." Why is that claim inaccurate?
A) The normal curve is a theoretical frequency distribution of all possible values in a population, and no real distribution exactly fits it
B) The normal curve applies only to nominal-level data
C) The normal curve requires a sample of at least 200 before it can be applied
D) The normal curve describes samples rather than populations, so populations fit it less well
Answer: A
Rationale: The normal curve is a theoretical frequency distribution of all possible values in a population; however, no real distribution exactly fits the normal curve. It was developed by Johann Gauss in 1795 at age 18.
Trap: Because so much of inferential statistics depends on the normal curve, students treat it as a description of real data. It is a model, not a measurement.

Q: Which statements about the normal curve and standard deviations are accurate? Select all that apply.
A) Approximately 68% of values fall within 1 SD above and below the mean
B) Exactly 95% of values fall within 2 SDs above and below the mean
C) 95.5% of values fall between -2 and +2 SDs
D) 99% of values fall within 2.58 SDs
E) Each tail beyond 1.96 SDs from the mean contains 2.5% of values
Answer: A, C, D, E
Rationale: The landmarks are 68% within 1 SD, 95% within 1.96 SDs, 95.5% within 2 SDs, and 99% within 2.58 SDs; the tails below -1.96 and above +1.96 SDs hold 2.5% each.
Trap: (B) is the fine-grained inversion. 95% goes with 1.96 SDs, not 2 SDs; a full 2 SDs captures 95.5%. Both numbers are in the chapter and the exam can use either.

Subtopic: one-tailed versus two-tailed tests

Q: A researcher hypothesizes that anxiety scores will differ between two groups but does not predict which group will score higher. Which test is indicated, and where does the 5% lie?
A) One-tailed, with all 5% in the upper tail
B) One-tailed, with all 5% in the lower tail
C) Two-tailed, with 2.5% in each tail
D) Two-tailed, with 5% in each tail
Answer: C
Rationale: A two-tailed test is the analysis of a nondirectional hypothesis, and the 5% is split across both tails at 2.5% each.
Trap: (D) doubles the alpha without meaning to. Splitting 5% across two tails yields 2.5% per tail, not 5% per tail.

Q: A researcher predicts specifically that the intervention group will have lower anxiety scores than the control group. Which statement about the appropriate test is accurate?
A) A two-tailed test is required, because comparing two groups always splits the 5% across both tails
B) A one-tailed test is indicated, but it carries less power than a two-tailed test
C) A one-tailed test is indicated, and it increases the possibility of a Type II error
D) A one-tailed test is indicated, and it is more powerful, decreasing the possibility of a Type II error
Answer: D
Rationale: A one-tailed test is used when the hypothesis is directional, with all 5% in a single tail; one-tailed tests are uniformly more powerful than two-tailed tests, decreasing the possibility of a Type II error.
Trap: (B) and (C) both assume that concentrating the alpha in one tail is a concession. It is the opposite — the cost of a one-tailed test is that the researcher must have sufficient knowledge of the variables to predict which tail the difference will fall in.

Topic: descriptive statistics
Subtopic: measures of central tendency

Q: A researcher summarizes the blood types of a sample. Which measure of central tendency is appropriate?
A) The mode
B) The median
C) The mean
D) The standard deviation
Answer: A
Rationale: The mode is the numerical value or score that occurs with greatest frequency and is the appropriate measure for nominal data.
Trap: The mode does not necessarily indicate the center of the data set, which is why students reject it — but for nominal categories no other measure of central tendency applies.

Q: A study's income data contain several extreme outliers. Which measure of central tendency is the research report most likely to present, and why?
A) The mean, because it uses every score in the data set
B) The median, because it is not affected by extreme scores or outliers
C) The mode, because outliers cannot be the most frequent value
D) The mean, because outliers cancel out above and below
Answer: B
Rationale: If the study has outliers, the mean is most affected by these, and the median might be the measure of central tendency included in the research report.
Trap: (A) is true as a description of the mean and is exactly why it fails here — using every score is what lets a handful of extreme values drag it.

Q: Which statements about measures of central tendency are accurate? Select all that apply.
A) In a perfect normal distribution the mean, median, and mode are equal
B) The median is the 50th percentile of an ungrouped frequency distribution
C) The mean is the measure least affected by extreme scores
D) With an even number of scores the median is the average of the two middle scores and may not be an actual score in the data set
E) A distribution with more than two modes is described as bimodal
Answer: A, B, D
Rationale: All three measures coincide in a perfect normal distribution; the median is the exact center, or 50th percentile, and with an even number of scores it is the average of the two middle scores, so it may not be a member of the data set.
Trap: (C) reverses the outlier rule — the mean is the measure most affected by extreme scores. (E) misnames the count: two modes is bimodal, more than two is multimodal.

Subtopic: dispersion and standard deviation

Q: A sample has a mean score of 5.28 with an SD of 2.22. Approximately 68% of the sample falls between which two values?
A) 0.84 and 9.72
B) 2.22 and 7.50
C) 3.06 and 7.50
D) 5.28 and 7.50
Answer: C
Rationale: One SD below the mean is 5.28 minus 2.22 = 3.06 and one SD above is 7.50, so about 68% of the sample falls in that interval. Two SDs would give 0.84 to 9.72.
Trap: (A) is the 2 SD interval, which holds about 95.5%, not 68%. Match the percentage in the stem to the number of SDs before computing.

Q: A report gives a mean pain score of 5.28 with an SD of 2.22. What does the standard deviation tell the reader?
A) The distance between the highest and lowest scores in the sample
B) The proportion of participants who scored above the mean
C) The interval within which the population mean falls 95% of the time
D) The average deviation of a value from the mean, indicating the degree of error that would result if the mean alone were used to interpret the data
Answer: D
Rationale: The standard deviation is the square root of the variance and is the average difference or deviation value; it indicates the degree of error that would result if the mean alone were used to interpret the data.
Trap: (C) describes the 95% confidence interval, which is a different reported statistic even though both accompany a mean.

Subtopic: confidence intervals and z-scores

Q: Which statements about confidence intervals and z-scores are accurate? Select all that apply.
A) A 95% CI indicates that 95% of the time the population mean would fall within that interval
B) A 95% CI indicates that 95% of the participants' scores fall within that interval
C) Confidence intervals can be calculated around parameters such as correlation coefficients and t-test values
D) The mean expressed as a z-score is 0
E) A z-score of -2 indicates a score 2 SDs above the mean
Answer: A, C, D
Rationale: The 95% CI indicates that 95% of the time the population mean would fall within this interval; CIs are usually calculated for 95% and 99% and can be built around any population parameter, including correlation coefficients and t-test values. A z-score expresses deviation from the mean in SD units, so the mean itself is 0.
Trap: (B) is the standard misreading of a CI — it is a statement about where the population mean sits, not about where participants sit. (E) drops the sign: values above the mean are positive z-scores, below are negative.

Topic: correlation and prediction
Subtopic: interpreting r

Q: A study reports r = -0.72 between hours of sleep and perceived fatigue. How should this be interpreted?
A) A weak relationship, because the coefficient is negative
B) A moderate relationship in which both variables change in the same direction
C) A strong relationship establishing that sleep loss causes fatigue
D) A strong inverse relationship, in which high scores on one variable occur with low scores on the other
Answer: D
Rationale: Strength is judged by the absolute value: below 0.3 is weak, 0.3 to 0.5 moderate, and above 0.5 strong. A negative correlation is inverse, meaning a high score on one variable occurs with a low score on the other.
Trap: (A) is the single most common correlation error — reading the minus sign as weakness. Strength decreases as r approaches 0 from either direction, so -0.72 is strong.

Q: Which statements about the Pearson product-moment correlation are accurate? Select all that apply.
A) It examines the relationship between two variables measured on a single sample
B) It indicates both the nature and the magnitude of the relationship
C) It establishes whether variable A leads to or causes variable B
D) Its values range from -1 to +1, with 0 indicating no relationship
E) It is symmetrical, giving no indication of the direction of the relationship
Answer: A, B, D, E
Rationale: Pearson correlation is an inferential technique for bivariate correlations, giving the nature (positive or negative) and the magnitude of a relationship between two variables measured on one sample, with values from -1 to +1.
Trap: (C) is the causal reading. Because correlational outcomes are symmetrical, it is not possible to establish whether A causes B or B causes A — the analysis gives no indication of direction at all.

Subtopic: explained variance and prediction

Q: A study reports r = 0.6 for anxiety and depression. What percentage of the variance is explained by the relationship between the two variables?
A) 6%
B) 36%
C) 60%
D) 64%
Answer: B
Rationale: Explained variance is indicated by r squared and expressed as a percentage: r = 0.6 gives r squared = 0.36, or 36% explained variance.
Trap: (C) reads r itself as a percentage. The correlation and the proportion of variance it explains are different numbers, and the second is always the smaller for any r between 0 and 1.

Q: A regression model is reported with R squared giving 26% and model significance tested by ANOVA, F(4,79) = 7.051, p < 0.001. What does the 26% represent?
A) The percentage of variance in the dependent variable explained by the model
B) The percentage of participants whose outcomes the model predicted correctly
C) The correlation between the predictors and the outcome
D) The probability that the model result reflects a Type I error
Answer: A
Rationale: In regression, R squared multiplied by 100% gives the percentage of variance explained; the chapter's worked example reports 26%, with the model tested for significance by ANOVA at F(4,79) = 7.051, p < 0.001.
Trap: (D) confuses the model's explanatory power with the p value. Type I risk here is carried by p < 0.001, not by R squared. Standardized beta coefficients, not R squared, identify which individual predictors are significant.

Topic: choosing a statistical test
Subtopic: the four judgments

Q: Which judgments determine which statistical analysis is appropriate for a study? Select all that apply.
A) Whether the question focuses on differences or on relationships and associations
B) The level of measurement of the data
C) How many groups are being compared
D) Whether the samples are independent or paired
E) The alpha level the researcher selected
Answer: A, B, C, D
Rationale: Four judgments determine the appropriate analysis: differences versus relationships, level of measurement, number of groups, and independent versus paired samples.
Trap: (E) matters enormously for interpreting the result but plays no part in choosing the test. Alpha is set before data collection regardless of which test will be run.

Subtopic: parametric versus nonparametric

Q: Body weight in kilograms is measured for all participants, but the Kolmogorov-Smirnov test shows the values are not normally distributed. Which approach is appropriate?
A) Parametric, because weight is ratio-level data
B) Parametric, because the Kolmogorov-Smirnov test checks only for outliers
C) Nonparametric, because parametric analysis requires interval or ratio data and a normal distribution
D) Nonparametric, because ratio-level data are always analyzed nonparametrically
Answer: C
Rationale: Parametric analyses are conducted when variables are at the interval or ratio level and the values are normally distributed; both conditions are required. Nonparametric analyses are conducted when variables are at the nominal and ordinal levels.
Trap: (A) is the reflex answer because level of measurement is the criterion students memorize first. Interval or ratio data that are not normally distributed do not automatically qualify for parametric analysis.

Subtopic: independent versus paired groups

Q: Each participant's pretest score serves as the control for that same participant's posttest score. How are these observations classified?
A) Independent, because each participant contributes one score to each condition
B) Independent, because no randomization to separate arms occurred
C) Paired, but only if the participants were also randomly assigned
D) Paired (dependent), because the observations are related to one another
Answer: D
Rationale: Paired or dependent groups exist when participants or observations are related in some way to the selection of other participants or observations, which includes a participant serving as their own control with the pretest as the control.
Trap: Misclassifying a pretest-posttest design as independent samples changes the correct test. Paired designs also include matched pairs and twins with one in each group, and groups sometimes matched on age and severity of illness.

Q: Participants are randomly assigned to either a treatment group or a control group, and no participant appears in both. How are these samples classified?
A) Independent, because the selection of one participant is unrelated to the selection of the others
B) Paired, because both groups were drawn from the same recruitment pool
C) Independent, only if the groups are also matched on age and severity of illness
D) Paired, because random assignment produces equivalent matched groups
Answer: A
Rationale: Independent groups exist when the selection of one study participant is unrelated to the selection of other participants, as when participants are randomly assigned to treatment and control groups.
Trap: This is the mirror of the pretest-posttest item. (C) and (D) both treat matching as something random assignment accomplishes; matching is a deliberate pairing procedure and is what makes groups dependent.

Subtopic: matching the test to the comparison

Q: Two independent groups are compared on mean serum sodium, a ratio-level variable that is normally distributed. Which test is appropriate?
A) Chi-square test of independence
B) t-test
C) ANOVA
D) Post hoc analysis
Answer: B
Rationale: The t-test is used to examine differences between two groups with interval or ratio level data.
Trap: (C) is the reflex answer for anyone who has learned ANOVA as the general difference test. ANOVA is for three or more groups; using the wrong one of this pair is the classic error in both directions.

Q: Three clinic sites are compared on whether patients did or did not return for follow-up, a yes or no outcome. Which test is appropriate?
A) t-test, because the outcome has two categories
B) ANOVA, because there are three groups
C) Chi-square test of independence, because the outcome is nominal
D) Pearson product-moment correlation, because two variables are involved
Answer: C
Rationale: The chi-square test of independence is used with three or more groups when the data are nominal or ordinal; ANOVA and ANCOVA require interval or ratio data.
Trap: (B) is the reflex answer because "three or more groups" points straight at ANOVA. Number of groups alone never settles the test — level of measurement has to be checked with it, and nominal data rule out the parametric options.

Q: A researcher compares four groups using ANOVA and reports a significant F value. Which statements are accurate? Select all that apply.
A) ANOVA tests for differences in variance, not in means alone
B) The significant F value identifies which specific pair of groups differs
C) Post hoc analyses are conducted to determine the location of the differences among groups
D) Bonferroni correction, Newman-Keuls, Tukey HSD, Scheffe, and Dunnett are post hoc options
E) Between-group variance is the variation of the group means around the grand mean
Answer: A, C, D, E
Rationale: ANOVA tests for differences in variance, partitioned into within-group variance and between-group variance around the grand mean; with more than two groups it is not possible to determine from the ANOVA where the significant differences occur, so post hoc analyses are conducted.
Trap: (B) is the single most tested misreading of ANOVA. A significant F tells you that the groups differ, not which ones — with only two groups the location would be obvious, which is precisely why post hoc tests exist for three or more.
