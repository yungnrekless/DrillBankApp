# Chapter 3 drills — Introduction to Qualitative Research

Authored from `sources/Ch3_Fact_Bank.md` (Grove & Gray, 7th ed.).
The fact bank names two highest-yield clusters — the four qualitative
approaches with their parent disciplines, and the four trustworthiness criteria
that replace validity and reliability — so those anchor the topic list, with
mirror-image pairs written for every inversion the fact bank flags as a trap
(Husserl versus Heidegger, emic versus etic, transferability versus
generalizability, homogeneous versus heterogeneous samples).

---

Topic: qualitative approaches
Subtopic: matching approach to purpose

Q: A nurse educator notices that new graduates are leaving her unit within six months and conducts a qualitative study to promote understanding of the experience from the perspective of the nurses living it and, if possible, to solve the problem. Which approach is this?
A) Grounded theory research
B) Descriptive phenomenological research
C) Exploratory-descriptive qualitative research
D) Ethnographic research
Answer: C
Rationale: Exploratory-descriptive qualitative research is conducted to provide information that will promote understanding of an experience from the perspective of the persons living the experience and possibly solve a problem. It addresses an issue or problem in need of a solution and understanding.
Trap: Every qualitative approach studies experience from the participant's perspective, so that phrase alone does not decide it. The give-away is the intent to solve a problem, which no other approach in this chapter claims as an outcome.

Q: Which pairings of a qualitative approach with its origin or its intended outcome are accurate? Select all that apply.
A) Phenomenological research comes from philosophy and yields a composite of the essence of the experience
B) Grounded theory research comes from sociology and yields a theory with relational statements between concepts
C) Ethnographic research comes from anthropology and yields a picture of the culture as a whole
D) Exploratory-descriptive qualitative research comes from psychology and yields a validated instrument
E) Each approach is based on a philosophical orientation that influences the interpretation of the data
Answer: A, B, C, E
Rationale: The chapter assigns phenomenology to philosophy, grounded theory to sociology by way of symbolic interaction, and ethnography to anthropology, and states that each approach is based on a philosophical orientation that influences interpretation of the data.
Trap: (D) is the fabricated pairing. The fact bank leaves the discipline cell blank for exploratory-descriptive work, and its outcome is understanding and possibly a solution, never an instrument.

Subtopic: grounded theory

Q: A researcher studies how nurses and families negotiate goals of care in the ICU, an area in which little previous research has been conducted, and ends the report with a diagram of concepts linked by relational statements. Which approach is this, and what does grounded refer to?
A) Grounded theory; the theory was developed from the data the participants provided
B) Grounded theory; the study was grounded in an established theoretical framework that it then tested
C) Ethnography; the description was grounded in a year of immersion in the unit's culture
D) Interpretative phenomenology; the interpretation was grounded in a naive reading of the texts
Answer: A
Rationale: Grounded means the theory developed from the research, grounded in the real world and based on data provided by participants. Grounded theory is used most frequently in areas where little previous research has been conducted, and fully developed studies result in theoretical frameworks with relational statements between concepts, sometimes with a diagram.
Trap: (B) inverts the direction of the logic. Grounded theory is inductive: it produces a theory rather than testing one.

Q: Most grounded theory methodology is based on which theory, and who developed it?
A) Hermeneutic theory, developed by Martin Heidegger
B) Symbolic interaction theory, developed by Glaser and Strauss
C) Phenomenological theory, developed by Edmund Husserl
D) Symbolic interaction theory, developed by George Herbert Mead
Answer: D
Rationale: Most grounded theory methodology is based on symbolic interaction theory, developed by the social psychologist George Herbert Mead in 1934, which explores how people define reality and how their beliefs are related to their actions.
Trap: (B) names the right theory with the wrong authors. Glaser and Strauss developed the grounded theory method itself in 1967, through interviews to understand the perspectives of persons who were dying.

Subtopic: ethnography

Q: An ethnographer joins a hospital night shift for a year and learns the staff's own terms, routines, and meanings as the staff themselves understand them, recognizing the uniqueness of each individual. Which approach is this, and is it the typical goal of ethnography?
A) An etic approach, and it is the typical goal
B) An emic approach, and it is the typical goal
C) An emic approach, but the etic approach is the typical goal
D) An etic approach, but the emic approach is the typical goal
Answer: B
Rationale: The emic approach studies behaviors from within the culture and recognizes the uniqueness of the individual, and it is the typical goal of ethnography. Ethnography requires immersion; early researchers spent a year or longer in another culture.
Trap: Morgan-Trimmer and Wood argue for using both emic and etic perspectives, which makes the two-sided options attractive, but that recommendation does not change which one the chapter names as typical.

Q: A second researcher on the same night-shift study deliberately stands back and analyzes the unit's elements as a naive outsider would. This describes which approach?
A) Etic
B) Emic
C) Bracketing
D) Triangulation
Answer: A
Rationale: The etic approach views the culture as a naive outsider and analyzes its elements as a researcher.
Trap: (C) is tempting because bracketing also involves holding the researcher's own frame at a distance, but bracketing belongs to Husserlian phenomenology, not to ethnography, and the word naive appears in both definitions.

Q: Which of the following would the chapter classify as material culture? Select all that apply.
A) Buildings used for cultural events
B) Symbolic meaning and social customs that become apparent only over time
C) Symbols
D) Family traditions
E) Networks of social relations
Answer: A, C, D, E
Rationale: Material culture is all created or constructed aspects of culture, including buildings for cultural events, symbols, family traditions, networks of social relations, and beliefs reflected in social and political institutions.
Trap: (B) is the nonmaterial half, which is symbolic meaning, social customs, and beliefs. Family traditions and social networks feel intangible, but the chapter lists both under material culture, so sorting by whether you can touch it will fail.

Topic: phenomenology
Subtopic: descriptive versus interpretative

Q: A researcher states that the aim of her study is to capture the experience of chemotherapy in its essence, without interpreting, explaining, or theorizing. Whose tradition is this, and which type of phenomenological research?
A) Heidegger; interpretative phenomenological research
B) Heidegger; hermeneutic phenomenological research
C) Husserl; descriptive phenomenological research
D) Mead; descriptive phenomenological research
Answer: C
Rationale: Husserl focused on the phenomenon itself and aimed to capture experience in its essence without interpreting, explaining, or theorizing. He supports descriptive phenomenological research, whose purpose is to describe experiences as they are lived, capturing the lived experience.

Q: A study analyzes participants' interview texts beginning with a naive reading, looks for the hidden meaning in how participants gave meaning to their illness, and presents a rich word picture as interpreted by the researcher. This describes:
A) Descriptive phenomenological research consistent with Husserl
B) Interpretative phenomenological research using hermeneutics, consistent with Heidegger
C) Grounded theory using symbolic interaction, consistent with Mead
D) Ethnographic research using an emic approach, consistent with anthropology
Answer: B
Rationale: Interpretative phenomenological research is consistent with Heidegger and involves analyzing the data and presenting a rich word picture of the phenomenon as interpreted by the researcher. Hermeneutics is an interpretative method involving textual analysis that begins with a naive reading of the texts.
Trap: The phrase naive reading pulls toward Husserl, whose bracketing requirement is about describing the phenomenon in a naive way. But a naive reading of texts is the hermeneutic starting point, and hermeneutics is Heideggerian.

Subtopic: bracketing

Q: Before beginning interviews, a phenomenologist writes out her own assumptions about living with chronic pain and sets them aside so she can describe the phenomenon in a naive way. What is this, and whose requirement is it?
A) Triangulation; a requirement of ethnographic fieldwork
B) Bracketing; a requirement of Heidegger's interpretative phenomenology
C) Saturation; a requirement of grounded theory
D) Bracketing; a requirement of Husserl's descriptive phenomenology
Answer: D
Rationale: Bracketing is setting aside one's own biases and preconceptions in order to describe the phenomenon in a naive way, and it is a Husserl requirement supporting descriptive phenomenological research.
Trap: (B) is the classic swap the fact bank flags. Heidegger argued that it is impossible to set aside preconceptions and understand the world naively, so bracketing can never be attached to interpretative or hermeneutic work.

Q: Which statements reflect Heidegger's position as the chapter presents it? Select all that apply.
A) The focus is how participants have interpreted or given meaning to their experiences
B) The analysis looks for the hidden meaning in participants' interpretations
C) Researchers must set aside their own biases before data collection begins
D) It is impossible to set aside preconceptions and understand the world naively
E) It supports interpretative phenomenological research
Answer: A, B, D, E
Rationale: Heidegger focused on how participants interpreted or gave meaning to their experiences and on the hidden meaning in those interpretations, argued that setting preconceptions aside is impossible, and supports interpretative phenomenological research.
Trap: (C) is Husserl's bracketing requirement, and selecting it is the single most predictable error in this chapter. Heidegger did not soften bracketing; he denied it was possible.

Topic: qualitative sampling
Subtopic: purposeful sampling

Q: A researcher recruits eight nurses specifically because each one worked a mass-casualty event, rather than drawing names at random from the staff roster. What sampling method is this, and what is the stated justification?
A) Purposeful sampling; participants are recruited for their particular knowledge, experience, or views related to the study
B) Convenience sampling; participants are recruited because they are the most easily available
C) Random sampling; it reduces selection bias in qualitative work
D) Purposeful sampling; it guarantees the findings will apply to all emergency nurses
Answer: A
Rationale: Sampling in qualitative research is purposeful. The researcher recruits participants because of their particular knowledge, experience, or views related to the study.
Trap: (D) names the right method and attaches a quantitative justification. Purposeful sampling is chosen for depth of experience, not to make findings apply to a whole population.

Q: A grounded theory team deliberately recruits participants who differ in age, role, and years of experience. What kind of sample is this, and why is it chosen?
A) A homogeneous sample, because the central focus is the phenomenon itself
B) A homogeneous sample, because grounded theory requires similarity to reach saturation faster
C) A heterogeneous sample, because participants with different characteristics support theory development
D) A heterogeneous sample, because saturation cannot be reached with similar participants
Answer: C
Rationale: Heterogeneous samples, in which participants have different characteristics, are used frequently for grounded theory to support theory development. Homogeneous samples, in which participants have similar characteristics, are used when the central focus is the phenomenon.
Trap: There is no blanket rule that qualitative samples must be homogeneous. Match the sample type to the method: phenomenon in focus means homogeneous, theory being built means heterogeneous.

Subtopic: sample size and saturation

Q: A doctoral student's phenomenological study has enrolled nine participants, and the three most recent interviews produced nothing the earlier interviews had not already covered. What should the student conclude?
A) The sample is too small, because qualitative studies require more than 30 participants
B) Saturation has been reached, because additional participants are not providing new information
C) A power analysis is needed to confirm whether the minimum sample size has been met
D) Data collection must continue until the planned enrollment target is reached
Answer: B
Rationale: Saturation occurs when additional participants or data sources do not provide new information. Phenomenological studies typically use 10 or fewer participants if the topic is narrow and participants provide adequate data.
Trap: (D) defines saturation as hitting a planned number. Saturation is defined by no new information, not by a count, and the sizes the chapter gives are typical rather than rules.

Q: Which statements about sample size in qualitative research are accurate? Select all that apply.
A) Phenomenological studies typically use 10 or fewer participants if the topic is narrow and participants provide adequate data
B) Grounded theory studies may require more than 30 participants to develop a robust theory
C) Sampling continues until saturation
D) Qualitative researchers deliberately recruit fewer participants but ensure that each one has experience with the research topic
E) A power analysis determines the minimum sample size for a qualitative study
Answer: A, B, C, D
Rationale: The chapter gives 10 or fewer as typical for phenomenology and more than 30 as possible for grounded theory, states that sampling continues until saturation, and notes that qualitative researchers deliberately recruit fewer participants while ensuring each has experience with the topic.
Trap: (E) is a quantitative tool. Power analysis determines the minimum sample size for a quantitative study; qualitative sample size is governed by saturation.

Topic: researcher and participant relationship
Subtopic: degree of involvement

Q: A quantitatively trained colleague criticizes a phenomenological study because the researcher became personally close to the participants over months of interviews. How does the chapter frame that involvement?
A) It is a source of bias in both quantitative and qualitative research
B) It is a threat to dependability that must be corrected with an audit trail
C) It is acceptable only if the researcher brackets the relationship after data collection
D) It is considered a source of bias in quantitative research but is thought by qualitative researchers to be a critical element of the research process
Answer: D
Rationale: One of the important differences between quantitative and qualitative research lies in the degree of involvement of the researcher with the participants; involvement considered a source of bias in quantitative research is thought by qualitative researchers to be a critical element of the research process. The researcher influences the people being studied and in turn is influenced by them.
Trap: (B) sounds rigorous and uses real vocabulary from the chapter, but the audit trail documents that methods were implemented appropriately. It is not a device for neutralizing researcher closeness.

Q: Qualitative reports call the individuals studied participants rather than subjects. What reason does the chapter give?
A) They were not randomly assigned to groups
B) The researcher and participants carry out the study cooperatively
C) They provide narrative data rather than measured data
D) The sample is purposeful rather than representative
Answer: B
Rationale: The term participants is used because the researcher and participants carry out the study cooperatively.
Trap: (D) is a true statement about qualitative sampling but is not the reason the chapter gives for the terminology. The reason is the cooperative relationship, which fits with the researcher's involvement being treated as a critical element.

Topic: qualitative data collection and analysis
Subtopic: data sources and coding

Q: Which statements about qualitative data collection and analysis are accurate? Select all that apply.
A) Data are collected through interviews, focus groups, observation, and examination of documents and media materials
B) Data are words and images instead of numbers
C) Interpretation is avoided so that the findings remain purely descriptive
D) A code is a symbol or abbreviation used to classify words or phrases in the data
E) Observation is a fundamental method of gathering data, especially for ethnographic studies
Answer: A, B, D, E
Rationale: Qualitative data are words and images rather than numbers, collected by interview, focus group, observation, and examination of documents and media; observation is fundamental, especially in ethnography. Analysis moves from codes to themes to interpretation.
Trap: (C) stops the analysis one step short. Interpretation asks what the findings mean and may focus on the usefulness of the findings for clinical practice or move toward theorizing.

Topic: rigor and trustworthiness
Subtopic: trustworthiness criteria

Q: Which statements about rigor in qualitative research are accurate? Select all that apply.
A) The degree of rigor is the extent to which the findings are dependable, confirmable, credible, and transferable
B) Rigor is established by demonstrating that the findings generalize to the target population
C) An audit trail is one way a researcher can assure a reader that the methods of the study were implemented appropriately
D) Rigor is assessed in relation to the detail built into the design, the carefulness of data collection, and the thoroughness of the analysis
E) Studies that are dependable and confirmable can be said to have truth, value, or credibility
Answer: A, C, D, E
Rationale: The chapter states that the extent to which findings are dependable, confirmable, credible, and transferable is the degree of rigor, assessed through design detail, careful data collection, and thorough analysis, with the audit trail assuring the reader that methods were implemented appropriately.
Trap: (B) imports the quantitative standard. Rigor in qualitative research is assessed differently precisely because the desired outcome is different.

Q: A qualitative team interviews patients, interviews their family members, and reviews clinic documents, then converges these into one perspective on the topic. What is this strategy, and what does it support?
A) Triangulation, a strategy for credibility
B) Bracketing, a strategy for dependability
C) Saturation, a strategy for transferability
D) Methodological congruence, a strategy for confirmability
Answer: A
Rationale: Triangulation means the researchers use at least two different perspectives to converge into a unique perspective on the topic, and it is named among the credibility strategies along with engaging family members, using multiple sources, and linking codes and themes with exemplar quotations.
Trap: (D) borrows a real term from the chapter with the wrong meaning. Methodological congruence is the fit among purpose, questions, and methods that makes a study a cohesive whole, not a data-convergence strategy.

Subtopic: transferability versus generalizability

Q: A nurse manager reads a qualitative study of burnout conducted in a 200-bed rural hospital and asks whether she can apply the findings on her own similar rural unit. Which criterion is she invoking?
A) Generalizability, the desired outcome for qualitative studies
B) Credibility, the truth value of the findings
C) Dependability, the appropriate implementation of the methods
D) Transferability, the applicability of the findings to similar individuals
Answer: D
Rationale: Transferability is the applicability of the findings to similar individuals and is the desired outcome for qualitative studies; findings cannot be generalized but may be applied in other contexts or with other participants.
Trap: (A) reverses the pair and mislabels it. Generalizability is not the desired outcome for qualitative studies at all.

Q: A quantitative study draws a sample that is representative of the target population so its results apply to that population. Which term names that desired outcome, and to which type of research does the chapter assign it?
A) Transferability; qualitative research
B) Transferability; quantitative research
C) Generalizability; quantitative research
D) Generalizability; qualitative research
Answer: C
Rationale: Generalizability is a desired outcome for quantitative studies when the sample is representative of the target populations.
Trap: This is the mirror image of the transferability item, and the pair is the one the fact bank flags most explicitly. An option praising a qualitative study for being generalizable to the population contradicts the chapter outright.
