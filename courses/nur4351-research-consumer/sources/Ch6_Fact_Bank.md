## Key terms

| Term | Definition as given |
|---|---|
| **primary source** | "Written by the person who **originated or is responsible for generating** the ideas published." A research report written **by the researchers who conducted the study** is a primary source. |
| **secondary source** | "**Summarizes or quotes** content from primary sources." Authors "paraphrase the works of researchers and theorists and present **their interpretation** of what was written." |
| **peer-reviewed** | The author "submitted a manuscript to a publication editor, who identified **scholars familiar with the topic** to review the manuscript," who advise whether it is accurate, meets quality standards, and is appropriate for the journal. |
| **theoretical literature** | "Concept analyses, models, theories, and conceptual frameworks that support a selected research problem and purpose." |
| **empirical literature** | "Knowledge derived from research"; **data-based** — reports of research and published studies, plus unpublished studies such as theses and dissertations. |
| **relevant studies** | "Those with a **direct bearing** on the problem of concern." |
| **current sources** | "Those published **within 5 years** before publication of the manuscript." |
| **seminal studies** | "The **first** studies on a particular topic that signaled the beginning of a **new way of thinking**"; sometimes called **classic** studies. |
| **landmark studies** | "**Significant research projects that have generated knowledge that influences a discipline** and sometimes society as a whole." Frequently **replicated** or used as the basis for further studies. |
| **article** | "A paper about a specific topic," possibly published with others on similar themes in journals (periodicals), encyclopedias, or edited books. |
| **encyclopedia** | "An authoritative compilation of information on alphabetized topics that may provide background information and lead to other sources, but is **rarely cited** in academic papers." |
| **thesis** | "A report of a research project completed by a **postgraduate student** as part of the requirements for a **master's** degree." |
| **dissertation** | "A report of an extensive, sometimes original, research project completed as the **final requirement for a doctoral degree.**" |
| **keywords** | "Terms that serve as **labels for publications** on a topic." |
| **conclusion (in a review)** | "A statement about the **state of knowledge** in relation to the topic area, which includes **what is known and not known** about the area." |
| **CrossRef** | "A registration agency for **DOIs** so that citations can be linked across databases and disciplines." |

## §1 Purpose of the literature review

**A review in a published report does three things:**
1. **Describes the current knowledge** of a practice problem
2. **Identifies the gaps** in that knowledge base
3. **Explains how the study being reported has contributed** to building knowledge in the area

**Scope rule:** "broad enough to allow the reader to become familiar with the research problem and **narrow enough to include only the most relevant sources.**"

**Reviewing the literature is the FIRST STEP in implementing EBP and identifying research problems.**

The review of literature in a research report is "a **summary of current knowledge** about a particular practice problem and includes **what is known and not known** about this problem."

## §2 Source quality — the rules that grade your paper

**Most references in quality reviews are PRIMARY sources that are PEER-REVIEWED.**

| | **Primary** | **Secondary** |
|---|---|---|
| **Who wrote it** | The person who **originated** the ideas — the researcher who ran the study, the theorist who built the theory | Someone **summarizing or paraphrasing** the primary author |
| **Risk** | — | "Information in secondary sources **may be misinterpretations** of the primary authors' thoughts" |
| **When to use** | Default | **Only if** primary sources cannot be located, **or** the secondary source provides "creative ideas or a unique organization of information not found in a primary source" |

**Peer review** means the manuscript "has undergone **significant scrutiny** and its content is considered **trustworthy.**"

**Two content types cited in reviews:**
- **Theoretical literature** — concept analyses, models, theories, conceptual frameworks. Found in books, periodicals, monographs. Provides the basis for the **research framework.**
- **Empirical literature** — **data-based** knowledge from research. Includes published studies **and unpublished** master's theses and doctoral dissertations.

**Currency and relevance:**
- **Current = within 5 years** before publication.
- **Relevant = direct bearing** on the problem.
- Sources should be **comprehensive as well as current** — some problems have been studied for decades, so reviews properly include **seminal** and **landmark** studies conducted years ago.

> **Seminal vs. landmark:** **seminal = FIRST**, signaled a new way of thinking (also called *classic*). **Landmark = INFLUENTIAL**, generated knowledge that shaped the discipline, often replicated. An old citation is not automatically a weakness — if it is seminal or landmark, it belongs there.

## §3 Literature review by research type — the timing differences

### Quantitative research

- **Purpose:** "to **direct the planning and execution** of a study."
- **Timing:** the **major review is performed at the BEGINNING** of the research process, **before** the study is conducted.
- A **limited review is conducted AFTER** the study is completed, to identify studies published since the original review — **especially if a year or longer** has passed since the study began. **Both reviews appear in the report.**
- The purpose is **similar across all four quantitative types** (descriptive, correlational, quasi-experimental, experimental).
- Citations may appear in **all sections**; the review section **may not be labeled** and may be **integrated into the introduction.** Same for the **framework**.
- The **discussion section** compares this study's findings with other studies' findings and states **conclusions that synthesize** previous research with the present findings.

### Qualitative research

The introduction is similar (background and significance), **but a deeper review may deliberately be omitted, for two reasons:**
1. Qualitative studies "are often conducted on topics about which **we know very little**, so little literature is available to review."
2. Some qualitative researchers **deliberately do not review the literature deeply beforehand** "because they **do not want their expectations about the topic to bias** their data collection, data analysis, and findings." This is consistent with **remaining open to participants' perspectives** (see Ch 3).

**Timing varies by qualitative approach:**

| Approach | When the literature is reviewed |
|---|---|
| **Phenomenology** | **Likely to DELAY** the review until **after data collection and initial analysis**; reviewed in later stages and while interpreting findings |
| **Grounded theory** | **Minimal review at the beginning** — merely to make the researcher aware of what has been done and that a problem exists. **Not used to direct data collection or theory development.** Literature is used primarily to **explain, support, and extend the theory generated** |
| **Ethnography** | **Similar to quantitative.** Early ethnographies of unexplored groups had no culture-specific literature available, but **theoretical and philosophical literature** was used to provide a framework or perspective |

## §4 Conducting the review

**Databases (Table 6.2) — know what each contains:**

| Database | Content |
|---|---|
| **CINAHL** | Nursing and allied health journals — clinical, theoretical, and research articles; many full-text |
| **MEDLINE** | Biomedical journals deemed reputable by the **National Library of Medicine**; abstracts with links to some full text |
| **PubMed** | **Free access to MEDLINE**, available to patients and consumers |
| **PsycARTICLES** | Journals published by the **American Psychological Association** and affiliates |
| **Google Scholar** | Broad-based academic writing across many disciplines |
| **Academic Search Complete** | Multidisciplinary |
| **Health Source: Nursing/Academic Edition** | Journals for physicians, nurses, other professionals; many full-text; patient medication education materials |
| **Psychological and Behavioral Sciences Collection** | Psychiatry, psychology, behavioral health |

**General search engines** (Google, Google Scholar, Yahoo) "will identify scholarly publications, but **may include sources that are not reliable**" and "may **not be the most current**." Sources from them "should be assessed for quality, such as **evidence of peer review.**"

**Keywords** narrow a search. Tip from the chapter: when you find one good article, **look under the abstract to see whether its search terms are listed** and reuse them. **"A librarian is an information specialist"** — consulting one "may save you time and make searching more effective."

**The process steps** (from the chapter's headings): prepare to review → **select databases and search terms** → **search the databases** → **refine your search** → **review the abstracts to identify relevant studies** → **ensure citation information is recorded** → **read the articles** → **develop an outline to organize the information** → **create the reference list.**

> **The synthesis standard — this is the grading criterion:** "A literature review that [is] a **series of paragraphs, in which each paragraph is a description of a single study, with no link to other studies** being reviewed, **does not provide evidence of adequate analysis or synthesis** of the literature." One-study-per-paragraph is a *summary*, not a *synthesis*.

A **conclusion** in a review is "a statement about the **state of knowledge**... which includes **what is known and not known** about the area."

## Source conflicts and traps

- **A research report written by the researchers who conducted the study IS a primary source.** Students routinely misclassify journal articles as secondary because they are "published." Primary/secondary turns on **who generated the ideas**, not the format.
- **Secondary sources are permitted only in two narrow cases:** the primary source **cannot be located**, or the secondary source offers **creative ideas or unique organization** unavailable in the primary. An option treating secondary sources as freely interchangeable is wrong.
- **"Current" has a number: within 5 years.** But **old is not automatically bad** — seminal and landmark studies belong in a comprehensive review. A stem criticizing a review solely because it cites a 1967 study may be testing this.
- **Seminal = first/classic; landmark = influential/discipline-shaping.** These are frequently swapped.
- **Qualitative researchers may deliberately AVOID an early literature review** — to prevent biasing data collection. This is a **methodological strength**, not sloppiness. An option calling a phenomenological study weak for delaying its review misreads the chapter.
- **Grounded theory does a minimal early review; phenomenology delays it; ethnography is like quantitative.** The specific approach determines the timing — an item may hinge on which one.
- **In quantitative research the MAJOR review comes BEFORE the study**, with a **limited** review after (especially if ≥1 year has passed). Both go in the report. An option saying the review is done only after data collection describes a *qualitative* pattern.
- **Unpublished theses and dissertations count as EMPIRICAL (data-based) literature.** An option excluding them from empirical sources is wrong. Note also: **thesis = master's, dissertation = doctoral.**
- **Theoretical vs. empirical literature.** Theoretical = concepts, models, theories, frameworks. Empirical = data-based research. The framework section is built from **theoretical** literature.
- **One-study-per-paragraph is NOT synthesis.** The chapter says so explicitly. If a stem describes a review as a string of unlinked study descriptions, the correct appraisal is that it **lacks adequate analysis/synthesis.**
- **PubMed is free MEDLINE**, not a separate body of literature. **CINAHL is the nursing-specific one.** A question asking which database to search for nursing clinical literature wants **CINAHL.**
- **Google Scholar is not equivalent to a bibliographic database** — it may return unreliable and non-current sources and requires a peer-review check.
- **The review section may not be labeled.** It "may be integrated into the introduction," as may the framework. An option claiming a study lacks a literature review because there is no heading is a misappraisal.
