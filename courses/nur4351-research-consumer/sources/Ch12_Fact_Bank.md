|---|---|
| **critical appraisal / critique** | "An examination of the **quality of a study** to determine the **credibility and meaning of the findings** for nursing." |
| **intellectual critical appraisal** | Critique "associated with **critical thinking and evaluation**" — "directed **at the product that is created**, such as a study, **rather than at the creator**, and involves the evaluation of the quality of the product." |

## §1 What critical appraisal is (and is not)

- **"Critique" is not "criticize."** The word is "frequently viewed as negative," but in the arts and sciences critique means **critical thinking and evaluation** — "tasks requiring carefully developed intellectual skills."
- **An intellectual critical appraisal is directed at the PRODUCT, not the CREATOR.** It evaluates the quality of the study, not the researcher.
- **The full definition:** "a careful and complete examination of a study to judge its **strengths, weaknesses, credibility, meaning, and significance for practice.**"

**A high-quality study** (five markers):
1. Focuses on a **significant problem**
2. Demonstrates **sound methodology**
3. Produces **credible findings**
4. Indicates **implications for practice**
5. Provides a **basis for additional studies**

**Critically appraising studies is an essential step toward basing practice on current research findings.** Ultimately the findings from several quality studies "can be **synthesized** to provide empirical evidence for use in practice."

## §2 Who appraises, and why

| Who | Why |
|---|---|
| **Students** | Learning to read and comprehend published reports. Basic research and appraisal knowledge is acquired in the **baccalaureate** program; **more advanced analytic skills at master's and doctoral levels.** **QSEN** identifies striving for EBP as a competency for **associate and baccalaureate (prelicensure)** students |
| **Practicing nurses** | So practice is "based on **current research evidence and not on tradition or trial and error**." **EBP is essential in agencies seeking or maintaining Magnet status** |
| **Nurse educators** | To expand their clinical knowledge base and **update curriculum content**; they **serve as role models** for students |
| **Researchers** | For proposals, publication, and building on prior work |

**When appraisals happen:** reading a published research report, **selection of abstracts** for conference presentation, **article selection for publication**, and **evaluation of research proposals** for implementation or funding.

> **A caution for practicing nurses:** "Reading research journals and posting or emailing current studies at work can **increase nurses' awareness** of study findings **but are not sufficient for critical appraisal to occur.**" Nurses must **question the quality of the studies, credibility of the findings, and meaning for practice** — e.g. through a **research journal club.**

## §3 Box 12.1 — the 10 key principles (the grading rubric)

1. **Read and critically appraise the ENTIRE study** — examine the quality of **all aspects** of the report.
2. **Examine the organization and presentation** — a well-prepared report is **complete, concise, clearly presented, and logically organized**, without excessive jargon; references **current, complete, and consistently formatted.**
3. **Examine the significance of the problem** for nursing practice.
4. **Indicate the type of study conducted and identify the steps or elements** of the study.
5. **Identify the strengths AND weaknesses** — "**all studies have strengths and weaknesses**, so attention must be given to all aspects."
6. **Be objective and realistic** — "try not to be **overly critical** in identifying weaknesses or **overly flattering** in identifying strengths."
7. **Provide specific examples** of strengths and weaknesses.
8. **Provide a rationale** for your comments — **document your ideas with sources from the current literature.**
9. **Evaluate the quality of the study** — credibility of findings, **consistency with other studies**, quality of conclusions.
10. **Discuss the usefulness of the findings for practice** — link them to previous studies and examine them for clinical use.

## §4 The three-step appraisal process (both study types)

**The same three steps apply to quantitative and qualitative studies:**

1. **Identify the steps or elements of the study** (the research process in the report)
2. **Determine study strengths and weaknesses/limitations**
3. **Evaluate the credibility and meaning of the study findings**

> **Where you are as a beginner:** "Because you are new to critical appraisal of research, you will probably be focusing on **step 1** of identifying the steps of the research process. As you gain experience, you might perform **two or three steps simultaneously.**"

The detailed question sets live in **Box 12.2 (quantitative)** and **Box 12.3 (qualitative)**. They stress examining **the expertise of the authors**, **reviewing the entire study**, **addressing strengths and weaknesses**, and **evaluating credibility of findings.**

**"Adding together the strong points from multiple studies slowly builds a solid base of evidence for practice."**

## §5 Appraising QUALITATIVE studies — trustworthiness

**Trustworthiness = the qualitative equivalent of rigor.** It is "the extent to which a qualitative study is **dependable, confirmable, credible, and transferable.**"

**The four criteria and their quantitative analogues:**

| Qualitative criterion | What it means | Analogous to |
|---|---|---|
| **Dependability** | **Documentation of steps taken and decisions made** during analysis — supported by the **audit trail** | **Reliability** |
| **Confirmability** | Other researchers can **review the audit trail and agree the conclusions are logical** | **Reliability** |
| **Credibility** | The reader's **confidence that results reflect the views of the participants** | **Validity** |
| **Transferability** | Findings are **applicable in other settings with similar participants** — **NOT generalizable** | (External validity) |

**"When a study's findings are appraised to be confirmable and dependable, they have more credibility."**

**Appraising rigor** means looking for information about **the carefulness of data collection and the thoroughness of data analysis**, and checking the **logical links among study elements** — which "are critical to the credibility of the study." The chapter's example questions:
- Is the **purpose consistent with the research questions**?
- Are the **purpose and research questions appropriate to address the research problem**?
- Is the **selected qualitative approach the best way to answer the research questions**?

**Common weaknesses in qualitative studies:** "**logical inconsistencies and improperly applied methods**" — the same as in quantitative research.

**Consistency with method experts matters:** guidance "provided by a specific expert in the method used by the researchers" is important — "areas of **consistency are strengths**, whereas areas of **inconsistency may indicate weaknesses.**"

> **The chapter's own worked criticism, worth knowing as a model:** in one appraised study, "although **Max van Manen** is a commonly cited contemporary expert in hermeneutic phenomenology, **the researchers did not cite Heidegger, the originator** of hermeneutic phenomenology." Failing to cite the originating philosopher of your method is a legitimate appraisal finding.

**Why it feels hard:** "Because qualitative research has **fewer rules**, critically appraising qualitative studies can seem daunting" — which is why the Box 12.3 questions provide the structure.

## §6 Appraising QUANTITATIVE studies — what gets examined

The chapter's worked appraisal walks these areas: **study strengths and limitations**, **design**, **measurement strategies**, and **statistical analyses and results.**

> **The chapter's worked design criticism — memorize this pattern:** a study was judged to have "a **weak design because there was only ONE GROUP and NO separate comparison group** to determine if the treatment was effective **or if the change from pretest to posttest was caused by extraneous variables.**" The design was a **one-group pretest–posttest** (participants completed the MLHFQ scale as pretest, received a 4-week transition-to-care intervention, then the posttest).

That single sentence contains the most testable appraisal judgment in the chapter: **a one-group pretest–posttest design cannot separate the intervention's effect from extraneous variables**, because there is no comparison group.

**Author expertise counts.** The appraisal notes the researcher's credentials as relevant — e.g. a **DNP-prepared family nurse practitioner with both clinical and research experience** in the study's clinical area.

## Source conflicts and traps

- **"Critique" targets the PRODUCT, never the creator.** An option describing critical appraisal as evaluating the researcher's competence — or as fault-finding — contradicts the definition of *intellectual* critical appraisal.
- **Every study has BOTH strengths and weaknesses.** Principle 5 says so, and principle 6 warns against being "overly critical" *or* "overly flattering." An appraisal that lists only weaknesses is as flawed as one that lists only strengths — a likely stem.
- **Appraisal comments require examples AND rationale documented from the literature** (principles 7 and 8). An option treating an unsupported opinion as an adequate appraisal is wrong.
- **The three steps are the same for quantitative and qualitative studies.** Only the detailed question sets differ (Box 12.2 vs. 12.3).
- **Trustworthiness has four parts: dependable, confirmable, credible, transferable.** Mapping them to quantitative concepts: **dependability/confirmability ≈ reliability**, **credibility ≈ validity**, **transferability** replaces generalizability. A stem asking for the qualitative analogue of reliability wants **dependability** (or confirmability), not credibility.
- **Qualitative findings are NOT generalizable.** They are **transferable to similar settings and participants.** Same rule as Ch 3 — an option praising a qualitative study's generalizability is the distractor.
- **Confirmability depends on the audit trail.** Without documented decisions, another researcher cannot confirm the conclusions. An option defining confirmability as "the researcher confirming findings with participants" describes member checking, not confirmability as defined here.
- **A one-group pretest–posttest design is WEAK** — no comparison group means change cannot be attributed to the intervention rather than extraneous variables. This connects directly to Ch 8's internal validity.
- **Reading and circulating journal articles is NOT critical appraisal.** The chapter says awareness is "not sufficient for critical appraisal to occur." An option treating a journal-article email list as evidence of EBP is wrong.
- **Basic appraisal skills are baccalaureate-level; advanced analytic skills are master's/doctoral.** Consistent with Ch 1's Table 1.4 role expectations.
- **Logical inconsistency is a weakness in BOTH study types.** Qualitative research having "fewer rules" does not exempt it from requiring logical links among purpose, questions, and method.
