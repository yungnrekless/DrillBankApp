## Key terms

| Term | Definition as given |
|---|---|
| **research** | "A diligent systematic inquiry or study that validates and refines existing knowledge and develops new knowledge." The word itself means **"to search again" or "to examine carefully."** *Diligent systematic study* indicates **planning, organization, and persistence.** |
| **nursing research** | "A scientific process that validates and refines existing knowledge and generates new knowledge that **directly and indirectly** influences nursing practice." |
| **evidence-based practice (EBP)** | Evolves from **the integration of the best research evidence with clinical expertise and the patient's circumstances and values** to produce quality health outcomes (IOM, 2001; Straus et al., 2011). |
| **best research evidence** | "The empirical knowledge generated from the **synthesis of quality health studies** to address a clinical problem." |
| **EBP competency (QSEN)** | "Integrating the best current evidence with clinical expertise and patient/family preferences and values for delivery of optimal health care" (QSEN, 2017). |
| **knowledge** | "Essential information that is acquired in a variety of ways and is expected to be an accurate reflection of reality that is used to direct a person's actions" (Kaplan, 1964). |
| **traditions** | "'Truths' or beliefs based on customs and trends," transferred from the past by role modeling and communication. |
| **authority** | "A person with expertise and power who is able to influence opinion and behavior." |
| **borrowing** | "The appropriation and use of knowledge from other fields or disciplines to guide nursing practice." |
| **trial and error** | "An approach with unknown outcomes," used in situations of uncertainty when other sources of knowledge are unavailable. |
| **personal experience** | "Gaining knowledge by being personally involved in an event, situation, or circumstance." |
| **role modeling** | "Learning by imitating the behaviors of an expert." An intense form is **mentorship.** |
| **intuition** | "An insight into or understanding of a situation or event as a whole that a person usually cannot explain logically." Often described as a **"gut feeling" or "hunch."** **Not the lack of knowing — it is the result of deep knowledge** (Benner, 1984). |
| **reasoning** | "The processing and organizing of ideas to reach conclusions." |
| **inductive reasoning** | Moves **from the specific to the general**; particular instances are observed and combined into a larger whole or general statement. |
| **deductive reasoning** | Moves **from the general to the specific**, or from a general premise to a particular situation or conclusion. |
| **quantitative research** | "A formal, objective, systematic process in which **numerical data** are used to obtain information about the world." |
| **qualitative research** | "A systematic **subjective** approach used to describe life experiences and situations and give them meaning" (Creswell & Poth, 2018). |
| **mixed methods research** | "An approach to inquiry that **combines quantitative and qualitative research methods in a single study.**" |
| **outcomes research** | Research that "focuses on **examining the results of care and determining the changes in health status** for the patient and family." |
| **nursing-sensitive outcome** | "An individual, family, or community **state, behavior, or perception** that is measured **along a continuum** in response to nursing intervention(s)" (Moorhead et al., 2013). |
| **phenomenological research** | "An **inductive holistic** approach used to describe an experience as it is lived by individuals," e.g. the lived experience of losing a child. |
| **grounded theory research** | "An **inductive** research technique used to **formulate, test, and refine a theory** about a particular phenomenon" (Charmaz, 2014). |
| **ethnographic research** | Developed by **anthropology** for investigating **cultures** through in-depth study of members of the culture. |
| **exploratory-descriptive qualitative research** | Conducted "to address an issue or problem in need of a solution and/or understanding." |
| **descriptive research** | "Explores new areas of research and describes situations as they exist in the world." |
| **correlational research** | "Examines **relationships**"; develops and refines **explanatory** knowledge. |
| **quasi-experimental / experimental research** | "Determine the **effectiveness of nursing interventions** in predicting and controlling the outcomes desired for patients and families." |
| **systematic review** | "A structured, comprehensive synthesis of the research literature to determine the best research evidence available to address a healthcare question." Involves **identifying, locating, appraising, and synthesizing** quality research evidence. |
| **meta-analysis** | Conducted "to **combine or pool the results from previous quantitative studies into a single statistical analysis**" that provides strong evidence about an intervention's effectiveness. |
| **meta-synthesis** | "The systematic compilation and integration of **qualitative** study results to expand understanding and develop a unique interpretation of study findings in a selected area." |
| **mixed-methods systematic review** | The synthesis of **quantitative, qualitative, and mixed methods** studies (Higgins & Green, 2008). |
| **evidence-based guidelines** | "Rigorous and explicit clinical guidelines that have been developed based on the **best research evidence available** in that area," usually by a **team or panel of experts** working to **consensus.** |

## §1 The three foundational definitions — do not blur them

- **Research** = diligent systematic inquiry that **validates and refines existing knowledge AND develops new knowledge.** Note it does *both*; an option offering only "creates new knowledge" is incomplete.
- **Nursing research** = the same, but specifically knowledge that **"directly and indirectly influences nursing practice."** The phrase *directly and indirectly* is the giveaway.
- **EBP** = **integration of three things**: (1) **best research evidence**, (2) **clinical expertise**, and (3) **patient circumstances/values.** Research evidence alone is *not* EBP.

> **The single most testable idea in the chapter:** EBP is a **three-part integration**, not just "using research." Any option that defines EBP as applying research findings while omitting **clinical expertise** and **patient values/preferences** is the distractor. The QSEN wording says the same thing: "best current evidence **with** clinical expertise **and** patient/family preferences and values."

**The ultimate goal of nursing** is an EBP that promotes **quality, safe, and cost-effective outcomes** for patients, families, healthcare providers, and the healthcare system.

## §2 The eight ways of acquiring knowledge

Nursing "has historically acquired knowledge through **traditions, authority, borrowing, trial and error, personal experience, role modeling, intuition, and reasoning.**"

| Way | Core idea | The catch the chapter names |
|---|---|---|
| **Tradition** | "Truths"/beliefs from customs and trends; policy manuals carry them | Can **narrow and limit** knowledge; traditions go unquestioned because they are "supported by those with power and authority" |
| **Authority** | A person with **expertise and power** who influences opinion and behavior | Authorities "must be careful to teach and practice based on research evidence because they are influencing the actions of others" |
| **Borrowing** | Taking knowledge from medicine, sociology, psychology, physiology, education | **Two kinds:** applying it directly *without* integration (the medical model — less useful) vs. **integrating** it within nursing's focus (therapeutic communication — more useful) |
| **Trial and error** | Approach with **unknown outcomes**, used under uncertainty when other sources are unavailable | Knowledge gained is often **not shared**, so ineffective interventions continue |
| **Personal experience** | Gaining knowledge by direct involvement | Graded by **Benner's five levels** (below) |
| **Role modeling** | **Imitating the behaviors of an expert**; intense form = **mentorship** | New graduates get this via internships/residencies |
| **Intuition** | Insight into a situation as a whole that cannot be explained logically | **It is deep knowledge, not absence of knowledge** |
| **Reasoning** | Processing and organizing ideas to reach conclusions | Splits into **inductive** and **deductive** |

**Benner's (1984) five levels of experience** — note this was a **phenomenological qualitative study**:
1. **Novice** — no personal experience with the work; has preconceptions from education
2. **Advanced beginner** — "just enough experience to recognize and intervene in recurrent situations" (e.g. managing pain)
3. **Competent** — generates plans and achieves **long-range goals**; conscious, deliberate, organized action
4. **Proficient** — views the patient **as a whole and as a member of a family and community**; adjusts to each patient's response
5. **Expert** — extensive background; intervenes skillfully; grasps a situation **intuitively, with accuracy and speed**

**Inductive vs. deductive — the chapter's own worked examples:**

| | Direction | Example given |
|---|---|---|
| **Inductive** | **Specific → general** | *A headache is an altered level of health that is stressful. A terminal illness is an altered level of health that is stressful.* → **Therefore all altered levels of health are stressful.** |
| **Deductive** | **General → specific** | *All humans experience loss. All adolescents are humans.* → **Therefore all adolescents experience loss.** |

**Inductive reasoning is what synthesizing multiple studies uses** — it is the logic behind building EBP from a body of findings.

## §3 The four research methods (Box 1.1) and what each is for

The chapter ties each method to a **goal of the profession**:

| Method | The goal it serves |
|---|---|
| **Qualitative** | "Promoting an understanding of patients' and families' **experiences** with health and illness" |
| **Quantitative** | "Testing the **effectiveness of nursing interventions**" |
| **Mixed methods** | "Combining quantitative and qualitative methods to expand understanding of **complex** health problems" |
| **Outcomes** | "Examining the **outcomes of care** to determine the **quality, safety, and cost-effectiveness** of nursing care" |

**Box 1.1 — the classification used by this text (memorize the membership; SATA-friendly):**
- **Types of quantitative research (4):** descriptive, correlational, quasi-experimental, experimental
- **Types of qualitative research (4):** phenomenological, grounded theory, ethnographic, exploratory-descriptive
- **Mixed methods research**
- **Outcomes research**

**Table 1.3 — quantitative vs. qualitative (the highest-yield comparison in the chapter):**

| Characteristic | **Quantitative** | **Qualitative** |
|---|---|---|
| **Philosophic origin** | **Logical positivism** | **Naturalistic, interpretive, humanistic** |
| **Basis of knowing** | **Cause and effect relationships** | **Meaning, discovery, understanding** |
| **Theoretical focus** | **Tests theory** | **Develops theory** and frameworks |
| **Researcher involvement** | **Objective** | **Shared interpretation** |
| **Common measurement** | Scales, questionnaires, physiological measures | Unstructured interviews, observations, focus groups |
| **Data** | **Numbers** | **Words** |
| **Analysis** | **Statistical analysis** | **Text-based analysis** |
| **Findings** | Description of variables, relationships, intervention effectiveness; **generalization** | Unique, dynamic, focused on **understanding**; facilitates **theory development** |

**Similarities** (both methods share these): both require **researcher expertise**, involve **rigor**, and **generate scientific knowledge** for practice.

**Outcomes research** — four areas the chapter says require investigation:
1. **Patient responses** to nursing and medical interventions
2. **Functional maintenance or improvement** of physical, mental, and social functioning
3. **Financial outcomes** achieved with provision of healthcare services
4. **Patient satisfaction** with health outcomes, care received, and providers

The **Nursing Outcomes Classification (NOC)** standardizes terminology for nursing-sensitive outcomes across specialties and settings.

## §4 The nurse's research role by education level (Table 1.4)

**"No matter their education or position, all nurses have roles in research."** The role **expands** with education, clinical expertise, and career path.

| Preparation | Research expectations |
|---|---|
| **BSN** | **Read and critically appraise studies**; use best research evidence in practice **with guidance**; **assist** with problem identification and **data collection** |
| **MSN** | **Critically appraise and synthesize** studies to **develop and revise protocols, algorithms, and policies**; implement best evidence; collaborate in research and provide clinical expertise |
| **DNP** | Participate in **evidence-based guideline development**; develop, implement, evaluate, and revise protocols/policies/guidelines; **conduct clinical studies**, usually in collaboration |
| **PhD** | **Major role such as primary investigator**; contribute to empirical knowledge; **obtain funding**; **coordinate research teams** of BSN, MSN, and DNP nurses |
| **Postdoctorate** | Implement a **funded program of research**; lead interdisciplinary teams; **identified as experts**; **mentor PhD-prepared researchers** |

**The DNP/PhD split:** **DNP = practice-focused** (highest level of clinical expertise; translates scientific knowledge for use in practice). **PhD = research-focused** (generates nursing knowledge; nurse scientists).

**Your level (BSN):** skilled in **critical appraisal of studies, use of appropriate research evidence in practice, adherence to IRB guidelines, and appropriate data collection.**

## §5 The four synthesis strategies (Table 1.5)

"Research evidence in nursing is usually synthesized by the following processes: (1) **systematic review**; (2) **meta-analysis**; (3) **meta-synthesis**; and (4) **mixed-methods systematic review**."

| Strategy | What it synthesizes | How |
|---|---|---|
| **Systematic review** | The research literature on **one healthcare question** | **Structured, comprehensive**: identify, locate, appraise, synthesize. Used to build **standardized guidelines** (acute pain, high BP, depression) |
| **Meta-analysis** | **Quantitative studies only** | **Statistically pools results** into a single analysis. Strongest when pooling **quasi-experimental and experimental** studies; can also pool **correlational** studies for type/strength of relationships |
| **Meta-synthesis** | **Qualitative studies** | Systematic compilation and integration; focus is on **interpretation**, *not* combining results |
| **Mixed-methods systematic review** | **Quantitative + qualitative + mixed methods** studies | May include qualitative, quasi-experimental, correlational, and/or descriptive designs |

Synthesis "is best conducted by **at least two people** or even a team."

> **Why qualitative studies cannot enter a meta-analysis:** "Because qualitative studies **do not produce statistical findings**, they cannot be included in a meta-analysis." This is the reason meta-synthesis exists as a separate process.

## §6 Levels of research evidence (Fig. 1.3 pyramid)

A **continuum** — highest quality at the **top**, weakest at the **base**:

| Rank | Evidence |
|---|---|
| **Strongest (top)** | **Systematic reviews and meta-analyses** of high-quality quasi-experimental and experimental studies |
| Very strong | **Meta-analyses** of correlational, quasi-experimental, experimental, and outcomes studies |
| Quality syntheses | **Mixed-methods systematic reviews and meta-syntheses** |
| Direction for future research — **not ready for practice** | Individual **correlational, predictive correlational, and cohort** studies |
| Initial knowledge / basis for further study | **Descriptive and qualitative** studies |
| **Weakest (base)** | **Opinions of expert committees and authorities** |

- Among **individual** studies, **experimental studies such as RCTs provide the strongest research evidence.**
- **Replication** of studies with similar methodology **increases the strength** of the evidence.
- The best evidence (systematic reviews, meta-analyses, meta-syntheses, mixed-methods systematic reviews) is what **evidence-based guidelines** are built from.

## Source conflicts and traps

- **EBP is three parts, not one.** Best research evidence **+ clinical expertise + patient circumstances/values.** The distractor drops the patient's values, or drops clinical expertise, and calls "applying research findings" EBP by itself.
- **Expert opinion is the WEAKEST evidence, not the strongest.** It sits at the **base** of the pyramid. An option ranking "the opinion of an expert committee" or "a respected authority's recommendation" above a meta-analysis is inverted. This is the same trap as the "authority" way of knowing — authority is a *historical* way of knowing, explicitly contrasted with research evidence.
- **Systematic review vs. meta-analysis.** A systematic review is the **structured synthesis process**; a meta-analysis **statistically pools** results. Many systematic reviews *contain* meta-analyses, so "meta-analysis" is not a synonym for "systematic review."
- **Meta-analysis = quantitative only; meta-synthesis = qualitative.** Because qualitative studies produce no statistics, they **cannot** be pooled in a meta-analysis. An option describing a meta-analysis of interview data is wrong.
- **Meta-synthesis interprets; quantitative synthesis combines.** The chapter is explicit that meta-synthesis focuses "on interpretation rather than on combining study results."
- **Inductive = specific → general; deductive = general → specific.** The classic swap. Cue: **synthesizing many studies into a best practice is INDUCTIVE.** Starting from a theory's proposition and applying it to one patient is deductive.
- **Intuition is not ignorance.** "Intuition is **not** the lack of knowing; rather, it is a result of **deep knowledge.**" An option dismissing intuition as unscientific guessing contradicts the chapter (which cites Benner).
- **Correlational and cohort studies "provide direction for future research but are NOT ready for use in practice."** A stem asking whether a single correlational study justifies a practice change is testing this line.
- **Quantitative tests theory; qualitative develops theory.** Reversing this is the Table 1.3 trap. Same for philosophic origin: **quantitative = logical positivism**, **qualitative = naturalistic/interpretive/humanistic.**
- **BSN-level expectations are bounded.** The BSN nurse **reads, critically appraises, uses evidence with guidance, and assists** with problem identification and data collection. An option assigning the BSN nurse the role of **primary investigator** or **guideline developer** overreaches — those are PhD and DNP roles respectively.
- **DNP = practice-focused, PhD = research-focused.** Swapping them is a likely item.
- **Two kinds of borrowing.** Direct application *without* integration (medical model) vs. **integration** within nursing's focus. The chapter calls the second "more useful in nursing."
- **Trial and error's named weakness is that findings are not shared**, so ineffective interventions persist — not that it is simply unscientific.
