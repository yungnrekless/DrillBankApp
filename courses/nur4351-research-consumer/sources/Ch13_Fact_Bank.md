## Key terms

| Term | Definition as given |
|---|---|
| **EBP** | "A complex phenomenon that requires **integration of the best research evidence with clinical expertise, patient circumstances, and patient values** in the delivery of quality, safe, cost-effective care." |
| **systematic review** | "A **structured, comprehensive synthesis of the research literature** to determine the best research evidence available to address a healthcare question or problem." |
| **meta-synthesis** | "The systematic compilation and integration of **qualitative** studies to expand understanding and develop a unique interpretation of study findings in a selected area." |
| **mean difference** | "A standard statistic that identifies the **absolute difference between two groups.**" |
| **research-based protocols** | "**Structured guidelines** for implementing nursing interventions in practice that are **based on current research evidence.**" |
| **translational research** | Termed **"bench to bedside" research**, involving "the **translation of basic scientific discoveries into practical applications.**" |
| **evidence-based practice centers (EPCs)** | AHRQ-funded centers that "review all relevant scientific literature on clinical, behavioral, organizational, and financial topics to produce **evidence reports and technology assessments.**" |

## §1 Benefits and challenges of EBP

**Benefits:** "The greatest benefits of EBP are **improved outcomes for patients, providers, and healthcare agencies.**" Research syntheses provide the basis for **evidence-based guidelines** that identify "the best treatment plan, or the **gold standard** for patient care."

**Where guidelines come from** (know these three):
- **National Guideline Clearinghouse (NGC)** — **United States**
- **Cochrane Collaboration** — **United Kingdom**
- **Joanna Briggs Institute** — **Australia**

**Institutional drivers:**
- Leaders recognize EBP "promotes quality outcomes, **improves nurses' satisfaction**, and facilitates achievement of **accreditation requirements.**"
- An organization with an **EBP culture** had "substantial improvements in several patient outcomes" (Melnyk et al., 2016).
- **The Joint Commission** revised accreditation criteria "to emphasize patient care outcomes achieved through EBP."
- **Magnet status** — approved through the **American Nurses Credentialing Center (ANCC)** — "documents the excellence of nursing care." The **Magnet Recognition Program® emphasizes EBP** as a way to improve quality and revitalize the nursing environment; agencies seeking or maintaining it **must document research-related outcomes.**

**Challenges — "practical and conceptual":**

| Challenge | Detail |
|---|---|
| **Limited research evidence** | "One of the **most serious concerns** is the limited research evidence available regarding the **effectiveness of many nursing interventions.**" RCTs and other intervention studies "are still limited in nursing" |
| **Few syntheses in nursing** | Systematic reviews and meta-analyses in nursing "are limited when compared with other disciplines, such as **medicine and psychology**" |
| **Population data → individual patients** | Evidence "is generated based on **population data** and then is applied in practice to **individual patients**," who "respond in unique ways or have unique circumstances and values" |

**The response to that last challenge:** the **NIH supports translational research** to improve use of evidence across patient populations and settings. Patients with **poor outcomes under a guideline should be reported** and, if possible, **published as a case study.** **Electronic health records (EHRs)** "make it more feasible to determine patient outcomes of care delivered using EBP guidelines."

## §2 The PICO clinical question format

**P – Population**, **I – Intervention**, **C – Comparison intervention**, **O – Outcome.**

**The chapter's worked example — aspiration during IM injections:**

| Element | Content |
|---|---|
| **P** | Infants, toddlers, children, and adults receiving immunizations by the **IM route for prophylactic purposes** |
| **I** | IM injection given **without aspiration** in the right site based on volume of medication and age |
| **C** | IM injection given **with 5 to 10 seconds of aspiration in all sites**, regardless of age and volume |
| **O** | IM injection of vaccine **without complications** |

**The evidence (Box 13.1 clinical practice guideline) — this is clinically testable content:**

- **Older guidelines** (Nicoll & Hesby, 2002; Cocoman & Murray, 2008) recommended **aspirating 5–10 seconds** with each IM injection to prevent injecting into the bloodstream.
- **Current evidence** (Sisson, 2015): **NO aspiration** in the **deltoid, ventrogluteal, and vastus lateralis** sites. **Aspirate ONLY in the dorsogluteal site**, because of the **close proximity of the gluteal artery** — and researchers recommend the **dorsogluteal site not be used if possible.**

**Site selection by age:**
- **Infants** — **vastus lateralis** is preferred
- **Toddlers and children** — **vastus lateralis or deltoid**
- **Adults** — **ventrogluteal or deltoid**

**Volume:**
- **Small volumes (≤ 2 mL)** — deltoid for toddlers, children, adults; vastus lateralis for infants
- **Large volumes (2–5 mL)** — **ventrogluteal for adults**; limited volume in vastus lateralis for infants, toddlers, children

> **The research-utilization gap this example exists to illustrate:** **74% of nurses were still aspirating after IM injections 90% of the time** (Thomas et al., 2016), and nurses still over-used the **dorsogluteal** site despite evidence favoring ventrogluteal (Wynaden et al., 2015). Researchers therefore recommended **additional education in nursing programs and continuing education.** This is the chapter's central point: **evidence existing is not the same as evidence being used.**

## §3 Sources of evidence and their strength

- **"The strongest evidence comes from systematic reviews that include meta-analyses of RCTs."**
- "However, **meta-syntheses, mixed-methods systematic reviews, and individual studies also provide important evidence** for changing practice."
- Research evidence "can be combined with **other sources of knowledge (e.g., theories, scientific principles, expert opinion, case reports)** to provide fairly strong evidence for developing research-based protocols."

## §4 The two EBP models

> "The **two models most commonly used in nursing** to implement research evidence in practice are the **Stetler Model of Research Utilization** (Stetler, 2001) and the **Iowa Model of Evidence-Based Practice** (Iowa Model Collaborative, 2017)."

### Stetler Model — five phases

Its intent is "to make using research evidence in practice a **conscious, critical thinking process that is initiated by the user.**" Evidence can be used at the **institutional level** (synthesized findings → policies, algorithms, procedures, protocols, formal programs) or the **individual level** (RNs, educators, policy makers making practice, education, and political decisions).

| Phase | Name | What happens |
|---|---|---|
| **I** | **Preparation** | Determine the **purpose, focus, and potential outcomes** of the change; then conduct a **detailed literature search** to determine the strength of evidence |
| **II** | **Validation** | **Critically appraise** the reports for **scientific soundness.** If studies are **limited in number, weak, or both**, findings are **inadequate and the process STOPS.** A systematic review/meta-analysis/meta-synthesis **greatly strengthens** the evidence |
| **III** | **Comparative Evaluation / Decision Making** | Four parts + three possible decisions (below) |
| **IV** | **Translation / Application** | **Planning for and actually using** the evidence — determining exactly **what** knowledge will be used and **how** it will be applied |
| **V** | **Evaluation** | — |

**Phase III's four parts:** (1) **substantiation of the evidence**; (2) **fit** of the evidence with the healthcare setting; (3) **feasibility** of using the findings; (4) **concerns with current practice.**

**Feasibility = Stetler's "three Rs":** (1) potential **Risks**; (2) **Resources** needed; (3) **Readiness** of those involved.

**Phase III's three possible decisions:**
1. **To use** the research evidence
2. **To consider using** it — when "a change is complex and involves multiple disciplines," more time is needed to coordinate
3. **NOT to use** it — "because the current evidence is **not strong**, or the **risks or costs of change** are too high in comparison with the benefits"

**Phase IV — three kinds of application:** **cognitive**, **instrumental**, or **symbolic.** *Cognitive application* means "the research base is a means of **modifying a way of thinking** or one's appreciation of an issue" — e.g. improving understanding of a situation or allowing analysis of practice dynamics.

### Iowa Model

"Promotes the implementation of EBP by nurses in **clinical agencies.**" Revised and validated by the **Iowa Model Collaborative (2017).**

**It starts with TRIGGERING ISSUES or OPPORTUNITIES:**

| Type | Examples |
|---|---|
| **Problem-focused triggers** | Clinical problems and data from **risk management, process improvement, benchmarking, and financial reports** |
| **Knowledge-focused opportunities** | **New research findings**; changes in **national agency or organizational standards and guidelines**; an **expanded philosophy of care**; an **updated professional model of care (a Magnet recognition requirement)**; questions from the **institutional standards' committee** |

**The flow:** topics are **evaluated and prioritized based on agency needs** → if a topic is an **agency priority**, a **group is formed** to search for the best evidence → if evidence is inadequate, **more studies are needed** → **research-based protocols/algorithms/guidelines/policies** are developed → **pilot-tested** → **evaluated** for impact on patient care, personnel, and the agency → if outcomes are favorable, **adopt** → administrators and personnel **integrate and sustain** the change → key outcomes **monitored through QI.**

## §5 EPCs and translational research

**Evidence-based practice centers (EPCs):** launched by the **AHRQ** in **1997**, initially **12 centers** in the **United States and Canada.** AHRQ became a "**science partner**" with public and private organizations. AHRQ awards **5-year contracts.** Topics are **nominated by nonfederal partners** (professional societies, health plans, insurers, employers, patient groups). Reports are used "to inform and develop **coverage decisions, quality measures, educational materials, tools, guidelines, and research agendas.**" EPCs also **conduct research on the methodology of systematic reviews.**

**Translational research** — developed in response to challenges to EBP:
- Called **"bench to bedside"** research: translating **basic scientific discoveries into practical applications.**
- "Basic research discoveries from the **laboratory** setting need to be **tested in human studies**," and "outcomes from human **clinical trials** need to be **adopted and maintained in clinical practice.**"
- **NCATS** (National Center for Advancing Translational Sciences) was created in **2011** as part of the **NIH.**
- The **CTSA** (Clinical and Translational Science Awards) consortium started with **12 centers**, expanded to **39 in April 2009**, and was **fully implemented in 2012** with about **60 institutions.**
- The **CTSA Consortium is mainly focused on medical research**; **Titler (2004)** defined translational research for **nursing.**

## Source conflicts and traps

- **Aspiration during IM injections has REVERSED.** Current evidence = **no aspiration** in **deltoid, ventrogluteal, vastus lateralis**; aspirate **only dorsogluteal** (gluteal artery) — and **avoid dorsogluteal if possible.** An option recommending routine 5–10-second aspiration in all sites is the **outdated** guideline the chapter uses as its example of practice lagging evidence.
- **Ventrogluteal, not dorsogluteal, is the recommended adult site** for large volumes. This is the specific practice error the chapter documents nurses still making.
- **Stetler Phase II can STOP the process.** If the studies are few or weak, the findings are "considered inadequate for use in practice, **and the process stops.**" An option requiring the project to continue to implementation regardless of evidence strength is wrong.
- **Phase III has THREE decisions, not two** — use, **consider using**, or **not use.** "Consider using" applies when a change is **complex and multidisciplinary.**
- **Stetler's three Rs are Risks, Resources, Readiness** — the feasibility test, not the whole model.
- **Stetler vs. Iowa.** **Stetler** = a **user-initiated, conscious critical-thinking process** usable by an **individual nurse** or institution, organized in **five phases.** **Iowa** = an **agency-level** model that begins with **triggering issues/opportunities** and runs through **piloting** before adoption. If a stem describes a *trigger* from risk-management data, that is **Iowa**; if it describes an individual nurse deciding whether to apply findings, that is **Stetler**.
- **Iowa triggers are of two kinds:** **problem-focused** (risk management, process improvement, benchmarking, financial reports) and **knowledge-focused** (new findings, new standards, updated professional model of care). Sorting a given example into the right kind is a likely item.
- **A pilot test comes BEFORE adoption in the Iowa Model** — and a favorable pilot is what makes adoption appropriate. Skipping straight to agency-wide rollout is the distractor.
- **The strongest evidence = systematic reviews containing meta-analyses of RCTs** — but the chapter explicitly preserves a role for **meta-syntheses, mixed-methods reviews, and individual studies.** An absolute statement that only meta-analyses may inform practice overstates the text.
- **Expert opinion and case reports are legitimate SUPPLEMENTS** here — combined with research evidence to build protocols — even though Ch 1's pyramid ranks expert opinion weakest **on its own.** Both statements are true; the difference is *alone* vs. *combined with research evidence*.
- **Magnet status is granted by the ANCC**, and an **updated professional model of care is a Magnet requirement** that can itself act as an Iowa trigger.
- **NGC = US, Cochrane = UK, Joanna Briggs = Australia.** Country/organization matching is an easy item to write.
- **The core EBP challenge is that evidence is population-level but patients are individuals.** An option claiming guidelines should be applied uniformly regardless of patient circumstances contradicts both this chapter and the three-part EBP definition from Ch 1.
