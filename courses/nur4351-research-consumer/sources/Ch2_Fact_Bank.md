## Key terms

| Term | Definition as given |
|---|---|
| **quantitative research** | "A formal, objective, rigorous, and systematic process for generating numerical information about the world." |
| **descriptive research** | "The exploration and description of phenomena in real-life situations." Provides "an accurate account of characteristics of particular individuals, situations, or groups using numbers." |
| **correlational research** | "The systematic investigation of relationships between or among variables." |
| **quasi-experimental research** | Purpose is "to examine causal relationships or determine the effect of one variable on another"; involves **implementing an intervention** and examining its effects. |
| **experimental research** | "An objective, systematic, and **highly controlled** investigation conducted for the purposes of **predicting and controlling** phenomena in nursing practice." |
| **basic research** | Also **pure** or **bench** research. "Scientific investigations conducted for the pursuit of knowledge for its own sake or for the pleasure of learning and finding truth." |
| **applied research** | Also **practical** research. "Scientific investigations conducted to generate knowledge that will **directly influence or improve clinical practice.**" Purpose: solve problems, make decisions, predict or control outcomes in real-life practice. |
| **rigor** | "The striving for excellence in research, which requires **discipline, adherence to detail, precision, and accuracy.**" |
| **precision** | "Encompasses accuracy, detail, and order." Most explicit example is **the measurement or quantification of the study variables.** |
| **control** | "The imposing of rules by researchers to **decrease the possibility of error**, thereby increasing the probability that the study's findings are an accurate reflection of reality." |
| **design** | "The rules used to achieve control in research." Also: "a **blueprint** for the conduct of a study that maximizes control over factors that could interfere with the study's desired outcome." |
| **extraneous variables** | Variables that "exist in **all** studies and can interfere with obtaining a clear understanding of the relationships among the study variables." |
| **natural (field) setting** | "An **uncontrolled** real-life situation or environment"; the researcher **does not manipulate or change** the environment. |
| **partially controlled setting** | "An environment that the researcher **has manipulated or modified in some way.**" |
| **highly controlled setting** | "An **artificially constructed** environment developed for the sole purpose of conducting research" (laboratories, research centers, test units). |
| **sampling** | "A process of selecting participants who are **representative of the population** being studied." |
| **random sampling** | Each member of the population "is selected **independently** and has an **equal chance, or probability,** of being included in the study." |
| **bias** | "Slanting of findings away from what is true or accurate." |
| **intervention fidelity** | "The detailed development of a quality intervention and the **consistent implementation** of this intervention." |
| **variables** | "Concepts at various levels of abstraction that are **measured, manipulated, or controlled** in a study." |
| **research concepts** | The term used for **more abstract** concepts (creativity, empathy, social support), vs. concrete ones (temperature, weight, BP) called *variables*. |
| **conceptual definition** | "Provides a variable or concept with **theoretical meaning**"; comes from a theorist's definition or concept analysis. Links the study framework to the study variables. |
| **operational definition** | Developed "so that the variable **can be measured** or an intervention implemented in a study." |
| **population** | "All elements (individuals, objects, or substances) that meet certain criteria for inclusion in a study." |
| **sample** | "A subset of the population selected for a particular study"; its members are the **participants.** |
| **pilot study** | "Often a **smaller version** of a proposed study," conducted to refine the sampling process, intervention, or measurement of variables. |
| **research problem** | "An area of concern in which there is a **gap in the knowledge** needed for nursing practice." |
| **concept** | "A term that abstractly names and describes an object or phenomenon, providing it with a separate identity and meaning." |

## §1 The four types of quantitative research (Fig. 2.1) — the core of the chapter

**They build on each other.** "When little knowledge is available, **descriptive** studies are conducted that provide a basis for **correlational** research. Descriptive and correlational studies are conducted frequently to provide a basis for the more highly controlled **quasi-experimental and experimental** studies."

| Type | What it does | Control | Intervention? |
|---|---|---|---|
| **Descriptive** | Explores and describes phenomena **in real-life situations**; describes what exists, determines frequency, categorizes | **Minimal** | No |
| **Correlational** | Systematically investigates **relationships** between/among variables | **Minimal** | No |
| **Quasi-experimental** | Examines **causal relationships** / the effect of one variable on another | Moderate | **Yes** |
| **Experimental** | **Highly controlled**; predicts and controls phenomena | **Highest** | **Yes** |

**Descriptive research** — usually conducted with **large numbers** of participants, in **natural settings**, with **no manipulation.** Outcomes: identification and description of concepts, identification of **possible** relationships, and **development of hypotheses** that provide a basis for future quantitative research.

**Correlational research** — measures variables in a sample, then uses correlational statistics to determine **degree/strength** and **type** of relationship.
- **Range: −1 (perfect negative) to +1 (perfect positive), with 0 indicating no relationship.**
- **Positive relationship** = variables vary **together**; both increase or decrease together. *Example: the more minutes people exercise each week, the greater their bone density.*
- **Negative relationship** = variables vary in **opposite directions**; as one increases, the other decreases. *Example: as smoking pack-years increase, life span decreases.*
- **The primary intent is to explain the nature of relationships — NOT to determine cause and effect.** But the relationships found **generate hypotheses** for quasi-experimental and experimental studies that do examine causality.

**Quasi-experimental vs. experimental — the difference is the LEVEL OF CONTROL.** Quasi-experimental studies have **less control** over (1) implementation of the intervention, (2) management of the setting, and/or (3) selection of participants.

> **Why nurses do more quasi-experimental than experimental research:** "When studying human behavior, especially in clinical settings, researchers frequently are **unable to select the participants randomly** or control certain variables related to the intervention or setting. As a result, **nurse researchers conduct more quasi-experimental studies than experimental studies.**"

## §2 Basic vs. applied research

| | **Basic** (pure, bench) | **Applied** (practical) |
|---|---|---|
| **Purpose** | Knowledge **for its own sake**; "the pleasure of learning and finding truth" | Knowledge that will **directly influence or improve clinical practice** |
| **Aim** | Establish **general scientific principles** | **Solve problems, make decisions, predict or control outcomes** in real-life practice |
| **Typical work** | Laboratory investigations with animals or humans; physiological functioning, genetics, pathological processes | Clinical studies in practice settings |

Basic research **provides a basis for** applied research. The chapter's example: Wacker et al. (2016) studied rickets muscle/bone pathology **in mice** — a basic experimental study. **NINR funds basic/bench research**; the **NHGRI** conducts laboratory research on human genetic makeup.

## §3 Rigor and control — the quality vocabulary

**A rigorously conducted quantitative study has:** (1) **precise measuring tools**, (2) a **representative sample**, and (3) a **tightly controlled study design.**

**Precision** is most explicitly shown in **measurement.** The chapter's contrast: using a **cardiac monitor** to record heart rate is more precise than **palpating a radial pulse for 30 seconds** and writing it on a data sheet. Precision enables **transparency**, which enables **replication.**

**Control** = imposing rules to decrease error. The rules that achieve control **are the design.** Studies range from **uncontrolled to highly controlled.**

**Extraneous variables** exist in **all** studies. The chapter's worked example: studying the effect of relaxation therapy on incisional pain, you must control **type of surgical incision** and **time, amount, and type of pain medication** — e.g. by selecting only patients with **abdominal incisions** receiving **one type of IV pain medication.**

**The three research settings:**

| Setting | Definition | Typically used for |
|---|---|---|
| **Natural (field)** | Uncontrolled real-life environment; researcher **does not manipulate** it | Descriptive and correlational studies |
| **Partially controlled** | Environment the researcher **has manipulated or modified** in some way | An increasing number of nursing studies |
| **Highly controlled** | **Artificially constructed** environment built solely for research | Bench and experimental studies |

**Sampling vs. assignment — a critical two-step distinction.** A randomly *selected* sample is very difficult to obtain in nursing, so quantitative studies "often are conducted with **nonrandom samples.**" To increase control and rigor and decrease bias, participants initially selected **nonrandomly** are often **randomly assigned** to intervention or control groups.

> **The chapter's worked example:** Liao et al. (2017) obtained their sample of older adults by **nonrandom convenience sampling**, but the design "was **strengthened by the random assignment**" of those adults to the exercise intervention or usual activity. **Nonrandom selection + random assignment is a real and common combination** — one does not imply the other.

**A study intervention needs to be:** (1) **clearly and precisely developed**; (2) **consistently implemented**; and (3) **examined for effectiveness** through quality measurement of the dependent variables. Development + consistent implementation = **intervention fidelity.**

## §4 Conceptual vs. operational definitions

Researchers **operationalize** variables by giving both:

- **Conceptual definition** — gives the variable **theoretical meaning**; comes from a **theorist's definition or concept analysis**; **links the study framework to the study variables.**
- **Operational definition** — makes the variable **measurable** (or the intervention implementable).

**The chapter's worked pair** (Moon et al., 2017, primary stressors):
- *Conceptual:* "the CRs' dementia status, level of care provided by CG, and whether care was provided on a regular schedule."
- *Operational:* dementia status defined by NHATS participant or proxy report; a **level of care variable built as the sum of five questions** (chores, shopping, personal care, getting around home, transportation) scored **1 = rarely to 4 = every day**, higher scores = higher involvement; regular schedule coded **0 = varied, 1 = regular.**

> **How to tell them apart instantly:** if it names **numbers, scales, coding, or instruments**, it is the **operational** definition. If it names **meaning drawn from theory**, it is **conceptual.**

**Variables vs. research concepts:** concrete concepts (temperature, weight, blood pressure) are called **variables**; abstract ones (creativity, empathy, social support) are sometimes called **research concepts.**

## §5 Nursing process vs. research process

**Similarities:** both involve **abstract critical thinking and complex reasoning**; both identify new information, discover relationships, and make predictions. In both, information is gathered, observations made, problems identified, plans developed (**methodology**), and actions taken (**data collection and analysis**). Both are reviewed for effectiveness — **the nursing process is evaluated; outcomes are determined in the research process.**

**Differences — the research process is:**

| Dimension | Nursing process | Research process |
|---|---|---|
| **Complexity** | Less complex | **More complex**; rigorous application of varied research methods |
| **Focus** | A **specific patient and family** | **Large groups** (e.g. a population of patients with hypertension) |
| **Theoretical underpinnings** | Weaker | **Much stronger**; all steps logically linked to each other and to the study's theoretical foundations |
| **Precision, rigor, control** | Less required | **Greater** required |
| **Outcomes** | Applied to that patient | **Shared widely** via presentations and publications; can be **synthesized** across studies into evidence |

**The nursing process is a subset of the problem-solving process.**

The quantitative research process involves **conceptualizing** a project, **planning and implementing** it, and **communicating the findings.** It is depicted as a **circle** because "there is a flow **back and forth**" as steps are developed.

## Source conflicts and traps

- **Correlational research does NOT establish cause and effect.** "The primary intent of correlational studies is to explain the nature of relationships in the real world, **not to determine cause and effect.**" Any option concluding that a correlational finding proves one variable causes another is the distractor. Correlational findings **generate hypotheses** for causal studies.
- **Quasi-experimental vs. experimental is decided by CONTROL, not by whether there's an intervention.** Both have interventions. Quasi-experimental has less control over intervention implementation, setting, and/or participant selection. If the stem says participants **could not be randomly selected**, think quasi-experimental.
- **Nurses conduct MORE quasi-experimental than experimental studies** — because clinical settings rarely permit random selection and full control. An option claiming experimental designs are the most common in nursing is wrong.
- **Random selection ≠ random assignment.** A study can use a **convenience (nonrandom) sample** and still **randomly assign** participants to groups — the chapter gives exactly this example. An option treating "random assignment" as proof the sample was randomly selected is inverted.
- **A correlation of 0 means no relationship, not a negative one.** Range is **−1 to +1.** A negative correlation is a *real, strong* relationship when near −1 — "negative" describes **direction**, not weakness. This is the classic misread.
- **Positive relationship = same direction (both up or both down)**, not "good." The smoking/life-span example is a **negative** relationship; the exercise/bone-density example is **positive.**
- **Basic vs. applied.** Basic = knowledge for its own sake, often **bench/animal** work. Applied = **directly improves clinical practice.** An option calling a laboratory mouse study "applied research" is wrong.
- **Design IS the rules for control.** The chapter defines design two ways — "the rules used to achieve control" and "a blueprint... that maximizes control." An option calling design merely "the plan for data collection" is too narrow.
- **Extraneous variables exist in ALL studies**, including descriptive ones — not only in experiments. Control **reduces** their influence; it never eliminates them.
- **Descriptive and correlational studies are still RIGOROUS** despite minimal control. The chapter says they "are rigorously conducted but are often designed with minimal researcher control." Low control ≠ low rigor — a likely trap.
- **Conceptual vs. operational definitions.** Conceptual = theoretical meaning from the framework. Operational = how it is **measured.** Swapping them is the highest-probability item from this section.
- **A pilot study is a smaller version of a proposed study**, run to refine sampling, intervention, or measurement — not a failed study or a preliminary literature search.
