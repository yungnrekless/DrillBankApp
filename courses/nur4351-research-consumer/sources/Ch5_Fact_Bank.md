## Key terms

| Term | Definition as given |
|---|---|
| **research topic** | "A concept or broad issue that is important to nursing," such as acute pain, chronic pain management, coping with illness, health promotion. |
| **research problem** | "An area of concern in which there is a **gap in the knowledge** needed for nursing practice." |
| **research purpose** | "A clear concise statement of the **specific goal or focus** of a study." |
| **research objective** | "A clear, concise, **declarative** statement expressed in the **present tense** to identify the goals of the study." Sometimes called **aims.** |
| **research question** | "A clear, concise, **interrogative** statement worded in the **present tense**, includes **one or more variables**, and is expressed to guide the implementation of studies." |
| **hypothesis** | "A formal statement of the **expected relationship(s) between two or more variables in a specified population.**" |
| **associative hypothesis** | Proposes relationships among variables "that occur or exist together in the real world so that when one variable changes, the other changes" — but does **not** indicate one causes the other. |
| **causal hypothesis** | "Proposes a **cause and effect** interaction between two or more variables," i.e. independent and dependent. |
| **simple hypothesis** | States the relationship between **two** variables. |
| **complex hypothesis** | States the relationships among **three or more** variables. |
| **nondirectional hypothesis** | "States that a relationship **exists** but does **not predict the nature** (positive or negative) of the relationship." |
| **directional hypothesis** | "States the **nature** (positive or negative) of the interaction between two or more variables." |
| **statistical (null) hypothesis** | "Used for **statistical testing** and for interpreting statistical outcomes." Even if not stated, **it is implied because it is the converse of the research hypothesis.** |
| **testable hypothesis** | "One that clearly predicts the relationships among variables and contains variables that are **measurable or able to be manipulated** in a study." |
| **research variables** | "The qualities, properties, or characteristics identified in the research purpose and objectives or questions that are **measured** in a study." |
| **independent variable** | "The intervention or treatment that is **manipulated or varied by the researcher** to create an effect on the dependent variable." Also called **intervention, treatment, or experimental variable.** |
| **dependent variable** | "The **outcome** that the researcher wants to predict or explain." Changes in it "are presumed to be **caused by** the independent variable." |
| **extraneous variables** | Variables that "exist in **all** studies and can affect the measurement of study variables and the relationships among these variables." |

## §1 Topic → problem → purpose (the funnel)

- **Research topic** = a **broad** concept important to nursing (chronic pain, health promotion).
- **Research problem** = "an area of concern in which there is a **gap in the knowledge** needed for nursing practice." **The problem provides the basis for developing the research purpose.**
- **Research purpose** = "a clear concise statement of the **specific goal or focus** of a study."

**A published problem statement is built from parts** the chapter names in its worked example: **problem significance** → **problem background** → **problem statement** → then the **research purpose.**

**What makes a problem worth studying** (the chapter's criteria sections): **application to nursing practice**, **expands previous research**, and **addresses nursing research priorities.**

**Feasibility** is judged on: **researcher expertise**, **money commitment**, and **ethical considerations.**

## §2 Objectives, aims, and questions

| | **Research objective / aim** | **Research question** |
|---|---|---|
| **Grammatical form** | **Declarative** statement | **Interrogative** statement |
| **Tense** | **Present tense** | **Present tense** |
| **Content** | Usually focuses on **one or two variables**; identifies or describes them | Includes **one or more variables** |
| **Typically found in** | **Descriptive and correlational** quantitative studies | Both |

**Objectives may also** identify relationships among variables, or determine **differences between two or more existing groups** (e.g. females and males).

**Qualitative vs. quantitative objectives:** both focus on exploration, description, and relationships, but qualitative objectives are "commonly **broader in focus** and include concepts that are **more complex and abstract.**" Qualitative aims focus on **participants' experiences**, **theory development**, **understanding cultures** of groups/institutions, and description of **challenges, reasons for behaviors, and perceptions.**

**Qualitative research is most appropriate** when the focus is to obtain a **personal perspective** of a situation, experience, or event.

## §3 The four hypothesis dimensions (Box 5.2) — highest-yield structure

**Every hypothesis is classified on all four axes simultaneously.**

| Axis | Option A | Option B |
|---|---|---|
| **Relationship type** | **Associative** — variables change together, no causation claimed | **Causal** — independent variable causes an effect on dependent variable |
| **Number of variables** | **Simple** — **two** variables | **Complex** — **three or more** variables |
| **Direction** | **Nondirectional** — a relationship exists, nature not predicted | **Directional** — predicts positive or negative |
| **Form** | **Research** hypothesis | **Statistical / null (H₀)** hypothesis |

**How to spot a directional hypothesis:** the words **positive, negative, less, more, increase, decrease, greater, higher, or lower.**

**When each is used:**
- **Nondirectional** when "the direction of the relationship being studied is **not clear** in clinical practice or in the theoretical or empirical literature."
- **Directional** hypotheses "are developed from **theoretical statements (propositions), findings of previous studies, and clinical experience.**" As knowledge increases, researchers can predict direction.

> **The rule that generates exam items:** **ALL CAUSAL HYPOTHESES ARE DIRECTIONAL.** "The independent variable increases or decreases each dependent variable; thus **all causal hypotheses are directional.**" So "causal + nondirectional" is an impossible combination — a guaranteed distractor.

**The chapter's worked classifications** (learn these as templates):

| Hypothesis | Classification |
|---|---|
| "Hours playing video games is related to body mass index in school-age children" | **Simple** (2 variables), **associative**, **nondirectional** |
| "Parents will be **more likely** to misperceive the weight status of **younger** children" | **Simple**, **associative**, **directional** (negative relationship) |
| "Children of overweight or obese parents are more likely to be obese" | **Associative** (predicts two positive relationships) |
| "Participants who perform brisk walking exercise combined with music... exhibit **higher** sleep quality scores" | **Causal**, **complex** (3 variables), **directional**, **positive** |

**Diagramming convention:** **straight lines without arrows = associative** (linear relationships/associations); **arrows (→) = causal.**

**Which studies need hypotheses:**
- **Quasi-experimental and experimental** studies "should include hypotheses to predict the study outcomes."
- **Predictive correlational** studies that measure independent variables to predict a dependent variable **often** include hypotheses.

**Hypotheses also influence** the study design, sampling method, data collection and analysis process, and interpretation of findings.

**A clearly stated hypothesis includes three things:** the **independent variables** to be manipulated or measured, the proposed **outcomes/dependent variables** to be measured, and the **population** to be studied.

## §4 What makes a hypothesis TESTABLE

A testable hypothesis:
1. **Clearly predicts relationships** among variables.
2. Contains variables that are **measurable or able to be manipulated.** The **independent** variable must be clearly defined (often by a **protocol**) so it can be implemented precisely and consistently; the **dependent** variable must be defined to indicate how it will be precisely and accurately measured.
3. Predicts a relationship that can be **"supported" or "not supported"** by the data.

**How each type is tested:**
- **Associative** hypothesis → **correlational analyses** (existence, type, and strength of relationship).
- **Causal** hypothesis → statistical tests of **differences between means**, such as **t-test or ANOVA**, for experimental vs. comparison/control groups.
- **It is the statistical (null) hypothesis — stated or implied — that is actually tested** to determine whether the independent variable produced a significant effect.

**What a hypothesis must NOT contain:**
- **Not** the presence or absence of a significant difference — "determination of the level of significance is only a **statistical technique applied to sample data.**"
- **Not methodological points.** Phrases such as **"measured by," "in a random sample of," and "using ANOVA" are inappropriate** because they limit the hypothesis to one study's measurement methods, sample, or analysis techniques.
- Hypotheses **must reflect the variables and population outlined in the research purpose.**

## §5 The four variable types

| Type | What it is | Where it appears |
|---|---|---|
| **Research variables** | Qualities/properties/characteristics **measured** in a study, observed **as they exist in a natural setting, without a treatment** | **Descriptive and correlational** studies — no independent variables manipulated, **no cause-and-effect examined** |
| **Independent** | The **intervention/treatment manipulated** by the researcher to create an effect. Also *intervention, treatment, experimental* variable | Quasi-experimental, experimental — and, in **predictive correlational** studies, the measured predictors |
| **Dependent** | The **outcome** to predict or explain; its changes are **presumed caused by** the independent variable | Correlational, quasi-experimental, experimental |
| **Extraneous** | Exist in **all** studies; affect measurement and relationships | **Primary concern in intervention studies** |

**Extraneous variables are classified two ways:** **recognized or unrecognized**, and **controlled or uncontrolled.** "Some extraneous variables are **not recognized until the study is in progress or has been completed**, but their presence influences the study outcome."

Researchers control them through **specific designs, intervention protocols, and sample criteria.** **Environmental control** is "an extremely important part of conducting an experimental study."

> **A subtlety worth knowing:** in **predictive correlational** studies, the measured predictors **are called independent variables** even though nothing is manipulated. The chapter says so explicitly — "the variables measured to predict a single dependent variable are called independent variables." So "independent variable" does not always mean "manipulated intervention."

## Source conflicts and traps

- **All causal hypotheses are directional.** The chapter states it outright. Any option describing a "causal nondirectional hypothesis" is impossible and is the distractor.
- **Simple vs. complex is a COUNT, not a difficulty judgment.** Simple = exactly **two** variables; complex = **three or more.** A hypothesis can be simple *and* causal, or complex *and* associative.
- **Associative ≠ causal.** Associative hypotheses "**do not indicate that one variable causes an effect on another**." A stem drawing a causal conclusion from an associative hypothesis is wrong — the same trap as Ch 2's correlational-research rule.
- **The null hypothesis is what gets tested**, even when only the research hypothesis is printed — "even if the null hypothesis is not stated, it is **implied because it is the converse of the research hypothesis.**"
- **A hypothesis must NOT name methods.** "Measured by," "in a random sample of," "using ANOVA" are all inappropriate. An option praising a hypothesis for specifying its measurement instrument is inverted — that is a *flaw*.
- **A hypothesis should not state significance.** Significance is a statistical technique applied to sample data, not part of the prediction.
- **Objective = declarative; question = interrogative.** Both present tense. Swapping the grammatical forms is a likely item. "Aims" is a synonym for objectives.
- **Research variables belong to studies with NO treatment.** If a stem describes measuring variables as they naturally exist with no intervention, the correct label is **research variables**, not independent/dependent.
- **Independent variables exist in predictive correlational studies too**, without manipulation. An option asserting that an independent variable must always be manipulated is too absolute for this chapter.
- **Extraneous variables exist in ALL studies** — including descriptive and correlational ones — but are of **primary concern** in intervention studies. They may be **unrecognized** until after the study.
- **A directional hypothesis can predict a NEGATIVE relationship and still be "directional."** Direction means the nature is specified, not that it is positive. The McKee "younger children" example is directional *and* negative.
- **Problem vs. purpose.** The **problem** is the **knowledge gap**; the **purpose** is the **specific goal of this study.** The problem gives rise to the purpose, not the reverse.
