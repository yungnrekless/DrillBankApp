## §1 Concepts underlying design

**Causality.** In a critical appraisal, determine whether the study's purpose is to **examine causality**, **examine relationships** (correlational), or **describe variables** (descriptive). You can often tell from the **purpose statement and the framework's propositions.**

**Multicausality.** "**Very few phenomena in nursing can be clearly linked to a single cause and a single effect.**" Studies from a multicausal perspective include **more variables.** Because causal relationships are complex, "a theory is unlikely to identify every element involved in causing a particular outcome" — but the more causal factors identified and controlled, the clearer the understanding.

**Probability.** Researchers "recognize that a particular cause **probably will** result in a specific effect," and design studies to examine **the probability that a given effect will occur under a defined set of circumstances.**

**Bias.** Quantitative researchers "place great value on **identifying and removing sources of bias.**" Sources named: **attitudes or motivations of the researcher (conscious or unconscious), the environment, selection of participants, composition of the sample, groups formed, measurement methods, the data collection process, and statistical analyses.**

> **A caution the chapter states plainly:** "**Not all biases can be identified from the published study report.** The article may not provide sufficient detail about the methods of the study to detect possible biases." An appraisal that claims a study is bias-free is overreaching.

**Prospective = forward; retrospective = backward.**

## §2 Study validity overview

**Study validity = the truth or accuracy of the findings.** "Design validity encompasses the **strengths and threats** to the quality of a study design." Critical appraisal requires identifying **design strengths** *and* thinking through **threats to validity.**

**The four types (Table 8.1):**

| Type | Asks | One-line memory hook |
|---|---|---|
| **Construct** | Do the **operational definitions match the conceptual definitions**? | *Are we measuring what we say we're measuring?* |
| **Internal** | Are the effects a **true reflection of reality**, not extraneous variables? | *Did the intervention really cause it?* |
| **External** | Can findings be **generalized beyond the sample**? | *Who else does this apply to?* |
| **Statistical conclusion** | Are the **statistical conclusions** about relationships/differences accurate? | *Did the numbers tell the truth?* |

## §3 The four validities and their threats — the core structure

### Construct validity — fit between conceptual and operational definitions

Operational definitions (**methods of measurement**) "must accurately reflect the theoretical constructs or concepts." Developing construct validity for an instrument "often requires **years of scientific work.**"

**Threats:**

| Threat | What it is |
|---|---|
| **Inadequate definitions of constructs** | "A deficiency in the **conceptual or operational definition** leads to low construct validity." The conceptual definition ideally emerges from a **concept analysis** |
| **Mono-operation bias** | **Only one method of measurement** is used, so **fewer dimensions** of the construct are measured. **Construct validity greatly improves with more than one instrument** |
| **Experimenter expectancies (Rosenthal effect)** | The researcher's expectations **bias the data** |

**The chapter's mono-operation example:** if pain is the dependent variable, use more than one measure — a **pain rating scale**, **verbal reports**, **physical responses** (increased pulse, BP, respirations), and **observed behaviors** (crying, grimacing, guarding, pulling away).

**Fixes for experimenter expectancy:** some researchers **choose not to be involved in data collection**; in others, **data collectors are blinded** to group assignment. "Using nonbiased data collectors or those who are blinded to group assignment **increases the construct design validity.**"

### Internal validity — is the effect real?

"Internal validity is a concern in **all studies**, but is a **major focus in studies examining causality.**" The appraisal question: *"Is there **another explanation (rival hypothesis)** for the finding other than the one I have proposed?"*

**Threats:**

| Threat | What it is | Key detail |
|---|---|---|
| **Selection** | How participants are chosen and **grouped** | "**More likely to occur in studies in which randomization is not possible.**" **Random assignment decreases** this threat |
| **Attrition** | Participants **dropping out** | A threat when (1) dropouts **differ in type** from those who remain, or (2) there is a **difference in number and type** of dropouts between groups. **Attrition > 25% could affect the accuracy of results** |
| **History** | An **unrelated event during** the study | Example: several nurses **quit** during a cardiac-rehab support study |
| **Maturation** | Participants growing older/wiser/stronger/more tired/more experienced | "**More likely in longitudinal studies with repeated measures.**" The changes are **unplanned and unrecognized** |

### External validity — can it be generalized?

"With the **most serious threat**, the findings would be **meaningful only for the group studied.**" Generalization "is usually **narrower for a single study** than for **multiple replications** using different samples from different populations and settings."

**The three interaction threats:**

| Threat | What it is | The chapter's example |
|---|---|---|
| **Interaction of selection and intervention** | A high **refusal rate** limits the sample in non-obvious ways | If **39%** declined, the sample may be "volunteers, 'do-gooders,' or those with nothing better to do" — generalizing to all nurses/patients is not justified |
| **Interaction of setting and intervention** | Bias in **which settings agree** to participate | Hospitals seeking **Magnet designation** welcome nursing studies; others resist — the two types "may be different in important ways." Also, a **low-income clinic** may serve patients with **lower health literacy** vs. an insured clinic with more college-educated patients |
| **Interaction of history and intervention** | Circumstances **when the study was conducted** affect generalization | Three patients dying from **dialysis equipment failure** during a dialysis-support study |

**Reducing the selection threat:** limit demands on participants — e.g. choose instruments that are **valid and reliable but have fewer items** to decrease **participant burden**; develop and communicate the intervention skillfully.

> "**Logically, one can never generalize to the future**; however, **replicating the study during various time periods** strengthens the usefulness of findings over time."

### Statistical conclusion validity — did the numbers tell the truth?

**Two steps in inferring cause:** (1) determine whether the independent and dependent variables **are related** (via statistical analysis); (2) **identify differences** between and among groups.

**Threats:**

| Threat | What it is |
|---|---|
| **Low statistical power** | "Increases the probability of concluding that there is **no** significant relationship/difference **when actually there is one — a Type II error.**" Most likely when the **sample size is small** or the **power of the statistical test is low** |
| **Unreliable measurement methods** | "A measure is **reliable if it gives the same result each time** the same situation or variable is measured." Physiological measures that measure consistently are **precise** (a thermometer showing the same reading on repeat testing) |
| **Limited intervention fidelity** | If administration **varies from one person to another**, "the chance of detecting a true difference **decreases**." Data collectors must be **trained** for consistency |
| **Extraneous variances in the setting** | — |

## §4 The design families

The chapter's design sections: **descriptive designs**, **correlational designs**, **elements of designs examining causality**, and **experimental designs.**

| Design | What it does |
|---|---|
| **Simple descriptive** | Describes variables as they exist |
| **Comparative descriptive** | Describes and **compares** variables across groups |
| **Descriptive correlational** | Describes **relationships** among variables |
| **Predictive correlational** | Measures independent variables to **predict** a dependent variable |
| **Model testing** | Tests a proposed model of relationships |
| **Quasi-experimental / experimental** | Test the **effect of an intervention**; involve **manipulation** |

**Manipulation** is "a form of control **generally used in quasi-experimental and experimental studies.**"

**The RCT** is "a type of experimental design often considered to be **one of the strongest designs to examine cause and effect.**"

## Source conflicts and traps

- **The four validities are frequently swapped.** Anchor them: **construct = measurement matches concept; internal = the effect is real; external = generalizes; statistical conclusion = the analysis is accurate.** An item describing "findings apply only to the group studied" is **external validity**, not internal.
- **Internal validity matters in ALL studies**, not only experiments — but it is the **major focus** in causal studies. An option restricting internal validity to experimental designs is too narrow.
- **Attrition has a number: >25%** could affect accuracy. But attrition is a threat **because of WHO drops out**, not merely how many — differential dropout between groups is the real problem.
- **Selection threat is worst when randomization is NOT possible**, and **random assignment decreases** it. This connects to Ch 2: nonrandom selection + random assignment is the common nursing compromise.
- **History vs. maturation.** **History = an external EVENT** during the study (nurses quitting, equipment failure). **Maturation = CHANGE WITHIN the participants** (older, wiser, more tired). Both are internal-validity threats and are routinely swapped. Maturation is most likely in **longitudinal** studies.
- **History appears in BOTH internal and external validity** — as a plain "history" threat to internal validity, and as "interaction of history and intervention" for external validity. An item may hinge on which is being asked.
- **Low statistical power causes a TYPE II error** (missing a real effect), not a Type I. Most likely with a **small sample.**
- **Mono-operation bias is about ONE measurement method, not one variable.** The fix is **multiple instruments** for the same construct.
- **The Rosenthal effect is a CONSTRUCT validity threat in this text**, not internal validity. The fix is **blinding data collectors** or removing the researcher from data collection.
- **Reliable ≠ valid.** Reliability here means "**gives the same result each time**" — consistency, not accuracy. A consistently wrong scale is reliable. (Ch 10 develops this.)
- **You can never generalize to the future.** Replication across time periods is the mitigation, not a claim of future validity.
- **A high refusal rate threatens EXTERNAL validity.** Researchers **must report the refusal rate.** An option treating refusals as irrelevant because the achieved sample was large enough misses this.
- **Not all bias is detectable from the published report.** The chapter says so directly — a published article may omit the methodological detail needed to spot bias.
- **Multicausality is the nursing norm.** "Very few phenomena in nursing can be clearly linked to a single cause and a single effect." An option asserting a single cause for a nursing outcome is usually the distractor.
