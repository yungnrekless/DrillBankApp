| **accessible population** | "The portion of the target population to which the researcher has **reasonable access.**" |

## §1 The population funnel

**Population → target population → accessible population → sample → element**

- **Population** = the group of individuals or elements to be studied.
- **Target population** = **everyone who meets the sampling criteria** (e.g. adult males 18+, type 2 diabetes, hospitalized with a lower-extremity infection).
- **Accessible population** = the part the researcher **can reasonably reach** (e.g. patients with diabetes in an acute care hospital in Dallas, Texas).
- **Sample** = obtained **from the accessible population** using a sampling method.
- **Element** = the individual unit — a person, event, or object.

**Generalization flows back up the funnel:** researchers generalize **from the sample → to the accessible population → then, more abstractly, to the target population.**

**What determines how far you can generalize:** "the **quality of the study** and **consistency of the study's findings with previous research.**" High quality + consistent with prior work = more confidence. The chapter's example: findings on males with type 2 diabetes in Dallas may generalize to males with type 2 diabetes in **Texas urban hospitals**, or more broadly to **urban hospitals in the southern United States.**

## §2 Sampling criteria

- **Inclusion criteria** = what you **must have** to be in the target population.
- **Exclusion criteria** = what **removes you even if you meet inclusion criteria.**

**The chapter's worked example** (preoperative teaching about early ambulation → length of stay, older adults with knee replacement):
- **Inclusion:** adults **60 years or older**, **able to speak and read English**, undergoing **surgical replacement of one knee joint**
- **Exclusion:** history of **previous joint replacement surgery**, diagnosis of **dementia**, **debilitating chronic muscle disease**

**The trade-off:** criteria should produce a sample that is **heterogeneous, with a broad range of values.** "If the sampling criteria are **too narrow and restrictive**, researchers may have difficulty obtaining an adequately sized sample... which can **limit the generalization** of findings."

> **A caution the chapter raises:** researchers sometimes **generalize beyond their sampling criteria.** If a sample was limited to English speakers because the teaching and instruments were in English, generalizing to non-English speakers may be unjustified — "perhaps non–English-speaking persons, because they come from another culture, do not respond to the teaching in the same way." **When appraising, examine the inclusion and exclusion criteria to judge whether the generalization is appropriate.**

## §3 Representativeness, random vs. systematic variation

**Representativeness must be evaluated in three areas:** the **setting**, the **characteristics of the participants**, and the **distribution of values** on measured variables.

**Setting matters.** Private hospitals "are less likely to recruit participants with low incomes." Research centers differ from community, public, veterans', and rural hospitals. **"People who do not have access to care are usually excluded from studies."** Rural residents may respond differently than urban. **Researchers who gather data across a variety of settings have a more representative sample than those using a single setting.**

**Characteristics:** age, gender, ethnicity, income, education — the **demographic or attribute variables.** Analyzing them produces the **sample characteristics**, which "must be reasonably representative of the characteristics of the population." **If the study includes groups, the groups must have comparable demographic characteristics.**

**Large databases produce more representative samples.**

| | **Random variation** | **Systematic variation (systematic bias)** |
|---|---|---|
| **What it is** | **Expected** differences among participants — some above, some below the mean | Participants' values **differ from the population in a specific way** |
| **Direction** | Scatters **both ways** around the mean | **Mostly the same direction** — sample mean is higher or lower than the population mean |
| **Effect of increasing sample size** | **Decreases** it → more values near the mean → **improves representativeness** | **NO effect.** "Increasing the sample size has **no effect** on systematic variation" |

**The chapter's example of systematic bias:** if all participants in a knowledge study have **IQ above 120**, their test scores will be higher than a population mean built from a wide IQ range (mean 100).

> **Why systematic bias is dangerous in intervention studies:** "When systematic bias occurs in quasi-experimental or experimental studies, it can lead the researcher to **conclude that the treatment has made a difference, when in actuality the values would have been different even without the treatment.**"

## §4 The four calculable rates — memorize the formulas

**Refusal rate** = (number refusing ÷ number approached) × 100%
**Acceptance rate** = (number accepting ÷ number approached) × 100%
**Attrition rate** = (number withdrawing ÷ sample size) × 100%
**Retention rate** = (number completing ÷ sample size) × 100%

**Shortcuts the chapter states:** acceptance rate = **100% − refusal rate**; retention rate = **100% − attrition rate.**

**The chapter's fully worked example** (preoperative teaching study):

| Step | Numbers |
|---|---|
| 80 approached, 4 refuse | **Refusal rate = 4/80 = 5%** |
| 80 − 4 = 76 accept | **Acceptance rate = 76/80 = 95%** — "a study strength" |
| Of n = 76, **31 withdraw** (12 treatment, 19 comparison) | **Attrition rate = 31/76 = 41%** |
| Groups of 38 each | **Treatment: 12/38 = 32%. Comparison: 19/38 = 50%** |
| 45 of 76 retained | **Retention rate = 45/76 = 59%** |

**Interpretation rules:**
- **High acceptance / low refusal → less chance for systematic variation → sample more likely representative.**
- **Higher retention rate → more representative → "the more likely the study results are an accurate reflection of reality."**
- **Systematic variation is greatest** when **a large number withdraws**, OR when **a large number withdraws from one group but not the other(s).**
- **Researchers report the refusal rate OR the acceptance rate, but not both** — same for attrition vs. retention. **Best practice: report both the number withdrawing and the rate, plus rationales for withdrawal.**
- Direction of differential attrition is not fixed: **comparison-group participants may be more likely to withdraw** (no treatment), **but attrition can be higher in the treatment group if the intervention is complex or time-consuming.**

> In the worked example, the study has **strong potential for systematic variation** because attrition was **41%** *and* the comparison group's **50%** exceeded the treatment group's **32%.**

## §5 Probability (random) sampling methods

**Probability sampling methods "are designed to increase representativeness and decrease systematic variation or bias."** Each element has an **opportunity to be selected.**

> **Why randomness matters:** without it, researchers "**might tend (consciously or unconsciously) to select individuals whose conditions or behaviors are consistent with the study hypotheses**" — excluding people for being "too sick, not sick enough, coping too well, not coping adequately, uncooperative, or noncompliant." Random sampling "leaves the selection to chance, thereby **increasing the validity** of their study findings."

**A sampling frame is required** — a list of every member of the population. **HIPAA has increased the difficulty of obtaining a complete sampling frame** because of its requirements to protect health information.

| Method | How it works | Notes |
|---|---|---|
| **Simple random** | Randomly select elements from the sampling frame — names in a container, a **computer program (most common)**, or a **table of random numbers** | The **most basic** probability plan. A random numbers table is "useful only when the population number is **less than 100**" |
| **Stratified random** | Define **strata** on variables known to be critical, then randomly sample within each | Ensures **all levels of the variable are adequately represented.** Achieves the same representativeness with a **smaller sample** than simple random |
| **Cluster** | Sample **states → cities → hospitals → units**; several stages = **multistage sampling** | Used when (1) a **geographically dispersed** sample is needed but travel cost is prohibitive, or (2) **individual elements cannot be listed** so no sampling frame exists |
| **Systematic** | Select **every kth element** from an ordered list, **starting at a random point.** **k = population size ÷ desired sample size** | **If the starting point is NOT random, the sample is nonprobability.** Provides "a **random but not equal** chance for inclusion" |

**Stratification variables commonly used:** age, gender, race and ethnicity, socioeconomic status, diagnosis, geographic region, type of nursing care, site of care.

**Two ways to allocate a stratified sample:**
1. **Proportional to the population** — if the population is 60/20/15/5%, a sample of 100 takes 60/20/15/5.
2. **Equal numbers per stratum** — e.g. 25 participants in each of four age bands. **But: "With equal numbers of study participants in each group, the smaller groups are overrepresented, which can create sampling error."**

**Systematic sampling worked example:** N = 1200, desired n = 100 → **k = 12** → include every 12th person.

**A benchmark worth remembering:** in the simple-random example, a **41.5% response rate for mailed questionnaires was judged adequate because "the response rate to questionnaires averages 25%–50%."**

## §6 Nonprobability (nonrandom) sampling methods

| Method | How it works | Representativeness |
|---|---|---|
| **Convenience (accidental)** | Participants included "merely because they happen to be in the right place at the right time"; enroll available participants until the desired size is reached | **Weakest** — "provides little opportunity to control for biases" |
| **Quota** | Convenience sampling **plus** a strategy to include underrepresented subgroups; **goal is to replicate the proportions of subgroups in the target population** | "Offers an **improvement in representativeness over convenience sampling**" |

**Convenience sampling — the balanced view the chapter actually gives:**
- Multiple biases may exist, "some of which may be **subtle and unrecognized.**"
- **But** "serious biases are **not always** present in convenience samples," and a convenience sample "is **acceptable when it is used with reasonable knowledge and care.**"
- It is **inexpensive, accessible, and less time-consuming**, and "provides a means to conduct studies on nursing interventions when researchers **cannot use probability sampling** methods."
- **Many quasi-experimental studies and RCTs in nursing and medicine use convenience sampling.**

> **The critical distinction — random assignment is NOT a sampling method:** "Random assignment to groups, **which is not a sampling method but a design strategy**, **does not alter the risk of biases resulting from convenience sampling** but does **strengthen the equivalence of the study groups.**" To strengthen representativeness, researchers **increase the sample size.**

**Quota sampling** "is similar to that used in stratified random sampling" and requires the researcher to **identify subgroups and their proportions in the target population.** Underrepresented types named: **females, minority groups, older adults, and the poor, rich, and undereducated.**

## §7 Sample size in QUANTITATIVE studies — power analysis

**Power = "the ability of the study to detect differences or relationships that actually exist"** = **the ability to reject a null hypothesis correctly.**

**The numbers to memorize:**
- **Minimum acceptable power = 0.8 (80%).**
- That level "results in a **20% chance of a Type II error**."
- **Alpha (α), the level of significance, is frequently set at 0.05** in nursing studies.

**Where power analysis belongs:** results "should be included in the **sample section** of the study." Researchers must **also** perform a power analysis to evaluate sample adequacy **for all nonsignificant findings**, reported in the **discussion section.**

> **The appraisal question the chapter hands you:** for a study with a nonsignificant result — "**Is there really no difference? Or was an actual difference not found because of inadequacies in the research methods, such as small sample size?**"

**A sobering statistic:** of 103 RCTs reviewed, power analyses were reported for **81 (79%)** — but **24 (30%) of those did not include the required number of participants** indicated by their own power calculations. **Low-powered studies increase the risk of a Type II error.**

**Five factors influencing sample size (because they affect power):**

| Factor | Rule |
|---|---|
| **Effect size** | The **larger** the effect, the **smaller** the sample needed. Effect size = **how false the null hypothesis is** |
| **Type of study** | **Descriptive (especially surveys) and correlational studies need VERY LARGE samples.** **Quasi-experimental and experimental studies use smaller samples** — "as control increases, the sample size can be decreased." **Matched pairs** increase power and decrease sample size needed |
| **Number of variables** | **More variables → larger sample.** Multiple dependent variables increase the needed size. **Exception: using demographic variables ONLY to describe the sample does not cause a power problem** |
| **Measurement sensitivity** | **More precise instruments → smaller sample.** Less-developed tools produce **more variance** from the true score, and "as variance in instrument scores increases, the sample size needed to obtain significance increases" |
| **Data analysis techniques** | **Interval/ratio-level analyses are more powerful** than nominal/ordinal. "**Larger samples are needed when the power of the planned statistical analysis is weak.**" For **t-test and ANOVA, equal group sizes increase power** |

**The measurement-sensitivity example:** if a participant's true anxiety score is **80**, a poorly developed tool may yield readings from **70 to 90**, while a well-developed scale yields values close to 80.

**The effect size definition, precisely:** "If the null hypothesis is false, it is false **to some degree**; this is the effect size." **The statistical test tells you whether there is a difference; the EFFECT SIZE tells you how big it is.**

## §8 Sampling in QUALITATIVE research

| Method | What it is | Used for |
|---|---|---|
| **Purposeful (purposive/judgmental/selective)** | Researcher **consciously selects** participants — typical or atypical, similar or varied | "The ultimate goal is selecting **information-rich cases**." Best "to gain insights into a **new area**, discover new meaning, and/or obtain **in-depth understanding of a complex experience**" |
| **Network (snowball/chain/nominated)** | Found participants **help locate others** with similar characteristics | Participants "difficult or impossible to obtain in other ways"; **socially devalued populations** (alcohol dependence, child abuse, sexual offenses, drug addiction, criminal acts) and groups **outside the healthcare system** (widows, grieving siblings, people successful at lifestyle changes) |
| **Theoretical** | Gather data from anyone who can provide **relevant, varied, rich information for theory generation**; continue **until codes are saturated** | **Most frequent in GROUNDED THEORY.** "**Diversity in the sample is encouraged** so that the theory covers a wide range of behaviors in varied situations" |

**Purposeful sampling's criticism and its remedy:** it is criticized "because it is **difficult to evaluate the accuracy or relevance of the researcher's judgment.**" To offset this, researchers **must report the characteristics they desired in participants and provide a rationale** for selecting them.

**Maximum variation** (used in the chapter's worked example): "the researcher for diversity selects a small number of units or cases with **maximum variation relevant to the research question.** In maximum variation, **heterogeneity in cases is important.**"

**Theoretical sampling and saturation:** "**A code is saturated if it is complete and the researcher can see how it fits in the theory.** When a code or concept is unclear, the researcher **continues to seek participants and gather data.**"

**Researchers often start with purposeful or convenience sampling and then expand using network sampling.**

### Sample size in qualitative studies — four factors

| Factor | Rule |
|---|---|
| **Scope of the study** | **Broad scope → MORE participants.** "A qualitative study of the experience of **living with chronic illness in older adulthood** would require a large sample"; the **lived experience of adults over 60 with Parkinson disease** could give credible findings with a smaller sample |
| **Nature of the topic** | **Clear, easily discussed topic → FEWER participants.** "If the topic is **difficult to define and awkward for people to discuss, more participants** are often needed." Sensitive topics named: **medication administration errors, gender identity, loss of a child, history of child sexual abuse** |
| **Quality of the data** | Richer data → fewer participants needed |
| **Design of the study** | — |

The sample is adequate when **saturation is reached**, patterns or themes emerge, and "the researcher has adequate data to answer the research question while appropriately implementing the study design."

## §9 Research settings (restated from Ch 2)

- **Natural (field) setting** — uncontrolled, real-life; researcher does not manipulate it.
- **Partially controlled setting** — manipulated or modified in some way.
- **Highly controlled setting** — artificially constructed solely for research.

## Source conflicts and traps

- **Increasing sample size fixes RANDOM variation but does NOTHING for SYSTEMATIC variation.** This is the chapter's sharpest point and a guaranteed item. A stem proposing a larger sample to fix a biased selection process is the distractor.
- **Random assignment is a DESIGN strategy, not a sampling method.** It "does not alter the risk of biases resulting from convenience sampling." An option claiming random assignment makes a convenience sample representative is wrong — it strengthens **group equivalence**, not representativeness.
- **Systematic sampling is only probability sampling IF the starting point is random.** "If the initial starting point is not random, the sample is a **nonprobability** sample." It also gives a **random but not EQUAL** chance of inclusion — so it is not equivalent to simple random sampling.
- **Target vs. accessible population.** **Target = everyone meeting criteria** (all adults with type 2 diabetes); **accessible = who you can actually reach** (that Dallas hospital). Generalization goes sample → accessible → target. Swapping the two is a classic item.
- **Inclusion vs. exclusion criteria.** Exclusion criteria apply to people who **already meet** the inclusion criteria. An option treating "does not meet inclusion criteria" as an exclusion criterion misreads the definition.
- **Stratified random sampling with EQUAL group sizes overrepresents the smaller strata** and "can create sampling error." Proportional allocation matches the population. An option assuming equal-size strata is always the better choice is wrong.
- **Quota sampling is NONPROBABILITY** despite resembling stratified random sampling. The resemblance is to the *stratification*, not to the randomness — the underlying selection is still convenience.
- **Convenience sampling is not automatically fatal.** The chapter explicitly says serious biases "are not always present" and it is "acceptable when used with reasonable knowledge and care." An option calling any convenience-sampled study invalid overstates the text.
- **Power = 0.8 minimum, which carries a 20% Type II error risk; alpha is usually 0.05.** Confusing power (0.8) with alpha (0.05), or attaching the 20% to Type I error, are the likely swaps.
- **Low power → TYPE II error** (missing a real effect). Same rule as Ch 8's statistical conclusion validity.
- **A nonsignificant finding requires a power analysis before you believe it.** The chapter frames this as an appraisal duty — "Is there really no difference, or was the sample too small?"
- **Effect size and sample size are INVERSELY related** — a large effect needs a smaller sample. And note the definition: the effect size tells you **how big** the difference is; the statistical test only tells you **whether** there is one.
- **Descriptive and correlational studies need LARGER samples than experimental ones** — counterintuitive, and therefore a good item. More control permits a smaller sample.
- **Demographic variables used only to describe the sample do not hurt power** — only variables entered into the analyses to answer questions or test hypotheses drive up the required sample size.
- **Theoretical sampling belongs to grounded theory**; **network/snowball** is for hard-to-reach populations; **purposeful** is the general qualitative default. Matching method to purpose is the most likely qualitative item.
- **In qualitative work, a BROADER scope and a MORE SENSITIVE topic both require MORE participants.** Students often assume qualitative always means "small n" — the size depends on scope, topic, data quality, and design.
- **Cluster sampling exists for two specific reasons** — geographic dispersion cost, or **no obtainable sampling frame.** An option choosing cluster sampling purely to improve representativeness misses its rationale.
- **HIPAA makes complete sampling frames harder to obtain** — a real, citable constraint on probability sampling in nursing research.
