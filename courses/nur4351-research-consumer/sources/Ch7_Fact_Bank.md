## §1 The building blocks of a theory (Fig. 7.1)

**A theory = concepts + statements presenting a view of a phenomenon.**

- **Concepts** name and describe; each **must be defined by the theorist.** "Theories with **clearly identified and defined concepts** provide a **stronger basis for a research framework.**"
- **Statements** describe **how the concepts are connected.**
- **Phenomena** are what the theory is about.

**The chapter's worked example:** Toulouse and Kodadek stated that adherence to medications "improves clinical outcomes and reduces the development of complications, patient suffering, and healthcare costs." → **Concepts:** adherence to medications, clinical outcomes, complications, patient suffering, healthcare costs. **The statement expresses the relationships among them.**

**Philosophies** describe "viewpoints on **what reality is, how knowledge is developed, and which ethical values and principles should guide our practice.**"

**Assumptions** are untested but taken as true. The chapter's example: *"People want to assume control of their own health problems."* — "You may doubt the truth of that assumption... **Nonetheless, theorists begin with some assumptions, explicit or implicit.**"

**The four metaparadigm concepts of nursing: person, environment, health, nursing.**

## §2 The abstraction ladder: construct → concept → variable (Fig. 7.2)

**Most abstract → most concrete:**

| Level | Definition | The chapter's social-support example |
|---|---|---|
| **Construct** | Broadest — may **encompass several concepts** | **Resources** |
| **Concept** | The basic element of a theory | **Social support** (another resource concept: **household income**) |
| **Variable** | **Narrow, measurable**, values can vary | **Emotional support** |
| *(Measurement)* | The **specific method for assigning numerical values** | The **Functional Social Support Questionnaire** three-item subscale, summed to a total score |

**The three FSSQ emotional-support items:** "People care what happens to me," plus items on **feeling loved** and **receiving praise for doing a good job.**

**Conceptual definitions are CONNOTATIVE, not denotative:**
> The chapter's illustration: a **conceptual** definition of *home* "might include **feelings of security, love, and comfort**," whereas the **dictionary (denotative)** definition is "narrower and more specific — a home is a dwelling in which a group of people live, who may or may not be related."

**The chain in quantitative studies:** "each variable is ideally associated with a **concept, conceptual definition, and operational definition.**" The **conceptual definition comes from a theory and provides the basis for the operational definition.** The **operational definition** is how the concept is **manipulated** (intervention/independent variable) or **measured** (dependent/outcome variable).

**"Conceptual definitions may be explicit or implicit. It is important that you identify the researcher's conceptual definitions of study variables when you critically appraise a study."**

## §3 Statements, propositions, and hypotheses — the hierarchy

**"The statements of relationships are what are TESTED through research."** Testing a theory "involves determining the truth of **each statement** in the theory... **Many studies are required to validate all the statements in a theory.**"

**From abstract to testable:**

| Level | Where it lives | The chapter's Table 7.2 example |
|---|---|---|
| **General proposition** | More abstract theories | "**Health-related quality of life is related to depression.**" |
| **Specific proposition** | Less abstract frameworks (**middle range theories**) | "Among persons on **antiretroviral therapy for HIV infection**, domains of health-related quality of life are related to depressive symptoms." |
| **Hypothesis** | Written at the **lowest** level of abstraction, **to be tested in a study** | "Among **African American** persons on antiretroviral therapy for HIV infection, **poor physical functioning and increased role limitations** are related to **increased frequency and severity** of depressive symptoms." |

**"Specific propositions in less abstract frameworks (middle range theories) may lead to hypotheses. Hypotheses are developed based on propositions from a grand or middle range theory."**

## §4 Levels of theoretical thinking

| Level | Scope | Notes |
|---|---|---|
| **Grand nursing theories** | **Most abstract**; encompass "nursing actions and patient responses in **multiple settings**" | Early scholars called these **conceptual models or conceptual frameworks** |
| **Middle range (substantive) theories** | **Less abstract, narrower** | "**More closely linked to clinical practice and research**," so nurses and researchers "find them to be helpful." **More commonly applied as frameworks in nursing studies** |
| **Practice (situation-specific) theories** | **Most specific** — a type of middle range theory | "Designed to propose specific approaches to **particular nursing practice situations**" |

**The four grand nursing theories (Table 7.3):**

| Theory | Author(s) | Core idea |
|---|---|---|
| **Adaptation Model** | **Roy** and Andrews (2008) | In response to **focal, contextual, and residual stimuli**, people adapt using processes and systems, some **automatic** and some **learned**. Goal: **return to homeostasis and promote growth** |
| **Self-Care Deficit Theory of Nursing** | **Orem** (2001; Orem & Taylor, 2011) | Ability to self-care is affected by **developmental stage, presence of disease, and available resources**, and may result in a **self-care deficit.** Nursing provides care **in proportion to the person's self-care capacity** |
| **Science of Unitary Human Beings** | **Rogers** (1970) | Persons and environment are **energy fields interacting as open systems**; the fields **produce patterns** usable for identification |
| **Theory of Goal Attainment** | **King** (1992) | Within systems, persons are **goal-oriented**; nurse and patient set **mutually agreed-upon goals**; through interaction the nurse **educates, supports, and guides** |

**Building knowledge around a grand theory requires "an organized program of research and a group of scholars"** — the chapter's examples: the **Roy Adaptation Association** (analyzes, critiques, and synthesizes all English-language RAM studies; RAM has been used for **over 25 years**), the **Society of Rogerian Scholars** (journal: *Visions*), and the **International Orem Society** (journal: *Self-Care, Dependent-Care, & Nursing*).

**The nine middle range theories with ongoing use (Table 7.4):** **Caring** (Swanson), **Comfort** (Kolcaba), **Inner strength** (Roux et al.), **Nursing intellectual capital** (Covell), **Self-transcendence** (Reed), **Transitions** (Meleis), **Uncertainty in illness** (Mishel), **Unpleasant symptoms** (Lenz et al.), **Women's anger** (Thomas).

**Where middle range theories come from — three origins:** they "may **emerge from a grounded theory study**, be **deduced from a grand nursing theory**, or may be **created through a synthesis of the literature** on a particular topic."

**Why substantive theories are used as frameworks:** they "have **clearly identified concepts, definitions of concepts, and relational statements.**"

## §5 Research frameworks — explicit vs. implicit

> **The rule your appraisal assignment turns on:** "**EVERY quantitative study has an implicit or explicit framework.** This is true whether the study has a physiological, psychological, social, or cultural focus. **A clearly expressed framework is one indication of a well-developed quantitative study.**"

So the appraisal question is never *"is there a framework?"* — there always is one. It is **"is it explicit or implicit, and how well developed?"**

**Implicit frameworks:** the ideas "remain **nebulous and are vaguely expressed**." The researcher believes the variables are related "but this notion is expressed **only in concrete terms**" with "**little attempt to explain WHY** the variables are thought to be related." Sometimes basic ideas appear in the **introduction or literature review** as linkages found in previous studies, "**but then the researcher stops, without fully developing the ideas as a framework.**"

**What to do about it as an appraiser:** "**In most cases, a careful reader can EXTRACT an implicit framework from the text.** When researchers do not clearly describe the framework, **you may want to draw a model** based on the information provided. Having a model helps you **visualize the framework and how the variables are linked.**"

**The cost of leaving it implicit:** implicit frameworks "**provide limited guidance** for the development and conduct of a study and **limit the contribution of the study findings to nursing knowledge.**"

**Models and maps:** a model "includes **all the major concepts**." **"Each linkage shown by an arrow is a graphic illustration of a relational statement (proposition) of the theory."**

**How theory guides different designs (Lor et al., 2017):**
- **Descriptive studies** — use "concepts from the theory to **inform data collection**"
- **Experimental studies** — "concepts from the theory **guide overall design**"

**Where frameworks come from — four sources:**
1. **Grand nursing theories**
2. **Middle range theories** from nursing **and other professions**
3. **Syntheses of concepts and relationships from more than one theory**
4. **Syntheses of research findings**

Sources 3 and 4 are **tentative theories** — "usually developed for a particular study."

**Physiological study frameworks** come from **physiology, genetics, pathophysiology, and physics** — this is **scientific theory**, which "has **extensive research evidence** to support its claims," with concepts "clearly linked to study variables" and "**valid and reliable methods** for measuring each concept."

## Source conflicts and traps

- **Every quantitative study HAS a framework** — explicit or implicit. An option stating that a study "has no framework" is wrong by the chapter's own rule; the correct appraisal is that the framework is **implicit** and therefore **limits the study's contribution.**
- **Construct → concept → variable, most to least abstract.** A **construct is broader than a concept**, and a **variable is narrower**. Reversing construct and concept is the standard swap. *Resources* (construct) contains *social support* (concept), measured as *emotional support* (variable).
- **Conceptual definitions are CONNOTATIVE and come from theory; dictionary definitions are DENOTATIVE.** A conceptual definition is **more comprehensive** than a dictionary one, not more precise.
- **Conceptual definition → operational definition is a one-way dependency.** The conceptual definition **comes from the theory and provides the basis for** the operational one. (Consistent with Ch 2 and Ch 5.)
- **Grand → middle range → practice, in decreasing abstraction.** **Middle range theories are the ones most commonly used as research frameworks**, because they have clear concepts, definitions, and relational statements — *not* grand theories, despite grand theories being better known.
- **General proposition → specific proposition → hypothesis.** Hypotheses sit at the **lowest** level of abstraction and are what actually get tested. An option placing the hypothesis above the proposition inverts the hierarchy.
- **A single study does not validate a theory.** "Many studies are required to validate all the statements in a theory." An option concluding a theory is proven by one supporting study is wrong.
- **Assumptions are NOT tested** — they are "taken for granted or considered true, even though they have not been scientifically tested." An option describing assumptions as findings or as hypotheses is wrong.
- **Match the grand theorists to their core concept:** **Roy = adaptation** (focal/contextual/residual stimuli); **Orem = self-care deficit**; **Rogers = energy fields/unitary human beings**; **King = mutually set goals.** This is the most likely straight-recall item in the chapter.
- **Middle range theories have three possible origins** — grounded theory, deduction from a grand theory, or literature synthesis. An option requiring that they always derive from grand theory is too narrow.
- **Practice theories = situation-specific theories**, and they are a **subtype of middle range**, not a fourth independent level.
- **In a model, each arrow IS a proposition.** A stem asking what an arrow represents wants **relational statement/proposition**, not "correlation" or "causation."
- **Metaparadigm = person, environment, health, nursing.** Four, and only these four.
