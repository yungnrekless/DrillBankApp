## Key terms

| Term | Definition as given |
|---|---|
| **therapeutic research** | "Provides patients with an opportunity to **receive an experimental treatment that might have beneficial results.**" |
| **nontherapeutic research** | "Conducted to **generate knowledge for science**; the results **may help future patients but are not expected to benefit those acting as research participants.**" |
| **respect for persons** | "People should be treated as **autonomous agents**, with the right to choose whether or not to participate in research and **to withdraw** from a study." |
| **beneficence** | "Encourages the researcher to **do good and 'above all, do no harm.'**" |
| **justice** | "Human subjects have a **right to fair treatment**, which includes **access to the potential benefits** of a study and **not overexposure to its risks.**" |
| **autonomy** | "Having the **freedom to conduct their lives as they choose, without external controls.**" |
| **privacy** | "The freedom people have to determine the **time, extent, and general circumstances** under which their private information will be shared with or withheld from others." |
| **invasion of privacy** | "Occurs when private information is shared **without a person's knowledge or against his or her will.**" |
| **identifiable private information (IPI)** | "Private information for which the **identity of the subject is or may be readily ascertained** by the investigator." |
| **broad consent** | Consent "through which a potential subject gives researchers permission to **store, maintain, and use IPI for other studies.**" |
| **anonymity** | "**Complete anonymity** exists when the researcher **cannot link a subject's identity with her or his individual responses.**" |
| **confidentiality** | "The researcher's **safe management of information or data** shared by a subject to ensure that the data are kept private from others." |
| **breach of confidentiality** | Occurs "when a researcher, by accident or direct action, **allows an unauthorized person to gain access to the raw data.**" |
| **informed consent** | "**Voluntary agreement of a competent subject** to participate in a study **after comprehending knowledge to assess the benefits and risks.**" |
| **consent form** | "A **written document** that includes the elements of informed consent required by the Common Rule." |
| **voluntary agreement** | "The prospective subject has decided to take part in a study **of his or her own volition, without coercion or any undue influence.**" |

## §1 The four historical studies — know what each violated

| Study | Years | What happened | Rights violated |
|---|---|---|---|
| **Nazi medical experiments** | **1933–1945** | Prisoners of war and Jews in concentration camps exposed to **high altitudes, freezing temperatures, poisons, infections, untested drugs, and surgery without anesthesia** | **Unjust selection of subjects, involuntary participation, permanent damage including death.** Also "**poorly conceived and conducted**" — generated "little, if any, useful scientific knowledge" |
| **Tuskegee Syphilis Study** | **1932–1972 (40 years)** | US Public Health Service study of the **natural history of syphilis in black men** in Tuskegee, Alabama. **Treatment withheld even after penicillin was known effective in the 1940s**; "deliberate steps were taken to deprive them of treatment" | Many **not informed** of purpose/procedures; **some unaware they were subjects** |
| **Willowbrook Study** | **mid-1950s–early 1970s** | Dr. Saul Krugman **deliberately infected children with hepatitis** at an institution for the mentally retarded (Staten Island). The institution **closed to new inmates for overcrowding but the research ward kept admitting** — "parents could only admit their children if they were willing to give permission for their child to be in the study" | **Coerced consent**; use of a **vulnerable population** |
| **Jewish Chronic Disease Hospital** | **1960s** | **Live cancer cells injected into 22 patients**; patients not told they were in a study or what they received | **Self-determination and protection from harm.** Also **never reviewed** by the research committee, and the **patients' own physicians were unaware** |

**Timeline details worth knowing about Tuskegee:** by **1936** evidence showed the syphilis group had more complications; **ten years later** the death rate was **twice** the control group's. Reports were **published every 4–6 years** and **in 1969 the CDC reviewed the study and decided it should continue.** It stopped only after a **1972 account in the Washington Star** sparked public outrage.

> **The chapter's causal chain:** the **Tuskegee** exposure produced the public outcry that led to the commission that wrote the **Belmont Report.**

## §2 The standards that resulted

| Standard | Year | Origin / focus |
|---|---|---|
| **Nuremberg Code** | **1949** | Released after the **Nuremberg Tribunals** of German war crimes |
| **Declaration of Helsinki** | **1964** (most recent revision by the **WMA**, 2013) | Built on the Nuremberg Code; **major focus was clarifying therapeutic vs. nontherapeutic research** |
| **Belmont Report** | **1979** | **The first major US ethical guide**, by the National Commission for the Protection of Human Subjects; formed **in response to the Tuskegee outcry** |
| **Common Rule (Code of Federal Regulations)** | most recently revised **2017**, effective **January 2018** | Called the "Common Rule" because of **similarities among departments' CFR chapters** |

**Box 4.1 — the Nuremberg Code's four categories:**
- **Rationale for study:** conducted only when the knowledge **cannot be gained any other way**; based on **previous studies including animal experimentation** and natural history of disease; designed to yield **results needed by society**; **anticipated results justify** conducting it
- **Rights of human subjects:** **voluntary consent**; **may withdraw at any time with no penalty**
- **Risk avoidance:** **no unnecessary physical or mental suffering and injury**; **study terminated if the risk of harm increases**
- **Resources:** adequate resources to protect subjects

**Therapeutic vs. nontherapeutic** (the Helsinki distinction): **therapeutic** offers the patient a chance at a **beneficial** experimental treatment; **nontherapeutic** generates knowledge for science and is **not expected to benefit the participants themselves.**

**The 2017 Common Rule revision had two goals:** **enhance protection of human subjects** *and* **reduce the administrative burden** of regulating research.

## §3 The three ethical principles → five human rights

| Principle | Leads to | Applied as |
|---|---|---|
| **Respect for persons** | **Autonomy** | Right to choose whether to participate and to withdraw; **extra protection for those with diminished autonomy** |
| **Beneficence** | **Promote good** | "Do good and above all, do no harm"; **the foundation for analyzing benefits and risks** |
| **Justice** | **Fairness** | Fair treatment: **access to benefits, not overexposure to risks** |

**Beneficence in practice** — the chapter's concrete example: in a study where enhanced diabetic education improved glucose control in the treatment group, "a researcher guided by beneficence would **provide the intervention for the subjects in the control group**" at the end of the study. Beneficence **also** involves "**maintaining the integrity of the study and disseminating the findings**" — because "findings of **poorly designed and implemented studies have no benefit.**"

**Justice in practice:** "inclusion and exclusion criteria for subjects in studies must have **scientific and/or logical explanations.**"

**Those with diminished autonomy** entitled to additional protection: **children, people who are terminally or mentally ill, and prisoners.**

**The five human rights requiring protection:**
1. **Self-determination**
2. **Privacy**
3. **Anonymity and confidentiality**
4. **Protection from discomfort and harm**
5. **Fair selection and treatment**

**The ANA Code of Ethics for Nurses (2015)** "reiterates these rights and provides nurses with guidelines for ethical conduct in nursing practice and research."

> **The nurse's obligation:** nurses "who critically appraise published studies, review research for conduct in their agencies, or **assist with data collection** for a study, have an **obligation to determine whether the rights of the research participants have been protected.**"

**Right to self-determination** is based on **respect for persons.** A subject "who consents and begins a study is allowed to **withdraw from the study at any time, without penalty.**" **Culture must be considered** — "autonomy has different interpretations and implications in varying cultures."

## §4 Privacy, anonymity, and confidentiality

**Private information includes** "a person's **attitudes, beliefs, behaviors, opinions, and records.**"

**Privacy is protected if** the subject **is informed, consents, and voluntarily shares** the information.

**HIPAA Privacy Rule** protects identifiable information related to "a person's **past or current health condition**, the **care received**, and **payment for health care** received."

**Anonymity vs. confidentiality — the distinction:**
- **Complete anonymity** = the researcher **cannot link identity to responses.** In most studies **researchers DO know who their subjects are**, so complete anonymity is uncommon.
- **Confidentiality** = safe management of the data so it stays private from others.

**Confidentiality rests on two premises (ANA, 2015):**
1. "Because individuals **own their personal information**, they may share it to the extent they wish and with whom they wish."
2. "**Accepting personal information comes with an obligation to maintain confidentiality**, an obligation that is **even greater for researchers and nurses.**"

> **Where breaches are most likely — a genuinely testable contrast:** "In **quantitative** studies, results are presented for **subgroups or groups**, and a breach of confidentiality is **less likely.** In **qualitative** studies, however, a breach is **MORE likely** because the researcher gathers data from **fewer participants** and reports **long quotes**." Qualitative researchers also form close relationships in which "detailed stories of the participants' lives are shared."

**Breaches are especially harmful** when the information involves **religious preferences, sexual practices, income, racial prejudices, drug use, or child abuse.** Confidentiality must be maintained **during data collection, analysis, AND reporting.**

**Broad consent (new in the revised Common Rule)** — for storing and reusing IPI and identifiable biospecimens. Beyond standard consent components it must include: **use of biospecimens for commercial profit** and that **subjects will receive a share** of any profits; whether analysis will include **whole genome sequencing**; **types of research** the data may be used for; **length of time data will be kept (which may be indefinite)**; a statement that **subjects will not be informed about specific studies** conducted; that **results will not be shared unless clinically relevant**; and **contact information.**

## §5 The four elements of informed consent

**"Informed consent is incomplete or unethical unless four elements are incorporated":**

| Element | Requirement |
|---|---|
| **1. Disclosure** | The researcher **discloses essential information in a way that can be understood** |
| **2. Comprehension** | The extent to which the potential subject **actually understands** it |
| **3. Competence** | The subject is **capable of understanding the benefits and risks** |
| **4. Voluntary agreement** | Participation **of one's own volition, without coercion or undue influence** |

**Disclosure — what changed in 2017:** the revision "**changed the focus of the consent process from sharing every possible detail** of a study to providing **the information that a reasonable person would need** to make a decision."

**Box 4.4 — components of informed consent.** It must be:
- **Comprehendible** — opportunity to discuss and decide **without pressure**, understandable language, presented meaningfully rather than as unrelated facts
- **Cautious** — "the subject **does not waive any legal rights** or release researcher or funder from negligence"
- **Complete but concise** — enough for a reasonable person to decide
- **Comprehensive** — **purpose and procedures; length of involvement; benefits and risks/discomforts; compensation for research-related injury; alternative treatments; confidentiality of records; contact persons; right to refuse or withdraw without loss of benefits**

**Comprehension:** "the researcher must **take the time to TEACH the subjects** about the study." How much depends on the subject's knowledge of research and of the topic. **Nurses often serve as patient advocates** and "need to **assess whether patients involved in research understand** the purpose and potential risks and benefits."

**Competence:** "Persons with **diminished autonomy because of legal or mental incompetence or confinement to an institution are not legally competent** to consent." Investigators must **report the competence of subjects and the consent process used.**

**For federally funded clinical trials, a copy of the IRB-approved consent form must be posted on a publicly available website.**

## §6 Institutional Review Boards

**Composition — "at least FIVE members":**
- Varying backgrounds — **cultural, economic, educational, gender, racial**
- **At least one member with scientific expertise**
- **At least one community member without scientific training**
- **One member not affiliated with the institution in any way** (may be the same as the nonscientist)
- If the institution regularly reviews studies with **vulnerable** subjects, **one or more members should have experience working with such persons**

**Conflict of interest:** an IRB member who proposes a study "**could not be involved in the deliberations and decision** about the study other than to provide information."

**Federal recognition:** the IRB must **register with the Office of Human Research Protection (OHRP)** and obtain a **federal-wide assurance (FWA)** — documentation of adopting an ethical standard (e.g. the Belmont Report) and agreeing to comply with the Common Rule. It must have **written policies** and **maintain records of all studies reviewed and decisions.**

> **The rule students most often miss:** "**The IRB chairperson and/or committee, NOT the researcher, decide the level of the review** required for each study."

**Levels of review:** **exempt**, **expedited**, and full.
- **Exempt studies "pose no apparent risks."** Once exempt, **no continuing review is required** (unlike other levels). **"Studies conducted by nurses frequently are evaluated as being exempt from review."**
- The revised Common Rule **broadened** the types of study that are exempt.

**Box 4.5 — categories of exempt studies:** conducted in **established education settings**; using **educational tests, surveys, interviews, or observation of public behavior** with subjects unidentified and data not linked to them; using a **benign behavioral intervention** with unlinked data; or using data that were **publicly available, de-identified, or collected to evaluate government projects/public services.**

**"Benign behavioral interventions"** = "**short noninvasive actions** that are **not expected to have a long-term adverse impact or be embarrassing or offensive.**"

## §7 Research misconduct

**The Office of Research Integrity (ORI)**, part of DHHS, defines and manages research misconduct.

**The three forms:** **fabrication** (making up results), **falsification** (manipulating materials/processes or changing/omitting data), and **plagiarism** (appropriating another's ideas, processes, results, or words without credit — **including material obtained through confidential peer review** of others' proposals and manuscripts).

**Key qualifiers:**
- **"It does NOT include honest error or differences in opinion."**
- It is "an **intentional act** that involves a **significant departure** from accepted practice."
- **"Before action is taken, an allegation must be confirmed by a PREPONDERANCE OF EVIDENCE."**

**Nursing relevance:** the **seven ORI cases in 2016 were all laboratory cellular studies** that "rarely include nurse researchers" — **however, "research misconduct DOES occur in the nursing profession."** A study of research coordinators found they "**often learned of the misconduct firsthand**," and "**the principal investigator was usually identified as the responsible party.**" Five categories were identified, beginning with **protocol violations** and **consent violations.**

## Source conflicts and traps

- **Match each historical study to its distinctive violation.** **Tuskegee = withholding available treatment** (penicillin) and deception; **Willowbrook = deliberately infecting children + coerced parental consent** via admission; **Jewish Chronic Disease = injecting live cancer cells without consent AND bypassing institutional review**; **Nazi = involuntary participation, unjust selection, death.** These are routinely swapped.
- **Tuskegee ran 40 years and the CDC voted in 1969 to continue it.** It ended only after **press exposure in 1972**, not internal review. An option crediting scientific oversight with stopping it is wrong.
- **The Belmont Report is the US standard and came AFTER Tuskegee (1979); the Nuremberg Code (1949) is international and came after the Nazi trials.** Sequence and jurisdiction are both testable.
- **Respect for persons → autonomy; beneficence → do good/no harm; justice → fairness in selection and access.** A stem about **unfair inclusion/exclusion criteria** is testing **justice**, not beneficence.
- **Beneficence includes offering the intervention to the control group afterward** and **disseminating findings** — not just avoiding harm. A poorly designed study is itself an ethical failure under beneficence.
- **Anonymity ≠ confidentiality.** Complete anonymity means **even the researcher cannot link identity to data** — uncommon, since "in most studies, researchers know the identity of their subjects." Confidentiality is about **managing** known identities. Calling a study with signed consent forms "anonymous" is the error.
- **Breach of confidentiality is MORE likely in qualitative studies** — small samples plus long verbatim quotes. Students often assume the reverse.
- **The four consent elements are disclosure, comprehension, competence, voluntary agreement.** Note that **comprehension is separate from disclosure** — telling is not the same as understanding, and the researcher must **teach.**
- **Consent must not waive any legal rights** or release the researcher from negligence. A consent form containing such a waiver is invalid.
- **The IRB — not the researcher — determines the level of review.** A researcher declaring their own study exempt is the distractor.
- **Exempt studies require no continuing review**; other levels do. And **nurse-conducted studies are frequently exempt** — an option treating exempt status as unusual or improper misreads the chapter.
- **IRB minimum is five members, including at least one scientist, one nonscientist, and one unaffiliated person.** A stem describing an all-clinician IRB is noncompliant.
- **Research misconduct requires INTENT** — "honest error or differences in opinion" are explicitly excluded — and requires **a preponderance of evidence** before action.
- **Plagiarism includes material seen during confidential peer review.** This is the least intuitive part of the ORI definition.
- **Therapeutic vs. nontherapeutic research** turns on whether **the participants themselves** might benefit, not on whether the study is useful.
