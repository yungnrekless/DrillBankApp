## Key terms

| Term | Definition as given |
|---|---|
| **qualitative research** | "A systematic approach used to describe experiences and situations **from the perspective of persons in the situation.**" Also: "a systematic approach used to collect **textual** data related to the phenomenon the study addresses." |
| **phenomena** | "The conscious awareness of experiences that comprise the lives of humans" (van Manen, 2017). |
| **phenomenology** | "Both a **philosophy** and group of research methods congruent with the philosophy that guides the study of experiences or phenomena." |
| **descriptive phenomenological research** | Supported by **Husserl**; purpose is "to describe experiences as they are lived" — to capture the **"lived experience."** |
| **interpretative phenomenological research** | Consistent with **Heidegger**; "involves analyzing the data and presenting a **rich word picture** of the phenomenon, as interpreted by the researcher." |
| **bracketing** | Setting aside one's **own biases and preconceptions** to describe the phenomenon "in a naïve way" (a **Husserl** requirement). |
| **hermeneutics** | A type of **interpretative** phenomenological method congruent with **Heidegger**; "involves textual analysis that begins with a naïve reading of the texts." |
| **grounded theory research** | "An **inductive** technique that emerged from the discipline of **sociology**." **"Grounded" means the theory developed FROM the research** — grounded in the real world, based on data provided by participants. |
| **symbolic interaction theory** | The theory most grounded-theory methodology is based on; developed by **George Herbert Mead** (1934), a social psychologist. "Explores how people define reality and how their beliefs are related to their actions." |
| **ethnographic research** | Developed by **anthropologists** "as a method to study how cultures develop and are maintained over time." Also names **the product** — the written description of the culture. |
| **emic approach** | "Studying behaviors **from within the culture**, that recognizes the uniqueness of the individual." **The typical goal of ethnography.** |
| **etic approach** | "To view the culture as a **naïve outsider** and analyze its elements as a researcher." |
| **material culture** | "All created or constructed aspects of culture" — buildings for cultural events, symbols, family traditions, networks of social relations, beliefs reflected in social/political institutions. |
| **nonmaterial culture** | **Symbolic meaning, social customs, and beliefs** — may become apparent only over time. |
| **exploratory-descriptive qualitative research** | Conducted "to provide information that will promote understanding of an experience from the perspective of the persons living the experience and **possibly solve a problem.**" |
| **participants** | The term for individuals in qualitative studies, "because the researcher and participants carry out the study **cooperatively.**" |
| **purposeful sampling** | The researcher "recruits participants **because of their particular knowledge, experience, or views** related to the study." |
| **heterogeneous sample** | Participants have **different** characteristics; used frequently for **grounded theory** to support theory development. |
| **homogeneous sample** | Participants have **similar** characteristics; used when the central focus is **the phenomenon.** |
| **saturation** | "When **additional participants or data sources do not provide new information.**" |
| **triangulation** | "The researchers use **at least two different perspectives** to converge into a unique perspective on the topic." |
| **observation** | "A fundamental method of gathering data for qualitative studies, **especially ethnography** studies." |
| **code** | "A symbol or abbreviation used to **classify words or phrases** in the data." |
| **methodological congruence** | When the "purposes, questions, and methods of research are interconnected and interrelated" and the study is described as a **"cohesive whole."** |
| **audit trail** | "One way that a researcher can assure a reader that the methods of the study were implemented appropriately." |
| **generalizability** | "A desired outcome for **quantitative** studies when the sample is representative of the target populations." |
| **transferability** | "Applicability of the findings to similar individuals" — **the desired outcome for QUALITATIVE studies.** |

## §1 The four qualitative approaches — know the origin of each

| Approach | Comes from | Broad question / aim | Outcome |
|---|---|---|---|
| **Phenomenological** | **Philosophy** (Husserl, Heidegger) | **"What is this lived experience like?"** | A **composite of the essence** of the experience |
| **Grounded theory** | **Sociology** (via symbolic interaction, Mead) | Understand the **interaction between self and group** / underlying **social processes** | **A theory** — "theoretical frameworks with relational statements between concepts" |
| **Ethnographic** | **Anthropology** | Understand a **culture** — environment, people, power relations, communication patterns | A picture of the culture as a whole |
| **Exploratory-descriptive** | — | Address "an issue or problem in need of a solution and/or understanding" | Understanding; possibly a solution |

**Each approach is based on a philosophical orientation that influences the interpretation of the data.**

## §2 Phenomenology — the Husserl/Heidegger split (highest-yield)

Phenomenologists "view the person as **integrated with the environment.** The world shapes the person, and the person shapes the world."

| | **Husserl** | **Heidegger** |
|---|---|---|
| **Focus** | **The phenomenon itself** | How participants have **interpreted or given meaning** to their experiences |
| **Aim** | "Capture experience in its...essence, **without interpreting, explaining, or theorizing**" | Analyze participants' interpretations, **looking for the hidden meaning** |
| **Supports** | **Descriptive** phenomenological research | **Interpretative** phenomenological research |
| **Bracketing?** | **YES** — researchers must **bracket/set aside their own biases** and describe the phenomenon naïvely | **NO** — argued it is **impossible** to set aside preconceptions and understand the world naïvely |
| **Associated method** | — | **Hermeneutics** (textual analysis beginning with a naïve reading) |

> **The trap:** bracketing belongs to **Husserl/descriptive** phenomenology. Heidegger explicitly **rejected** the possibility of bracketing. An item pairing "bracketing" with interpretative/hermeneutic phenomenology is the distractor.

## §3 Grounded theory

- **"Grounded" = the theory developed FROM the research** — grounded in the real world, from participants' data. It is an **inductive** technique.
- From **sociology**; built on **symbolic interaction theory** (**Mead**, 1934).
- **Symbolic interaction premises:** reality is created by **attaching meanings to situations**; meaning is expressed in **symbols** (words, religious objects, patterns of behavior, clothing); symbolic meanings **differ for each individual** and we cannot completely know another's; in social life meanings are **shared by groups** and communicated to new members through **socialization**; group life is based on **consensus and shared meanings**; interaction may **redefine** meaning or construct new meanings.
- **Used most frequently** to study areas "in which **little previous research** has been conducted" and to gain a new viewpoint in familiar areas.
- **Developed by Glaser and Strauss (1967)** through interviews to understand the perspectives of **persons who were dying.**
- **Intended outcome:** "fully developed grounded theory studies result in **theoretical frameworks with relational statements between concepts**"; some provide a **diagram.**

## §4 Ethnography

- Developed by **anthropologists**; studies a group who **share a culture** — their origins, past ways of living, and ways of surviving through time. Requires **immersion** (early researchers spent **a year or longer** in another culture).
- **"Ethnography" also names the product** — the written description of the culture.
- **Emic = from WITHIN the culture** (the insider view; the **typical goal**). **Etic = as a naïve OUTSIDER** analyzing its elements. Morgan-Trimmer and Wood (2016) argue for **using both.**
- **Material culture** = constructed/created things (buildings, symbols, family traditions, social networks, institutional beliefs). **Nonmaterial culture** = symbolic meaning, social customs, beliefs — often apparent only over time.
- Cultures also have **ideals** people hold as desirable "even though they do not always live up to these standards."

## §5 Sampling, sample size, and the researcher–participant relationship

**Sampling in qualitative research is PURPOSEFUL** — participants are recruited for their **particular knowledge, experience, or views.** (Network and theoretical sampling also appear; see Ch 9.)

**Sample size varies by method:**

| Method | Typical size given |
|---|---|
| **Phenomenological** | **10 or fewer** participants, if the topic is narrow and participants provide adequate data |
| **Grounded theory** | May require **more than 30** participants, to develop a robust theory |

- **Sampling continues until SATURATION** — when additional participants or data sources **do not provide new information.**
- Qualitative researchers "deliberately recruit **fewer** participants but ensure that each one has experience with the research topic."
- **Contrast with quantitative:** a **power analysis** determines the minimum sample size for a quantitative study (see Ch 9).

**Researcher–participant relationship — a defining contrast:**

> "One of the important differences between quantitative and qualitative research lies in the **degree of involvement of the researcher** with the participants. This involvement, **considered to be a source of BIAS in quantitative research**, is thought by qualitative researchers to be a **CRITICAL ELEMENT** of the research process."

- The researcher creates a **respectful relationship**, honest and open about purpose and methods; aims are **negotiated** with participants, honoring their perspectives and values.
- "The researcher influences the people being studied and, in turn, **is influenced by them.**"
- **The researcher's personality is a key factor**; skills in **empathy and intuition** are cultivated.
- Researchers must "be **open to the perceptions of the participants**, rather than to attach their own meaning to the experience."

## §6 Data collection, analysis, and rigor

**Data collection methods:** **interviews, focus groups, observation, and examination of documents and media materials.** Data are **words and images, instead of numbers.**

**Analysis:** codes → themes → interpretation. A **code** classifies words or phrases; **interpretation** asks "what do the findings mean?" and "may focus on the usefulness of the findings for clinical practice or may move toward theorizing."

**Rigor in qualitative research is assessed differently** because the desired outcome is different. It is assessed in relation to:
- the **detail built into the design**,
- **carefulness of data collection**, and
- **thoroughness of analysis.**

**The four trustworthiness criteria** — "the extent to which the findings of a qualitative study are **dependable, confirmable, credible, and transferable** is the degree of rigor of the study":

| Criterion | Meaning in the chapter |
|---|---|
| **Dependability** | Methods implemented appropriately; supported by an **audit trail** |
| **Confirmability** | Same — the reader can appraise it from sufficient reported detail |
| **Credibility** | "Studies that are dependable and confirmable can be said to have **truth, value, or credibility**" |
| **Transferability** | Findings **cannot be generalized** but may be applied "in other contexts or with other participants" |

**Rigor also requires** (Key Points): congruence with the **philosophical perspective**; appropriateness of collection, analysis, and interpretation; maintenance of an **audit trail**; and **logic of the findings** evident in the report.

**Methodological congruence** — the fit among purpose, questions, and methods — "has **special significance in qualitative research** because of the **evolving nature** of studies."

**Credibility strategies named:** **triangulation** (at least two perspectives converging), engaging family members, use of multiple sources, and **linking codes and themes with exemplar quotations.**

## Source conflicts and traps

- **Generalizability is for QUANTITATIVE; transferability is for QUALITATIVE.** The chapter states this explicitly. An option praising a qualitative study for being "generalizable to the population" contradicts the text — qualitative findings "cannot be generalized but may be applied in other contexts."
- **Bracketing = Husserl = descriptive phenomenology.** Heidegger **denied** bracketing was possible. Attaching bracketing to interpretative/hermeneutic work is the classic swap.
- **Researcher involvement is BIAS in quantitative research but a CRITICAL ELEMENT in qualitative research.** An item calling researcher closeness a threat to a phenomenological study's rigor misreads the chapter.
- **Saturation is defined by NO NEW INFORMATION, not by a number.** An option defining saturation as "reaching the planned sample size" or a fixed count is wrong. Note the sizes given are *typical*, not rules: phenomenology ≤10, grounded theory >30.
- **Grounded theory = sociology; ethnography = anthropology; phenomenology = philosophy.** Mixing the parent disciplines is a likely item. Symbolic interaction (**Mead**) belongs to **grounded theory.**
- **"Grounded" means grounded in DATA, not grounded in existing theory.** A grounded theory study **produces** a theory; it does not test one. It is **inductive.**
- **Emic = insider, etic = outsider.** Emic is the *typical goal* of ethnography. Reversing them is the trap.
- **Heterogeneous samples support grounded theory** (range of experiences to build a theory); **homogeneous** when the focus is the phenomenon itself. An option requiring homogeneity for all qualitative work is wrong.
- **Qualitative studies use "participants," not "subjects"** — because researcher and participants "carry out the study cooperatively."
- **Power analysis is a QUANTITATIVE tool.** It determines minimum sample size for quantitative studies; qualitative sample size is governed by **saturation**, not power.
- **Ethnography's "product" is also called an ethnography** — the term names both the method and the written description. A stem using it in the second sense is not an error.
