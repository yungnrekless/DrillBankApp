## §1 The two basic sciences (p. 139–140)

- **Toxicology** = studies the poisonous effects of chemicals (the health effects of chemical exposure).
- **Epidemiology** = the science of the strength of association between exposures and health effects in populations. **Environmental epidemiology** is that applied to environmental exposures.
- **Epidemiological triangle** (agent, host, environment) from Ch. 10 applies directly: the agent is the chemical/physical hazard, the host is the exposed person/population, the environment is the medium.
- Global framing: WHO reports **23% of all deaths could be prevented through healthier environments**, and an estimated **24% of the global burden of disease** is attributable to environmental factors.

**Chemical agent categories (multidisciplinary approaches):** metals and metallic compounds (arsenic, cadmium); hydrocarbons (benzene, toluene, ketones, formaldehyde); irritant gases (ammonia, hydrochloric acid, sulfur compounds); pesticides (organophosphates, carbamates, chlorinated compounds).

## §2 The exposure pathway (p. 149) — the five-element chain (high-yield)

For an exposure to cause harm, **all five factors must be in place and connected:**
1. A **source of harm** with chemical and/or physical properties.
2. An **environmental medium** for transport: air, water (surface water or groundwater), or soil.
3. A **receptor population** within the exposure pathway.
4. A **route of exposure**: for humans, **inhalation, ingestion, or skin (dermal) absorption.**
5. An **adequate amount (dose)** of the chemical to result in harm.

- **Break any link and the harm is interrupted** — this is the conceptual basis for nursing intervention (remove the source, block the medium, protect the receptor, cut off the route, or reduce the dose).
- The **three routes of exposure** (inhalation, ingestion, dermal absorption) are the most likely single fact to be tested from this list.

## §3 Children's heightened vulnerability (p. 149) — a recurring test theme

Children are not small adults. The chapter gives specific mechanisms:
- **Breathe more rapidly than adults** → proportionately greater exposure to air pollutants. Lungs reach full function around **age 6** and keep changing through adolescence.
- **Shorter stature → lower breathing zones**, so closer contact with chemical and biological agents on floors, carpeting, and the ground.
- **Mouthing behavior** (putting things in their mouths) increases ingestion, notably of lead dust.
- **Immature protective mechanisms:** the blood-brain barrier is immature; kidneys filter less effectively, increasing the dose retained.
- **Fetal windows of heightened sensitivity:** during fetal development there are periods when even extraordinarily small exposures can permanently alter normal development.

**Lead as the worked example:** most likely in homes built **before 1978** (when lead was banned in household paint). Intact lead poses no risk; the risk comes when it **flakes or turns to dust** on surfaces or soil. Fetal effects: premature birth, low birth weight, brain and nerve damage. Child effects: brain, nervous system, and kidney damage; behavior, hearing, learning, and bone marrow problems. **EPA: lead poisoning is 100% preventable.**

### Lead screening and the nurse's role (p. 140)

Nurses are expected to **know the public health laws, particularly as they pertain to lead-based paint poisoning.** The three nursing actions the chapter names:

- **Set up a blood-lead screening program** through the **local health department.**
- **Educate local health providers to encourage them to systematically test children for lead poisoning.**
- **Work with local landlords** to improve the condition of their housing stock.

**The multidisciplinary lead team** may include a **housing inspector** with lead-based paint expertise or a **sanitarian** to assess lead-associated risks in the home; **clinical specialists** to manage the patient's health needs; **laboratory workers** to assess lead levels **in blood as well as in the paint, house dust, and drinking water**; and **lead-based paint remediation specialists.** It can involve the local health department, the state department of environmental protection, the housing department, and a tertiary care center.

**Factors that contributed to the reduction of lead levels in the United States** (all five named):
1. Elimination of **lead in paint**
2. Reduction of **lead in gasoline**
3. Reduction in manufactured **food and drink cans and household plumbing components containing lead solder**
4. **Lead screening laws**
5. **Lead paint abatement programs** in communities

## §4 Assessment tools (p. 139, 149)

- **Windshield survey:** a helpful **first step** to understanding potential environmental health risks in a community. Determine whether an exposure is in the air, water, soil, or food (or a combination).
- **I PREPARE** — the exposure-history mnemonic (Box 8.2):
  - **I** — Investigate potential exposures
  - **P** — Present work
  - **R** — Residence
  - **E** — Environmental concerns
  - **P** — Past work
  - **A** — Activities
  - **R** — Referrals and resources
  - **E** — Educate
- **Nursing process applied:** Assessment (environmental history questions) → Diagnosis (relate disease to environmental factors) → Goal setting (outcomes that mitigate/eliminate risk) → Planning (use community policy and law) → Intervention (coordinate medical, nursing, and public health action) → Evaluation (immediate and long-term results).

## §5 Air and water quality (p. 144–146)

- **Point sources** = identifiable sites such as **smokestacks.** **Nonpoint sources** = diffuse, such as **vehicles** (cars, trucks, buses). The **Clean Air Act** regulates air pollution from **both.** Motor vehicles are the greatest single source of air pollution.
- **Carbon monoxide** is a particularly dangerous gas.
- **Air Quality Index (AQI):** reports levels of health concern for air pollution.
- **Consumer confidence report (CCR):** the annual drinking-water quality report, part of the public's **right to know.**
- **Right-to-know laws** let professionals and community members look up environmental hazard information by zip code.

---

## §6 Risk assessment, risk communication, and outrage (p. 146–149)

- **Risk** is a familiar term in nursing practice; **risk assessment** weighs hazards and their potential for harm.
- **Risk communication** combines good-communication principles: the **right information** (accurate, relevant, concise, understandable), at the right time, from a trusted source. **Nurses are often cited in surveys as trusted sources**, which matters because **outrage diminishes when people get information from a source they trust.**
- Worked outrage examples: involuntary secondhand-smoke exposure raised public outrage enough to produce smoke-free-space legislation; during COVID-19, outrage factors included people not masking or distancing when asked.

## §7 Government environmental protection (p. 150) — the regulatory chain

The government manages exposures by **developing and enforcing standards and regulations** that limit pollution. Know the sequence:

**Permitting → Standards → Compliance → Enforcement → Monitoring**

- **Permit:** a legally binding document; multiple permits may be required (stormwater, construction, operation).
- **Environmental standards:** a permitted emission level, a **maximum contaminant level**, an action level for cleanup, or a risk-based standard.
- **Compliance:** the processes for ensuring permit and standard requirements are met.
- **Enforcement:** the government actions that follow compliance to control pollution.
- **Monitoring:** ongoing measurement confirming standards are met.
- Cleanup/**remediation** of environmental damage is another control step. Public information and involvement (citizen advisory panels, community forums) are integral to standard-setting.

## §8 Persistent toxins and bioaccumulation (p. 152)

- **PBTs and POPs** = chemicals that **do not break down** in air, water, or soil, or in the plant, animal, and human bodies they pass through.
- **Bioaccumulation:** the body burden of a persistent toxin **increases up the food chain.** Worked example: **methylmercury** in fish (larger fish eat smaller fish → burden rises); **dioxin** (an endocrine-disrupting chemical and strong carcinogen) has accumulated in breast tissue and is found in cow and human milk.
- Mercury, like lead, is an **element** and therefore persists indefinitely in waterways and oceans.

---

## §9 Environmental health disparities and justice (p. 152)

- **Environmental health disparities:** certain risks disproportionately affect **poor people and people of color.** A poor person of color is more likely to live near a hazardous waste site or incinerator and to have children exposed to lead.
- **Environmental justice** (environmental equity): the movement and legal framework addressing this. **Environmental Justice Act (1993)**; **Executive Order 12898 (1994)**, "Federal Actions to Address Environmental Justice in Minority Populations."

---

## §10 Waste management and levels of prevention

- **The 3 Rs:** **Reduce** (cut consumption, packaging, nonessentials) → **Reuse** (reusable over disposable) → **Recycle** (paper, glass, cans, plastic). Order reflects priority.
- **Three waste-disposal methods:** **incineration** (burning; ash and air emissions must then be controlled), **water discharge**, and **landfilling/burying in soil.**

**LEVELS OF PREVENTION box — Related to the Environment: Lead Exposure:**
- **Primary:** use only non-lead-based paint.
- **Secondary:** if lead is found in paint, remove it and replace with non-lead paint.
- **Tertiary:** at the first sign of symptoms of lead exposure, take steps to reduce blood lead levels.

## Source conflicts and traps

- **The exposure pathway has five links, and breaking any one prevents harm.** A stem asking how to interrupt an exposure is testing this: removing the source, blocking the medium, protecting the receptor, cutting the route, or lowering the dose all work. An option implying all five must be addressed simultaneously misses the point.
- **Three routes of exposure only: inhalation, ingestion, dermal absorption.** Any fourth "route" (e.g., "genetic") is a distractor.
- **Point vs. nonpoint sources.** Point = identifiable single site (smokestack, pipe). Nonpoint = diffuse (vehicles, runoff). Calling vehicle emissions a "point source" is the trap. The Clean Air Act covers both.
- **Intact lead is not the hazard; lead dust and flakes are.** A scenario with well-maintained lead paint and no deterioration is lower risk; the danger is disturbance (renovation, peeling). Homes before 1978 are the flag.
- **Lead screening is a community/population action, not just a clinic order.** The nurse's named role is to **set up a blood-lead screening program through the local health department** and get providers to **systematically test children** — a stem asking the nurse's best action for community lead risk wants the population-level screening/education answer, not a single-patient intervention. Screening is **secondary** prevention (detecting existing exposure); using non-lead paint is **primary**; treating an elevated blood lead level is **tertiary**.
- **Children's vulnerability is physiologic, not behavioral alone.** Faster breathing, lower breathing zone, immature blood-brain barrier and kidneys, and fetal sensitivity windows are all named. An option attributing children's risk solely to "carelessness" is wrong.
- **Windshield survey is the first step**, not a definitive assessment. Treating it as a complete exposure assessment is the distractor.
- **The regulatory chain order:** permit → standard → compliance → enforcement → monitoring. Compliance is "ensuring requirements are met"; enforcement is the government action. Swapping the two definitions is a likely trap.
- **PBTs/POPs are defined by not breaking down**, which is why they bioaccumulate. An option calling them "rapidly degrading" is wrong.
- **Bioaccumulation increases up the food chain** (larger fish, then humans). Any option saying concentration decreases at higher trophic levels is inverted.
- **Environmental justice is about disproportionate burden on poor communities and people of color**, not equal exposure for all. An option framing it as "everyone is exposed equally" contradicts the chapter.
- **Nurses are trusted sources, and trust reduces outrage.** A risk-communication item may hinge on choosing the trusted-source response over a technically dense but impersonal one.
