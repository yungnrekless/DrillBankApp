## §1 Disasters: Definition, Types, and Scope (p. 287–289)

**Definition:** A disaster is **any natural or human-made incident that causes disruption, destruction, or devastation requiring EXTERNAL ASSISTANCE.**

> ⚠️ **The "external assistance" clause is the discriminator.** An event a community can absorb with its own resources is not a disaster by this definition.

### Box 16.1 — Types of Disasters

| Natural | Human-Made |
|---|---|
| Hurricanes; tornadoes; hailstorms; cyclones; blizzards; droughts; floods; mudslides; avalanches; earthquakes; volcanic eruptions; **communicable disease epidemics**; **lightning-induced forest fires**; tsunamis; thunderstorms and lightning; extreme heat and cold | Conventional warfare; nonconventional warfare (nuclear, chemical); transportation accidents; structural collapse; explosions and bombings; **fires**; hazardous materials incident; pollution; **civil unrest (riots)**; terrorism (chemical, biological, radiological, nuclear, explosives); **cyberattacks**; airplane crashes; radiological incidents; nuclear power plant incidents; **critical infrastructure failures**; water supply contamination |

> ⚠️ **Note the two easy inversions:** **communicable disease epidemics** and **lightning-induced forest fires** are classified as **NATURAL**. **Fires** (generally) and **critical infrastructure failures** are classified as **HUMAN-MADE**.

**The incident triad example (memorize this one):** A **9.0 magnitude earthquake** struck northeastern Japan on **March 11, 2011**, quickly followed by a **tsunami**. These dual natural disasters caused an estimated death toll of **20,000** — but **there was a third, human-made component to complete the incident triad: a nuclear reactor crisis.** An independent parliamentary investigation later found the **Fukushima nuclear disaster to be the result of a mix of several human-made factors.**

> **The key framing sentence:** **"Although natural events such as earthquakes or hurricanes often TRIGGER disasters, predictable and preventable HUMAN-MADE FACTORS can INCREASE the effects of the disaster."**

**Complex humanitarian emergency (CHE):** A **"humanitarian crisis in a country, region or society where there is total or considerable breakdown of authority resulting from internal or external conflict and which requires an international response that goes beyond the mandate or capacity of any single and/or ongoing UN country programme."**

### Factors that contribute to disasters

- **Urbanization and overcrowding of cities** have increased the danger of natural disasters because **communities have been built in areas vulnerable to such disasters** — known tornado zones, near rivers, on flood plains.
- **Overcrowding and urban development have also increased HUMAN-MADE disasters.** **The stress of overcrowding has caused civil unrest and riots.** Modern wars are waged over **land rights and space**.
- **School violence**, a human-made disaster, has increased in intensity and magnitude.
- **People in industrialized countries are becoming LESS self-sufficient** because they rely heavily on technology and on social and economic systems within their community.
- ⚠️ **"People who live on the brink of disaster every day physically, emotionally, or economically are among the FIRST to be affected when disaster strikes."**
- **Disasters that have little or no advance notice**, such as earthquakes or bioterrorism, **often have more casualties due to the lack of time to prepare or obtain treatment.** **Individuals can also be injured while attempting to prepare for the disaster or while evacuating.**

### The counterintuitive trend

> ⚠️ **"Although the number of disasters worldwide continues to GROW, the number of lives lost has DECREASED."** The increase in lives saved may be due to **better disaster forecasting and early warning systems.**

**Global projections and costs:**
- By **2050**, at least **46% of the world's population** will live in areas vulnerable to natural floods, earthquakes, and severe storms. **80% of the world's population will live in developing countries.**
- Economic losses from disasters now reach an annual average of **US $250 to $300 billion**, expected to rise to **$415 billion in 2030** (not including global COVID-19 costs).
- ⚠️ **"The costs of disasters in MORE DEVELOPED countries are HIGHER than in developing countries because of the costs of labor, materials, and complex infrastructures including technology."** But **"developing countries experience a disproportionate BURDEN from natural disasters"** because they are poor and have limited resources.
- **"Disasters in less developed communities can destroy decades of progress in a matter of hours, in a manner that rarely happens in more developed countries."**

**Who is excessively affected and least able to rebound (WHO, 2017):** **the poor, elderly, ethnic minorities, people with disabilities, and women and children in developing communities.**

**Political dimension:** **"Some countries will not accept aid from nations they do not consider to be allies or supporters."** Some governments **thwart the efforts of international aid workers**, as seen in Syria.

**What can still be done:** **"Although natural disasters cannot be prevented, much can be done to prevent further increases in accidents, death, and destruction AFTER impact."** Two requirements are named:
1. **A concise, realistic, and well-rehearsed disaster plan is essential.**
2. **Open, clear, and ongoing communication among involved workers and organizations is critical.**

1. **Biosurveillance**
2. **Countermeasure stockpiling and distribution**

1. **Safety and security**
2. **Food, water, and shelter**
3. **Health and medical**
4. **Energy (power and fuel)**
5. **Communications**

### The NHSS / PAHPA line

- **PAHPA (2006)** — the **Pandemic and All-Hazards Preparedness Act.** Goal: **improve the nation's ability to DETECT, PREPARE FOR, and RESPOND TO a variety of public health emergencies.** **It directed the NHSS.**
- **Reenacted in 2013 and 2019**, now called the **Pandemic and All-Hazards Preparedness and Advancing Innovation Act (PAHPIA)**. **Funds public health and hospital preparedness programs as well as medical countermeasures under the BioShield Project.**

**NHSS three overarching objectives (2019–2022):**
1. **Prepare, mobilize, and coordinate the Whole-of-Government** to bring the full spectrum of federal medical and public health capabilities to support state, local, tribal, and territorial authorities
2. **Protect the nation from health effects of emerging and pandemic infectious diseases as well as CBRN threats**
3. **Leverage the capabilities of the private sector**

**NHSS outcomes:** **community strengthening, integration of response and recovery systems, and seamless coordination among all levels of the public health and medical system.**

### The five biodefense programs under PAHPRA — memorize what each does

| Program | What it is |
|---|---|
| **BioWatch** | **An EARLY WARNING SYSTEM for biothreats**; uses an **environmental sensor system to test the AIR** for biological agents in several major metropolitan areas |
| **BioSense** | **A DATA-SHARING program** to facilitate **surveillance of unusual patterns or clusters of diseases.** Shares data with local and state health departments; **part of the BioWatch system** |
| **Project BioShield** | **Develops and PRODUCES new drugs and vaccines** as countermeasures against potential bioweapons and deadly pathogens |
| **Cities Readiness Initiative** | Aids cities to **increase their capacity to DELIVER medicines and medical supplies** during a large-scale public health emergency |
| **Strategic National Stockpile (SNS)** | **A CDC-managed program with the capacity to provide LARGE QUANTITIES of medicine and medical supplies.** **Deployed through a combination of state-level request and the public health system** |

**Public Health Information Network (PHIN)** — CDC-developed for **electronic exchange of information among governmental agencies.** Six components: **early event detection, outbreak management, connecting laboratory systems, countermeasure and response administration, partner communications and alerting, and cross-functional components.**

### Emergency Support Functions (ESFs)

**The NRF includes 15 ESFs**, which **provide a mechanism to bundle federal resources and capabilities.** Functions include transportation, communications, public works and engineering, firefighting, information and planning, mass care, emergency assistance, temporary housing and human services, logistics, **public health and medical services**, search and rescue, oil and hazardous materials, agriculture and natural resources, energy, public safety and security, long-term community recovery, and external affairs.

**ESF-8: Public Health and Medical Services** — the one to know.

| | Detail |
|---|---|
| **Provides** | Guidance for **medical and mental health personnel, medical equipment and supplies, assessment of the status of the public health infrastructure, and monitoring for potential disease outbreaks** |
| **Primary coordinating agency** | **USDHHS** |
| **Supporting agencies** | **USDHS, the American Red Cross, the Department of Defense, and the Department of Veterans Affairs** |
| **Contains** | **The NDMS, which includes the DMATs** |
| **Also** | **Provides federal assistance to SPECIAL NEEDS SHELTERS** |

### FEMA's role — named verbatim in the blueprint

> **"The Federal Emergency Management Agency (FEMA) coordinates comprehensive, ALL-HAZARD planning at the NATIONAL level, assuring a menu of exercises and plan templates to address plausible incidents in any given community."**

**Emergency management generally** is "responsible for developing and coordinating emergency response plans within their defined area, whether **local, state, federal, or tribal.**" **Emergency management personnel at the state and local level work closely with their communities and response partners, providing opportunities to train, exercise, evaluate, and update disaster plans.**

**FEMA also:** publishes the citizen guide **"Are You Ready?"**; runs the **Emergency Management Institute**; administers **HSEEP** and the **National Exercise Program (NEP)**.

### Volunteer and response organizations

| Organization | Role |
|---|---|
| **NDMS** (National Disaster Medical System) | Enables nurses to work on specialized teams such as DMAT. **Part of ESF-8** |
| **DMAT** | Activated by the **US Public Health Service in a presidentially declared disaster**, including overseas war; **can also be activated by the Assistant Secretary for Health if requested by a state health officer.** Deployed **within hours**. ⚠️ **"Because of the nature of this country's disasters since the initiation of DMATs, these teams have been used PRIMARILY TO STAFF COMMUNITY HEALTH OUTPATIENT CLINICS in the affected areas."** |
| **MRC** and **CERT** | Support emergency preparedness and response **in local jurisdictions** |
| **American Red Cross (ARC)** | Provides **a comprehensive program of disaster training for health professionals**; training in **disaster health services and disaster mental health** for both local response and national deployment. **Shelters are generally the responsibility of the local ARC chapter.** |

**After ARC disaster training, nurses can:** join a local disaster action team; act as a liaison with local hospitals; determine health-services support for shelter sites; plan on a multidisciplinary team for optimal patient service delivery; address the logistics of health and medical supplies; and teach disaster nursing in the community.

> ⚠️ **The spontaneous volunteer problem:** "**The importance of being adequately trained and PROPERLY ASSOCIATED WITH AN OFFICIAL RESPONSE ORGANIZATION to serve in a disaster cannot be overstated.** In a disaster, many untrained and ill-equipped individuals rush in to help. **Spontaneous volunteer overload creates added burden on an already tense situation that now includes role conflict, anger, frustration, and helplessness.**"

## §3 The Disaster Management Cycle (p. 290)

> **"Disaster management includes FOUR stages: PREVENTION (including mitigation and protection), PREPAREDNESS, RESPONSE, and RECOVERY."** (Fig. 16.2)

**Original source of the cycle:** National Governors' Association Center for Policy Research, *Comprehensive Emergency Management: A Governor's Guide*, **1979**.

**Nursing skills that apply across all four phases:** **assessment, priority setting, collaboration, health education, disease screening, and mass clinic expertise.** Nurses also **provide essential public health services, make referrals, serve as liaisons among organizations, health care, and social service providers, and provide psychological first aid, triage, and rapid needs assessment.**

### Phase 1 — Prevention (Mitigation and Protection)

**All-hazards mitigation (prevention)** = **reducing risks to people and property from natural hazards BEFORE they occur.**

| Measure type | Examples |
|---|---|
| **Structural** | **Protecting buildings and infrastructure from the forces of wind and water** |
| **Nonstructural** | **Land development restrictions** |

**Prevention also covers human-made hazards:** the ability to **deter potential terrorists, detect terrorists before they strike, and take action to eliminate the threat.**

**Prevention activities may include:** heightened inspections, improved surveillance and security operations, **public health and agricultural surveillance and testing**, **immunizations**, **isolation or quarantine**, and halting CBRNE threats.

**Three named nursing roles in prevention:**
1. **Awareness and education** — holding or attending community meetings on disaster preparedness; informing the community about educational resources such as FEMA's **"Are You Ready?"** guide
2. **Organization and participation** — organizing and participating in **mass prophylaxis and vaccination campaigns**
3. **Advocacy** — **identifying environmental hazards**, serving on the public health team for mitigation work, and **supporting effective building codes and proper land use**

**High-risk targets nurses should be aware of:** military and civilian government facilities, health care facilities, international airports and other transportation systems, large cities, high-profile landmarks. **Terrorists might also target large public gatherings, water and food supplies, banking and finance, information technology, postal and shipping services, utilities, or corporate centers.**

**Healthy People 2030 objectives related to disaster mitigation:**
- **PREP-003:** Increase the proportion of adults who **know how to evacuate** in case of a hurricane, flood, or wildfire
- **PREP-D01:** Increase the proportion of **parents and guardians who know the emergency or evacuation plan at their children's school**
- **PREP-D02:** Increase the proportion of adults who **prepare for a disease outbreak after getting preparedness information**

## §4 Preparedness (p. 291–295)

### Personal preparedness — the blueprint names "importance of family having a disaster plan"

> **"Nurses who are disaster victims themselves and must provide care to others will experience considerable stress. Conflicts between family and work-related duties are INEVITABLE."**

The chapter's example: **a nurse who is also the mother of a young child will not be able to participate fully, if at all, in disaster relief efforts UNTIL SHE HAS MADE ARRANGEMENTS FOR HER CHILD.** Personal and family preparation in advance **can help ease some of the conflicts that arise and allow nurses to attend to patient needs SOONER.**

> **"In addition, nurses assisting in disaster relief efforts must be as healthy as possible, BOTH PHYSICALLY AND MENTALLY, to serve patients, families, and other disaster victims."**

**"Preparedness is multifaceted; THE FAMILY OF EACH NURSE MUST BE INCLUDED AND INFORMED about the disaster plan."**

**How to protect the family — involve children and adolescents in:**
- **Writing preparedness or response plans**
- **Rehearsing the plan**
- **Preparing disaster kits**
- **Becoming familiar with their school emergency plan and where families should reunite**
- **Finding out where the evacuation shelters are located**
- **Identifying the evacuation routes**
- **Learning about the range of potential hazards in their vicinity**

**Natural and human-made hazards, including terrorism, should be discussed.** **Vulnerable types of infrastructure such as dams, chemical plants, bridges, and transportation should be pointed out.**

> ⚠️ **Why involve children (this is the testable rationale):** **"Discussion offers children and adolescents an opportunity to express their feelings. The ability to CONTROL AS MUCH AS THEY CAN during each phase of a disaster provides them with the ability to BOUNCE BACK."**

### HOW TO Be Red Cross Ready — the three steps

**1. GET A KIT.**
- **At least 3 days of nonperishable food and water — 1 gallon per person per day** for drinking and hygiene
- **Battery-powered or hand-crank radio** (NOAA weather radio if possible)
- **Flashlight and extra batteries**
- **First aid kit, medications, and medical items**
- **Copies of all important documents** (proof of address, deed/lease, passports, birth certificates, insurance policies)

**Additional items the chapter names:** manual can opener; list of medications and dosages; list of allergies and physician names; candles and matches; sanitation supplies (toilet paper, soap, feminine hygiene items, plastic garbage bags); special items for infants, older adults, or disabled family members; an extra pair of eyeglasses; pet supplies. ⚠️ **Store documents in a WATERPROOF container.**

**2. MAKE A PLAN.**
- **Choose TWO places to meet — one right outside your home** (for a sudden emergency such as a fire) **and another outside your neighborhood** (in case you cannot return home or are asked to evacuate)
- **Choose a contact person OUT OF THE AREA.** ⚠️ **"It may be easier to call long distance or text if local phone lines are overloaded or out of service."**
- **Tell everyone where emergency information and supplies are kept**
- **Practice evacuating your home TWICE A YEAR.** **Drive your planned evacuation route and plot ALTERNATIVE routes on a map in case main roads are impassable**
- **Don't forget your pets** — keep a phone list of "pet-friendly" motels/hotels and animal shelters along your evacuation route

**3. BE INFORMED.** Know the risks where you live, work, learn, and play. **Find out how you would receive information from local officials.** **Learn first aid and CPR/AED**, "especially during a disaster in which emergency responders may not be as available."

**Maintenance schedule (from the QSEN box):**
- **Review the plan every 6 months**
- **Replace stored WATER every 3 months and stored FOOD every 6 months**
- **Test smoke detectors MONTHLY; change batteries at least once a year**
- **Conduct fire and emergency evacuation drills**

### What nurses add to their "be ready" kit

**Identification badge and driver's license; proof of licensure and certification; pocket-size reference book; blood pressure cuff (adult and child) and stethoscope; gloves, mask, and other PPE; first aid kit with CPR barrier and tourniquet; crank radio and cell phone charger; cash, credit card; important papers and contact information in hard copy; sun protection and insect repellent; sturdy shoes with socks; medical identification of allergies and blood type; medications for self; weather-appropriate clothing including rain gear; toiletries; watch, cell phone with charger and preentered emergency numbers; flashlight and extra batteries; record-keeping materials including pencil/pen; map of area.**

### Pets and special-needs registration

- **Many special-needs shelters encourage PREREGISTRATION** for physically or mentally challenged people
- ⚠️ **"Most shelters do NOT allow pets other than 'pocket' pets."** Alternatives: a **special pet shelter** or **placing the pet in a bathroom with sufficient food and water.** **A note should be placed on the front door for emergency personnel as to where the pet might be found**
- **Many local emergency management offices are considering incorporating pets into local disaster plans**

### Professional preparedness

**Adequately prepared nurses can serve as leaders and enable others to have a smoother RECOVERY phase.**

- Nurses **read and understand workplace and community disaster plans** and **participate in disaster drills and community mock disasters**
- **Disaster management in the community is about POPULATION HEALTH**, and **the three core public health functions are used just as in day-to-day operations: ASSESSMENT, POLICY DEVELOPMENT, and ASSURANCE**
- **"Although disaster work is not highly technological, there is increasing information provided in a wireless format."**
- **Fieldwork, including shelter management, requires that nurses be CREATIVE AND WILLING TO IMPROVISE in delivering care**
- **All workers should be certified in first aid and CPR**

**Box 16.2 — Core Competencies for Disaster Medicine and Public Health (11 competencies, abbreviated):** personal and family preparedness (1.0); knowledge of one's expected role(s) in response plans (2.0); situational awareness of health hazards before, during, and after (3.0); effective communication (4.0); personal safety measures (5.0); surge capacity assets (6.0); clinical management principles for all ages and populations (7.0); public health principles and practices (8.0); **ethical principles** (9.0); **legal principles** (10.0); **short- and long-term considerations for recovery** (11.0).

### Community preparedness

> **"The level of community preparedness for a disaster is only as good as the people and organizations in the community make it."**

- Some communities **have a written disaster plan and participate in yearly disaster drills**; others **"are less prepared and depend on luck and the fact that they are unlikely to experience a disaster."**
- **Most health care facilities have written disaster plans and require employees to perform annual mock drills, but MANY BUSINESSES LACK these requirements.**
- **PPD-8 emphasizes that true preparedness is a WHOLE COMMUNITY EVENT.**
- **Stakeholders include, FIRST AND FOREMOST, THE GENERAL PUBLIC** — plus all levels of government, public health agencies, hospitals, first responders, emergency management, health care providers, schools and universities, the private sector, businesses, and NGOs such as the ARC.
- **Mutual aid agreements and prior planning** help bridge barriers and **establish relationships BEFORE the incident.** Barriers may involve **regulatory authority and jurisdictional boundaries**, or **organizational control versus the common good.**

**Two principles of disaster planning (DHS, 2014) — memorize the rationale:**
> **Disaster planning involves SIMPLICITY and REALISM with BACKUP CONTINGENCIES because (1) THE DISASTER WILL NEVER BE AN "EXACT FIT" FOR THE PLAN, and (2) ALL PLANS MUST BE IMPLEMENTATION READY, NO MATTER WHO IS PRESENT TO START THEM.**

**Warning systems and reluctant evacuees:**
- The community must have **an adequate warning system AND a backup evacuation plan** to remove individuals hesitant to leave
- ⚠️ **"Some people refuse to leave their homes because they are AFRAID THEIR POSSESSIONS WILL BE LOST OR DESTROYED either by the disaster or FROM LOOTING after the disaster."**
- **Law enforcement personnel or others in authority may have to speak DIRECTLY to these reluctant residents**
- ⚠️ **"Some people mistakenly believe that EXPERIENCE WITH A PARTICULAR TYPE OF DISASTER IS ENOUGH PREPARATION FOR THE NEXT ONE."** They must be convinced that **predisaster warnings are official, serious, and personally important** before they are motivated to act

### Community resilience

**Defined as "the SUSTAINED ABILITY OF A COMMUNITY TO WITHSTAND AND RECOVER FROM ADVERSITY"** (Links et al., 2018). **A central theme in disaster planning.**

**"PREINCIDENT CAPACITY HAS A DIRECT IMPACT ON RESILIENCE."**

**The eleven prefunctioning domains:** communication, economy, education, food and water, government, housing, health care, public health, nurturing care, transportation, and well-being. **Strengths or deficits in these domains either increase or decrease resilience.**

- **Population factors that contribute to resilience:** **vulnerability, inequity, and deprivation**
- **Prevention/mitigation factors:** **natural systems, engineered systems, and countermeasures**
- **"The nurse works to build on the strengths and mitigate areas of weakness to build resiliency."** **"Healthier communities are better able to bounce back."**

### Exercises and drills

**Two exercise categories (FEMA, 2018b):**

| Category | Examples |
|---|---|
| **Discussion-based simulations** | **"Tabletops"** |
| **Operations-based events** | **Drills, functional exercises, full-scale exercises** — involve **escalating the scope and scale testing** of the preparedness and response network using a specific plan |

- **"Although practice will not ensure a perfect response to disaster, disaster and mass casualty drills and exercises are extremely valuable components of preparedness."**
- **After the exercise, the lessons learned through AFTER-ACTION REPORTS are used to update disaster plans and subsequent operations.**
- **National Exercise Program (NEP)** — tests and validates core capabilities. An annual **Capstone Exercise** (formerly the National Level Exercise) is conducted **every 2 years.** ⚠️ **The 2020 exercise was scheduled to focus on cybersecurity; in light of COVID-19, the focus was changed to a pandemic.**
- **HSEEP is the NATIONAL STANDARD for all exercises.**
- **"Nurses and other health care providers MUST be included as a part of the exercise's planning, response, and after-action activities."**

## §5 Response (p. 295–301)

### The escalation ladder — know the order

1. **The FIRST level of disaster response occurs at the LOCAL level**, with mobilization of **the fire department, law enforcement, public health, and emergency services**
2. **If disaster needs exceed local resources**, the **county or city emergency management agency** coordinates activities through an **emergency operations center**. Local responders within a county typically sign a **regional or statewide mutual aid agreement** allowing the sharing of personnel, equipment, services, and supplies
3. **When STATE resources and capabilities are overwhelmed, GOVERNORS may request FEDERAL assistance under a presidential disaster or emergency declaration**
4. **If the event is considered an INCIDENT OF NATIONAL SIGNIFICANCE** (a potential or high-impact disaster), appropriate response personnel and resources are provided
5. **Once a federal emergency has been declared, the NRF may take effect**

**"The initial scope of disaster assessment is usually measured in DOLLARS, HEALTH RISK AND INJURY, and/or LIVES LOST. The more destruction and lives at risk, the greater is the degree of attention and resources provided."**

### Triage — the three priority levels

**Definition:** **"The process of separating casualties and allocating treatment on the basis of an individual's POTENTIAL FOR SURVIVAL."**

| Priority | Who | Time frame |
|---|---|---|
| **Highest** | Individuals with **life-threatening injuries** who **also have a HIGH PROBABILITY OF SURVIVAL once stabilized** | Immediate |
| **Second** | Victims with injuries that have **systemic complications that are NOT YET life threatening** | **Could wait 45 to 60 minutes** |
| **Last** | Victims with **LOCAL injuries WITHOUT immediate complications** | **Can wait several hours** |

> ⚠️ **The survival clause is what makes disaster triage different from ED triage.** Highest priority requires **BOTH** a life-threatening injury **AND** a high probability of survival once stabilized. A patient with catastrophic, non-survivable injuries is not highest priority under this rule.

**"Establishing a priority of needs that benefit THE LARGEST AGGREGATE of affected individuals with THE MOST CORRECTABLE PROBLEMS is consistent with the basic tenets of triage."**

**"When rescue workers arrive on the scene, plans for triage should begin IMMEDIATELY."** If a nurse is the first to arrive, **"the more usual skills of community assessment, case finding and referring, prevention, health education, surveillance, and working with aggregates will be PUT ASIDE TEMPORARILY so that the nurse can deal with LIFE-THREATENING PROBLEMS."**

### Rapid needs assessment — the six elements

**Elements of a rapid needs assessment (Stanley et al., 2008):**
1. **Determining the magnitude of the incident**
2. **Defining the specific health needs of the affected population**
3. **Establishing priorities and objectives for action**
4. **Identifying existing and potential public health problems**
5. **Evaluating the capacity of the local response, including resources and logistics**
6. **Determining the external resource needs for priority actions**

**Sudden-impact vs. gradual-onset — different assessment focus:**

| Disaster type | Examples | Assessment focus |
|---|---|---|
| **Sudden-impact** | **Tornadoes, earthquakes** | **Ongoing hazards, injuries and deaths, shelter requirements, and clean water** |
| **Gradual-onset** | **Famines** | **Mortality rates, nutritional status, immunization status, and environmental health** |

**Assessment methods:** interview, observation, individual physical examinations, health and illness screening, surveys (sample and special health), and records (census, school, vital statistics, disease reporting).

> **"Ongoing assessments or SURVEILLANCE REPORTS are just as important as initial assessments."** They **indicate the continuing status of the affected population and the effectiveness of ongoing relief efforts.**

**The misuse-of-resources problem:** **"Lack of or inaccurate information regarding the scope of the disaster and its initial effects contributes to the MISUSE OF RESOURCES."** Two named examples: **too many volunteers who lack official sponsorship converge and are disappointed when their help cannot be used**; **well-meaning people send clothes and food to disaster sites that LACK STORAGE AND DISTRIBUTION ABILITIES.** ⚠️ **"Contributions that add to the stress of coping with the disaster can be a burden."**

### The first goal of response

> **"The FIRST GOAL of any disaster response is to REESTABLISH SANITARY BARRIERS as quickly as possible."** Basic needs: **water, food, waste removal, vector control, shelter, and safety.**

**Disease prevention is an ongoing goal**, especially if there is an interruption in the public health infrastructure. ⚠️ **"Infectious disease outbreaks occur in the RECOVERY phase of disasters, and occasionally DISASTER WORKERS INTRODUCE NEW ORGANISMS INTO THE AREA."**

### Response to biological incidents

**Why biological agents are uniquely dangerous:** **only a small amount is needed to affect thousands of people**, and **some agents are easy to conceal, transport, and disseminate.**

> ⚠️ **The delayed-recognition problem (high-yield):** **"An unannounced dissemination of a biological agent MAY EASILY GO UNNOTICED, and the victims may have LEFT THE AREA OF EXPOSURE long before the act of terrorism is recognized. The first signs may not be apparent for DAYS OR WEEKS, until the victims become ill and seek a health evaluation. IN THIS CASE, THE HEALTH CARE PROFESSIONALS ARE CONSIDERED THE 'FIRST ON THE SCENE.'"**

**"Chemical terrorist attacks require a VERY DIFFERENT response."**

**Five components of a comprehensive public health response to outbreaks of illness (Rotz et al., 2000):**
1. **Detecting the outbreak**
2. **Determining the cause**
3. **Identifying factors that place people at risk**
4. **Implementing measures to control the outbreak**
5. **Informing the medical and public communities about treatments, health consequences, and preventive measures**

**"IDENTIFYING THE CHEMICAL OR BIOLOGICAL AGENT IS THE FIRST PRIORITY."** **Rapid identification is vital to protect health care workers and any others affected.**

- ⚠️ **"Results of a biological release are HARD TO RECOGNIZE because MANY BIOLOGICAL AGENT SYMPTOMS MIMIC INFLUENZA OR OTHER VIRAL SYNDROMES."**
- **Only about a dozen pathogens pose a major threat**, even though there are thousands of pathogens
- **Although an aerosol release may be a likely vehicle, certain biological agents could also be released through the water and food supply**
- **Quarantine of those exposed to contagious agents may be considered in some instances**
- **The CDC is an excellent source of information on biological agents**; fact sheets include **methods of transmission and the communicability period**

### The four community stress phases — expect an item

| Phase | What happens | Associated with |
|---|---|---|
| **1. Heroic** | **Overwhelming need for people to do whatever they can to help others survive.** First responders **work hours on end with no thought of their own personal or health needs**; they **fight needed sleep and refuse rest breaks.** Imported responders may be **unfamiliar with the terrain and inherent dangers.** ⚠️ **"Exhausted, overworked responders present a DANGER TO THEMSELVES AND THE COMMUNITY SERVED"** — those with oversight **may need to ORDER helpers to take breaks** | **Response** |
| **2. Honeymoon** | **Survivors may be REJOICING that their lives and the lives of loved ones have been spared.** Survivors **gather to share experiences and stories**; **the repeated telling creates BONDS among the survivors.** A sense of **thankfulness** is inherent | **Response** |
| **3. Disillusionment** | Occurs **after time elapses and people begin to notice that additional help and reinforcement may NOT be immediately forthcoming.** Results in **a sense of DESPAIR**; **exhaustion starts to take its toll.** **The community begins to realize that a return to the previous normal is UNLIKELY** and that major changes and adjustments must be made | **Recovery** |
| **4. Reconstruction** | ⚠️ **THE LONGEST phase.** Homes, schools, churches, and other community elements are **rebuilt and reestablished.** **The goal is to return to a NEW STATE OF NORMALCY** | **Recovery** |

**In the disillusionment phase specifically:** public health nurses should **identify groups and population segments particularly at risk for BURNOUT AND EXHAUSTION, including responders and volunteers involved in rescue efforts.** They may need **breaks and reminders for nourishment.** Those **in shock and consumed by grief will need compassionate care, with possible referrals to mental health counseling.**

### Individual stress reactions

**"Individuals react to the same disaster in DIFFERENT ways depending on their AGE, CULTURAL BACKGROUND, HEALTH STATUS, SOCIAL SUPPORT STRUCTURE, and GENERAL ABILITY TO ADAPT TO CRISIS."**

**People affected by a disaster often have an EXACERBATION OF AN EXISTING CHRONIC DISEASE.** The chapter's example: **emotional stress may make it difficult for people with diabetes to control their blood glucose levels.**

> ⚠️ **The immunologic mechanism (a likely item):** **"GRIEF results in harmful effects on the IMMUNE SYSTEM, reducing the function of cells that protect against VIRAL INFECTIONS AND TUMORS. Hormones produced by the body's FIGHT-OR-FLIGHT mechanism also play a role in mediating the effects of grief."**

**Box 16.4 — Know When to Seek Help (symptoms that may require assistance):**

| Emotional | Physical | Behavioral |
|---|---|---|
| Anxious, fearful, or sad; **extreme sense of urgency or panic**; angry (especially if the event involved violence); **guilty, even when you had NO CONTROL over the traumatic event**; **heroic, like you can do anything**; **too much energy or no energy**; **disconnected, not caring about anything or anyone**; **numb, unable to feel either joy OR sadness** | Stomachaches and diarrhea; headaches or other pains for no obvious reason; **eating too much or too little**; sweating or having chills; **tremors or muscle twitches; being jumpy or easily startled** | Trouble remembering, thinking, making decisions, or concentrating; feeling confused or numb; **worrying excessively**; trouble talking about what happened and listening to others; trouble sleeping; increase or decrease in energy or activity; feeling sad or crying often; **using alcohol, tobacco, illegal drugs, or prescription medicines to reduce distress**; **outbursts of anger, feeling irritated, blaming others**; difficulty helping others and accepting help; **being RELUCTANT TO ABANDON PROPERTY** |

**Children's reactions (also Box 16.4):** **regressive behaviors (bedwetting, thumb sucking, crying, clinging to parents); FANTASIES THAT THE DISASTER NEVER OCCURRED; nightmares; school-related problems, including inability to concentrate and REFUSAL TO GO BACK TO SCHOOL.**

**Additional detail on children (NIMH):** Children may **reexperience images of the traumatic event or have recurring thoughts or sensations**, or **intentionally avoid reminders, thoughts, and feelings.** They may have **arousal or heightened sensitivity to sights, sounds, or smells** and **exaggerated responses or difficulty with usual activities.**

> ⚠️ **The media point:** **"Children NOT IMMEDIATELY AFFECTED by a disaster can also experience effects from it; the constant bombardment of disaster stories on television can cause fear in children. They may believe that the event could happen to them or their family, believe someone will be injured or killed, or think they will be left alone. IT IS BEST TO TURN OFF THE NEWS and engage in activities with family, friends, and neighbors, AS PARENTS' REACTION TO A DISASTER GREATLY INFLUENCES THEIR CHILDREN'S."**

**Older adults:** reactions depend on **physical health, strength, mobility, independence, and income.** They **react deeply to the loss of personal possessions because of the SENTIMENTAL VALUE and IRREPLACEABLE value.** ⚠️ **"They may try to CONCEAL THE SERIOUSNESS OF THEIR HEALTH CONDITIONS OR LOSSES IF THEY FEAR THE LOSS OF INDEPENDENCE."**

**Children in childcare facilities — an overlooked population:** They are cared for in locations ranging from freestanding buildings to provider homes, **tend to have little if any security against a disaster**, and **people come and go all day.** **A plan must be in place for how the children can be reunited with their parents.** **Because of their small size, someone may have to help them leave the facility.** They are **more vulnerable to chemical and biological agents because of their immature physiological and psychological development**, and have **a smaller fluid reserve than adults, so are more susceptible to dehydration.**

**Childcare emergency supplies (in addition to the family readiness kit):** copies of the child's medical information; **comfort items such as games, toys, blanket, crayons, markers, and paper**; and if the facility assembles the kit, **the attendance list of children.**

### Box 16.5 — Populations at Greatest Risk for Disruption After Disaster

**Older adults; women; pregnant women; children; persons with disabilities (hearing impaired, visually impaired); individuals with chronic disease; individuals with chronic mental illness; low income; homeless; non-English-speaking; rural populations; tourists and persons new to an area; single-parent families; substance abusers; undocumented residents.**

> **"Many of them are the SAME populations who are also at risk for adverse health effects BEFORE a disaster."**

### Nursing role in sheltering

**Shelters are generally the responsibility of the local ARC chapter**, although in massive disasters **the military may set up "tent cities" or bring in trailers.**

> **"Nurses, because of their comfort with delivering aggregate health promotion, disease prevention, and emotional support, make IDEAL SHELTER MANAGERS and team members."**

- **Each person who comes to the shelter is ASSESSED to determine what type of facility is most appropriate for their needs.**
- ⚠️ **"Although initially PHYSICAL health needs may be the priority, especially among older adults and the chronically ill, MANY OF THE PREDOMINANT PROBLEMS IN SHELTERS REVOLVE AROUND STRESS."** Sources of that stress: **the shock of the disaster itself, the loss of personal possessions, the fear of the unknown, LIVING IN PROXIMITY TO TOTAL STRANGERS, and even BOREDOM.**

**Shelter nursing functions:** provide **referrals**; meet health care needs such as **helping patients get prescription glasses, medications, first aid, and appropriate diet adjustments**; **keep patient records**; **ensure emergency communications**; **provide a safe environment.**

**Common-sense approaches for shelter residents:**
- **Listening to victims tell and retell their disaster story and current situation**
- **Encouraging residents to share their feelings with one another if appropriate**
- **Helping residents make decisions**
- ⚠️ **Delegating tasks (reading, crafts, playing games with children) to TEENAGERS and others TO HELP COMBAT BOREDOM**
- **Providing basic necessities (food, clothing, rest)**
- **Attempting to recover or obtain needed items (prescription glasses, medication)**
- **Providing basic compassion and dignity (privacy when appropriate and if possible)**
- **Referring to a mental health counselor or other source of help**

**Special needs shelters:** **Individuals who are MEDICALLY DEPENDENT AND NOT ACUTELY ILL but have varied physical, cognitive, and psychological conditions should be directed to a SPECIAL NEEDS SHELTER.** The federal government provides assistance through **ESF-8**.

> ⚠️ **The tradeoff:** **"Special needs shelters REDUCE THE SURGE DEMANDS on hospitals and long-term care facilities. Although helpful in reducing surge, TOO MANY REFERRALS CAN CREATE TENSION among the special needs shelters, the regular shelters, and the health care facilities as ROLES AND RESPONSIBILITIES BECOME BLURRED and overall resources and personnel limited. CAREFUL PREPLANNING for a community's special needs population is essential."**

### Media and the Public Information Officer

> **"The NIMS approach uses PUBLIC AFFAIRS SPOKESPERSONS for formal communication. The PUBLIC INFORMATION OFFICER (PIO) is a person with the authority and responsibility to communicate information to the public. Because nurses are considered highly trustworthy, they are often asked to do media interviews."**
>
> **The correct first approach when a nurse is asked for an interview: REFER THE MEDIA TO THE PIO REPRESENTING THE AGENCY.** (Not scheduling it for later, and not saying you are too busy.)

### Psychological stress of disaster workers

**Why the work is stressful:** responders may be exposed to **chaotic environments, long hours, rapidly changing information and directives, long wait times before getting to work, noisy environments, and living quarters that are less than ideal.** **Responders may not recognize the need for self-care and neglect monitoring their own emotional and physical health.** **As recovery efforts span weeks to months, there is increasing risk for adverse effects to responders.**

> **"NO ONE who experiences a disaster either personally or in a professional capacity is untouched by it."** And: **"SELF-CARE IS AS IMPORTANT AS THE CARE THAT IS PROVIDED TO COMMUNITY MEMBERS."**

**Symptoms signaling a need for stress management assistance (ARC, 2012):**
- **Being reluctant or refusing to leave the scene until the work is finished**
- **Denying needed rest and recovery time**
- **Feeling overriding stress and fatigue**
- **Engaging in UNNECESSARY RISK-TAKING activities**
- **Difficulty communicating thoughts, remembering instructions, making decisions, or concentrating**
- **Engaging in unnecessary arguments**
- **Having a limited attention span**
- **REFUSING TO FOLLOW ORDERS**

**Physical symptoms:** tremors, headaches, nausea, and cold or flulike symptoms.

> **"Suppressing feelings of guilt, powerlessness, anger, and other signs of stress will eventually lead to symptoms such as irritability, fatigue, headaches, and distortions of bodily functions. IT IS NORMAL TO EXPERIENCE STRESS, BUT IT MUST BE DEALT WITH. THE WORST THING ANYONE CAN DO IS TO DENY THAT IT EXISTS."**

**Reactions after returning home:**
- **"Most reactions are considered NORMAL and are TEMPORARY, resolving in days or a few weeks."**
- **Delayed stress reactions** — occurring **once the disaster is over** — can include **exhaustion and an inability to adjust to the slower pace of work or home**
- **Workers may be disappointed if family members and friends do not seem as interested** in what they have been through
- **They may feel frustration and conflict** if their needs seem inconsistent with those of family and coworkers, or if they left the disaster site **thinking that much more could have been done**
- ⚠️ **"Issues or problems that ONCE SEEMED PRESSING MAY NOW SEEM TRIVIAL. ANGER may emerge as others present problems that seem trivial in contrast to those faced by the victims who were left behind."**
- ⚠️ **"Disaster workers may FANTASIZE ABOUT RETURNING TO THE DISASTER SITE if they think their actions are appreciated more there than at home or the office."**
- **"MOOD SWINGS ARE COMMON and serve to resolve conflicting feelings."**
- **"Feelings or actions that PERSIST or that the worker perceives are INTERFERING WITH DAILY LIFE should be dealt with by a trained mental health professional."**

**Coping strategies (SAMHSA, 2013):** rest and recovery time; **focusing on accomplishments**; calming strategies such as relaxation techniques or hobbies; taking deep breaths; listening to music; **self-care through healthy food and drink, exercise, and sleep**; **talking to others — a trusted friend, family member, faith-based leader, or professional counselor.**

## §6 Recovery (p. 301–302)

**Definition:** **"Recovery is about returning to a NEW NORMAL with the goal of reaching a level of organization that is AS NEAR THE PREDISASTER LEVEL AS IS POSSIBLE."**

> **"This is often the HARDEST part of the disaster."**

### Who does what in recovery

| Entity | Role |
|---|---|
| **The government** | **Takes the LEAD in rebuilding efforts** |
| **The business community** | **Tries to provide economic support** |
| **Religious organizations** | **Help with rebuilding efforts** |
| **The Internal Revenue Service** | **Educates victims as to how to write off losses** |
| **Housing and Urban Development (HUD)** | **Provides GRANTS for temporary housing** |
| **The CDC** | **Provides continuing SURVEILLANCE AND EPIDEMIOLOGICAL SERVICES** |
| **Voluntary agencies** | **Continue to assess individual and community needs and meet them when possible** |
| **Habitat for Humanity** | **Plays a valuable role when housing is destroyed** |

**National Disaster Recovery Framework** — a guide enabling effective recovery support to disaster-impacted states, tribes, territorial and local jurisdictions; **provides a flexible structure that enables disaster recovery managers to operate in a unified and collaborative manner.**

> ⚠️ **"The BEST TIME to start thinking about the LESSONS LEARNED from a recent disaster is DURING THE RECOVERY PHASE of the disaster cycle."**

### Levels of Prevention box — Related to Disaster Prevention Management

| Level | Intervention as printed |
|---|---|
| **Primary** | **Participate in developing a disaster management plan for the community.** |
| **Secondary** | **Assess disaster victims and TRIAGE for care.** |
| **Tertiary** | **Participate in HOME VISITS to uncover dangers that may cause additional injury to victims or cause other disasters (e.g., house fires from faulty wiring).** |

### Recovery-phase nursing

**"Flexibility is essential in the recovery operation."**

**Physical hazards of cleanup:**
- **"Community cleanup efforts can cause many physical and psychological problems; for example, the physical stress of moving heavy objects can cause BACK INJURY, SEVERE FATIGUE, and even DEATH FROM HEART ATTACKS."**
- **Post-disaster cleanup may lead to unintentional injuries:** **falls, contact with live wires, accidents while cutting items, heart attacks from overexertion and stress, and auto accidents caused by road conditions or absent traffic signals**
- ⚠️ **"Nurses need to educate the community about... hazards related to CARBON MONOXIDE POISONING from using LANTERNS, GAS RANGES, GENERATORS, or from BURNING CHARCOAL to heat an enclosed area."**

**What to assess during home visits:** **faulty housing structure or lack of water or electricity; objects blown into the yard by a tornado or that floated in from a flood; the dangers of living or DEAD animals and rodents.** ⚠️ **"Poisonous snakes may be found in and around homes once the waters from a flood start to recede."**

**Ongoing teaching:** **teach proper hygiene** and **make sure immunization records are current, given the threat of disease.**

**Community data points to watch in recovery:** **ongoing illnesses and injuries related to the disaster; diseases and acute respiratory infections related to disruption of environmental or health services; health facility infrastructure in terms of adequate personnel, beds, and medical and pharmaceutical supplies; and environmental health assessment including water quantity and quality, sanitation, shelter, solid waste disposal, and vector populations.**

> **"The rapid needs assessment CONTINUES INTO an ongoing community needs assessment... as SOME CONDITIONS MANIFEST ONLY AFTER TIME ELAPSES. A major advantage of the recovery community assessment efforts is that they can be MORE IN-DEPTH, with GREATER CONFIDENCE in the results."**

**Psychological support in recovery:** **"Acute and chronic illness may become WORSE after a disaster. The psychological stress of loss, cleanup, or moving can lead to feelings of HOPELESSNESS, DEPRESSION, AND GRIEF in the DISILLUSIONMENT phase. Referrals to mental health professionals should continue THROUGHOUT the recovery phase AND AS LONG AS THE NEED EXISTS."**

> **"It is important to have a REALISTIC PERSPECTIVE related to how long recovery may take. It will take MONTHS OR YEARS to return to a semblance of normal, and THIS NEW NORMAL MAY BE DIFFERENT from the predisaster state."**

**Lesson from recent US disasters:** **the value of an ELECTRONIC MEDICAL RECORD became more apparent when hospitals, clinics, and health departments LOST RECORDS during a disaster and when people were relocated for substantial periods without access to their medications or medical records.**

## Source conflicts and traps

- ⚠️ **"When does disaster management begin?" — the textbook never states this in one sentence.** The blueprint names it as a topic, but there is no line reading "disaster management begins when…" The defensible answer from the chapter's own structure is **BEFORE the disaster — with PREVENTION/MITIGATION, the first phase of the cycle.** Supporting language: mitigation is **"reducing risks to people and property from natural hazards BEFORE they occur"**; **"preincident capacity has a direct impact on resilience"**; **"the ultimate goal is to stop violence before it begins"**-style framing recurs. A secondary defensible answer is **"before the disaster, with planning and preparedness."** If a Canvas module states a specific answer, that overrides this. **Flag for Canvas verification.**

- ⚠️ **PTSD is named in the blueprint but is barely in this chapter.** The term **"PTSD" appears essentially nowhere in Ch. 16's disaster stress discussion** — the chapter uses **"stress reactions," "delayed stress reactions,"** and **"traumatic event"** instead, and refers persistent problems to **"a trained mental health professional."** The chapter's actual content is the **four community phases**, **Box 16.4 symptoms**, and **delayed stress reactions**. If an item asks specifically about PTSD **criteria or symptoms**, the source is either **Ch. 27, p. 495** (which gives the four PTSD symptom clusters: **intrusion, avoidance, negative alterations in cognition and mood, and hyperarousal**) or a Canvas module. **Cross-reference Ch. 27 when drilling this.**

- ⚠️ **Prevention and mitigation are the SAME phase here, not different ones.** The chapter heads the section **"Prevention (Mitigation and Protection)"** and uses "all-hazards mitigation (prevention)" as a single concept. Some other sources treat mitigation as a fifth phase. **This textbook says FOUR stages: prevention, preparedness, response, recovery.** If an option offers five phases, it is wrong by this book.

- ⚠️ **Disaster triage priority requires BOTH life-threat AND survivability.** Highest priority goes to **"individuals with life-threatening injuries, BUT ALSO those who have a high probability of survival once they are stabilized."** The classic distractor is the most severely injured patient regardless of prognosis.

- ⚠️ **The four community phases map to two disaster phases, and it is not 2-and-2 by name.** **Heroic and honeymoon → RESPONSE. Disillusionment and reconstruction → RECOVERY.** The chapter says so explicitly, then discusses all four **inside the Response section** "for purposes of continuity," which is exactly the kind of layout that generates a mismatched item.

- ⚠️ **More disasters, fewer deaths.** The number of disasters worldwide **continues to grow** while **the number of lives lost has decreased.** An option stating both are rising is wrong.

- ⚠️ **Costs are higher in developed countries; burden is disproportionate in developing countries.** These sound contradictory but are both stated. **Dollar cost** is higher where labor, materials, and infrastructure are expensive. **Burden and inability to rebound** are worse where resources are limited.

- **Communicable disease epidemics are NATURAL disasters in Box 16.1.** Also lightning-induced forest fires. Conversely, **critical infrastructure failures** and **cyberattacks** are human-made. These four are the most invertible items in the box.

- **Store water every 3 months, food every 6 months, review the plan every 6 months, practice evacuating twice a year, test smoke detectors monthly.** Five different intervals in one box; easy to shuffle.

- **Two meeting places, one contact person, and the contact is OUT OF THE AREA.** The rationale — long distance may work when local lines are down — is what makes "out of the area" correct rather than "a trusted neighbor."

- **In a biological incident, health care professionals are the "first on the scene,"** not fire or EMS, because recognition may lag exposure by days to weeks. This inverts the usual response ladder and is a natural item.

- **The first goal of response is reestablishing SANITARY BARRIERS**, not treating casualties. Easy to miss because triage dominates the surrounding text.

- **Refer media to the PIO.** The Check Your Practice box gives three options and the correct one is referral, not scheduling or declining.

- **Most shelters do not accept pets.** This recurs three times in the chapter (preparedness kit, QSEN box, shelter section), which usually signals an item.

- **OCR bleed:** the opening paragraph of the chapter (around char 1,700) is severely scrambled, splicing the COVID-19 sentence with a mass-shooting reference and the "DISASTERS" section header. The ESF list on p. 296 also renders **"energy"** twice. All content above was read from surrounding clean text.
