## §1 Culture, Race, and Ethnicity (p. 121–122)

**The three-way distinction — this is the most likely single item in the chapter:**

| Concept | Basis | Key discriminator |
|---|---|---|
| **Culture** | Beliefs, values, assumptions about life, **transmitted across generations** | **Learned and shared**; can change (slowly) |
| **Race** | **Biological variation based on physical markers from genetic ancestry** — skin color, features, hair texture | **Biological**, not learned |
| **Ethnicity** | **Shared feeling of peoplehood** — nationality, region, ancestry, language, beliefs, traditions | **Identity and membership**; reflects cultural membership |

> ⚠️ **"Individuals may be of the same race but of different cultures."** The chapter's example: **African Americans**, who may have been born in or trace heritage to **Africa, the Caribbean, North America, or elsewhere**, are **a heterogeneous group and should not be viewed as culturally and racially homogeneous.**

**Three ways culture is transmitted:**
1. **Vertical** — **parents** are the primary sources
2. **Horizontal** — **people within the same generation** pass on information
3. **Oblique** — **between generations of people who are NOT related**: religious, social, and educational institutions, and peers

**Explicit vs. implicit behaviors:**

| Explicit (observable) | Implicit (less visible) |
|---|---|
| Language, interpersonal distance, kissing in public | **How individuals perceive health and illness**, body language, differences in language expressions, use of titles |

The chapter's example of implicit culture: a sign reading **"No smoking is permitted"** represents a culture that **values directness**; **"Thank you for not smoking"** represents one that **values indirectness.**

**Biracial classification:** ⚠️ **Before 1989, biracial babies who had one White parent were assigned the race of the non-White parent.** Currently the US Census Bureau **allows people to choose more than one race.**

**Population figures (July 1, 2019, US Census):** White alone, not Hispanic or Latino **60.4%**; Black or African American **13.4%**; Asian alone **5.9%**; American Indian and Alaska Native **1.3%**; Native Hawaiian and other Pacific Islander alone **0.2%**.

## §2 The Six Cultural Phenomena (Giger) — p. 122–124

These six recur through the chapter and map directly onto assessment. Expect a matching item.

### 1. Communication (p. 122)

**Effective cross-cultural communication is a CORE COMPETENCY for public health professionals** and is **the fourth domain** of the Quad Council C/PHN Competencies.

- **Verbal communication** — words used to express ideas and feelings. Variations: pronunciation, word meaning, voice quality, use of humor, speed of talking. Example: **"boot"** means footwear in the US and the trunk of a car in the UK.
- **Nonverbal communication** — body language or gestures. Includes **eye contact, gestures, body posture, facial expressions, touch, and silence.**

> **The classic scenario (memorize this one):** A nurse gave instructions to Asian American patients about taking antituberculin drugs. The patients **smilingly responded "yes, yes."** The nurse interpreted this as understanding and acceptance. **A week later the medications had not been taken.** The nurse understood that **acceptance by and avoidance of confrontation or disagreement with those in authority are important behaviors in the Asian American culture**, and adjusted: she **repeated the instructions, gave the patients an opportunity to raise questions and concerns, and had them REPEAT the instructions back.** She also **discussed the cultural meaning and treatment of tuberculosis.**

**"It is important to respect all information that a patient shares with you, EVEN WHEN the information is in conflict with your own value system."**

### 2. Space (p. 123)

**Personal space** = the **physical area individuals need between themselves and others to feel comfortable.** When violated, the patient may become uncomfortable.

- **Most cultural groups have spatial preferences.** One community may be comfortable with only a **9-inch distance between faces**, whereas another finds that distance **threatening and overly aggressive.**
- **You may offend the patient by placing yourself at a distance when his or her culture values close proximity, and vice versa.**
- COVID-19 emphasized this issue: social distancing at **at least 6 feet.**

### 3. Social organization (p. 123)

**The way a cultural group structures itself around the family to carry out role functions.**

- **In some cultures, family may include people who are NOT actually related to one another.**
- Find out **who is considered to be in the family, who the key decision makers are, and if the needs of the family supersede those of an individual.**
- **Some Hispanic and Asian cultures place the needs of the family above those of the individual.**
- **In the American Indian/Alaskan Native family, members honor and respect their elders.**
- ⚠️ **The nurse's balancing act:** "Nurses should **advocate for the individual** so that when families make decisions, the individual's needs are also considered. **However, members of the family may need to be included in the decision making.**"

### 4. Time perception (p. 123)

Cultures are **future, past, or present oriented.**

| Orientation | Description | Nursing implication |
|---|---|---|
| **Future** | Historically **American middle-class culture**; willing to **delay immediate gratification** until future goals are accomplished. Beginning to change. | Standard health-promotion framing works |
| **Present** | Greater value on **quality of life**; present time more important than future. **"The future is unknown, but the present is known."** | ⚠️ **Focus on the IMMEDIATE BENEFITS rather than emphasizing future outcomes** |
| **Past** | Example given: **the Vietnamese culture.** Focus on **wishes and memories of ancestors**, looking to them for direction in current situations. **Time is viewed as more flexible.** | Clarify perceptions to avoid misunderstanding |

> ⚠️ **"Nurses socialized in the Western culture may view time as money and equate punctuality with goodness and being responsible."** And: **"It is NOT realistic to expect patients to change their behavior and adopt the nurse's schedule."**

### 5. Environmental control (p. 123–124)

**The relationships between humans and nature.** Three worldviews:

| Worldview | Groups named | Health behavior consequence |
|---|---|---|
| **Mastery over nature** | (implied Western/biomedical) | Most likely to engage in prevention |
| **Dominated by nature** | **African Americans and Hispanics or Latinos** | Believe they have **little or no control over what happens to them**. May not adhere to a cancer protocol because **nothing will change the outcome; it is their destiny.** **LESS likely to engage in illness prevention activities.** |
| **Harmony with nature** | **African Americans, Asian Americans, and Native Americans/Alaskan Natives** | Perceive illness such as cancer as **disharmony with other forces**; medicine can **relieve symptoms but cannot cure the disease.** Seek treatment from the **mind, body, and spirit connection** because **healing comes from within.** Likely to look to **naturalistic solutions: herbs, acupuncture, and hot and cold treatments.** |

**Some patients may view their illness as punishment for misdeeds** and may have difficulty accepting care from nurses who do not share their belief.

### 6. Biological variations (p. 124)

**Physical, biological, and physiological differences that distinguish one racial group from another.** Occur in **growth and development, skin color, enzymatic differences, and susceptibility to disease.**

Other common variations: **eye shape, hair texture, adipose tissue deposits, shape of earlobes, thickness of lips, body configuration.**

**Specific facts:**
- **Lactose intolerance is much more common in African Blacks and African Americans** than in the general population.
- **Western-born neonates are slightly heavier at birth** than those born in non-Western cultures.
- **Mongolian spots** are **bluish discolorations** sometimes present on the skin of **African American, Asian American, Hispanic or Latino, and Native American/Alaskan Native babies.** ⚠️ **"These discolorations may be mistaken for BRUISES."**

> **The Mongolian spot scenario (an obvious item):** A school nurse observed a bluish discoloration on the thigh of a child with brown skin, **mistook it for a bruise, and reported it to the state child protective agency.** When the mother arrived, **she was accused of child abuse and had to disprove the allegation before her child could be released into her care.** This is the chapter's illustration of the harm of unrecognized biological variation.

### Nutrition (p. 124)

**"For many cultures, the preparation and eating of food are social activities"** — groups come together to celebrate life events and family rituals with food as the focus.

- **Efforts to understand dietary patterns should go BEYOND relying on membership in a defined group.**
- Knowing nutrition practices makes it possible to develop treatment regimens **that will not conflict with cultural food practices.**
- **Buddhists** — many are **vegetarian**. **Five Moral Precepts:** harming living things, stealing, engaging in sexual misconduct, lying, or consuming intoxicants (alcohol, tobacco, mind-altering drugs). **Eating meat conflicts with both the first and the fifth** (meat is seen as an intoxicant).
- **Muslims avoid pork and foods cooked with alcohol.**

**Box 7.1 — Assessment of Dietary Practices and Food Consumption Patterns:**
- What is the **social significance of food** in the family?
- What foods are most often bought for family consumption?
- **What foods are prohibited** for the family?
- Does **religion** play a significant role in food selection?
- **Who prepared the food? How is it prepared?**
- **How much** food is eaten? **When** is it eaten? **With whom?**
- What kind of restaurants does the family frequent?
- **Has the family adopted foods of other cultures?**
- What are the family's favorite recipes?

### Levels of Prevention box — Related to Cultural Differences (Hypertension, Stroke, Heart Disease)

| Level | Intervention as printed |
|---|---|
| **Primary** | **Provide health teaching about a balanced diet and exercise.** |
| **Secondary** | **Teach patients and/or family to monitor blood pressure.** Teach about diet, keeping in mind cultural preferences. **Talk about health beliefs and cultural implications, such as the use of alternative therapies; make sure alternative therapies are compatible with any medications that may be prescribed.** |
| **Tertiary** | **If blood pressure cannot be controlled by diet, refer to a physician or nurse practitioner for medication**; advise engagement in a **cardiac program** that will oversee diet and exercise. |

## §3 Immigrant Issues and the Four Types of Foreign-Born (p. 125–128)

**Scope:** As of 2018, **44.7 million immigrants** live in the United States — **1 in 7 US residents is foreign born.** Growth between 2017 and 2018 was **less than 0.5%**, the lowest annual increase since 2010.

**Origins (2018):** **Mexicans 25%**, followed by Indians, Chinese, Filipinos (each about **5%**); then El Salvador, Vietnam, Cuba, Dominican Republic (each **3%**); Korea and Guatemala (each **2%**).

**Demographics:**
- **Median age of the US immigrant population: 45.2 years** vs. **36.3 years** for native-born. (Many children of immigrants are born in the US, contributing to the younger native-born age.)
- **78%** of the US population ages 5+ reported speaking **English only** at home. After English, the most common languages are **Spanish**, then Chinese (Mandarin and Cantonese), Tagalog and Vietnamese, Arabic, French (including Cajun), and Korean.
- **47% of immigrants ages 5+ were Limited English Proficient (LEP).**
- **32% of immigrants ages 25+ had a bachelor's or higher degree**; **80% of Indian immigrant adults** did.
- **States with the largest number of immigrants, in order: California, Texas, Florida, New York, New Jersey.**
- **15% of immigrants had incomes below the official poverty line** vs. **13%** of the US-born population.

**Insurance (Artiga, Garfield, and Damico, 2020) — the gradient matters:**

| Group | Uninsured rate |
|---|---|
| **Citizens** (nonelderly) | **less than 1 in 10 (9%)** |
| **Lawfully present immigrants** | **23%** |
| **Undocumented immigrants** | **more than 4 in 10 (45%)** |

**Approximately one-third of immigrants are insured. Noncitizens are more likely to be uninsured than citizens.** **Citizen children with at least one noncitizen parent were significantly more likely to be uninsured** than children with citizen parents.

**Place of origin vs. nationality:** *Place of origin* is **distinguished from** *nationality*, which refers to **the place where the individual has or had citizenship.**

### The four categories of foreign-born

| # | Category | Definition | Benefit eligibility |
|---|---|---|---|
| **1** | **Documented immigrants** (lawful permanent residents) | **Not citizens** but legally allowed to live and work in the US, usually because they **fulfill labor demands or have family ties.** In 2016, **49% had naturalized** and become US citizens. | Usually a **5-year waiting period** living in the US after receiving "qualified" immigration status before eligible for **Medicaid and CHIP** |
| **2** | **Refugees and persons seeking asylum** | A person **outside the country of his or her nationality who is unable or unwilling to return because of persecution or a well-founded fear of persecution** based on **race, religion, nationality, membership in a particular social group, or political opinion.** **The Refugee Act of 1980** provided a uniform admission procedure. **The president sets the quota** — **30,000 admitted in 2019.** Referred primarily by the **UN High Commissioner for Refugees.** | ⚠️ **IMMEDIATELY eligible** for benefits described for legal immigrants: **TANF, SSI, and Medicaid** |
| **3** | **Non-immigrants** | Admitted for a **limited duration and specified purpose**; **permanent residence OUTSIDE the US.** Includes **students, tourists, temporary workers, business executives, career diplomats, their spouses and children, artists, entertainers, reporters, and those wishing to receive medical treatment.** | — |
| **4** | **Undocumented immigrants** | Crossed a border **illegally**, or **legal permission expired.** About **11% of the immigrant population.** | See below |

**Undocumented immigrant profile:** Mainly from **Mexico and Central America (71%)**, mostly **men (54%)**, **between 25 and 44 years of age (53%)**, **homeowners (~33%)**, **majority employed (64%) but uninsured (61%)**, and **slightly more than 50% live below the poverty level.**

**What undocumented immigrants ARE eligible to receive:**
- **Emergency medical services**
- **Immunizations**
- **Treatment for the symptoms of communicable diseases**
- **Access to school lunches**

**What they are NOT eligible for:** **Medicaid, CHIP, the Affordable Care Act (Obamacare), or purchasing coverage through the health insurance marketplace.**

**The tax paradox (a likely item):**
- **Immigrants who are authorized to work pay federal, state, and local taxes.**
- **Undocumented immigrants ALSO pay taxes but are NOT entitled to benefits like Medicare and Social Security.**
- **Both documented and undocumented immigrants contribute a large amount of money to the gross domestic product.**
- **The dilemma:** immigrants **typically pay FEDERAL taxes, yet the services they receive are paid for by STATES and localities**, and they are **not entitled to tax credits.**
- **Noncitizens are as likely as citizens to work**, but may be in jobs that **do not provide health coverage.**

**Health risks in immigrant populations:** Some groups are more at risk for **hepatitis B**, **tuberculosis**, **intestinal parasites**, and **visual, hearing, and dental problems.** **Many of these conditions are either preventable or treatable if managed correctly.**

**Barriers named:** **Fear of immigration enforcement** prevents many immigrants from seeking care in a timely manner. Also **living in low-income and segregated neighborhoods (ethnic enclaves)** and **working in low-wage occupations with exposure to toxic chemicals** and unsafe working situations.

> ⚠️ **The acculturation power shift (a testable family-dynamics point):** "Often **children and adolescents adjust to the new culture more easily than adults.** This can lead to a **shift in the balance of power between adults and children**, contributing to **family conflict and at times violence.** **Inability of elders to acculturate may play a large part in their lack of adherence to health care guidelines.**" Nurses should be alert for **warning signs of family stress and tension**, remembering that **older family members can help translate their culture**, beliefs, religious practices, dietary habits, support systems, and risk factors.

### Eight skills for working with immigrant populations

1. **Self-awareness** — recognize the values, beliefs, and practices that comprise **your own** culture
2. **Identify the patient's preferred or native language** — obtain an interpreter if needed
3. **Learn the health-seeking behaviors** of the patient and family; try using a **simple genogram**
4. **Get to know the community** where the patient lives
5. **Get to know traditional practices and remedies**; coordinate health education seminars **with** traditional healing courses "so you can work **with**, not against them"
6. **Learn how cultural subgroups explain common illnesses.** ⚠️ **Somatization** — in cultures where **body and mind are seen as one entity**, or where there is **high stigma associated with mental illness**, people **somatize**: **psychological distress is experienced as a physical illness**
7. **See things from the viewpoint of the patient, family, and community** and **accommodate rather than squash** the patient's view
8. **Conduct a cultural assessment** focusing on what is working, what is not working, and needed changes

### Interpreter vs. translator (a clean confusable pair)

| | Associated with |
|---|---|
| **Translator** | **WRITTEN documents** — medical records, legal documents |
| **Interpreter** | **VERBAL communication**, focused on **accurate expression of equivalent meanings rather than word-to-word equivalence** |

**"Health care institutions MUST provide patients with an interpreter."** The interpreter should have **knowledge of the patient's culture and medical terminology** and should be **trained, qualified, and hired** to ensure minimum standards. ⚠️ **"Using family members, friends, and staff who are not trained as health/medical interpreters can create errors in understanding and communicating, posing grave risks for the patient and liability to the health care institution."**

### HOW TO — Guidelines for Selecting and Using an Interpreter

1. **The educational level and socioeconomic status of the interpreter are important.** The interpreter should understand the community's interpretation of the disease and its health care practices around the disease.
2. **Gender and/or age may be of concern** — in some cultures women may prefer a female interpreter, men a male interpreter, and older patients a more mature interpreter. ⚠️ **AVOID USING CHILDREN AS INTERPRETERS, particularly when the patient is an adult.**
3. **Identify the patient's country of origin and language or dialect BEFORE selecting the interpreter.** Chinese patients speak different dialects depending on region of birth.
4. Evaluate the interpreter's **style, approach, and ability to develop a relationship of trust and respect.**
5. **The interpreter interprets EVERYTHING said by ALL the people in the interaction** and informs the nurse if content might be perceived as insensitive or harmful to the patient's dignity.
6. **The interpreter conveys the content AND the spirit of what is said, without omitting or adding.**
7. Make **phrase charts and picture cards** available.
8. **Observe the patient for nonverbal messages.** **If the patient's responses do not fit the question, check that the interpreter understood the question.**
9. **Increase accuracy by asking the interpreter to translate the patient's OWN WORDS**, and **ask the patient to repeat the information** that was communicated.
10. **The interpreter must maintain confidentiality** of all information and interactions.
11. **At the end of the interview, review the material with the patient AND the interpreter** to ensure nothing has been missed or misunderstood.

## §4 Developing Cultural Competence (p. 128–131)

### Orlandi's three stages (Table 7.1) — expect a matching item

| Dimension | Culturally **Incompetent** | Culturally **Sensitive** | Culturally **Competent** |
|---|---|---|---|
| **Cognitive** (thinking) | **Oblivious** | **Aware** | **Knowledgeable** |
| **Affective** (feeling) | **Apathetic** | **Sympathetic** | **Committed to change** |
| **Skills** (doing) | **Unskilled** | **Lacking some skills** | **Highly skilled** |

### Campinha-Bacote's five constructs — the model to know

| Construct | Definition | Memory hook |
|---|---|---|
| **1. Cultural awareness** | Self-examination of **one's OWN biases, stereotypes, and prejudices** | Looking **inward** |
| **2. Cultural knowledge** | Information about **organizational elements of diverse cultures**; the patient's **worldview from a native perspective** | Looking **outward** |
| **3. Cultural skill** | **Integrating awareness and knowledge** to obtain relevant cultural data | **Doing** |
| **4. Cultural encounter** | **Seeking cross-cultural interactions** to modify existing beliefs and **avoid stereotyping** | **Meeting** |
| **5. Cultural desire** | **Intrinsic motivation** — wanting to, not being directed to | **Wanting** |

**Cultural awareness — nurses who have developed it are able to:**
- **Learn about the cultural dimensions of patients**
- **Understand their own behavior and how it helps or hinders** the delivery of competent care to persons from cultures other than their own
- **Recognize that health is expressed differently across cultures** and that culture influences responses to health, illness, disease, and death

> **The breast self-examination scenario:** At a community outreach program, an African American woman **refused to give the return demonstration for breast self-examination**, saying **"My breasts are much larger than those on the model. Besides, the models are not like me. They are all White."** The nurse realized **she had made no reference to the influence of culture or race** in her talk. She **talked with the patient, asked for her recommendations, encouraged her to return**, and **coached her through the process** while noting that regardless of breast size, shape, and color, **the technique is the same.** She **subsequently purchased a model of an African American woman's breast** for future programs. **"Because this nurse was culturally aware, she neither became angry with herself or the patient, nor imposed her own values on the patient."**

**Cultural knowledge examples given:**
- **Middle Eastern women may not attend prenatal classes without encouragement and support** — attending is about **the future of the baby**, whereas the mother's main concern may be **the present.**
- **Nigerian women** might **begin prenatal classes but not continue**, because they **view birth as a natural process** and not one requiring a class.
- **"Nurses who lack cultural knowledge may develop feelings of inadequacy and helplessness."** It is **unrealistic to expect nurses to have knowledge of ALL cultures**, but they should know **how to obtain** knowledge of cultures they most frequently interact with.

**Cultural encounter — direct vs. indirect:**
- **Direct (face-to-face):** nurses learn **directly from their Puerto Rican American patients** about spicy food they avoid when breastfeeding
- **Indirect:** nurses **share that information with other nurses**

**Four reasons nurses must be culturally competent:**
1. **The nurse's culture often differs from that of the patient**, leading to different understandings of communication, behaviors, and plans for care
2. **Care that is not culturally competent may INCREASE the cost of health care and DECREASE the opportunity for positive outcomes.** ⚠️ **"Patients who do not feel understood may delay seeking care or may withhold key information."** The example: **if a person is afraid of disapproval, he may not tell the nurse that he is using BOTH folk medicine and Western medicine. The two may have cumulative or contradictory effects that could be dangerous.**
3. **To meet Healthy People 2030 objectives**
4. **Legal regulations and accreditation mandates** specify that culturally competent care must be provided so health disparities can be reduced and eliminated

**Healthy People 2030 objectives related to cultural issues:**
- **AHS-07:** Increase the proportion of persons with a **usual primary-care provider**
- **HC/HIT-01:** Increase the proportion of adults **whose health care provider checked their understanding**
- **HC/HIT-011:** Increase the proportion of adults **with limited English proficiency who say their providers explain things to them**

**Box 7.3 — Developing Cultural Competency: Points to Remember**
- **Culture is applicable to groups of Whites, such as Italians or Irish Americans, as well as to racial and ethnic minorities**
- Be sensitive to cultural implications during **each** interaction
- **Ask questions** to stimulate learning about how patients identify and express their cultural background
- **Much diversity exists WITHIN groups**; assess **both cultural group patterns AND individual variations** to avoid stereotyping
- When misunderstandings arise, **acknowledge the problem and take responsibility for your own errors**
- Be knowledgeable about **your own** cultural heritage, biases, beliefs, values, and practices
- **Avoid making assumptions about nonverbal cues** when interacting with unfamiliar cultures
- **Use a variety of sources, including patients**, to develop cultural knowledge
- **Developing cultural competence is an ongoing journey and an evolving process**

**Ten AAN/Transcultural Nursing Society guidelines** (formerly called standards), based on **social justice and human rights**: (1) knowledge of cultures, (2) education and training in culturally competent care, (3) critical reflection, (4) cross-cultural communication, (5) culturally competent practice, (6) cultural competence in health care systems and organizations, (7) patient advocacy and empowerment, (8) multicultural workforce, (9) cross-cultural leadership, (10) evidence-based practice and research.

## §5 Culturally Competent Nursing Interventions (p. 131) — the four action modes

**Leininger's three modes of action, based on NEGOTIATION between the patient and nurse**, plus **cultural brokering**:

| Mode | Trigger condition | Definition | Textbook example |
|---|---|---|---|
| **Cultural preservation** | Practice is **scientifically supported** | Nurse **supports and facilitates use of the practice ALONGSIDE biomedical care** | **Acupressure and acupuncture** — increasingly accepted by Western practitioners as legitimate |
| **Cultural accommodation** | Practice is **NOT HARMFUL** | Nurse **supports and facilitates** the patient's use of the practice | The **amniotic sac** request; **low-sodium soy sauce** substituted for high-sodium for older Chinese American patients with hypertension; **broiled and boiled instead of fried foods** |
| **Cultural repatterning** | Practice **IS HARMFUL** | Nurse works with patients to **reorder, change, or modify** the practice | The **Haitian herbal tea** that raised blood pressure; **baked tortillas and salsa** instead of fried tortillas, sour cream, and regular cheese for Hispanic teens |
| **Cultural brokering** | Conflict between the two care cultures | **Advocating, mediating, negotiating, and intervening BETWEEN the patient's culture and the biomedical culture on behalf of patients** | Teaching **migrant workers** about prevention, health maintenance, environmental sanitation and pesticides, and nutrition, because **it may be the only opportunity the nurse will have**; contacting **migrant health services for follow-up or referral** |

> **The three-way discriminator to memorize: PRESERVATION = scientifically supported. ACCOMMODATION = not harmful. REPATTERNING = harmful.**

**The amniotic sac example (accommodation) in detail:** Ms. Sanchez asked the delivery room nurse **not to discard a piece of the amniotic sac** present on her grandbaby's face and to **give it to her instead.** The grandmother believed **being born with a piece of the amniotic sac on the face was a visible sign that something special was going to happen** in the person's life; after drying it she would **keep it in a safe place** and **spend extra time protecting the baby from harm.** **Although the nurse did not know about this practice, she gave the grandmother the piece of the sac as requested.**

**The Haitian herbal tea example (repatterning) in detail:** A nurse giving prenatal instructions to pregnant Haitian women discovered many were **visiting an herbalist to obtain teas that would help them have a "strong baby."** The nurse **asked for the names of the herbs** and **scheduled a conference with the pharmacist.** She found **one of the herbs contributed to high blood pressure**, a problem many of the women were experiencing, **explained why they should not drink that tea**, and **enlisted the aid of the herbalist** because she understood the importance of supernatural causes of illness in Haitian culture.

**Islamic faith and care (p. 131):** The **Five Pillars of Islam** define the duties each Muslim should practice. **The second pillar, Salat**, says a Muslim **must pray five times a day while facing Mecca** — **an easterly direction in the United States** — in **a kneeling position on a prayer mat or carpet.** **It is important for the agency and health care professionals to make it possible for these patients to pray at the appointed times.** The Qur'an also dictates health care choices related to **contraception and birth, sanitary practices, dietary practices, and medical care concerns.**

## §6 Inhibitors to Developing Cultural Competence (p. 131–132)

**What causes the failure:** nurses may fail to provide culturally competent care if they **do not understand transcultural nursing**, if **supervisors are pressuring them to increase productivity by increasing caseloads**, or if they are **pressured by colleagues who are not knowledgeable about other cultures** and who are critical or offended when others use these concepts.

### The seven inhibitor terms — this is the blueprint's core list

| Term | Definition | The distinguishing feature |
|---|---|---|
| **Stereotyping** | Attributing certain beliefs and behaviors about a group to an individual **without giving adequate attention to individual differences** | **Applies a group trait to an individual** |
| **Prejudice** | A **deeply held reaction, often negative,** about another group or person, **with no regard for the worth of the person as an individual** | **A feeling/reaction, individual level** |
| **Racism** | **A form of prejudice** — the belief that persons born into a particular group are **inferior in intelligence, morals, beauty, or self-worth**; individuals may be **denied opportunities** because of race | **Inferiority + denial of opportunity** |
| **Ethnocentrism** | **A type of cultural prejudice at the POPULATION level** — one's own group **determines the standards for behavior by which all other groups are to be judged** | **My group is the yardstick** |
| **Cultural imposition** | **Ethnocentrism PLUS the ACT of imposing one's values on others** | **Belief + action** |
| **Cultural conflict** | A **perceived threat** arising from **a misunderstanding of expectations between patients and nurses** when either group is unaware of cultural differences | **Two-way misunderstanding; unavoidable** |
| **Cultural blindness** | **Ignoring all differences among cultures**, acting as though they do not exist, **treating all people the same** | **Contrasts with ethnocentrism**; "I treat everyone the same" |

**Cultural shock** — the feeling of **helplessness, discomfort, and disorientation** experienced by an individual attempting to understand or adapt to another cultural group that differs in practices, values, and beliefs. **Results from the anxiety caused by losing familiar sights, sounds, and behaviors.**

**Cultural relativism** — named as **the GOAL**: recognizing that patients have different approaches to health, and that **each culture should be judged on its own merit and not on the nurse's personal beliefs.**

**The three forms of racism (p. 132):**

| Form | Basis |
|---|---|
| **Individual** | Because of **characteristics of the group** the person is a member of — **skin color, hair texture, or facial features** |
| **Institutional** | **Discriminatory policies, priorities, and resource allocation** pertaining to certain groups |
| **Cultural** | A culture is **viewed in derogatory or stereotypical ways** because of, for example, **how a group dresses or the language the group uses** |

> **"Being aware of patients' cultural beliefs and knowing about other cultures may help nurses be less judgmental, more accepting of cultural differences, and less likely to engage in the behaviors just listed that inhibit cultural competence."**

## §7 Cultural Nursing Assessment (p. 132–133)

**Definition:** A **systematic way to identify the beliefs, values, meanings, and behaviors of people** while considering their **history, life experiences, and the social and physical environments** in which they live.

**Six skills that help the nurse be nonjudgmental:** **listening, explaining, acknowledging, recommending, understanding, and negotiating.**

**The negotiation sequence:**
1. **Nurses LISTEN to patients' perceptions of their problems**
2. **Nurses EXPLAIN to patients the nurses' perceptions of the problems**
3. **Both ACKNOWLEDGE and DISCUSS similarities and differences** between the two perceptions
4. **Develop suggestions and RECOMMENDATIONS for managing problems**
5. **NEGOTIATE with patients on nursing care actions**

### Brief vs. in-depth assessment

**During an INITIAL contact, perform a GENERAL cultural assessment** to obtain an overview. Ask about:
- **Ethnic background, language, education, religious affiliation, dietary practices, family relationships, hospital experiences, occupation and socioeconomic status, cultural beliefs**
- **Distinctive features, perceptions of the health issue, causation, treatment, anticipated results, and the impact the issue might have on the patient**

**Purpose:** helps nurses understand patients **from the patients' point of view**, recognize their uniqueness, and **thus avoid stereotyping.**

> ⚠️ **"Data for an IN-DEPTH cultural assessment should be gathered OVER A PERIOD OF TIME and NOT restricted to the first encounter with the patient."** This gives both patient and nurse time to get to know each other and helps the patient see the nurse in a **helping relationship.** This is a likely correct answer for "when should the nurse complete the cultural assessment?"

### The in-depth assessment — two phases

**Phase 1: Data collection (three steps)**
1. The nurse **collects self-identifying data** similar to those in the brief assessment
2. The nurse **raises a variety of questions** seeking information on the patient's **perception of what brings them to the health care system, the illness, and previous and anticipated treatments**
3. **After the nursing diagnosis is made**, the nurse **identifies cultural factors that may influence the effectiveness of nursing care actions**

**Phase 2: Organization**
Data related to the **patient's and family's views on optimal treatment choices** are examined, and **areas of difference between the patient's cultural needs and the goals of Western medicine are identified.**

### Suggestions when eliciting cultural information

> **"The KEY to a successful cultural assessment lies in nurses being aware of THEIR OWN culture."**

- **Be sensitive to cues in the environment**; be in tune with **verbal and nonverbal communications before taking action**
- **Know about the resources in the community** — schools, churches, clubs and other groups, hospitals, tribal councils, restaurants, taverns, bars
- **Know the specific areas to focus on BEFORE beginning** the assessment
- **Select a strategy** — in-depth interviews, informal conversations, observations of everyday activities or specific events, survey research, case-method approach
- **Identify a confidante who will help "bridge the gap"** between cultures. ⚠️ **Be aware that in some cultures the woman's husband or a close male family friend may be the person from whom the nurse may need to obtain the cultural information**
- **Know the appropriate questions to ask without offending the patient**
- **Interview other nurses or health care professionals** who have worked with the specific individual, family, or community
- **Use a trained interpreter** if the patient has limited English proficiency
- **Talk with formal AND informal community leaders**
- **Be aware that all information has both subjective and objective aspects; verify and cross-check before acting on it**
- **Avoid the pitfalls that may occur when making premature generalizations**
- **Be sincere, open, and honest with yourself and the patient**

**Resource named:** the **National Center for Cultural Competence (NCCC) at Georgetown University**, https://nccc.georgetown.edu.

**Nine questions for building culturally competent ORGANIZATIONS (Axner, 2015):** Who lives in the community right now? What kinds of diversity exist? What kinds of relationships are established between cultural groups? Are the different cultural groups well organized? What struggles exist between cultures? What struggles exist within cultural groups? Are these struggles openly recognized and talked about? Are there efforts to build alliances and coalitions between groups? What issues do different cultural groups have in common?

**Organizational actions named:** ensure your facility's **location is accessible and respectful of difference**; **collect resource materials** on culturally diverse groups for staff; **build a network of natural helpers, community "informants," and other "experts."** Another tool: the **Andrews/Boyle Transcultural Interprofessional Practice (TIP) Model (2019).**

## Source conflicts and traps

- ⚠️ **Preservation vs. accommodation vs. repatterning turns entirely on the practice's harm status.** **Scientifically supported → preserve. Not harmful → accommodate. Harmful → repattern.** The distractors will describe a practice and ask which mode applies; read for whether the text says the practice is supported, neutral, or dangerous. Note that acupuncture is used as the **preservation** example specifically because it is described as **"accepted by increasing numbers of Western practitioners as a legitimate method."**

- ⚠️ **Cultural blindness is the trap disguised as virtue.** "I treat all my patients exactly the same regardless of background" **sounds** like fairness and is explicitly named as **cultural blindness**. If an answer option has a nurse saying this, it is wrong.

- ⚠️ **Ethnocentrism vs. cultural imposition.** Ethnocentrism is the **belief**; cultural imposition is **the belief PLUS the act of imposing.** If the stem describes the nurse *thinking* her way is best, that is ethnocentrism; if she *acts on it* by overriding the patient, that is cultural imposition.

- ⚠️ **Stereotyping vs. prejudice.** Stereotyping is applying a **group generalization to an individual** (often not overtly hostile — "all Asian people are hardworking" is the textbook's own example of a *positive-sounding* stereotype). Prejudice is a **deeply held, often negative reaction.** A favorable generalization is still stereotyping.

- ⚠️ **The blueprint says "transitional medicine."** The textbook has **no such term.** This is almost certainly a typo for **traditional medicine**, which the chapter discusses at length (traditional healing practices, folk medicine, the traditional arena of care). Do not go looking for a "transitional medicine" definition; it does not exist in this chapter or in Ch. 24's folk medicine section.

- ⚠️ **"Folk healing" is named in the blueprint but the formal folk-medicine definition is in Chapter 24, not Chapter 7.** Ch. 7 covers folk practices in passing (the "popular arena," the somatization discussion, the warning that **folk practices are NOT restricted to lower socioeconomic classes** — "health professionals, such as nurses and physicians, may also use folk systems"). The **NCFH definition of folk medicine** and the **four folk illnesses (mal de ojo, susto, empacho, caída de mollera)** live in **Ch. 24, p. 434.** Cross-reference when drilling.

- **Refugees are the only foreign-born category with IMMEDIATE benefit eligibility.** Documented immigrants face a **5-year wait**; undocumented immigrants get only emergency care, immunizations, communicable disease symptom treatment, and school lunches. This three-way contrast is the most testable part of the immigration section.

- **Mongolian spots are not bruises.** The chapter builds an entire scenario around a nurse making this error and triggering a child-abuse investigation. Given that Ch. 27 also covers child abuse indicators heavily, an item could pit these against each other.

- **Time orientation drives the teaching approach, not the content.** For **present-oriented** patients, emphasize **immediate benefits**, not future outcomes. The trap answer will be "explain the long-term consequences of nonadherence."

- **Do not use children as interpreters**, particularly when the patient is an adult. Also do not use family members, friends, or untrained staff — the chapter names **grave risks for the patient and liability to the institution.**

- **Cultural desire cannot be taught in a classroom.** If a stem asks which of Campinha-Bacote's constructs is developed through education, cultural **desire** is the wrong answer; knowledge and skill are taught, encounters are sought, desire is intrinsic.

- **Nodding is not understanding.** The antituberculin scenario here is the same pattern as **simpatía** in Ch. 24. The correct action in both chapters is **have the patient repeat the instructions back.**

- **OCR bleed:** the key terms block on p. 119 renders out of alphabetical order and with a stray semicolon (`stereotyping;`). All 30 terms are captured above. The Box 7.2 "Early Cultural Awareness" questions are severely garbled in the raw pull (character-level interleaving) and are not reproduced here; they are reflective prompts rather than testable content.
