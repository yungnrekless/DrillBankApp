## §1 Palliative care vs. hospice (p. 534–537) — the single most testable distinction

- **Palliative care = the broad umbrella.** Occurs over a **longer period**, offers a wider range of services, and is **not limited to the days or weeks before death.** It can run alongside curative treatment.
- **Hospice = a subset of palliative care with a short timeframe.** A **formalized, funded program.**
- **Hospice eligibility:** the physician must indicate the patient has **6 months or fewer to live**, and the patient acknowledges a **terminal prognosis** and selects care that is **comfortable, not life-extending.**
- **Hospice often begins late** in the disease process; **barriers and stigmas** persist because some patients, families, and physicians equate hospice with hopelessness and giving up. Many who resist hospice will accept palliative care.
- **The goal of the hospice movement is to humanize the end-of-life experience.**

Mnemonic for the relationship: **palliative is the big circle, hospice is the small circle inside it near the end.** Palliative = comfort care anytime; hospice = comfort care with a 6-month prognosis and a decision to forgo life-extending treatment.

## §2 Home health care (p. 535)

- **Home health care** is a broad concept and one of the **fastest-growing** service industries.
- **Family caregivers** now provide care once delivered in hospitals; it can be confusing, stressful, and burdensome, and many are underprepared. Patients may need regular monitoring or 24-hour care; caregivers often need help identifying support services while protecting their own health.
- **Partnership with the patient and family is essential**; they must be involved in decisions for home-based services to be efficient.
- **Standard precautions** in the home mean **all blood and body fluids are treated as potentially infectious.** Infection prevention is a priority in home health and hospice.

## §3 The two documentation/terminology systems (p. 540–542) — know both

**OASIS (Outcome and Assessment Information Set):**
- A complex and important component of home health practice.
- Completed by the nurse or therapist (physical, occupational, or speech-language) while providing home care.
- The basis for **outcome measurement** and quality improvement (feeds OBQI).

**Omaha System:**
- Developed to **operationalize the nursing process**; practical, easily understood, computer-compatible, quantifiable.
- The **only ANA-recognized terminology developed inductively by and for nurses who practice in the community.**
- Primarily used by home health agencies.
- **Three components (memorize):**
  1. **Problem Classification Scheme**
  2. **Intervention Scheme**
  3. **Problem Rating Scale for Outcomes**
- The three components are designed to be **used together**: comprehensive, hierarchical, multidimensional, and computer compatible.

## §3b The initial home visit (Box 30.3, p. 541) — blueprint topic

**The governing principle:** when a nurse enters a patient's home, **he or she is a guest** and must recognize that **the services offered can be accepted or rejected.** The nurse **needs to earn the trust of the family and establish a partnership** with the patient and family.

> **"The first visit sets the stage for success or failure. The initial assessment of the client, the support system, and the environment is critical."**

**BOX 30.3 — Guidelines for Practice in a Home Visit** (and other noninstitutional settings), in order:

1. **Review patient data** — the referral from intake, hospital discharge summary, orders, and plan of care, to become familiar with the history, **purpose of the visit, and expectations.**
2. **Contact the patient or family** by phone using the referral information to **obtain agreement for the visit**, schedule the day and time, and discuss the address.
3. **Gather** agency and Medicare forms, a **nursing bag**, cell phone or pager, portable computing device, teaching materials, and supplies; understand the location; **follow agency policy when checking out for the visit.**
4. **Observe the neighborhood for resources and safety** when approaching the home.
5. **Greet the patient/family** to begin a positive, appropriate professional relationship; involve them as much as possible during the visit and postvisit activities.
6. **Obtain signatures** and complete needed forms.
7. **Provide care:** complete a **comprehensive assessment**, identify problems, provide interventions, evaluate outcomes. Interventions may include **teaching, guidance, and counseling; treatments and procedures; case management; and surveillance.**
8. **Discuss the plan of care** with the patient and family; modify as needed.
9. **Document** visit details in the home, preferably on a portable device and EHR.

**OASIS-C2** is used by interprofessional clinicians in **Medicare-certified** home health agencies at designated intervals; it is the outcome measurement tool **mandated by the CMS Conditions of Participation.**

## §4 Transitional care and collaboration (p. 539, 545)

- **Transitional care:** "a set of actions designed to ensure the coordination and continuity of health care as patients transfer between different locations and different levels of care." Nurse-Family Partnership (NFP) and transitional care are the two additional nurse-led models highlighted.
- **Interprofessional collaboration** is required by the nature of the practice; team disciplines (nurses, LPNs, chaplains, physicians, counselors, aides, pharmacists, volunteers) vary with the program, patient/family needs, location, and reimbursement.
- The **Triple Aim** model (Berwick, Nolan, Whittington, 2008) has long been the foundation of these community-based services: improve population health, improve the care experience, and reduce per-capita cost.

## §5 Levels of prevention (home health)

**LEVELS OF PREVENTION box — Related to Home Health:**
- **Primary:** the nurse administers seasonal or newer-strain flu vaccine, or provides case management so patients obtain vaccines at convenient locations.
- **Secondary:** the nurse monitors patients at home for **early signs of new health problems** to initiate prompt treatment (e.g., monitoring for medication side effects).
- **Tertiary:** the nurse provides instruction about **dietary modifications and insulin injections to patients with a new diabetes diagnosis**; patients and families implement the plan.

## Source conflicts and traps

- **Palliative is broad; hospice is the narrow subset.** The classic trap reverses them or claims palliative care requires a terminal prognosis. Palliative care is **not** time-limited and can accompany curative treatment; **hospice** is the one that requires the **6-months-or-fewer** prognosis and a decision to forgo life-extending care.
- **Hospice requires a physician-certified prognosis of 6 months or fewer.** Any option giving a different timeframe, or saying hospice is available to anyone regardless of prognosis, is the distractor.
- **OASIS vs. Omaha System.** OASIS is the outcome-and-assessment data set feeding quality improvement; the Omaha System is the ANA-recognized community-nursing **terminology** with three components (Problem Classification Scheme, Intervention Scheme, Problem Rating Scale for Outcomes). Attributing the "three components" to OASIS is a trap.
- **The Omaha System was developed inductively by and for community nurses** and is the only ANA-recognized such terminology. An option calling it a physician-developed or hospital-based system is wrong.
- **Standard precautions in the home = treat all blood and body fluids as potentially infectious**, the same principle as any setting.
- **Family caregivers are frequently underprepared**; a stem framing them as fully equipped by default misreads the chapter's emphasis on caregiver support and partnership.
- **Hospice often starts too late** because of stigma; the tested point is that late referral is a known barrier, not that hospice is meant to be brief.
- **The nurse is a guest in the home, and care can be refused.** The tested consequence is that the **first visit** is about **earning trust and building partnership**, not maximizing task completion. A stem where the nurse asserts control over the home, or proceeds without agreement, is the distractor. Remember the visit is **scheduled by phone in advance** — arriving unannounced is wrong.
- **The initial home assessment has three targets: the patient, the support system, and the environment.** An option naming only the patient's physical status is incomplete. **Safety and neighborhood resources are observed on approach**, before entering.
