## §1 The confusable pair: primary care vs. primary health care (highest yield)

| | **Primary care** | **Primary health care (PHC)** |
|---|---|---|
| Focus | **Individual** patient | **Population / community** |
| Meaning | First point of contact with the system for clinical care | Essential care universally accessible with **full community participation**, at affordable cost |
| Origin | Clinical delivery model | **Declaration of Alma-Ata, 1978 (WHO)** |
| Emphasis | Diagnosis and management of the individual | Prevention, equity, community-defined problems |

> **The trap:** the two terms are one word apart and constantly swapped in distractors. *Primary care* = the individual's **first contact** (a clinic visit). *Primary health care* = the **WHO/Alma-Ata population model** built on community participation and equity. If the stem mentions Alma-Ata, universal access, or community participation, the answer is **primary health care.**

## §2 The three system problems: cost, access, quality

Most industrialized countries share the same goal: **high-quality, affordable care.** The US system is persistently criticized on three fronts:

**Cost.**
- US health spending reached **$2.5 trillion in 2009** and is projected to reach **$5.7 trillion by 2026**, about **19.7% of GDP** — the highest share among industrialized nations.
- The **largest share of expenditures goes to acute care**, with physician services next; **public health receives the smallest share.**
- Other cost drivers: prescription drugs, technology, and care for chronic/degenerative diseases; the aging baby-boomer population swelling Medicare enrollment.

**Access.**
- The US is described as a **two-class system: private and public.** Those with insurance or ability to pay get superior care; the **working poor** (who earn too much for public funds but can't afford insurance) and undocumented immigrants get lower-quality care.
- Uninsured: **48 million (2012, pre-ACA) → 27.6 million (2016).**
- The uninsured receive **less preventive care, are diagnosed at more advanced stages, and receive less treatment.**
- **Federally funded community health centers** (>1300 nationwide) serve **medically underserved areas** (rural or urban), all ages/races/ethnicities, insured or not, using NPs, RNs, PAs, physicians, social workers, dentists.

**Quality.** Despite sophistication, the US lags other developed countries on outcomes relative to spending.

> **Blueprint cue — "health outcomes compared to other industrialized countries":** the US **spends the most** (highest % of GDP) but does **not** lead on outcomes; it is the only major industrialized country **without universal coverage**, producing the two-class access problem.

## §3 How the system is organized: federal → state → local

**Federal (USDHHS / HHS).** The agency most involved with US health and welfare. Administers **100+ programs**, maintains **10 regional offices**. Contains the office of the secretary and 11 agencies.

- **US Public Health Service (USPHS/PHS)** — a major HHS component; **eight agencies** including **CDC, FDA, HRSA, AHRQ, ATSDR, IHS, NIH, SAMHSA**.
- **Centers for Medicare & Medicaid Services (CMS)** — administers Medicare and Medicaid.
- **Department of Homeland Security (DHS)** — created 2003; runs the **CERT (Community Emergency Response Team)** program; goals: awareness, prevention, protection, response, recovery.

**State.** State health departments handle licensing, health statistics, communicable disease control, and coordinate local health. States hold **police power** to protect public health (ties to Ch 4).

**Local.** The local health department is where services actually reach the community; functions vary widely by locality.

## §4 Global health and the international comparison

- **WHO** — leads global health; issued the Declaration of Alma-Ata and the PHC model.
- **Millennium Development Goals (MDGs, 2000–2015):** 8 goals — eradicate poverty/hunger, universal primary education, gender equality, reduce child mortality, improve maternal health, combat HIV/malaria, environmental sustainability, global partnership.
- **Sustainable Development Goals (SDGs, 2015→):** 17 goals; **SDG 3 = Good Health and Well-Being.** Succeeded the MDGs.
- **ICN (International Council of Nurses)** — Leadership for Change program; nursing takes a leadership role in achieving health for all.
- **Health for All (HFA21)** — WHO strategic process for progressive improvement in population health.

> **Global immunity/travel note carried into later chapters:** increased world travel spreads viruses (Zika, chikungunya, COVID-19) across borders — this is the surveillance/emerging-disease link to Ch 11, 12, and 17.

## §5 Nursing's expanding role in primary care (blueprint: "nursing's role in health care reform")

Under the ACA's push toward primary care and away from hospital admissions, RNs increasingly: coordinate patient care, work across the interprofessional team, manage complex comorbidities, promote healthy lifestyles, reconcile medications and monitor polypharmacy, bridge community resources to people who need them, advocate for patients and communities, and push for policy change. **APNs (NPs, CNSs, CNMs)** are central to primary care teams, though **state nurse practice acts and medical practice acts** shape how fully they can fill the primary-care-physician shortage — which connects directly to the **scope-of-practice / Boards of Nursing** material in Ch 4.
