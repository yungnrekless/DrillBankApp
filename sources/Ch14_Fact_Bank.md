## §1 Health education across the three levels of prevention

Nurses are ideal to lead health promotion through education because they (1) **educate across all three levels of prevention** — primary, secondary, and tertiary — and (2) **work with individuals, families, groups, and communities.** The goal: help people attain optimal health, prevent problems, identify and treat problems early, and minimize disability.

> This is the tie-back to the prevention framework (Ch 1). Education *before* disease (teaching, promotion) = primary; education tied to *early detection* (screening instruction) = secondary; education for *managing existing disease / rehab* = tertiary.

## §2 The three domains of learning (highest yield)

| Domain | Verb | What it covers | Teaching example |
|---|---|---|---|
| **Cognitive** | **Thinking** | Memory, understanding, reasoning, problem solving | Teaching a newly diagnosed diabetic **what diabetes is** and the facts of self-care |
| **Affective** | **Feeling** | **Attitudes and values**; hardest to change | Helping someone **value** managing their condition; addressing feelings/beliefs |
| **Psychomotor** | **Acting** | **Motor skills** needing neuromuscular coordination | Teaching **self-injection**, dressing changes, glucose testing, crutch-walking |

**Psychomotor teaching sequence:** first **demonstrate** the skill (picture, model, video, live), then have the learner do a **return/repeat demonstration** to validate learning.

**Psychomotor learning requires three conditions:** the learner must have (1) the necessary **ability** (cognitive + physical), (2) a **sensory image** of how to do the skill, and (3) **opportunities to practice.**

> **The classic exam item:** match the activity to the domain. *Knows facts / explains* → **cognitive.** *Values / attitude / willingness* → **affective.** *Performs a hands-on skill* → **psychomotor.** Self-injection is the textbook psychomotor example.

## §3 Skills / characteristics of the effective educator

The basic instructional sequence (blueprint: "characteristics of effective educators"):
1. **Gain attention** and show the information is important/beneficial.
2. **State the objectives** of the instruction.
3. **Ask learners to recall prior knowledge** so new links to old.
4. **Present essential material** clearly, simply, organized, matched to the learners' strengths and limitations.
5. **Help learners apply** the information to their own lives.
6. **Have learners demonstrate** what they learned (so errors can be corrected).
7. **Provide feedback.**

Omitting steps → superficial, fragmented learning. Effective educators keep it **active** ("a lively format rather than passive encourages learning"), make the point **quickly** (short attention spans), use **teach-back**, and communicate in a **culturally responsive** way.

## §4 Barriers to learning (explicit blueprint item)

**Two most important learner-related barriers:** **low literacy** and **lack of motivation.**

**Learner-related barriers.**
- **Low literacy / low health literacy** — patients may be **embarrassed**, may **pretend to understand**, and **may not ask questions.** Assess literacy first; use plain language (Plain Writing Act of 2010) and teach-back.
- **Lack of motivation** — the learner doesn't yet see the need to change (use the HBM to plan around motivation).
- Groups more likely to have **low health literacy:** older adults (65+), those below the poverty line, medically underserved people, recent refugees/immigrants, and nonnative English speakers.

**Educator-related barriers** (with fixes): fear of public speaking (prepare, use icebreakers, practice), lack of credibility (prepare thoroughly, don't apologize), limited experience with the topic (use analogies/examples), difficulty with difficult learners (humor, small groups, redirect), not knowing how to gain participation (open-ended questions, small-group activities), poor timing (plan and rehearse), and equipment concerns (test it, have a backup).

> **Health literacy definition to memorize (IOM):** the capacity to **obtain, process, and understand** basic health information to make appropriate health decisions. In the NAAL, the **majority of adults had only intermediate literacy (53%)**; just 12% were proficient.

## §5 Motivation and the Health Belief Model (blueprint: "lack of motivation")

The **HBM** answers "what motivates a person to act?" — six components:
1. **Perceived susceptibility** — "Will something happen to me?"
2. **Perceived severity** — "If it happens, is it a big problem?"
3. **Perceived benefits** — "Will doing this really help?"
4. **Perceived barriers** — "Will there be obstacles (cost, unpleasantness)?"
5. **Cues to action** — "What would prompt me to act?"
6. **Self-efficacy** — "Can I really do this?"

Use the HBM when **learner motivation is a concern.** Origin story: 1950s free TB chest x-rays that people didn't use, prompting psychologists to study what motivates people to seek care.

## §6 Groups and levels of trust among participants (explicit blueprint item)

- **Group size:** **8–12** members works for groups focused on **individual health change**; **up to 25** can work when the focus is **community needs.** Large groups split into subgroups.
- **Cohesion** = the attraction holding members together; higher cohesion supports openness and trust.
- **Norms** guide behavior: **task norms** (getting the work done), **maintenance norms** (keeping relationships healthy), **reality norms** (shared view of what's real).
- **Trust develops over time** through cohesion and the handling of conflict. Members may express tension around **trust, closeness vs. separation, and dependence vs. independence.**
- **Conflict is normal and useful** — it signals that differing viewpoints must be considered. A group that **repeatedly avoids conflict becomes fragile** and unable to adapt. Effective facilitation respects diverse opinions and promotes their expression.

> **Test cue:** the "levels of trust among group participants" idea means trust is **built progressively** as the group develops cohesion and works through (rather than suppresses) conflict. Suppressing conflict damages the group; a small group (8–12) is optimal for the intimacy needed for individual behavior change.
