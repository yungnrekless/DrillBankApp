1. A woman with type 1 diabetes at 8 weeks of gestation reports frequent hypoglycemia, especially during sleep. How should this be interpreted?
  • AAs an error in her technique requiring retraining
  • ✓As the expected first-trimester pattern, in which maternal glucose is normally reduced and the insulin response enhanced, so the dose should be reduced by 10% to 25% at weeks 6 to 10
  • CAs early diabetic ketoacidosis
  • DAs a sign that she should stop insulin entirely
Rationale

In the first trimester "maternal blood glucose levels are normally reduced and the insulin response to glucose is enhanced," so "the insulin dose for the woman with well-controlled diabetes may have to be reduced to prevent hypoglycemia." Specifically: "in the first trimester, from weeks 6 to 10 of gestation, the insulin dose should be reduced by 10% to 25% to avoid hypoglycemia." Early hypoglycemia "occurs frequently, often during sleep."

Watch for

(A) treats a physiologic shift as a teaching failure, and it is the response that leaves the dose unchanged. Nausea, vomiting, and food cravings add dietary fluctuations on top of the hormonal change.

2. What happens to insulin requirements after the first trimester, and when do they peak?
  • ✓Insulin resistance begins as early as 14 to 16 weeks and continues to increase, with requirements peaking at 36 weeks and decreasing significantly after that
  • BRequirements continue to fall through the third trimester
  • CRequirements stay constant after 20 weeks
  • DRequirements peak at 24 weeks and then fall steadily
Rationale

"Because insulin requirements steadily increase after the first trimester, the insulin dose must be adjusted accordingly to prevent hyperglycemia. Insulin resistance begins as early as 14 to 16 weeks of gestation and continues to increase until it stabilizes during the last few weeks of pregnancy." And: "insulin requirements normally peak at 36 weeks of gestation and decrease significantly after that."

Watch for

(B) carries the first-trimester direction forward, which is the error that produces third-trimester hyperglycemia in a woman whose dose was never raised.

3. A woman with type 1 diabetes gave birth 6 hours ago and is eating a regular diet. What insulin dose should the nurse expect?
  • AHer full pregnancy dose, to prevent rebound hyperglycemia
  • ✓One-third to one-half of her most recent pregnancy dose, because the placental hormones that created the insulin resistance are gone
  • CDouble her prepregnancy dose
  • DNo insulin for the first week
Rationale

"During the first 24 hours postpartum, insulin requirements decrease substantially because the major sources of insulin resistance, the placental hormones, are greatly reduced," and "women with preexisting diabetes usually require only one-third to one-half of their most recent pregnancy insulin dose on the first postpartum day." A subcutaneous dose should be given at least 1 hour before discontinuing an IV insulin infusion.

Watch for

(A) is the clinically dangerous half of this curve, because continuing the pregnancy dose after the placenta is delivered produces severe hypoglycemia.

4. A breastfeeding woman with type 1 diabetes checks her glucose before a late-night feeding and it is 88 mg/dL. What should she do?
  • ATake her usual insulin dose and feed the baby
  • ✓Consume 15 g of carbohydrates without taking insulin, since insulin requirements decrease during lactation and hypoglycemia risk is highest after late-night feedings
  • CSkip the feeding and pump instead
  • DWait until the glucose falls below 70 mg/dL before acting
Rationale

"Insulin requirements in breastfeeding women decrease because of the carbohydrate used in human milk production," and they "are at increased risk for hypoglycemia after breastfeeding, particularly after late-night feeding sessions. Women should check their blood glucose level just before breastfeeding. If it is less than 100 mg/dL, they should consume 15 g of carbohydrates ... without taking insulin."

Watch for

(D) waits for the treatment threshold when this is a prevention threshold. The chapter also notes that frequent hypoglycemia can reduce milk production, and that the lactation dose must be recalculated at weaning.

6. How does the chapter define gestational diabetes mellitus, and what does the definition exclude?
  • ✓Carbohydrate intolerance that develops during pregnancy, identified during the second or third trimester, excluding women with preexisting type 1 or type 2 diabetes diagnosed during pregnancy
  • BAny elevated glucose value at any point in pregnancy
  • CDiabetes diagnosed at any time that resolves after birth
  • DInsulin resistance present before conception
Rationale

"Gestational diabetes mellitus (GDM) is defined as carbohydrate intolerance that develops during pregnancy," and "it also requires that carbohydrate intolerance be identified during the second or third trimester of pregnancy." The ADA definition "excludes women with preexisting diabetes (type 1 or type 2) that is diagnosed during pregnancy" — GDM is "simply diabetes diagnosed during pregnancy that is clearly not overt (preexisting) diabetes."

Watch for

(B) drops the timing clause, and the timing is what separates a woman whose fetus was exposed during organogenesis from one whose fetus was not.

7. A woman with no diabetes before pregnancy has two abnormal values on her oral glucose tolerance test, and her glucose is controlled by diet alone. Which White's class applies, and what would change it?
  • ✓Class A1; requiring insulin or an oral hypoglycemic medication would make her class A2
  • BClass A2; adding diet control would make her A1
  • CClass B, based on her age at onset
  • DClass F, if she develops proteinuria
Rationale

White's class A1 is a woman with "two or more abnormal values on OGTT, but fasting and postprandial glucose values are diet controlled," while class A2 is a woman "not known to have diabetes before pregnancy but requires either insulin or oral hypoglycemic medication for blood glucose control."

Watch for

(C) reaches into the pregestational classes. Classes B, C, and D grade by age at onset and duration of illness, while F, R, and T name which organ has failed — nephropathy, retinitis proliferans, and renal transplant. Pregestational women fall into classes B through T.

  • ADiabetic ketoacidosis
  • ✓Hypoglycemia, or insulin shock
  • CNormal pregnancy fatigue
  • DThyroid storm
Rationale

Table 29.2 gives hypoglycemia a rapid onset with irritability, hunger, sweating, nervousness, weakness, blurred vision, pallor, clammy skin, shallow respirations, and a rapid pulse, with urine negative for sugar and acetone and a blood glucose under 70 mg/dL.

(A) shares one symptom — headache appears in both columns — but nothing else. The cue that survives a blank moment is cold and clammy versus hot and dry: ketoacidosis brings flushed dry skin, rapid breathing, and a fruity acetone breath odor, with urine positive for sugar and acetone.

9. A pregnant woman with type 1 diabetes has a blood glucose of 240 mg/dL with nausea, vomiting, abdominal pain, and fruity breath. A colleague says the glucose is not high enough for ketoacidosis. How should the nurse respond?
  • AAgree, since DKA requires a glucose above 300 mg/dL
  • ✓Disagree: in pregnancy, DKA may occur with blood glucose levels barely exceeding 200 mg/dL, compared with 300 to 350 mg/dL in the nonpregnant state
  • CAgree, but recheck in 2 hours
  • DDisagree, because DKA is diagnosed by glucose alone
Rationale

"DKA may occur with blood glucose levels barely exceeding 200 mg/dL compared with 300 to 350 mg/dL in the nonpregnant state." It occurs most often in the second and third trimesters, "when the diabetogenic effect of pregnancy is greatest," and "DKA is a medical emergency ... Ketoacidosis occurring at any time during pregnancy can lead to intrauterine fetal death."

Watch for

(A) applies the nonpregnant threshold, which is precisely the delay this fact exists to prevent.

11. A pregnant woman's blood glucose is 62 mg/dL and she is alert. What does Table 29.2 direct?
  • ✓Consume 2 to 4 glucose tablets or gel, 8 to 16 g of carbohydrate, immediately, and recheck the blood glucose in 15 minutes
  • BGive 50% dextrose IV push
  • CAdminister 1 mg of glucagon intramuscularly
  • DGive a full meal and recheck in 1 hour
Rationale

"If blood glucose is <70 mg/dL, consume 2–4 glucose tablets or gel (8–16 g carbohydrate) immediately," then "recheck blood glucose level in 15 min." If still under 70, repeat, recheck again in 15 minutes, and if still low "notify health care provider immediately."

Watch for

(B) and (C) are the interventions for an unconscious woman, where oral glucose would be an aspiration risk. The parenteral options are 50% dextrose IV push, 5% to 10% dextrose IV drip, or 1 mg glucagon intramuscularly.

12. A woman with pregestational diabetes is carrying a fetus with an estimated weight of 4300 g. Why is the risk of shoulder dystocia greater than for another macrosomic infant of the same weight?
  • ✓Because infants of women with diabetes tend to have a disproportionate increase in shoulder, trunk, and chest size
  • BBecause the maternal pelvis is smaller in diabetes
  • CBecause diabetic pregnancies are always postterm
  • DThere is no difference in risk at the same weight
Rationale

"Infants born to women with diabetes tend to have a disproportionate increase in shoulder, trunk, and chest size. Because of this tendency, the risk for shoulder dystocia is greater in these infants than in other macrosomic infants." Macrosomia occurs in approximately 40% of pregestational diabetic pregnancies and up to 50% of pregnancies complicated by GDM.

Watch for

(D) treats weight as the whole story. The asymmetry between head and shoulders, not the number on the scale, is what makes the birth difficult.

maternal and fetal risks of diabetesthe macrosomic infant
13. What is the mechanism by which maternal hyperglycemia produces a macrosomic fetus?
  • AMaternal insulin crosses the placenta and acts as a growth hormone
  • ✓The fetal pancreas begins secreting insulin at 10 to 14 weeks and responds to maternal hyperglycemia with hyperinsulinism; insulin acts as a growth hormone, producing excess glycogen, protein, and adipose tissue
  • CExcess maternal glucose is stored directly as fetal fat without fetal insulin
  • DPlacental hormones directly stimulate fetal growth
Rationale

"The fetal pancreas begins to secrete insulin at 10 to 14 weeks of gestation. The fetus responds to maternal hyperglycemia by secreting large amounts of insulin (hyperinsulinism). Insulin acts as a growth hormone, causing the fetus to produce excess stores of glycogen, protein, and adipose tissue and leading to increased fetal size, or macrosomia."

Watch for

(A) has maternal insulin crossing the placenta, which it does not — the fetus makes its own, and that is also why the newborn becomes hypoglycemic when the maternal glucose supply stops at birth.

14. A woman diagnosed with gestational diabetes at 26 weeks asks whether her baby is at risk for birth defects. What does the chapter support?
  • ✓That no increase in birth defects has been found among infants of women who develop GDM after the first trimester, because the critical period of organ formation has already passed
  • BThat cardiac and neural tube defects are equally likely as in pregestational diabetes
  • CThat the risk is higher than in pregestational diabetes
  • DThat all diabetic pregnancies carry the same anomaly risk regardless of timing
Rationale

"No increase in the incidence of birth defects has been found among infants of women who develop GDM after the first trimester because the critical period of organ formation has already passed." In pregestational diabetes, by contrast, "hyperglycemia during the first trimester of pregnancy, when organs and organ systems are forming, is the main cause of diabetes-associated birth defects," affecting primarily the cardiovascular system and the CNS.

Watch for

(B) applies the pregestational risk to a woman whose hyperglycemia began after organogenesis. Both groups still face macrosomia and neonatal hypoglycemia, which come from later fetal hyperinsulinism.

15. Why does the chapter treat infection as more than a local problem in a pregnant woman with diabetes?
  • ✓Because infection causes increased insulin resistance and may result in ketoacidosis
  • BBecause antibiotics are contraindicated in diabetic pregnancy
  • CBecause infection lowers blood glucose dangerously
  • DBecause infection only affects the urinary tract in these women
Rationale

"Infections are more common and more serious in pregnant women with diabetes," because carbohydrate metabolism disorders alter resistance to infection — "the inflammatory response, leukocyte function, and vaginal pH are all affected." And: "Infection is serious because it causes increased insulin resistance and may result in ketoacidosis."

Watch for

(C) reverses the metabolic direction, which would make a nurse watch for the wrong complication entirely.

17. A woman with diabetes asks whether she should test her urine to guide her diet. What does the chapter direct, and what is the hemoglobin A1c caution?
  • ✓Dietary management must be based on blood, not urine, glucose levels; and iron deficiency anemia falsely increases the A1c level
  • BUrine glucose testing is the basis of dietary management; A1c is unaffected by anemia
  • CBoth are equally valid; A1c reflects 3 months of control in pregnancy
  • DNeither blood nor urine testing is needed if she follows her diet
Rationale

"Dietary management during diabetic pregnancy must be based on blood (not urine) glucose levels." The Nursing Alert states: "Iron deficiency anemia falsely increases the A1c level." Because red cells turn over more rapidly in pregnancy, the A1c reflects "only the previous 2 to 6 weeks," and levels under 6% to 6.5% in early pregnancy are associated with the lowest rates of adverse fetal outcomes.

Watch for

(C) carries the nonpregnant 3-month window into pregnancy, which would make a nurse over-read a single result.

19. A woman with diabetes plans to walk after dinner. Her glucose is 210 mg/dL and her urine is positive for ketones. What should the nurse advise?
  • AWalk as planned, since exercise will lower the glucose
  • ✓Avoid exercise, because hyperglycemia and ketosis can worsen with physical activity
  • CWalk after taking 15 to 30 g of carbohydrates
  • DWalk twice as long to clear the ketones
Rationale

"Women should avoid exercise if they have positive urine ketones or a blood glucose greater than 200 mg/dL because hyperglycemia and ketosis can worsen with physical activity." The other numeric rule runs the opposite way: "if the blood glucose is less than 100 mg/dL, women should consume 15 to 30 g of carbohydrates to prevent hypoglycemia."

Watch for

(A) applies ordinary diabetes teaching — activity lowers glucose — to the one situation where the chapter forbids it. (C) applies the low-glucose rule to a high-glucose reading.

  • BThirty minutes before meals, with an onset of 2 hours
  • CAt bedtime, with no peak
  • DTwice daily regardless of meals, with a peak at 6 to 12 hours
Rationale

22. A woman is drawing up NPH and rapid-acting insulin in one syringe. What is the correct sequence?
  • ✓Inject air into the NPH vial, inject air into the rapid-acting vial, withdraw the rapid-acting dose, then withdraw the NPH dose without adding more air to the NPH vial
  • BInject air into the rapid-acting vial first, then withdraw NPH first
  • CWithdraw NPH first, then rapid-acting
  • DDraw each into a separate syringe, since they cannot be mixed
Rationale

The teaching box gives the order: air into the NPH vial first, then air into the rapid-acting vial, then withdraw the rapid-acting dose, then "without adding more air to the NPH vial, carefully withdraw NPH dose." 24. A woman with severe obesity, a strong family history of type 2 diabetes, and GDM in a previous pregnancy presents for her first prenatal visit at 10 weeks. When should she be screened?
  • AAt 24 to 28 weeks, like all other women
  • ✓At the initial prenatal visit, by one of the methods used to diagnose diabetes in the nonpregnant population, and rescreened at 24 to 28 weeks if that result is negative
  • COnly if she develops glycosuria
  • DAt 36 weeks
Rationale

"Although most women are screened for GDM between 24 and 28 weeks of gestation, women with strong risk factors should be screened earlier in pregnancy" — those with "severe obesity, a strong family history of type 2 diabetes, and a history of GDM in a previous pregnancy" are tested at the initial visit using nonpregnant diagnostic methods, and "if negative, they should be rescreened at 24 to 28 weeks of gestation for GDM."

(C) waits for a sign that arrives late or not at all, and "more than half of all women diagnosed with GDM do not have these risk factors" — which is why screening is universal rather than risk-based.

25. A woman is given a 50-g oral glucose load without fasting and has a 1-hour plasma glucose of 152 mg/dL. What happens next?
  • AShe is diagnosed with GDM
  • ✓This is a positive screen in the two-step method, so she returns on another day for a 3-hour 100-g oral glucose tolerance test, and GDM is diagnosed if two or more values are met or exceeded
  • CThe test is repeated fasting
  • DNo further testing is needed if she has no risk factors
Rationale

The two-step approach begins with "a 50-g oral glucose load followed by a plasma glucose measurement 1 hour later. The woman need not be fasting. A glucose value of 130 to 140 mg/dL or higher is considered a positive screen," followed by "a 3-hour (100-g) oral glucose tolerance test (OGTT) on another day." GDM is diagnosed "if two or more values are met or exceeded."

Watch for

(A) treats a screen as a diagnosis. Approximately 95% of United States obstetricians continue to use the two-step approach.

26. How does the one-step method differ from the two-step method?
  • ✓It uses a 75-g OGTT between 24 and 28 weeks and diagnoses GDM if only one glucose value is exceeded, significantly increasing the incidence of GDM — likely double that found with the two-step method
  • BIt uses a 100-g load and requires two abnormal values
  • CIt is a nonfasting screen only
  • DIt is performed only in the first trimester
Rationale

The one-step method is "a 75-g OGTT diagnostic test ... between 24 and 28 weeks" with fasting, 1-hour, and 2-hour draws, and "a diagnosis of GDM is made if only one glucose value is exceeded." It "significantly increases the incidence of GDM, likely double that found using the two-step method." ACOG keeps the two-step method because there is no evidence the one-step method improves outcomes while it does increase costs.

Watch for

(B) swaps the two methods' glucose loads and diagnostic rules. The cue is to count the values needed: two for the two-step, one for the one-step.

gestational diabetes management and follow-upwhen medication starts
28. A woman with GDM has fasting glucose values persistently above 95 mg/dL despite diet and exercise. What happens next, and which medication is preferred?
  • ✓Pharmacologic therapy is begun, and insulin is the preferred medication recommended by the ADA
  • BDiet therapy is intensified for another month before any medication
  • CGlyburide is started first because it is least likely to cross the placenta
  • DNo treatment is needed unless postmeal values are also elevated
Rationale

"If fasting plasma glucose levels are persistently greater than 95 mg/dL, 1-hour postmeal levels are persistently greater than 140 mg/dL, or 2-hour postmeal levels are persistently greater than 120 mg/dL despite nutritional therapy and exercise, pharmacologic therapy is begun," and "insulin is recommended by the ADA as the preferred medication for women with GDM." About one-third of women with GDM require medication.

Watch for

(C) inverts the placental transfer comparison and the recommendation. Metformin "readily crosses the placenta in levels as high as maternal levels," glyburide crosses "in smaller amounts," and yet "current recommendations suggest the use of metformin over glyburide" — while insulin remains preferred because the oral agents cross the placenta and lack long-term neonatal safety data.

30. During labor for a woman with GDM, what glucose range is targeted and why, and how is it usually achieved?
  • ✓80 to 110 mg/dL, because levels in this range decrease the incidence of neonatal hypoglycemia; excellent control is usually possible by avoiding IV fluids containing dextrose
  • B140 to 180 mg/dL, to provide energy for labor
  • CBelow 60 mg/dL, to prevent fetal hyperinsulinism
  • DAny level, since labor is too short to matter
Rationale

In GDM, "blood glucose levels are monitored every 1 to 2 hours to maintain levels between 80 and 110 mg/dL. Levels within this range decrease the incidence of neonatal hypoglycemia," and "it is usually possible to maintain excellent glucose control in women with GDM during labor by avoiding the use of IV fluids containing dextrose."

Watch for

(B) borrows the rationale that applies to pregestational diabetes, where a dextrose-containing solution is piggybacked once active labor begins or glucose falls below 70 — a different plan for a different metabolic starting point.

gestational diabetes management and follow-upwhen medication starts
31. What postpartum follow-up does the chapter recommend after a pregnancy complicated by GDM?
  • ✓A 75-g 2-hour OGTT or fasting plasma glucose at 6 to 12 weeks postpartum, then lifelong repeat screening at least every 3 years
  • BNo follow-up, since GDM resolves at birth
  • CA single fasting glucose at the 6-week visit and nothing further
  • DAnnual A1c testing beginning at age 45
Rationale

ACOG recommends "assessing all women who had GDM for carbohydrate intolerance with a 75-g, 2-hour OGTT or a fasting plasma glucose level at 6 to 12 weeks postpartum," then "lifelong repeat screening at least every 3 years." Up to one-third are found to have diabetes or impaired glucose metabolism at that screening, and the chance of developing type 2 diabetes within 10 to 20 years is as high as 70%.

Watch for

(B) treats resolution of the pregnancy as resolution of the risk. The chapter's counterweight is that "progression to type 2 diabetes is not inevitable" for women who exercise, become less sedentary, and eat a healthy diet.

  • ✓Hourly, with short-acting insulin titrated to keep the level under 110 mg/dL, because hyperglycemia during labor can cause metabolic problems in the neonate, particularly hypoglycemia
  • BEvery 4 hours, with a target of 140 mg/dL
  • COnce on admission, since labor is brief
  • DHourly, with a target above 140 mg/dL to fuel labor
Rationale

"During active labor, blood glucose levels should be checked hourly," with short-acting insulin titrated to maintain levels "less than 110 mg/dL," because "hyperglycemia during labor can cause metabolic problems in the neonate, particularly hypoglycemia."
