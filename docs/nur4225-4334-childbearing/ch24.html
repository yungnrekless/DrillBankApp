apgar scoringgeneral
1. A newborn is limp and apneic at birth with a heart rate of 60. What is the nurse's priority?
  • AAssign the 1-minute Apgar score, then begin resuscitation based on the result
  • ✓Initiate resuscitation before the 1-minute Apgar score is determined
  • CWait until 1 minute has elapsed to allow spontaneous recovery
  • DAssign the 1-minute and 5-minute scores, then notify the provider
Rationale

The chapter states the ordering rule explicitly: "If resuscitation is required, it should be initiated before the 1-minute Apgar score is determined." The score describes the newborn's transition and response to resuscitative efforts; it does not gate the decision to resuscitate.

Watch for

(A) is the reflex pick because the score is scheduled at 1 minute and reads like a trigger for action. Scoring first costs the infant a minute of ventilation, which is why the chapter puts the priority in writing.

apgar scoringgeneral
2. A newborn grimaces when the soles are stimulated, has some flexion of the extremities, a heart rate of 118, a weak cry, and a pink body with blue extremities. What is the Apgar score?
  • A5
  • ✓6
  • C7
  • D8
Rationale

Heart rate at or above 100 scores 2; a slow, weak cry scores 1; some flexion of the extremities scores 1; a grimace scores 1; body pink with blue extremities scores 1. The total is 6, which falls in the 4-to-6 band indicating moderate difficulty.

Watch for

(D) results from crediting the pink body as "completely pink" and the flexion as "well flexed," which is the most common inflation — both of those signs have a middle value, and acrocyanosis scores 1 rather than 2.

apgar scoringgeneral
3. A newborn's 5-minute Apgar score is 6. How should the nurse proceed?
  • ANo further scoring is required; document and continue routine care
  • ✓Repeat the assessment every 5 minutes for up to 20 minutes
  • CRepeat the assessment at 10 minutes only
  • DRepeat the assessment every minute until the score reaches 7
Rationale

"An Apgar score is assigned at 1 and 5 minutes after birth." The repeat rule keys to the 5-minute score: "For scores less than 7 at 5 minutes, the assessment should be repeated every 5 minutes for up to 20 minutes."

Watch for

(A) treats 6 as adequate because it is above the severe-distress band, and it stops the monitoring the chapter requires. Note that a low 1-minute score does not by itself trigger the repeat schedule — the 5-minute score is what does.

apgar scoringgeneral
4. Parents whose newborn had a 1-minute Apgar score of 4 ask whether this means their baby will have brain damage. Which response is accurate?
  • AA low score at 1 minute is strongly predictive of later neurologic impairment
  • ✓Apgar scores do not predict future neurologic outcome; they describe the newborn's transition and response to resuscitative efforts
  • CThe 1-minute score predicts outcome, but the 5-minute score does not
  • DOnly scores of 0 to 3 predict neurologic outcome
Rationale

"Apgar scores do not predict future neurologic outcome for the newborn but are useful in describing the newborn's transition to the extrauterine environment and response to resuscitative efforts." The bands describe the present moment: 0 to 3 severe distress, 4 to 6 moderate difficulty, 7 to 10 minimal or no difficulty adjusting to extrauterine life.

Watch for

(A) is what parents fear and what a nurse may unintentionally confirm by hedging. The score is a description of a transition, not a prognosis, and saying so plainly is the teaching the chapter supports.

apgar scoringgeneral
5. The nurse is assessing color in a newborn with darkly pigmented skin during Apgar scoring. Which technique does the chapter specify?
  • AAssess the color of the trunk and compare it with the extremities
  • ✓Visualize the buccal mucosa, conjunctiva, nail beds, lips, earlobes, and soles of the feet, using the buccal mucosa until a pulse oximeter can be applied
  • CAssess color only after the infant has been dried and warmed
  • DDefer color scoring and assign a score of 2 by default
Rationale

The NURSING ALERT states that "color can be evaluated most accurately by visualization of the buccal mucosa (oral mucous membranes), conjunctiva, nail beds, lips, earlobes, and soles of the feet. Evaluation of skin color in newborns with darker skin is best accomplished by visualizing the buccal mucosa until a pulse oximeter can be applied."

Watch for

(A) uses the site that produces the least reliable reading in exactly the infants for whom accuracy matters most. Note also that heart rate for the Apgar may be assessed "by palpation of the umbilical cord," not only by stethoscope, and that reflex irritability is scored on a response to stimulus while muscle tone is scored on resting flexion.

eye prophylaxisgeneral
6. A mother whose prenatal testing showed a chlamydial infection asks whether the newborn's eye ointment will protect the baby. Which response is accurate?
  • AErythromycin ointment prevents both gonococcal and chlamydial conjunctivitis
  • ✓Erythromycin ointment is bactericidal for gonorrhea but is not effective in preventing chlamydial conjunctivitis, which is treated with oral erythromycin or azithromycin
  • CErythromycin ointment prevents chlamydial but not gonococcal infection
  • DNo eye prophylaxis is needed when the maternal organism is chlamydia
Rationale

Erythromycin ophthalmic ointment is "bacteriostatic and bactericidal for N. gonorrhoeae," and eye prophylaxis exists to prevent ophthalmia neonatorum, which "without prompt treatment ... can lead to blindness." But "topical antibiotics such as erythromycin are not effective in preventing chlamydial conjunctivitis. Oral erythromycin or azithromycin is used to treat chlamydial conjunctivitis."

Watch for

(A) is the reflex pick because the ointment is given to every newborn regardless of maternal history, which reads as broad coverage. Its target organism is named in the medication guide, and chlamydia is outside it.

eye prophylaxisgeneral
7. Which technique correctly describes administration of erythromycin ophthalmic ointment?
  • AApply a 1-cm ribbon to the upper conjunctival sac, spreading from outer to inner canthus
  • ✓Apply a 1-cm ribbon to the lower conjunctival sac of each eye, spreading from the inner canthus to the outer canthus, and wipe away excess after 1 minute
  • CApply a 2-cm ribbon directly onto the cornea and wipe immediately
  • DApply the ointment and irrigate the eyes with sterile saline after 30 seconds
Rationale

The medication guide directs applying "a 1-cm ribbon of ointment to the lower conjunctival sac of each eye," spreading "from the inner canthus of the eye to the outer canthus," not touching the tube to the eye, gently massaging the closed eyelids, and after 1 minute wiping away excess. A sterile cotton ball wipes each eyelid beforehand, and the unused ointment is discarded.

Watch for

(A) reverses both the sac and the direction, and the direction reversal is the more available error because inner-to-outer is the opposite of how many students learned to clean an eye. (D) removes the medication before it can act.

eye prophylaxisgeneral
8. A newborn's eyes appear reddened and slightly swollen 12 hours after erythromycin ointment was given. How should the nurse interpret this?
  • AAn allergic reaction requiring the provider's immediate attention
  • ✓A chemical conjunctivitis that lasts 24 to 48 hours, with temporarily blurred vision, which is an expected adverse effect
  • CEarly ophthalmia neonatorum, meaning the prophylaxis failed
  • DEvidence the ointment was administered incorrectly
Rationale

The medication guide lists the adverse reaction directly: the ointment "can cause chemical conjunctivitis that lasts 24–48 h; vision can be blurred temporarily."

Watch for

(C) reads the expected reaction as the disease it was given to prevent, and it pulls because the two look alike. The time course is the discriminator: a chemical response appears within hours of the dose and resolves inside two days.

eye prophylaxisgeneral
9. Parents ask whether eye prophylaxis can wait until after the first breastfeeding. Which response is correct?
  • AIt must be given immediately at birth with no delay permitted
  • ✓It is usually given within the first hour and may be delayed up to 2 hours until after the first breastfeeding, to facilitate eye contact and bonding
  • CIt may be delayed until discharge if the parents prefer
  • DIt is optional and is given only on parental request
Rationale

Prophylaxis is "usually administered within the first hour after birth ... It may be delayed up to 2 hours until after the first breastfeeding so that eye contact and parent-infant attachment and bonding are facilitated." The medication guide specifies administering the ointment within 1 to 2 hours of birth.

Watch for

(C) turns a bounded delay into an open one. Eye prophylaxis "is mandated by law in most U.S. states without regard to the mode of birth"; where refusal is permitted, "parents may refuse eye prophylaxis by signing a form that becomes part of the newborn record."

10. A newborn weighs 1400 g. Which dose of phytonadione is correct?
  • A1 mg, or 0.5 mL, intramuscularly
  • ✓0.5 mg, or 0.25 mL, intramuscularly
  • C2 mg orally
  • D1 mg intravenously over 1 minute
Rationale

The recommendation is a single dose of phytonadione 0.5 to 1 mg, split by weight: "a 0.5-mg (0.25-mL) dose to newborns weighing ≤1500 g and a 1-mg (0.5-mL) dose to newborns weighing >1500 g."

Watch for

(A) is the correct dose for the more common patient and ignores the weight split, which is why it is the reflex pick. The cut point is 1500 g, and this infant falls below it.

11. Parents refusing the vitamin K injection ask whether an oral form could be given instead. Which response is accurate?
  • AOral vitamin K is equivalent and is an acceptable alternative
  • ✓Oral administration of vitamin K is not as effective in preventing late hemorrhagic disease
  • COral vitamin K is preferred but is not stocked in most facilities
  • DVitamin K may be given intravenously instead, as a rapid push
Rationale

The route is intramuscular, because "oral administration of vitamin K is not as effective in the prevention of late hemorrhagic disease." The intravenous route is reserved for a preterm infant with little to no muscle mass, where the medication is "diluted and given over 10–15 min" with cardiorespiratory monitoring, because "rapid IV administration of vitamin K can cause cardiac arrest."

Watch for

(A) offers an inferior option the chapter names as inferior, and it is tempting because it appears to meet the family halfway. (D) describes the route that can kill the infant if it is given the way the option states.

newborn intramuscular prophylaxisvitamin K
12. Which consequence does the chapter specifically attach to parental refusal of vitamin K in a male newborn?
  • AAn increased risk of jaundice requiring phototherapy
  • ✓A significantly greater likelihood of bleeding after circumcision, with many providers declining to perform the procedure
  • CA delay in newborn metabolic screening
  • DA contraindication to hepatitis B vaccination
Rationale

"Male infants who do not receive the vitamin K injection are significantly more likely to bleed after circumcision, and many providers will decline performing this procedure when vitamin K has been refused." The chapter also notes that parents who refuse vitamin K "are more likely to refuse immunizations."

Watch for

(A) attaches a real adverse effect of the drug to its refusal, which is backwards. Hemolysis, jaundice, and hyperbilirubinemia "have been reported" as adverse reactions to phytonadione itself, "particularly in preterm infants," so a preterm infant who becomes jaundiced after the injection is not necessarily having an unrelated problem.

  • AHepatitis B vaccine within 24 hours; immune globulin is not needed if the mother received antiviral therapy in pregnancy
  • ✓Hepatitis B vaccine and hepatitis B immune globulin within 12 hours after birth, regardless of any previous antiviral therapy during pregnancy, in separate sites with one in each vastus lateralis

14. A newborn weighing 3400 g is born to a mother who tested negative for hepatitis B. When should the vaccine be given?
  • AWithin 12 hours after birth
  • ✓Within the first 24 hours
  • CAt 1 month of age or at hospital discharge, whichever comes first
  • DAt the 2-month well-child visit
Rationale

For a mother who is negative with an infant of 2000 g or more who is medically stable, the direction is to "administer HepB vaccine within first 24 h." An infant under 2000 g born to a negative mother waits until "1 month of age or at hospital discharge, whichever comes first." When maternal status is unknown, the vaccine goes within 12 hours.

Watch for

(C) is the correct answer for a low-birth-weight infant of a negative mother, and (A) is the correct interval when the status is positive or unknown. Two variables set the timing — maternal status and infant weight — and dropping either produces a plausible wrong answer.

newborn jaundice screeninggeneral
15. A newborn appears jaundiced at 18 hours of age. What does the chapter direct the nurse to do?
  • ADocument the finding and reassess in the morning, since jaundice is common
  • ✓Measure a transcutaneous or total serum bilirubin level, because jaundice before 24 hours is likely due to a hemolytic process and is called pathologic jaundice
  • CInitiate phototherapy without measuring a level
  • DIncrease feeding frequency and recheck the skin in 12 hours
Rationale

"If a newborn appears jaundiced in the first 24 hours, a transcutaneous bilirubin (TcB) or total serum bilirubin (TSB) level should be measured. Jaundice before 24 hours is likely due to a hemolytic process and is called pathologic jaundice." Table 24.3 lists "none in the first 24 h of life" as the expected skin finding and jaundice within the first 24 hours as the deviation, associated with increased hemolysis, Rh isoimmunization, and ABO incompatibility.

Watch for

(A) applies the reassurance appropriate to physiologic jaundice to an infant inside the window that defines pathologic jaundice. The action here is to measure, which is what distinguishes this chapter's handling from Chapter 23's physiology.

newborn jaundice screeninggeneral
16. A nurse assesses a dark-skinned newborn for jaundice under the fluorescent lights of the nursery and documents no jaundice. Which critique applies?
  • AThe assessment is adequate as long as the skin was blanched
  • ✓Visual assessment alone does not accurately assess hyperbilirubinemia, especially in dark-skinned newborns, and assessment in natural light is recommended because artificial lighting distorts skin color
  • CJaundice cannot be assessed in dark-skinned newborns at all
  • DOnly a total serum bilirubin can be used at any age
Rationale

"Visual assessment of jaundice alone does not provide an accurate assessment of hyperbilirubinemia, especially in dark-skinned newborns." The technique is to blanch the skin and to assess "the conjunctival sacs and buccal mucosa ... especially in darker-skinned infants. Assessing for jaundice in natural light is recommended because artificial lighting and reflection from walls can distort the actual skin color."

Watch for

(A) credits a partial technique, and it pulls because blanching is the step students remember. Two things are wrong here: the light source and the reliance on inspection as a substitute for measurement.

newborn jaundice screeninggeneral
17. When does the professional recommendation place universal predischarge bilirubin screening?
  • AAt 12 hours of age for all newborns
  • ✓Between 24 and 48 hours after birth, or prior to discharge if discharged earlier
  • CAt 72 hours, when physiologic jaundice peaks
  • DOnly for infants with visible jaundice
Rationale

The recommendation is "universal predischarge bilirubin screening between 24 and 48 hours after birth or prior to discharge, if discharged earlier, to prevent severe hyperbilirubinemia and the neurologic complications of acute bilirubin encephalopathy and kernicterus."

Watch for

(D) restricts a universal screen to symptomatic infants, which defeats its purpose given that visual assessment is unreliable. (C) picks the physiologic peak at 72 to 96 hours, which is after most newborns have gone home.

newborn jaundice screeninggeneral
18. A newborn's transcutaneous bilirubin is 11 mg/dL. The phototherapy treatment threshold for this infant's age in hours is 13 mg/dL. What should the nurse do?
  • ANo further testing is needed, since the level is below 15 mg/dL
  • ✓Obtain a total serum bilirubin, because the transcutaneous level is within 3 mg/dL of the phototherapy threshold
  • CBegin phototherapy based on the transcutaneous value
  • DRepeat the transcutaneous measurement in 12 hours
Rationale

A serum level is checked "if the TcB level is greater than 15 mg/dL or if the TcB level is higher than or within 3 mg/dL of the phototherapy treatment threshold." At 11 against a threshold of 13, the gap is 2 mg/dL, so the relative trigger fires.

Watch for

(A) applies only the absolute trigger, which is the half of the rule that distractors keep and the reason a level as low as 11 can still require a serum draw. The relative trigger is what makes the threshold matter rather than the number alone.

newborn jaundice screeninggeneral
19. Which statement about interpreting a total serum bilirubin level is accurate?
  • AA fixed cutoff of 12 mg/dL defines hyperbilirubinemia at any age
  • ✓Levels are interpreted using an hour-specific nomogram, and if more than one measurement was done, the rate of increase can identify infants at risk
  • CInterpretation depends only on whether the infant is breastfed
  • DThe direct fraction alone determines whether treatment is needed
Rationale

"TSB levels are interpreted using an hour-specific nomogram," and "if more than one TcB or TSB measure was done, the rate of increase can be used to identify infants at risk."

Watch for

(A) is the intuitive model — one number, one cutoff — and it is exactly what an hour-specific nomogram replaces. A bilirubin value carries no meaning without the infant's age in hours, which is the same principle behind Chapter 23's pathologic criterion of a rise greater than 0.2 mg/dL per hour.

20. When are specimens for universal newborn metabolic screening usually collected, and why is the screen performed on every newborn?
  • AWithin the first 6 hours, because early detection improves outcomes
  • ✓After 24 hours of age and before discharge, because the majority of the disorders screened do not produce symptoms at birth
  • CAt the 2-week visit, once feeding is established
  • DOnly when a family history suggests a genetic disorder
Rationale

"Samples are usually collected in the hospital after 24 hours of age and before discharge." The rationale for universality is that "the majority of disorders included in the screening do not produce symptoms at birth." Screening covers 36 core disorders and 26 secondary disorders using tandem mass spectrometry, with capillary blood obtained by heelstick and collected on special filter paper.

Watch for

(D) makes a universal public health program selective, which the asymptomatic-at-birth rationale rules out. Note that "in the majority of states informed consent is not required for newborn screening."

newborn metabolic and hearing screeninggeneral
22. A newborn does not pass the hearing screen before discharge. What follow-up does the chapter specify?
  • AThe screen is definitive, so audiologic treatment should begin immediately
  • ✓The screen is typically repeated before discharge; if the infant still does not pass, a comprehensive audiologic evaluation should be done by 3 months of age
  • CRescreening should occur at the 1-year visit
  • DNo follow-up is needed, since most infants pass on repeat testing
Rationale

If the infant does not pass, "it is typically repeated before discharge," and if still not passing, "a comprehensive audiologic evaluation should be done by 3 months of age." Routine screening is recommended for all newborns "before hospital discharge or no later than 1 month of age." Approximately 3 out of every 1000 infants born each year have hearing loss.

Watch for

(A) treats a screening result as a diagnosis, which the chapter blocks: "Neither test is definitive in diagnosing hearing loss; they are used to determine whether further, more accurate hearing testing is needed."

newborn metabolic and hearing screeninggeneral
23. A parent asks how the hearing screen works. The nurse explains that a soft rubber earpiece emitting a clicking noise is placed in the outer ear and a healthy ear echoes the sound back to a microphone. Which test is this?
  • AThe auditory brainstem response
  • ✓The evoked otoacoustic emissions test
  • CTympanometry
  • DPure-tone audiometry
Rationale

In the evoked otoacoustic emissions test, "a soft rubber earpiece that makes a soft clicking noise is placed in the baby's outer ear. A healthy ear will echo the click sound back to a microphone." The auditory brainstem response instead involves "attaching sensors to the baby's forehead and behind each ear," and the sensors "measure the responses of the baby's acoustic nerve."

Watch for

(A) is the other method named, and the discriminator is what is being measured: an echo returning from the ear versus a nerve response recorded from scalp sensors. Either method, or a combination, may be used.

critical congenital heart disease screeninggeneral
24. At what age is screening for critical congenital heart disease performed, and at which sites?
  • AWithin 6 hours of birth, in both hands
  • ✓At 24 to 48 hours of age, in the right hand and one foot
  • CAt 24 to 48 hours of age, in the left hand and one foot
  • DImmediately before discharge, in either extremity
Rationale

"Screening is performed at 24 to 48 hours of age," and "oxygen saturation is measured in the right hand and one foot." The site logic follows the ductus arteriosus: the right hand is preductal and the foot is postductal, which is why a difference between them is meaningful.

Watch for

(A) uses both hands, which measures the same side of the circulation twice and cannot detect a preductal-to-postductal gradient. (C) picks the wrong hand, and the left hand is not reliably preductal.

critical congenital heart disease screeninggeneral
25. A newborn's screening oxygen saturation is 96% in the right hand and 93% in the foot. How should this be interpreted?
  • APassing, because a saturation above 95% was recorded in one extremity
  • ✓Not passing, because the absolute difference between the upper and lower extremity readings is 3% or more
  • CNot passing, because a saturation below 95% requires immediate evaluation
  • DPassing, because neither value is below 90%
Rationale

A passing result requires "oxygen saturation of greater than 95% in either extremity, with a less than 3% absolute difference between the upper and lower extremity readings." Here the upper value passes but the difference is exactly 3%, which is not less than 3%.

Watch for

(A) applies only the first half of the criterion, and it is the most available error because 96% looks reassuring on its own. The screen has two conditions and both must be met, which is what makes the gradient testable.

critical congenital heart disease screeninggeneral
27. A parent asks why an apparently healthy newborn needs a pulse oximetry screen. Which explanation reflects the chapter's rationale?
  • APulse oximetry diagnoses congenital heart defects noninvasively
  • ✓Hypoxemia can be the first sign a defect is present, and other symptoms can develop only after the newborn has been discharged
  • CThe screen replaces the newborn cardiac physical examination
  • DAll newborns have transient hypoxemia that must be documented
Rationale

The screen uses "pulse oximetry to measure oxygen saturation for the purpose of detecting hypoxemia," because "hypoxemia can be the first sign that a congenital heart defect is present, and other symptoms can develop once the newborn has been discharged." It detects defects "that manifest with hypoxemia in the absence of other physical symptoms." About 6 in 10,000 apparently healthy term or late-preterm infants will have critical congenital heart disease.

Watch for

(A) overstates what the screen does. It identifies infants who need the confirmatory test, which the chapter names as an echocardiogram, and it is a screen rather than a diagnostic study.

heelstick and blood samplinggeneral
28. Before performing a heelstick for a glucose level, the nurse notes the infant's foot is cool. Why does this matter?
  • AIt only makes the specimen harder to obtain
  • ✓Diminished blood flow makes the specimen harder to obtain and can cause the test result to be falsely low
  • CIt causes the result to be falsely high
  • DIt has no effect on the result if enough blood is collected
Rationale

The NURSING ALERT states: "A heel warmer should be applied before every glucose assessment via heelstick. If the extremity is cool, blood flow is diminished making it harder to obtain the needed specimen and potentially causing the test result to be falsely low." Warming for 5 to 10 minutes dilates the vessels, and disposable heel warmers "should be used with care to prevent burns."

Watch for

(C) reverses the direction of the artifact. This matters clinically because a falsely low glucose triggers an intervention the infant may not need, so a low value drawn from a cool foot deserves a procedural explanation before a physiologic one.

29. Where is a heelstick puncture correctly performed, and how deep?
  • AThe center of the heel, no deeper than 2.4 mm
  • ✓The outer aspect of the heel, no deeper than 2.4 mm in a term infant
  • CThe walking surface of the heel, no deeper than 4 mm
  • DThe great toe pad, at any depth
Rationale

"The puncture is made at the outer aspect of the heel," located by drawing an imaginary line "from between the fourth and fifth toes and parallel to the lateral aspect of the foot to the heel," with a second line from the great toe to the medial aspect of the heel. The lancet "penetrates no deeper than 2.4 mm in a term infant."

Watch for

(A) and (C) both target areas the chapter tells you to avoid, and both dangers are named. The most serious complication is "necrotizing osteochondritis resulting from lancet penetration of the bone," and repeated trauma to the walking surface "can cause fibrosis and scarring that can lead to problems with walking later in life."

30. Which sequence correctly describes heelstick technique?
  • ACleanse with antiseptic, puncture, then offer oral sucrose for comfort
  • ✓Warm the heel, offer oral sucrose, cleanse with antiseptic, then puncture with a spring-loaded automatic device
  • CPuncture with a manual lance blade, then warm the heel to improve flow
  • DCleanse with alcohol, puncture, and cleanse with alcohol again afterward
Rationale

The order the chapter gives is to warm the heel, then "after providing the infant with an oral sucrose solution, the area is cleansed with an appropriate skin antiseptic." A spring-loaded automatic puncture device "causes less pain and requires fewer punctures than a manual lance blade."

Watch for

(A) moves the comfort measure after the painful stimulus, which is the most common sequencing error and defeats the point of a pre-procedure intervention. Evidence-based measures named include a pacifier with sucrose, swaddling, containment, facilitated tucking, breastfeeding, and skin-to-skin contact with the mother, and the infant should also be cuddled and comforted after the procedure.

31. After a heelstick, what does the chapter direct the nurse to apply to the site?
  • AAn alcohol swab, followed by a bandage
  • ✓Gentle pressure with a dry gauze pad, with no further skin cleanser or alcohol
  • CA pressure dressing left in place for 4 hours
  • DAntibiotic ointment and a gauze wrap

"Gentle pressure is applied with a dry gauze pad. No further skin cleanser or alcohol should be applied because it will cause the site to continue to bleed."

(A) is the adult reflex — clean the site again after the stick — and here it prolongs bleeding, which the chapter states as the reason. The chapter also notes the behavioral cost of repetition: "after several heelsticks, infants have been observed to withdraw their feet when they are touched."

heelstick and blood samplinggeneral
32. A larger blood volume is required than a heelstick can provide. Which statement about venipuncture in the newborn is accurate?
  • AA heelstick is appropriate for any volume up to 5 mL
  • ✓A heelstick is appropriate when the volume required is less than 1 mL; venipuncture sites include the antecubital, saphenous, superficial wrist, and rarely scalp veins, and a tourniquet is optional
  • COnly scalp veins may be used in newborns
  • DA tourniquet is required for all newborn venipuncture
Rationale

"A heelstick is usually considered appropriate when the volume of blood required is less than 1 mL." Venipuncture is used for larger samples, at the "antecubital, saphenous, superficial wrist, and, rarely, scalp veins," using a 23- or 25-gauge butterfly or hypodermic needle with a syringe. "A tourniquet is optional but can help increase blood flow."

Watch for

(A) stretches the heelstick well past its stated volume limit, and it pulls because a heelstick feels less invasive and therefore preferable at any size. The volume, not the perceived invasiveness, decides the method.

newborn thermoregulation at the bedsidegeneral
33. A newborn is under a radiant warmer. Which practice does the chapter require, and why?
  • AManual control mode, so the nurse can adjust the output as needed
  • ✓Servo-controlled setting only, because a detached probe or manual mode will make the warmer increase heat output and potentially overheat the newborn
  • CManual mode with hourly temperature checks
  • DEither mode, as long as the probe is checked once per shift
Rationale

The SAFETY ALERT states that the thermistor probe must be checked periodically for secure attachment, because "if it becomes loose, the radiant heating device will increase heat output, potentially overheating the newborn. Using the manual control mode ... can have the same effect ... Therefore radiant warmers should be used only in the servo-controlled setting." The desired abdominal skin temperature is approximately 36.5° to 37°C.

Watch for

(A) and (C) both put a person in the feedback loop, which is what the alert identifies as the failure mode. A servo-controlled warmer reads the infant's own skin and stops; a manual one heats until someone notices.

  • AAt 2 hours of age, limited to 10 minutes

  • CAt 6 hours of age regardless of temperature, limited to 15 minutes

Rationale

"The initial bath for an uncompromised term infant should be postponed for at least 6 hours and until the newborn's skin temperature is stable as indicated by two consecutive axillary temperatures at or above 36.8°C (98.2 °F). The bath time should be limited to 5 minutes."

(C) satisfies only half the rule. Both conditions must be met, and the temperature condition is the one that gets dropped, because the 6-hour figure is the memorable one.

36. How often should the nurse assess a newborn's temperature after birth, and by which route?
  • ARectal, every 4 hours
  • ✓Axillary, every hour or more often as needed until the temperature stabilizes
  • CTemporal, once per shift
  • DAxillary, every 8 hours
Rationale

"The nurse assesses the axillary temperature of the newborn every hour (or more often as needed) until the newborn's temperature stabilizes." Table 24.3 gives the expected axillary temperature as 37°C with a normal range of 36.5° to 37.5°C, and notes that temperature is "stabilized by 8–10 h of age," with failure to stabilize by 6 to 8 hours listed as a deviation.

Watch for

(D) applies a routine well-baby interval to the transition period. The frequency is tied to a state — until stability — rather than to a fixed schedule, which is what makes the 8-to-10-hour stabilization expectation the relevant endpoint. If the mother received magnesium sulfate, the newborn is "less able to conserve heat by vasoconstriction," which supplies a maternal explanation for poor thermal stability.

37. Parents ask where their newborn should sleep at home. Which teaching reflects the chapter?
  • AIn the parents' bed, so long as no one smokes and the mother is breastfeeding
  • ✓In the parents' room on a separate sleep surface designed for infants, for at least the first 6 months; bed-sharing is not recommended in any circumstance
  • CIn a separate room from the start, to establish independent sleep
  • DIn a bassinet in the parents' room for the first month, then bed-sharing is acceptable
Rationale

"For at least the first 6 months, babies should sleep in the parents' room, but on a separate sleep surface designed for infants. Bed-sharing is not recommended in any circumstance." Infants are placed supine "for sleep during the first year of life to reduce the incidence of sudden infant death syndrome," on a firm, noninclined surface with a tight-fitting sheet.

Watch for

(A) attaches conditions to a categorical prohibition, and it is the most persuasive wrong answer because both conditions named are genuine risk reducers elsewhere in the box. (C) drops the room-sharing half, which is itself protective.

40. A nurse swaddles a newborn with the arms extended, legs flexed, and hips in neutral position without rotation. A student asks why the legs are not wrapped straight. Which explanation is correct?
  • AStraight legs make the swaddle come undone more easily
  • ✓Swaddling tightly with the legs extended is associated with increased risk for developmental dysplasia of the hip; the hips should be in slight flexion and abducted with the knees free to move
  • CStraight legs increase the risk of sudden infant death syndrome
  • DThere is no difference; either position is acceptable
Rationale

The SAFETY ALERT states that "swaddling an infant tightly with legs extended is associated with increased risk for hip dislocation (developmental dysplasia of the hip [DDH]). The correct way to swaddle an infant is with the hips in slight flexion and abducted, allowing freedom of movement of the knees." The blanket must not be too tight, as that can cause overheating or respiratory compromise, and there should be "space for two to three adult fingers between the infant's chest and the swaddle."

Watch for

(D) treats a stated hazard as a matter of preference, and it pulls because a tightly wrapped infant looks well cared for. A swaddled infant lies on the back, and swaddling stops by about 2 months of age, when the infant can roll over.

41. During skin-to-skin contact 45 minutes after birth, the nurse observes that the mother has dozed off and the newborn is prone on her chest with the face partly covered. What does the chapter identify as the concern and the response?
  • ANo concern; skin-to-skin contact is protective and should not be interrupted
  • ✓Sudden unexpected postnatal collapse, most of which occurs in the first 2 hours after birth and appears related to suffocation or entrapment, so the nurse repositions the newborn and continues vigilant monitoring
  • CCold stress; the newborn should be moved to a radiant warmer
  • DA feeding problem; the nurse should wake the mother to breastfeed
Rationale

"The vast majority of cases of SUPC occur during the first 2 hours after birth and appear to be related to suffocation or entrapment," and it "has occurred when newborns are held prone on the mother's chest or abdomen during SSC, and when mothers are not paying close attention." Risk factors include maternal fatigue, sedating medications, caregiver distraction, and primiparous status. (A) protects a beneficial practice by declining to supervise it, which is the exact failure the chapter describes. 42. A newborn's respiratory rate is 64 at 30 minutes of age. At 2 days the rate is 38. How should each be interpreted?
  • AThe first is tachypnea requiring evaluation; the second is bradypnea
  • ✓Both are within expected ranges for their respective ages, since the first period of reactivity runs 50 to 60 per minute, the second period 50 to 70, and stabilization at 1 to 2 days runs 30 to 40
  • CBoth are abnormal, since the overall normal range is 30 to 60
  • DThe first is normal and the second requires evaluation
Rationale

Table 24.3 gives an overall expected range of 30 to 60 per minute with normal variations by age: 50 to 60 in the first period of reactivity, 50 to 70 in the second period, and 30 to 40 once stabilized at 1 to 2 days. Respirations are counted for a full minute and tend to be shallow and irregular when the newborn is awake, and crackles may be heard after birth.

Watch for

(C) applies the single overall band to every age, which is the most available error because that range is the one most students memorize. Deviations are apneic episodes over 20 seconds, bradypnea under 30, and tachypnea over 60 sustained at rest.

43. A term newborn's head circumference is 35 cm and chest circumference is 32 cm. How should the nurse interpret this?
  • AHydrocephalus should be suspected, since the head exceeds the chest
  • ✓Expected, since the chest is normally 2 to 3 cm less than the head circumference and both are within term ranges
  • CMicrocephaly, since the head is under 36 cm
  • DThe measurements should be repeated because the difference is too small
Rationale

Term head circumference is 32 to 36 cm measured at the occipitofrontal circumference, and term chest circumference is 30 to 33 cm measured at the nipple line, "2–3 cm (0.8–1.2 in.) less than head circumference." The two are "approximately the same for first 1 or 2 days after birth."

Watch for

(A) treats the normal relationship as pathology. The diagnostic cue is the size of the difference, not its direction: hydrocephaly is flagged when the head is 4 cm or more greater than the chest, with widely separated sutures. Head circumference "may need to be remeasured on the second or third day" after molding and caput succedaneum resolve.

45. A term newborn who appears well and is feeding without difficulty has a serum glucose of 42 mg/dL at 8 hours of age. Which management does the chapter support?
  • AImmediate intravenous dextrose
  • ✓Early and frequent oral feedings, since the newborn is clinically stable and appears well
  • CWithhold feeding and recheck in 2 hours
  • DTransfer to the intensive care nursery
Rationale

"For newborns who are clinically stable and appear well, early and frequent oral feedings are suggested." Intravenous fluids are reserved for infants "at an increased risk for aspiration, have poor perfusion, or are thought to have a bowel obstruction," who "are not enterally fed." A glucose of 42 also sits within the standard 40 to 60 mg/dL range for an infant one day old or less.

Watch for

(A) escalates to an intravenous route in a stable feeding infant, and it pulls because the number looks low against adult expectations. (C) withholds the intervention the chapter recommends. Before drawing that glucose, recall that a cool extremity can make the heelstick result falsely low.
