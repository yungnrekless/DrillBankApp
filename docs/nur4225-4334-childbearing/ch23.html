1. A newborn 20 minutes old has a heart rate of 172, respirations of 74, audible fine crackles, and intermittent grunting. The infant is alert with spontaneous tremors. How should the nurse interpret these findings?
  • AThese are signs of respiratory distress syndrome requiring immediate escalation
  • ✓These are expected findings of the first period of reactivity, though grunting should cease within the first hour
  • CThese indicate transient tachypnea of the newborn and warrant a chest radiograph
  • DThese indicate the period of decreased responsiveness
Rationale

The first period of reactivity "lasts up to 30 minutes after birth." Heart rate rises rapidly to 160 to 180 beats/min before falling toward 100 to 120, respirations are irregular at 60 to 80 breaths/min, fine crackles can be heard, and "audible grunting, nasal flaring, and retractions can be present" but "these should cease within the first hour after birth." The infant is alert with spontaneous startles, tremors, and crying.

Watch for

(A) is the reflex pick because grunting and flaring are genuine distress signs at any other moment. What makes them expected here is the clock, not the signs. The same picture at 3 hours of age is a problem.

2. Ninety minutes after birth a newborn is sleeping, has markedly decreased motor activity, is pink, and has rapid but shallow and unlabored respirations. Which period does this describe?
  • AThe first period of reactivity
  • ✓The period of decreased responsiveness, which lasts 60 to 100 minutes
  • CThe second period of reactivity
  • DAn abnormal depression of consciousness requiring stimulation
Rationale

"After the first period of reactivity, the newborn either sleeps or has a marked decrease in motor activity. This period of decreased responsiveness lasts 60 to 100 minutes." The infant is pink, with respirations rapid up to 60 breaths/min, shallow but unlabored, and bowel sounds audible.

Watch for

(D) is the reflex pick for a nurse who expects a newborn to be increasingly alert as it recovers from birth. This sleep period is a normal stage of transition, and rousing the infant to feed during it is a common misreading of it.

3. At 4 hours of age a newborn has brief episodes of tachycardia and tachypnea with increased muscle tone, color changes, and increased mucus production, and passes meconium. Which period is this, and what is the nursing priority?
  • AThe first period of reactivity; prepare for the first feeding
  • ✓The second period of reactivity, occurring between 2 and 8 hours after birth; observe for the increased mucus, which is expected in this period
  • CAn abnormal cardiorespiratory event requiring notification
  • DThe period of decreased responsiveness ending
Rationale

"The second period of reactivity occurs approximately between 2 and 8 hours after birth and lasts from 10 minutes to several hours. Brief periods of tachycardia and tachypnea occur, associated with increased muscle tone, changes in skin color, and mucus production. Meconium is commonly passed at this time."

Watch for

(C) pulls because tachycardia, tachypnea, and color change are the vocabulary of deterioration everywhere else. The whole reason the chapter names these three periods is so a normal transitional event is not treated as a new problem.

4. Which statement about the transition to extrauterine life is accurate?
  • AThe major adaptations occur during the first 24 hours and are mediated by the parasympathetic nervous system
  • ✓The major adaptations occur during the first 6 to 8 hours, are mediated by the sympathetic nervous system, and most healthy newborns experience them regardless of gestational age or type of birth
  • COnly newborns delivered vaginally experience the transition periods
  • DVery preterm infants experience an accelerated version of the transition
Rationale

"The major adaptations associated with transition from intrauterine to extrauterine life occur during the first 6 to 8 hours after birth," are "mediated by the sympathetic nervous system," and involve heart rate, respirations, temperature, and gastrointestinal function. "Most healthy newborns experience this transition, regardless of gestational age or type of birth; very preterm infants do not because of physiologic immaturity."

Watch for

(D) inverts the one stated exception. Very preterm infants do not show the transition periods at all, because of physiologic immaturity, rather than showing them faster.

5. A nurse wants to teach new parents how to engage with their newborn. Which of the six sleep-wake states is the optimal one for this?
  • ALight sleep, because the infant is easily roused
  • BDrowsy, because the infant is transitioning toward alertness
  • ✓Quiet alert, in which infants smile, vocalize, move in synchrony with speech, and watch their parents' faces
  • DActive alert, because motor activity indicates readiness to interact
Rationale

The six states form a continuum from deep sleep to extreme irritability: two sleep states, deep and light, and four wake states, drowsy, quiet alert, active alert, and crying. "The optimal state of arousal is the quiet alert state. During this state, infants smile, vocalize, move in synchrony with speech, watch their parents' faces, and respond to people talking to them."

Watch for

(D) is the reflex pick because active alert sounds like the most engaged of the wake states, and its motor activity reads as responsiveness. The alertness that supports interaction is the quiet kind. The ability to make smooth transitions between states is called state modulation.

6. A newborn breaks eye contact by turning the head away and begins to fuss during a prolonged interaction with a visitor. Which interpretation is correct?
  • AThese are signs of neurologic immaturity and should be reported
  • ✓These are purposeful behaviors the infant uses to control arousal, alongside withdrawing, pushing away, and falling asleep
  • CThese indicate hunger
  • DThese are engagement cues indicating readiness for more stimulation
Rationale

The chapter names four purposeful behaviors infants use to maintain arousal: "actively withdrawing by increasing physical distance," "rejecting by pushing away with hands and feet," "decreasing sensitivity by falling asleep or breaking eye contact by turning the head," and "signaling behaviors such as fussing and crying." Distress or disengagement cues include facial grimace, splaying of fingers, gaze aversion, staring, regurgitation, and jitteriness.

Watch for

(D) reads the cue backwards, and it is the intuitive error because a caregiver enjoying the interaction wants to continue it. Engagement cues are alertness, eye-to-eye contact, facial gaze, smiling, vocalization, smooth movements, and flexion of the arms and legs.

7. A student asks what triggers the newborn's first breath. Which response is accurate?
  • AThe thoracic squeeze during vaginal birth is the single trigger
  • ✓There is no single trigger; breathing results from a combination of chemical, mechanical, thermal, and sensory factors
  • CClamping the cord is the sole trigger, by raising carbon dioxide levels
  • DCold exposure alone stimulates the medullary respiratory center
Rationale

"There is no single trigger for newborn respiratory function. The initiation of respirations in the neonate is the result of a combination of chemical, mechanical, thermal, and sensory factors." Chemical factors include chemoreceptor activation from the relative hypoxia of labor, producing declining oxygen tension, rising carbon dioxide, and lowered pH that stimulate the medullary respiratory center. Clamping the cord also drops levels of a prostaglandin that can inhibit respirations, and raises blood pressure, which increases circulation and lung perfusion.

Watch for

(A) and (D) each promote one real contributor to sole cause, and (A) additionally uses a mechanism the chapter has demoted. Any answer naming one trigger is wrong on the chapter's own framing.

8. An infant is born by scheduled cesarean birth before labor began. Which respiratory consideration applies?
  • AThe absence of labor eliminates the risk of retained lung fluid
  • BLung fluid clearance depends mainly on the thoracic squeeze, so this infant will have significant retained fluid
  • ✓Infants born by cesarean without labor can retain lung fluid and are more likely to develop transient tachypnea of the newborn
  • DThe infant will require surfactant administration
Rationale

"Infants born by cesarean birth in which labor did not occur before birth can experience some lung fluid retention ... These infants are also more likely to develop transient tachypnea of the newborn (TTN)." Clearance depends on reduced fluid production in the days before labor and a catecholamine surge that promotes clearance during labor, with movement "through active transport into the interstitium."

Watch for

(B) uses the retired explanation. Traditionally the thoracic squeeze was credited with significant clearance, but "it appears that this event plays a minor role." The reason cesarean without labor matters is the missing catecholamine surge, not the missing squeeze.

9. Which description of surfactant's function is accurate?
  • AIt raises surface tension so the alveoli recoil more completely on exhalation
  • ✓It lowers surface tension, reducing the pressure required to keep alveoli open with inspiration and preventing total alveolar collapse on exhalation
  • CIt clears fluid from the alveoli by active transport
  • DIt is manufactured in the type I lung cells during the first hours after birth
Rationale

Surfactant is "a lipoprotein manufactured in type II lung cells." It "lowers surface tension, therefore reducing the pressure required to keep the alveoli open with inspiration, and prevents total alveolar collapse on exhalation, thereby maintaining alveolar stability," producing increased lung compliance and helping establish functional residual capacity. Without it, "more pressure must be generated for inspiration, which can soon tire or exhaust preterm or sick term infants."

Watch for

(A) inverts the direction, and it is the intuitive inversion because higher tension sounds like it would help a lung empty. Lowering tension is what keeps the alveolus from collapsing shut and needing to be reinflated from nothing with each breath.

10. A sleeping newborn has a respiratory pause of 14 seconds, then resumes breathing. How should the nurse document this?
  • AApnea, which requires evaluation
  • ✓Periodic breathing, which is normal and occurs most often during active rapid eye movement sleep
  • CAn apparent life-threatening event
  • DBradypnea, which requires stimulation
Rationale

Newborn breaths are "shallow and irregular, ranging from 30 to 60 breaths/min, with periods of breathing that include pauses in respirations lasting less than 20 seconds," occurring "most often during the active (rapid eye movement [REM]) sleep cycle." However, "apneic periods longer than 20 seconds are abnormal and should be evaluated."

Watch for

(A) is the reflex pick for anyone who treats any pause as apnea. The line is 20 seconds, and this pause falls below it. A distractor that calls a 15-second pause pathologic and one that dismisses a 25-second pause both fail on the same threshold.

11. A 1-week-old has significant nasal congestion. Why is this a greater concern in a newborn than in an older infant?
  • ANewborns have smaller nasal passages that occlude with minimal secretions
  • ✓Newborns are by preference nose breathers, and the reflex to open the mouth when the nose is obstructed is not present in most infants until 3 weeks after birth, so cyanosis or asphyxia can occur
  • CNewborns cannot cough effectively to clear secretions
  • DNasal congestion in newborns always indicates infection
Rationale

"Newborn infants are by preference nose breathers, which enhances the ability to coordinate sucking, swallowing, and breathing. The reflex response to nasal obstruction is to open the mouth to maintain an airway. This response is not present in most infants until 3 weeks after birth; therefore cyanosis or asphyxia can occur with nasal blockage."

Watch for

(A) is anatomically plausible and is not the chapter's reason. The danger is the missing rescue reflex, which makes nasal obstruction an airway problem rather than a comfort problem. Note separately that a sneeze "is usually a response to mucus in the nose and not an indicator of upper respiratory infection."

12. A newborn 6 hours old has bluish hands and feet. The trunk, lips, and mucous membranes are pink. How should the nurse document and act on this?
  • ACentral cyanosis; administer oxygen and notify the provider
  • ✓Acrocyanosis, a normal finding in the first 24 hours after birth
  • CMottling secondary to cold stress; place under a radiant warmer immediately
  • DPeripheral vasoconstriction indicating sepsis
Rationale

"Acrocyanosis, the bluish discoloration of hands and feet, is a normal finding in the first 24 hours after birth." Normally the newborn's color changes from blue to pink within the first 3 to 5 minutes after birth.

Watch for

(A) is the single most swapped newborn finding. What makes cyanosis significant is its location: hands and feet is peripheral and expected early, while lips and mucous membranes is central.

13. A different newborn has bluish lips and oral mucous membranes. How should the nurse interpret this?
  • AAcrocyanosis, expected in the first 24 hours
  • ✓Central cyanosis, which is abnormal and signifies hypoxemia
  • CA normal variant if the infant is crying
  • DCircumoral pallor secondary to vasoconstriction
Rationale

"Central cyanosis is abnormal and signifies hypoxemia. With central cyanosis, the lips and mucous membranes are bluish (circumoral cyanosis)."

(A) is the mirror error of the previous item. There is a second reasoning trap embedded here: "Because central cyanosis is a late sign of distress, newborns usually have significant hypoxemia when cyanosis appears," so its absence does not establish adequate oxygenation.

14. A newborn 90 minutes old has a respiratory rate of 92, intermittent grunting, nasal flaring, and mild retractions. Which condition does this most likely represent?
  • ✓Mild transient tachypnea of the newborn, which usually resolves in 48 to 72 hours
  • BRespiratory distress syndrome requiring surfactant
  • CA pneumothorax
  • DCongenital heart disease
Rationale

Mild transient tachypnea presents "during the first 1 to 2 hours after birth" with tachypnea "up to 100 breaths/min" and "intermittent grunting, nasal flaring, and mild retractions," usually resolving in 48 to 72 hours. More serious problems "tend to last beyond the first 2 hours after birth," with rates that "can exceed 120 breaths/min" and "moderate to severe retractions, grunting, pallor, and central cyanosis."

Watch for

(B) is the reflex pick because tachypnea with grunting is the classic distress picture. Three things separate this from serious disease: the rate stays under 100, the timing is within the first 2 hours, and the retractions are mild.

15. The nurse observes that a newborn's abdomen rises exaggeratedly with inspiration while the chest falls. How should this be interpreted?
  • ANormal abdominal breathing, which is characteristic of newborns
  • ✓Seesaw or paradoxic respirations, which are abnormal and should be reported
  • CNormal for the first few hours only
  • DEvidence of an upper airway obstruction
Rationale

Abdominal breathing is normal in newborns because respiratory function is largely diaphragmatic, and "the chest and abdomen of the newborn rise simultaneously with inspiration." By contrast, "seesaw or paradoxic respirations (exaggerated rise in abdomen with respiration as the chest falls) instead of abdominal respirations are abnormal and should be reported."

Watch for

(A) is the reflex pick because abdominal breathing genuinely is normal, and one word separates the two findings. Simultaneous rise is normal; the chest falling as the abdomen rises is not.

17. What causes functional closure of the foramen ovale after birth?
  • ARising pulmonary vascular resistance raises right atrial pressure
  • ✓Falling pulmonary vascular resistance lowers right atrial pressure while increased pulmonary blood flow raises left atrial pressure
  • CThe rise in arterial oxygen tension to about 50 mm Hg
  • DClamping the umbilical vein
Rationale

The first breaths reduce pulmonary vascular resistance, so "pressure in the right atrium declines. Increased pulmonary blood flow from the left side of the heart increases pressure in the left atrium, which causes a functional closure of the foramen ovale." The chapter adds a caveat worth holding: "During the first few days of life, crying can temporarily reverse the flow through the foramen ovale and lead to mild cyanosis."

Watch for

(C) is the mechanism for the other shunt. In utero fetal oxygen tension is 20 to 30 mm Hg; after birth, when arterial oxygen tension approximates 50 mm Hg, "the ductus arteriosus constricts in response to increased oxygenation," closing functionally within the first 24 hours and anatomically within 2 to 3 months.

18. A term newborn in deep sleep has an apical heart rate of 92. What is the nurse's interpretation?
  • ABradycardia requiring immediate notification
  • ✓Within the expected range for deep sleep, which is approximately 80 to 100 beats/min
  • CBradycardia suggesting congenital heart block
  • DAn artifact of counting for less than one full minute
Rationale

The term newborn's heart rate is 120 to 160 beats/min, dropping to approximately 80 to 100 in deep sleep and rising to 180 or higher with crying. A rate over 160 or under 100 "should be reevaluated within 30 minutes to 1 hour or when the activity of the infant changes." Persistent bradycardia is defined lower, at "less than 80 beats/min," and can signal congenital heart block or hypoxemia.

Watch for

(A) applies the awake range to a sleeping infant, and it is the most available error because 92 falls below the number most students memorized. The activity state changes the expected range, and even a rate under 100 prompts reevaluation within 30 minutes to an hour rather than immediate escalation.

19. A newborn's systolic blood pressure at 1 hour of age is about 15 mm Hg lower than the value recorded at birth. What does this represent?
  • AHypovolemia requiring a fluid bolus
  • ✓A common expected drop in the first hour of life, after which blood pressure increases predictably over the first 5 days
  • CEvidence of a patent ductus arteriosus
  • DAn error in cuff size
Rationale

"A drop in systolic BP (approximately 15 mm Hg) in the first hour of life is common," and blood pressure then "increases predictably over the first 5 days of life and then levels off." The bedside shortcut the chapter gives is that "the mean arterial pressure (MAP) should be nearly equivalent to the weeks of gestation," so a 40-week infant should have a mean arterial pressure of at least 40 mm Hg.

Watch for

(A) treats a normal early dip as shock. Blood pressure at birth runs 75 to 95 systolic, falls to 50 to 70 by 12 hours, and rises to 60 to 90 by 96 hours, so comparing a 12-hour value against the birth value produces a false alarm.

20. A soft systolic murmur is heard in a newborn who is feeding well, is pink, and has normal vital signs. Which statement is accurate?
  • ✓Most murmurs in the neonatal period have no pathologic significance, and more than half disappear by 6 months of age
  • BAny murmur in a newborn indicates a structural defect
  • CThe absence of accompanying symptoms rules out a cardiac defect
  • DMurmurs in newborns always resolve by 6 months
Rationale

"Most heart murmurs heard during the neonatal period have no pathologic significance, and more than one-half of the murmurs disappear by 6 months of age." A murmur "and accompanying signs such as poor feeding, apnea, cyanosis, or pallor is considered abnormal."

Watch for

(C) is the reassuring conclusion the chapter explicitly blocks: "There can be significant cardiac defects without a murmur or other symptoms." Neither the presence nor the absence of a murmur settles a cardiac question, which is what makes both directions testable.

21. A nursing student reports hearing a third heart sound in a term newborn. Which response is correct?
  • AA third heart sound is a normal finding in newborns
  • ✓The third and fourth heart sounds are not audible in newborns, so this finding should be re-evaluated
  • CA third heart sound is normal only during the first hour of life
  • DA third heart sound confirms a patent ductus arteriosus
Rationale

Newborn heart sounds are "of higher pitch, shorter duration, and greater intensity than during adult life. The first sound is typically louder and duller than the second sound, which is sharp. The third and fourth heart sounds are not audible in newborns."

Watch for

(A) is the trap for a student carrying a rule across chapters. A third heart sound in the pregnant woman after midpregnancy is a normal finding described elsewhere in this text; the same sound is not audible in her newborn. The apical impulse in the newborn is found at the fourth intercostal space left of the midclavicular line and is often visible and easily palpable through the thin chest wall.

22. A newborn is placed on a cold, uncovered scale for weighing. Which mode of heat loss does this represent?
  • AConvection
  • BRadiation
  • ✓Conduction
  • DEvaporation
Rationale

Conduction is "the loss of heat from the body surface to cooler surfaces in direct contact." The intervention the chapter derives from it is that "the scales used for weighing the newborn should have a protective cover," and that the newborn is placed on "a prewarmed bed" under a radiant warmer.

Watch for

(B) is the classic swap. Radiation is "the loss of heat from the body surface to a cooler solid surface not in direct contact but in relative proximity." The cue that separates them is touch: direct contact is conduction, proximity without contact is radiation.

23. A newborn's bassinet is positioned next to an exterior window on a cold night. Which mode of heat loss is the primary concern, and what is the intervention?
  • AConduction; place a warm blanket under the infant
  • ✓Radiation; move bassinets and examining tables away from outside windows
  • CConvection; raise the room humidity
  • DEvaporation; dry the infant thoroughly
Rationale

Radiation is heat lost to a cooler solid surface "not in direct contact but in relative proximity," which is why "bassinets and examining tables are placed away from outside windows, and care is taken to avoid direct air drafts." Convection, the flow of heat to cooler ambient air, is addressed by keeping newborn care areas between 22°C and 26°C with humidity between 30% and 60%, wrapping infants, and using a cap.

Watch for

(A) is the mirror error of the previous item. The infant is not touching the window, so proximity rather than contact is doing the work.

24. Which mode of heat loss does the chapter identify as the most significant cause of heat loss in the first few days of life, and what is the corresponding first intervention after birth?
  • AConvection; place a cap on the head
  • BRadiation; move the bassinet from the window
  • ✓Evaporation; dry the newborn thoroughly and completely
  • DConduction; use a prewarmed bed
Rationale

Evaporation is "the loss of heat that occurs when a liquid is converted to a vapor. In the newborn, heat loss by evaporation occurs as a result of moisture vaporization from the skin," intensified "by failing to completely dry the newborn after birth or with bathing." It is "the most significant cause of heat loss in the first few days of life."

Watch for

(A) and (D) are both real interventions and neither addresses the largest early loss. On a priority stem about the newly born infant, the answer is dry and cover, with the radiant warmer offered as the less optimal alternative to skin-to-skin contact, which reduces conductive and radiant loss while supporting maternal-infant interaction.

newborn heat loss and thermoregulationgeneral
25. A cold-stressed newborn does not shiver. Which mechanism generates heat instead, and what triggers it?
  • AShivering thermogenesis, triggered by a core temperature below 36.5°C
  • ✓Nonshivering thermogenesis by brown fat metabolism, triggered when skin temperature falls below 35°C to 36°C
  • CIncreased thyroid hormone release, triggered by cord clamping
  • DGlycogenolysis in skeletal muscle, triggered by hypoglycemia
Rationale

"The shivering mechanism of heat production is rarely operable in the newborn unless there is prolonged cold exposure." Newborns produce heat through nonshivering thermogenesis, "triggered when the skin temperature decreases to less than 35°C to 36°C (95°F to 96.8°F). This is accomplished primarily by metabolism of brown fat, which is unique to the newborn," and secondarily by increased metabolic activity in the brain, heart, and liver. Brown fat sits in superficial deposits in the interscapular region and axillae and deep deposits at the thoracic inlet, along the vertebral column, and around the kidneys.

Watch for

(A) applies the adult mechanism, and it pulls because shivering is what every other patient does. Note also that brown fat reserves are finite: present for several weeks, "rapidly depleted with cold stress," and greater in a term newborn than in a preterm one.

  • ✓Hyperbilirubinemia, because excessive fatty acids displace bilirubin from albumin-binding sites

The chapter's chain runs from norepinephrine-mediated vasoconstriction through increased respiratory rate and oxygen consumption, to pulmonary vasoconstriction with falling oxygen tension and pH and altered surfactant synthesis, to a rising basal metabolic rate with anaerobic glycolysis and metabolic acidosis, then to displaced bilirubin and hypoglycemia. 27. Two newborns each have a temperature of 38.1°C. The first is flushed, warm to the touch in the hands and feet, and lying in extension. The second is pale, cool in the hands and feet, and appears stressed. What do these pictures suggest?
  • ABoth indicate environmental overheating
  • ✓The first suggests environmental overheating; the second suggests sepsis, in which skin vessels are constricted
  • CThe first suggests sepsis; the second suggests overbundling
  • DNeither is abnormal, since 38.1°C is within the normal newborn range
Rationale

A temperature above 37.5°C is abnormally high. In environmental overheating the skin vessels dilate, the skin appears flushed, the hands and feet are warm, and the infant "assumes a posture of extension." In sepsis the vessels are constricted, the color is pale, the hands and feet are cool, and the infant appears stressed.

Watch for

(A) is the reflex pick because overbundling is the common cause and the easy fix, and it is precisely how a septic newborn gets missed. The peripheral perfusion is the discriminator, not the temperature. Hyperthermia develops faster in a newborn than in an adult because of the larger relative surface area and poorly functioning sweat glands, and it can cause neurologic injury and seizures.

newborn jaundicegeneral
28. A newborn is visibly jaundiced at 14 hours of age. How should this be classified and acted on?
  • APhysiologic jaundice; reassure the parents and reassess in the morning
  • ✓Pathologic jaundice, because jaundice appearing within 24 hours of birth indicates a potential pathologic process requiring investigation
  • CBreast milk jaundice, which typically appears in the first day
  • DNormal, since jaundice occurs in about 60% of term newborns
Rationale

The SAFETY ALERT states that "the appearance of jaundice during the first 24 hours of life or persistence beyond the ages previously delineated usually indicates a potential pathologic process that requires investigation." Physiologic jaundice "appears after 24 hours of age."

Watch for

(D) uses a true statistic to reach the wrong conclusion. Physiologic jaundice does occur in approximately 60% of term newborns and usually resolves without treatment, but the timing is what makes it physiologic, and this infant is inside the 24-hour window.

newborn jaundicegeneral
29. A term newborn's total serum bilirubin is 6.5 mg/dL at 60 hours of age, with no jaundice noted before 36 hours. Which classification applies?
  • APathologic, because the level exceeds 6 mg/dL
  • ✓Physiologic, since levels progressively increase to an average peak of 5 to 6 mg/dL by 72 to 96 hours and jaundice appeared after 24 hours
  • CPathologic, because visible jaundice is always abnormal
  • DCannot be classified without a direct bilirubin level
Rationale

In physiologic jaundice, levels "progressively increase from 2 mg/dL in cord blood to an average peak of 5 to 6 mg/dL by 72 to 96 hours," plateau near 3 mg/dL by one week, and reach adult levels of 2 mg/dL or less by two weeks. Jaundice becomes visible when the total serum bilirubin exceeds 6 to 7 mg/dL. The pathologic criteria are a rise of more than 0.2 mg/dL per hour, a level above the 95th percentile for age in hours, a direct bilirubin above 1.5 to 2 mg/dL, or jaundice lasting more than 2 weeks.

Watch for

(A) is the mirror error of the previous item, answering with the number instead of the timing. A total serum bilirubin means nothing without the infant's age in hours, which is what the 95th-percentile criterion formalizes.

30. A nurse notes yellowing of a newborn's sclera and face but not the abdomen or legs. What does this pattern indicate?
  • AAn atypical distribution suggesting a hemolytic process
  • ✓The expected cephalocaudal progression, since jaundice appears first in the head, especially the sclera and mucous membranes
  • CCarotenemia rather than jaundice
  • DPhototherapy has been effective on the lower body
Rationale

Jaundice is "generally noticeable first in the head, especially in the sclera and mucous membranes, and progresses gradually to the thorax, abdomen, and extremities."

(A) treats the normal progression as an unusual pattern, and it pulls because "sclera only" sounds like a localized finding rather than the earliest stage of a generalized one. A distractor describing jaundice appearing first on the extremities reverses the direction.

31. A mother whose 3-day-old is jaundiced asks whether she should stop breastfeeding and switch to formula. The infant has lost 9% of birth weight and is latching poorly. Which response is correct?
  • AStopping breastfeeding for 48 hours will resolve the jaundice
  • ✓Breastfeeding does not cause the jaundice; ineffective breastfeeding contributes to it, so the priority is improving the effectiveness and frequency of feeding
  • CSupplementing with water between feedings will help clear the bilirubin
  • DThis is breast milk jaundice caused by factors in the milk, so temporary interruption is indicated
Rationale

Breastfeeding-associated jaundice, also called suboptimal intake or starvation jaundice, occurs "during the first week of life" when the infant is "not feeding effectively," with less caloric and fluid intake, ongoing weight loss, and possible dehydration. "Breastfeeding does not cause the jaundice; rather lack of effective breastfeeding contributes to the hyperbilirubinemia." Feeding reduces bilirubin because it "stimulates peristalsis and produces more rapid passage of meconium, thus diminishing the amount of reabsorption of unconjugated bilirubin," and colostrum is "a natural laxative."

Watch for

(A) prescribes the opposite of the treatment, and it is the intuitive answer because the jaundice carries breastfeeding's name. (C) fails on the mechanism: water does not stimulate stooling, which is how feeding lowers bilirubin.

  • ABreastfeeding-associated jaundice, requiring feeding support
  • ✓Breast milk jaundice, or late-onset jaundice, typically occurring at 5 to 10 days of age
  • CPathologic jaundice from ABO incompatibility
  • DAcute bilirubin encephalopathy
Rationale

Breast milk jaundice "usually occurs at 5 to 10 days of age. Infants are typically feeding well and gaining weight appropriately," and it is attributed to "factors in the breast milk (e.g., pregnanediol, fatty acids, and β-glucuronidase) that either inhibit the conjugation of bilirubin or decrease the excretion." It peaks in the second week and can persist for 3 to 12 weeks, with no signs of hemolysis or liver dysfunction.

Watch for

(A) is the mirror error of the previous item, and the discriminator is how the baby is feeding, not what the baby is being fed. Poor feeding with weight loss in the first week is the early-onset form; feeding well and gaining at 5 to 10 days is the late-onset form.

  • ✓A normal Moro reflex
  • CA tonic neck reflex
  • DA crossed extension reflex
Rationale

The Moro reflex produces "symmetric abduction and extension of arms; fingers fan out and form a C with thumb and forefinger," after which the arms are "adducted in embracing motion and return to relaxed flexion. (A) treats a normal reflex as pathology. What would be abnormal is an asymmetric response, which "can indicate injury to brachial plexus, clavicle, or humerus," or persistence past 6 months. "Loss of fluid through urine, feces, lungs, increased metabolic rate, and limited fluid intake can result in a 5% to 10% loss of the birth weight over the first 3 to 5 days," primarily "extracellular water loss." The neonate "should regain the birth weight within 10 to 14 days."
