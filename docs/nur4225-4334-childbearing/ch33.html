defining postpartum hemorrhagethe current definition
1. How does ACOG currently define postpartum hemorrhage, and what did it replace?
  • ✓Cumulative blood loss of 1000 mL or more, or bleeding associated with signs and symptoms of hypovolemia within 24 hours of the birth process regardless of type of birth; it replaced the classic definition of 500 mL or more after vaginal birth or 1000 mL or more after cesarean birth
  • BBlood loss of 500 mL after any birth
  • CAny bleeding requiring transfusion
  • DBlood loss of 2000 mL or more
Rationale

ACOG revised the definition "from the classic one (≥500 mL after vaginal birth or ≥1000 mL after cesarean birth) to (1) cumulative blood loss ≥1000 mL or (2) bleeding associated with signs/symptoms of hypovolemia within 24 hours of the birth process regardless of type of birth."

Watch for

(B) is the old vaginal-birth threshold, and the change matters because the new definition applies one number to both routes and adds a clinical criterion that does not depend on measurement at all.

defining postpartum hemorrhagethe current definition
2. Why did the definition change to emphasize quantitative measurement?
  • ✓Because visual estimation can either overestimate or underestimate actual blood loss, so quantitative measurement is a more accurate way to identify hemorrhage
  • BBecause blood loss is now measured by hemoglobin alone
  • CBecause visual estimation always overestimates
  • DBecause the old definition was too sensitive
Rationale

"In practice settings, visual estimation can either overestimate or underestimate actual blood loss, so quantitative measurement is a more accurate way to identify hemorrhage." The chapter adds that health care professionals "are highly inaccurate in estimating blood loss in terms of volume," and that inaccurate estimation "has been shown to delay appropriate response to hemorrhage."

Watch for

(C) keeps one direction of error. Most often estimates come in low, but the chapter names both directions, which is why measurement replaces judgment.

defining postpartum hemorrhageearly versus late
3. A woman develops heavy bleeding 10 days after birth. How is this classified, and what are the usual causes?
  • AEarly PPH, usually from uterine atony
  • ✓Late or secondary PPH, occurring more than 24 hours and up to 12 weeks after birth, usually from subinvolution of the uterus, infection, retained placental fragments, or coagulopathy
  • CNormal lochia
  • DLate PPH, which occurs only after 6 weeks
Rationale

"Late or secondary PPH occurs more than 24 hours and up to 12 weeks after the birth," and its usual causes are subinvolution of the uterus, infection, retained placental fragments, or coagulopathy. Early, acute, or primary PPH "occurs within 24 hours of the birth."

Watch for

(A) applies the early differential to a late bleed. Atony is the leading cause within the first 24 hours, but a woman bleeding on day 10 is being evaluated for something left behind or an infection.

defining postpartum hemorrhageearly versus late
4. Why does the chapter emphasize discharge teaching about the signs of hemorrhage?
  • ✓Because today's shortened stays increase the potential for acute episodes of PPH to occur outside the hospital or birth center, and PPH can occur with little warning and often is not recognized until the mother has profound signs or symptoms
  • BBecause hemorrhage never occurs in the hospital
  • CBecause families are legally responsible for monitoring
  • DBecause late PPH is more common than early PPH
Rationale

"Today's health care environment encourages shortened stays after birth, which increases the potential for acute episodes of PPH to occur outside the traditional hospital or birth center setting. Education of the woman and her family on the signs and symptoms of hemorrhage is critical to include in the nurse's discharge teaching." And: "It can occur with little warning and often is not recognized until the mother has profound signs or symptoms."

Watch for

(D) inverts the frequency — atony in the first hours is the leading cause — and the teaching exists because discharge now happens before that risk window has fully closed.

risk factors and causes of pphatony and its timing
7. When is the greatest risk for early postpartum hemorrhage, and why does uterine contraction control bleeding?
  • ✓During the first hour after birth; the body of the uterus is a basket-weave of interlacing smooth muscle bundles through which many large maternal blood vessels pass, and bleeding is controlled when those fibers are well contracted
  • BDuring the first 24 hours; bleeding is controlled by platelet plugs
  • COn the second postpartum day; bleeding is controlled by vasoconstriction alone
  • DAfter discharge; bleeding is controlled by the endometrium regenerating
Rationale

"The greatest risk for early PPH is during the first hour after birth." And: "The body of the uterus is like a basket-weave of strong, interlacing smooth muscle bundles through which many large maternal blood vessels pass. After the expulsion or removal of the placenta, bleeding is controlled when these smooth muscle fibers in the uterus are well contracted."

Watch for

(B) names the ordinary hemostatic mechanism, which is not what controls postpartum bleeding — the muscle compressing the vessels is.

risk factors and causes of pphatony and its timing
8. Bleeding occurs after the placenta has been expelled. What is the most likely source, and how does this differ from bleeding earlier in the third stage?
  • ✓Most often uterine atony, though it may also be a sign of uterine prolapse; bleeding between the birth of the newborn and separation of the placenta more often comes from lacerations of the birth canal or cervix, and bleeding between separation and expulsion from incomplete separation, undue manipulation of the fundus, or excessive cord traction
  • BAlways a laceration, at every point in the third stage
  • CAlways retained fragments
  • DThe source cannot be determined by timing
Rationale

The chapter sorts the etiology by stage: from birth of the newborn to separation of the placenta, "lacerations of the birth canal or the cervix"; from separation to expulsion, "incomplete placental separation, undue manipulation of the fundus, or excessive traction on the umbilical cord"; and after expulsion, "most often the result of uterine atony but may also be a sign of uterine prolapse." Uterine atony "is the leading cause of early PPH."

Watch for

(D) discards a differential the chapter builds explicitly around timing, and the timing is often the only clue available at the moment.

retained placenta and placenta accretaretained products of conception
9. The placenta has not been expelled 35 minutes after birth, despite an upright posture, gentle cord traction, and uterine massage. What is this called, and what follows?
  • ✓Retained placenta; the initial management is manual separation and removal by the midwife or physician, after which the woman has an increased risk for postpartum hemorrhage and infection
  • BNormal third stage; continue to wait
  • CUterine inversion
  • DPlacenta accreta by definition
Rationale

"When the placenta has not been expelled within 30 minutes after birth despite an upright posture of the woman, gentle traction on the umbilical cord, and uterine massage, it is described as a retained placenta." Initial management "is manual separation and removal by the midwife or physician," and "after removal of a retained placenta, the woman has an increased risk for PPH and infection."

Watch for

(D) jumps to the adherent-placenta diagnosis, which is a specific abnormality of implantation rather than a description of elapsed time.

10. A woman has excessive bleeding and a boggy uterus after birth, and retained placental fragments are suspected. What may be required?
  • ✓Manual exploration to remove the fragments, and uterine curettage if necessary
  • BObservation only
  • CImmediate hysterectomy
  • DBed rest and iron supplementation
Rationale

With retained fragments, "the client may have excessive bleeding and the uterus feels boggy (soft) due to uterine atony. The midwife or physician may attempt manual exploration to remove the fragments; uterine curettage may be necessary" — the removal of uterine contents using a curette or vacuum suction.

(C) skips the entire escalation ladder. Hysterectomy is reserved for uncontrolled bleeding after other measures fail.

11. How do placenta accreta, increta, and percreta differ?
  • ✓Accreta is slight penetration of the myometrium, increta is deep penetration of the myometrium, and percreta is perforation of the myometrium and uterine serosa, possibly involving adjacent organs
  • BAccreta is the deepest and percreta the most superficial
  • CThey describe the location of the placenta rather than its depth
  • DThey are three names for the same finding
Rationale

The chapter defines each: "Placenta accreta—slight penetration of myometrium"; "placenta increta—deep penetration of myometrium"; "placenta percreta—perforation of myometrium and uterine serosa, possibly involving adjacent organs."

Watch for

(B) reverses the spectrum, and the depth determines the outcome: "hysterectomy may be indicated for all three types of placental adherence if bleeding is uncontrolled and is almost always required for placenta percreta and placenta increta."

retained placenta and placenta accretathe accreta spectrum
12. A woman is diagnosed antenatally with placenta accreta spectrum. What does the chapter say about attempting manual removal of the placenta at birth?
  • AManual removal should be attempted first to avoid surgery
  • ✓Bleeding may not occur unless manual removal is attempted, and with more extensive forms bleeding becomes profuse when removal is attempted; less blood is lost if the diagnosis is made antenatally and no attempt is made to manually remove the placenta
  • CManual removal is always successful when the diagnosis is known
  • DThe diagnosis cannot be made before birth
Rationale

"Bleeding may not occur unless manual removal of the placenta is attempted. With more extensive forms ..., bleeding becomes profuse when removal of the placenta is attempted. Less blood is lost if the diagnosis is made antenatally and no attempt is made to manually remove the placenta." Diagnosis is possible before birth with ultrasound and MRI, and "cesarean birth is recommended when the diagnosis is made prenatally."

Watch for

(A) applies the retained-placenta response to an adherent one, and it is the action that converts a controlled situation into a profuse hemorrhage. The rising incidence is "thought to be associated with the rise in cesarean birth rates."

15. A woman has a fourth-degree perineal laceration. Which order should the nurse question?
  • AA stool softener
  • ✓A rectal suppository for pain
  • CIncreased dietary fiber and fluids
  • DChemical ice packs to the perineum for the first 24 hours
Rationale

The Nursing Alert states: "To avoid injury to healing tissues, a woman with third- or fourth-degree lacerations is not given rectal suppositories or enemas." Care after repair includes position changes, analgesia, warm or cold applications, and chemical ice packs to the perineum for the first 24 hours following vaginal birth, with increased fiber and fluids and stool softeners to reduce constipation.

Watch for

(A) and (C) are both part of the plan, and the reassurance that goes with them is worth knowing: "clients should be reassured that a bowel movement will not disrupt the integrity of the sutures."

16. A woman reports persistent, worsening perineal pain and a feeling of pressure in the vagina 2 hours after birth. What should the nurse suspect?
  • ✓A hematoma, since pain is the most common symptom and the woman may report persistent or worsening perineal or rectal pain or a feeling of pressure in the vagina
  • BNormal afterpains
  • CUterine atony
  • DEndometritis
Rationale

Women with hematomas report "persistent or worsening perineal or rectal pain or a feeling of pressure in the vagina." Vulvar hematomas are "the most common type and often occur in association with operative vaginal birth, an episiotomy, or primigravidity. Pain is the most common symptom, and most vulvar hematomas are visible." Hematomas "are generally surgically evacuated."

Watch for

(B) is the diagnosis that lets a bleeding collection go unrecognized, and Chapter 21's rule applies here too: excessive discomfort in the first 1 to 2 days suggests a hematoma.

17. Which type of hematoma is least common but life threatening, and how might it present?
  • AVulvar hematoma, presenting with visible swelling
  • ✓Retroperitoneal hematoma, caused by laceration of one of the vessels attached to the hypogastric artery — usually associated with rupture of a cesarean scar during labor — which can cause minimal pain, with the initial symptoms being signs of shock
  • CVaginal hematoma, presenting with visible bleeding
  • DCervical hematoma, presenting with fever
Rationale

Retroperitoneal hematomas "are the least common but are life threatening. They are caused by laceration of one of the vessels attached to the hypogastric artery, usually associated with rupture of a cesarean scar during labor." And: "a retroperitoneal hematoma can cause minimal pain, and the initial symptoms may be signs of shock."

Watch for

(A) is the common and visible type, which is precisely why the rare one is dangerous — the usual cue, pain, may be absent, and shock arrives first.

uterine inversion and subinvolutioninversion
18. Immediately after the placenta is delivered, the woman has sudden hemorrhage, shock, and pain, and the uterus is not palpable abdominally. What has occurred?
  • AUterine atony
  • ✓Uterine inversion, in which the fundus collapses into the uterine cavity, turning inside out
  • CPlacental abruption
  • DAmniotic fluid embolism
Rationale

"Uterine inversion occurs when the fundus collapses into the uterine cavity (turning inside out) after birth." And: "The primary presenting signs of uterine inversion are sudden and include hemorrhage, shock, and pain. The uterus is not palpable abdominally." It is rare — 1 in 2000 to 1 in 20,000 vaginal births — but potentially life threatening.

Watch for

(A) shares the hemorrhage but not the absent fundus. An abdominally unpalpable uterus in a woman who has just given birth is the finding that separates them.

  • ✓Excessive umbilical cord traction when the placenta is attached high in the uterus, and fundal pressure when the uterus is not well contracted
  • BProlonged labor and macrosomia
  • CPreeclampsia and magnesium sulfate
  • DChorioamnionitis and prolonged rupture of membranes

The chapter names two causes, both iatrogenic: "excessive umbilical cord traction when the placenta is attached high in the uterus (such as at the fundus) and fundal pressure when the uterus is not well contracted."

20. What is the management sequence for uterine inversion?
  • ✓Fluid resuscitation, replacement of the uterus within the pelvic cavity, and correction of associated clinical conditions; tocolytics or halogenated anesthetics may be given to relax the uterus before replacement, and oxytocic agents are administered after the uterus is repositioned
  • BOxytocin first, then replacement of the uterus
  • CImmediate hysterectomy
  • DAggressive fundal massage
Rationale

Management is "fluid resuscitation, replacement of the uterus within the pelvic cavity, and correction of associated clinical conditions. Tocolytics or halogenated anesthetics may be given to relax the uterus before attempting replacement. (B) reverses the drug order, which matters: a contracted uterus cannot be replaced, so relaxation comes first and contraction second. And afterward "care must be taken to avoid subsequent aggressive fundal massage."

uterine inversion and subinvolutionsubinvolution
21. A woman 3 weeks postpartum has prolonged lochial discharge, irregular bleeding, and a larger-than-normal boggy uterus on pelvic examination. What is this, and how is it treated?
  • ✓Subinvolution, usually caused by retained placental fragments and pelvic infection, treated with ergonovine or methylergonovine 0.2 mg every 3 to 4 hours for 24 to 48 hours, with dilation and curettage to remove fragments and antibiotics if infection is the cause
  • BNormal involution
  • CUterine inversion
  • DEndometriosis
Rationale

Subinvolution is "delayed return of the enlarged uterus to nonpregnant size and function," caused by "retained placental fragments and pelvic infection," and it presents with "prolonged lochial discharge, irregular or excessive bleeding, and sometimes hemorrhage," with "a larger-than-normal uterus that can be boggy." Treatment is methylergonovine or ergonovine, dilation and curettage, and antibiotics if infection is the cause.

Watch for

(B) is what a woman still bleeding at 3 weeks may be told if her lochia is read as simply prolonged — the boggy, enlarged uterus is what makes it a diagnosis.

22. When does quantification of blood loss begin for a vaginal birth, and how is it done?
  • ✓Immediately after birth of the neonate, before delivery of the placenta, using a calibrated under-buttocks drape and weighing all blood-soaked items
  • BAfter the placenta is delivered, by visual estimation
  • CAt the end of the recovery period, by counting pads
  • DOnly if hemorrhage is suspected
Rationale

For vaginal birth, quantification begins "immediately after birth of the neonate, before delivery of the placenta," using "a calibrated under-buttocks drape and weighing all blood-soaked items." first-line management of atonythe sequence
25. A woman has excessive postpartum bleeding with a boggy uterus. What is the initial intervention, and what follows?
  • ✓Firm massage of the uterine fundus, expression of any clots, elimination of bladder distention, and a continuous IV infusion of 10 to 40 units of oxytocin in 1000 mL of lactated Ringer's or normal saline; if the uterus does not contract in response to oxytocin, other uterotonic medications are administered
  • BImmediate administration of carboprost
  • CBimanual compression before any other measure
  • DTransfusion of packed red blood cells first
Rationale

"The initial intervention in management of excessive postpartum bleeding due to uterine atony is firm massage of the uterine fundus," followed by "expression of any clots in the uterus," "elimination of bladder distention," and "continuous IV infusion of 10 to 40 units of oxytocin added to 1000 mL of lactated Ringer's solution or normal saline solution." Other uterotonics follow if the uterus does not respond.

Watch for

(C) is a later step: bimanual compression means "inserting a fist into the vagina and pressing the knuckles against the anterior side of the uterus and then placing the other hand on the abdomen and massaging the uterus," used when the first measures have not worked.

  • ABecause misoprostol has no contraindications
  • ✓Because its entry reads "consider use only for clients with hypertension or asthma" — the two conditions that rule out methylergonovine and carboprost — so when those drugs are unavailable, misoprostol is what remains

Misoprostol's contraindication entry reads "consider use only for clients with hypertension or asthma," the mirror image of methylergonovine's hypertension contraindication and carboprost's asthma contraindication. The Emergency box lists rapid shallow respirations; a rapid, weak, irregular pulse; decreasing blood pressure marked as a late sign; cool, pale, clammy skin; decreasing urinary output; lethargy progressing to coma; anxiety progressing to coma; and decreased central venous pressure. Interventions are to call for assistance and equipment, start an IV infusion per standing orders, ensure a patent airway and give oxygen, and continue to monitor status.

Watch for

(E) describes a warm, well-perfused patient — the opposite of the peripheral vasoconstriction that diverts blood to the brain and heart in shock.

37. What are the two goals of managing hemorrhagic shock, and what is the fluid resuscitation rule?
  • ✓Restoring circulating blood volume and eliminating the cause of the hemorrhage; crystalloid solution is given rapidly at 3 mL infused for every 1 mL of estimated blood loss
  • BRestoring blood pressure alone; fluid is given at 1 mL per 1 mL of loss
  • CPreventing infection and maintaining comfort; fluid is restricted

Rationale

The goals are "restoring circulating blood volume and eliminating the cause of the hemorrhage (e.g., lacerations, uterine atony, or inversion)," restated as restoring "oxygen delivery to the tissues" and maintaining cardiac output. The rule: "a rapid IV infusion of crystalloid solution is given at a rate of 3 mL infused for every 1 mL of estimated blood loss (e.g., 3000 mL infused for 1000 mL of blood loss)." Two large-bore IV lines facilitate resuscitation.

Watch for

(B) under-replaces by a factor of three, and the chapter also warns the other way: "fluid resuscitation must be monitored carefully because fluid overload can occur," most often with colloid therapy.
