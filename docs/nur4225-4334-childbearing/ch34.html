1. How does the chapter define low birth weight, very low birth weight, and extremely low birth weight?
  • ✓Less than 2500 g, less than 1500 g, and less than 1000 g respectively, with low birth weight defined regardless of gestational age
  • BLess than 3000 g, less than 2000 g, and less than 1500 g
  • CLess than 2500 g only for preterm infants
  • DThe categories apply only before 37 weeks
Rationale

A low-birth-weight infant is "an infant whose birth weight is less than 2500 g (5.5 lb.), regardless of gestational age"; very low birth weight is "less than 1500 g (3.3 lb.)"; extremely low birth weight is "less than 1000 g (2.2 lb.)."

Watch for

(C) attaches a gestational-age condition the definition explicitly rejects, and that phrase is what allows a term growth-restricted infant and a preterm appropriately grown infant both to be low birth weight for entirely different reasons.

2. An infant's birth weight falls below the 10th percentile on intrauterine growth curves, with head circumference within normal parameters. How is this classified?
  • ASmall for gestational age with symmetric intrauterine growth restriction
  • ✓Small for gestational age with asymmetric growth restriction, in which head circumference remains within normal parameters while birth weight falls below the 10th percentile
  • CAppropriate for gestational age
  • DLarge for gestational age
Rationale

Small for gestational age describes "an infant whose rate of intrauterine growth was slow and whose birth weight falls below the 10th percentile." Asymmetric growth restriction is "growth restriction in which head circumference remains within normal parameters while birth weight falls below the 10th percentile," while symmetric restriction is "growth restriction in which weight, length, and head circumference all are affected."

(A) is the pattern in which everything is small, which points to a chronic, early insult rather than a late one — the same distinction Chapter 26 draws antenatally.

5. Two infants both weigh 2000 g: one is preterm and one is postterm. What principle does the chapter give?
  • ✓Their potential problems and care needs differ, and in general the closer infants are to term in gestational age and birth weight, the easier their adjustment to the external environment
  • BTheir care needs are identical because their weights are identical
  • CThe postterm infant is always sicker
  • DWeight alone determines risk
Rationale

"The potential problems and care needs of the preterm infant weighing 2000 g (4.4 lb.) differ from those of the term or postterm infant of equal weight," and "in general, the closer infants are to term in relation to gestational age and birth weight, the easier is their adjustment to the external environment." Preterm infants "are at risk because their organ systems are immature and they lack adequate nutrient reserves."

Watch for

(D) is the reasoning that would treat two 2000-g infants the same, when one has immature organs and the other has mature organs and a failing placenta behind it.

7. What is the normal respiratory rate for a newborn, and what are the early signs of respiratory distress?
  • ✓30 to 60 times per minute with significant reliance on the abdominal muscles; early signs are chest wall retractions, flaring of the nares, and expiratory grunting
  • B12 to 20 per minute; early signs are bradypnea and apnea
  • C60 to 80 per minute; the first sign is generalized cyanosis
  • D30 to 60 per minute; the first sign is seesaw breathing
Rationale

"Infants normally breathe between 30 and 60 times/min, relying significantly on their abdominal muscles." Early signs of distress are "chest wall retractions, flaring of the nares, and expiratory grunting," worsening to "seesaw breathing patterns, retractions, flaring of the nares, expiratory grunting, apneic spells," with color progressing "from pink to circumoral cyanosis and then to generalized cyanosis."

Watch for

(D) names a later sign as the first, which is the difference between intervening early and intervening after decompensation.

8. A preterm infant has pauses in breathing lasting up to 10 seconds followed by rapid respirations. How is this classified, and how does it differ from apnea?
  • ✓Periodic breathing, a pattern commonly seen in preterm infants; apnea is a cessation of breathing of 20 seconds or longer, or a shorter pause accompanied by bradycardia, cyanosis, or hypotonia
  • BApnea, requiring immediate resuscitation
  • CNormal newborn breathing at any gestational age
  • DPeriodic breathing, which is always pathologic
Rationale

Preterm infants "exhibit up to 10-second respiratory pauses followed by compensatory rapid respirations" — periodic breathing, "a respiratory pattern commonly seen in preterm infants." Apnea is "a cessation of breathing of 20 seconds or longer, or a shorter pause accompanied by bradycardia, cyanosis, or hypotonia."

Watch for

(B) treats a common pattern as an emergency, and the discriminator is not only the 20-second clock but the accompanying findings — a 12-second pause with bradycardia is apnea.

12. What is a neutral thermal environment, and how is it achieved?
  • ✓The environmental temperature at which oxygen consumption is minimal but adequate to maintain body temperature, achieved with a radiant warmer bed or an incubator with control settings
  • BThe temperature at which the infant shivers least
  • CA room temperature of 20°C
  • DThe temperature at which the infant's skin feels warm to touch
Rationale

"The goal of thermoregulation is to create a neutral thermal environment (NTE), which is the environmental temperature at which oxygen consumption is minimal but adequate to maintain body temperature," achieved with "a radiant warmer bed or ... an incubator with control settings." The nurse works against four mechanisms of heat transfer: convection, conduction, radiation, and evaporation.

Watch for

(D) substitutes a subjective check for a physiologic definition, and the definition is what explains why the goal is oxygen consumption rather than comfort.

cold stress in the preterm infantthe cold-stress cycle and its signs
13. How does the chapter describe the cold-stress cycle in a preterm infant?
  • ✓The infant tries to conserve heat and burns more calories, causing metabolism to increase and further stressing the already compromised neonate, and preterm infants have few reserves in terms of calories and fat stores
  • BThe infant shivers, generating enough heat to compensate
  • CMetabolism decreases, conserving energy
  • DCold stress affects only temperature, not metabolism
Rationale

"Because preterm infants have few reserves in terms of calories and fat stores, they can easily lose heat and develop hypothermia. Cold stress results when the infant tries to conserve heat and burns more calories, causing the metabolism to increase, further stressing the already compromised neonate." The chapter calls prevention "a critical nursing role."

Watch for

(C) reverses the metabolic direction, which would make cold stress an energy-sparing state rather than the energy-consuming spiral that produces hypoglycemia and acidosis.

16. When do retinal vessels develop, and when is the retina no longer susceptible to retinopathy of prematurity?
  • ✓They begin to form in utero at approximately 16 weeks in response to vascular endothelial growth factor and continue until maturity at approximately 42 to 43 weeks after conception; once the retina is completely vascularized, the vessels are not susceptible to ROP
  • BThey form entirely in the first trimester
  • CThey mature at 37 weeks
  • DThe retina remains susceptible throughout childhood
Rationale

"The normal retinal vessels begin to form in utero at approximately 16 weeks in response to the release of vascular endothelial growth factor (VEGF). These vessels continue to develop until they reach maturity at approximately 42 to 43 weeks after conception. Once the retina is completely vascularized, the retinal vessels are not susceptible to ROP."

Watch for

(C) ends the vulnerable window at term, when it actually extends past it — which is why screening continues until vascularization is complete rather than until discharge.

retinopathy of prematuritywhen the retina is vulnerable
17. What mechanism does the chapter propose for the injury in retinopathy of prematurity?
  • ✓The mechanism is unclear; fluctuating blood oxygen levels may temporarily stop the release of factors responsible for retinal vascularization, and as retinal tissue becomes hypoxic, vascular growth factors are released, producing neovascularization with capillary hemorrhages, fibrotic scarring, and possible retinal detachment
  • BDirect oxygen toxicity burns the retina
  • CBright light in the nursery damages the retina
  • DThe mechanism is fully understood and is purely genetic
Rationale

"The mechanism of injury in ROP is unclear. Fluctuating blood oxygen levels may temporarily stop the release of factors responsible for vascularization of the retina. As the retinal tissue becomes hypoxic, the release of vascular growth factors occurs, and neovascularization (development of new blood vessels) occurs in the retina and vitreous, with capillary hemorrhages, fibrotic scarring, and possible retinal detachment."

Watch for

(B) is the simplified version taught for decades, and the word that matters in the real mechanism is *fluctuating* — it is the swing rather than the level alone that the nursing intervention targets.

  • CAt discharge only
  • DAt 6 months of age

(B) treats a months-long evolving process as a one-time finding, and a normal early examination says nothing about what happens over the following weeks.

retinopathy of prematurityscreening and prevention
19. What does the chapter identify as the key to managing retinopathy of prematurity, and what treatments exist?
  • ✓Prevention and early detection, with blood oxygen levels closely monitored and significant fluctuations avoided, oxygen and ventilator settings adjusted to keep saturations within acceptable levels; treatments are laser photocoagulation or intraocular injections of anti-VEGF medications
  • BTreatment with corticosteroids
  • CImmediate surgery at diagnosis in every case
  • DNo treatment is available
Rationale

"The key to the management of ROP is prevention and early detection. Blood oxygen levels in the preterm infant should be closely monitored, and significant fluctuations should be avoided. Oxygen and ventilator settings should be adjusted to keep oxygen saturations within acceptable levels." Treatments are "laser photocoagulation or intraocular injections of anti-VEGF medications, which inhibit the release of VEGF."

Watch for

(D) would remove the reason for screening, and the anti-VEGF treatment mirrors the mechanism exactly — the same growth factor that builds the retina drives the damage when it surges.

20. What is bronchopulmonary dysplasia, and in which infants does it most commonly occur?
  • ✓A chronic pulmonary condition occurring most commonly in preterm infants requiring mechanical ventilation
  • BAn acute infection of the lungs in term infants
  • CA congenital malformation of the airways
  • DA complication of postmaturity
Rationale

Bronchopulmonary dysplasia is "a chronic pulmonary condition occurring most commonly in preterm infants requiring mechanical ventilation." Its etiology is multifactorial, including "pulmonary immaturity, surfactant deficiency, lung injury and stretch, barotrauma, inflammation caused by oxygen exposure, inadequate nutrition, fluid overload, ligation of a PDA, and familial predisposition."

Watch for

(D) attaches the complication to the wrong end of the gestational-age ladder. Postmature infants face meconium aspiration, not the ventilator-associated chronic lung disease of prematurity.

22. What do bronchopulmonary dysplasia and retinopathy of prematurity have in common, as the chapter presents them?
  • ✓Both are complications of the treatments that keep the preterm infant alive — oxygen and ventilation — and for both, prevention outranks treatment
  • BBoth are infections
  • CBoth occur only in term infants
  • DBoth are congenital
Rationale

The chapter presents both as the same causal family: oxygen and ventilation rescue the lungs and produce bronchopulmonary dysplasia and retinopathy of prematurity. For each, the stated key to management is prevention — avoiding oxygen fluctuations in one and minimizing ventilation exposure in the other.

(B) misclassifies the mechanism, which matters because it would point toward antibiotics rather than toward the ventilator and oxygen settings that are the actual targets.

23. Which three conditions does the chapter enumerate in the etiology of necrotizing enterocolitis?
  • ✓Intestinal ischemia, bacterial colonization of the initially sterile gastrointestinal tract with harmful organisms, and enteral feeding — especially formula feeding
  • BInfection, dehydration, and hypoglycemia
  • CPrematurity, jaundice, and hypothermia
  • DMaternal diabetes, macrosomia, and birth trauma
Rationale

The chapter enumerates intestinal ischemia — from "asphyxia/hypoxia or events that cause a redistribution of blood flow away from the GI tract (e.g., hypotension, hypovolemia, severe stress)" — bacterial colonization with organisms such as Klebsiella, Escherichia coli, and Clostridium, and enteral feeding: "the majority of infants with NEC had received some type of enteral feeding."

Watch for

(C) names prematurity, which is a risk factor in Box 34.6 rather than one of the three mechanisms — and knowing the mechanisms is what makes the protective factor make sense.

24. A nurse is caring for a very-low-birth-weight infant. What does the chapter identify as protective against necrotizing enterocolitis?

  • BEarly formula feeding to build gut tolerance
  • CWithholding all enteral feeding indefinitely
  • DProphylactic antibiotics
Rationale

"Breast milk seems to have a protective effect against the development of NEC—it is rare among infants who are exclusively fed breast milk," and "the use of probiotics such as Bifidobacterium infantis and Lactobacillus acidophilus assist in the establishment of normal bowel flora and appear to decrease the incidence of NEC."

28. A mother of a critically ill preterm infant grieves in preparation for her baby's possible death while still hoping the baby survives. Which parental task is this, and when does it begin?

  • BAccepting that the baby is not healthy and full term

In anticipatory grief, "the parent grieves in preparation for the infant's possible death, although the parent clings to the hope that the infant will survive," and the process "begins during labor ... and lasts until the infant dies or shows evidence of surviving." (B) is the next task, "grief and depression typify this phase, which persists until the infant is out of danger and is expected to survive" — a different emotional problem with a different endpoint.

"The family can be given a tour of the NICU or shown a video to prepare them for the sights and activities of the unit" when a high-risk birth is anticipated, and after an unexpected preterm birth, "the parents can be given a booklet, view a video, or have someone describe what they will see."

Watch for

(D) withholds the preparation that makes the first sight of the unit survivable, and the first stated purpose of the nurse's presence is to help parents see past the equipment.

  • ✓Because during labor and birth the increased oxygen demands of the postmature fetus are not fully met, and insufficient gas exchange in the postmature placenta increases the likelihood of intrauterine hypoxia, which can result in passage of meconium in utero and raise the risk for meconium aspiration syndrome

"The perinatal mortality rate is significantly higher in the postmature fetus and neonate," because "during labor and birth the increased oxygen demands of the postmature fetus are not fully met."
