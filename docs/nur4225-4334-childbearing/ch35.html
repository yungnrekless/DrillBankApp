1. Which immunoglobulin crosses the placenta, and when does it reach its nadir?
  • ✓IgG, reaching a nadir at 3 to 4 months of age for term newborns and at 3 months for preterm infants, with virtually all of the infant's IgG endogenous by 10 to 12 months
  • BIgA, reaching a nadir at 6 months
  • CIgM, reaching a nadir at 1 month
  • DAll immunoglobulins cross equally
Rationale

"Immunoglobulin G (IgG) crosses the placenta to provide protection to the newborn. IgG values typically reach a nadir at 3 to 4 months of age for term newborns and at 3 months for preterm infants. By 10 to 12 months of age, virtually all of the infant's IgG is endogenous."

Watch for

(D) is contradicted by the next sentence in the chapter: "IgA and IgM do not cross the placenta, and both require time to reach optimal levels after birth." That gap is what colostrum partly fills.

early versus late onset sepsisthe two patterns
5. How does the chapter define early-onset and late-onset neonatal sepsis?
  • ✓Early-onset is invasive bacterial infection of the blood or cerebrospinal fluid occurring within the first 72 hours of life; late-onset occurs after 72 hours and up to 30 days of life
  • BEarly-onset is within 24 hours; late-onset is after 24 hours
  • CEarly-onset is within the first week; late-onset after the first month
  • DThe two terms are interchangeable
Rationale

Early-onset sepsis is "invasive bacterial infection of the blood and/or cerebrospinal fluid (CSF) that occurs within the first 72 hours of life," and late-onset sepsis occurs "after 72 hours and up to 30 days of life."

Watch for

(B) moves the boundary to the first day, which would reclassify most early-onset cases and obscure the fact that the 72-hour line separates maternally transmitted infection from postnatally acquired infection.

6. Which organisms are associated with early-onset versus late-onset sepsis?
  • ✓Early-onset: group B streptococci, Escherichia coli, and coagulase-negative staphylococci; late-onset: coagulase-negative staphylococci, Klebsiella organisms, enterobacter, E. coli, or Candida species
  • BEarly-onset: Candida only; late-onset: group B streptococci
  • CBoth are caused only by group B streptococci
  • DOrganisms are identical in both
Rationale

Early-onset sepsis is associated with "group B streptococci (GBS), Escherichia coli, and coagulase-negative staphylococci," and late-onset with "coagulase-negative staphylococci, Klebsiella organisms, enterobacter, E. coli, or Candida species." Early-onset is "more often associated with maternal intrapartum transmission of invasive organisms," while late-onset "can include maternally derived infection or health care–acquired infection."

Watch for

(D) collapses a distinction that determines the empiric antibiotic choice, which differs between the two.

10. What does the chapter say about the signs of neonatal sepsis?
  • ✓As a general rule, the signs of early-onset sepsis are nonspecific and nonlocalizing, differential diagnosis can be difficult because the signs resemble noninfectious problems such as anemia or hypoglycemia, and late-onset signs are also nonspecific and similar
  • BThe signs are highly specific and localize to the site of infection
  • CFever is always present
  • DSigns appear only after 72 hours
Rationale

"As a general rule, the signs of early-onset sepsis are nonspecific and nonlocalizing," and "a differential diagnosis can be difficult because signs of sepsis are similar to signs of noninfectious neonatal problems such as anemia or hypoglycemia." Late-onset signs "are also nonspecific and similar to the signs of early-onset sepsis."

Watch for

(C) expects an adult presentation. The chapter's general list names "temperature instability (usually hypothermia)" — the newborn is more likely to be cold than febrile.

12. What does the chapter say about the leukocyte count in diagnosing neonatal sepsis?
  • ✓It is not a reliable indicator; the total neutrophil count, immature to total neutrophil ratio, absolute neutrophil count, platelet count, procalcitonin, and C-reactive protein can be used along with maternal and infant history and clinical examination
  • BAn elevated leukocyte count confirms sepsis
  • CA normal leukocyte count rules out sepsis
  • DNo laboratory studies are useful
Rationale

"The leukocyte count is not a reliable indicator of sepsis; however, the total neutrophil count, immature to total neutrophil ratio, absolute neutrophil count, platelet count, procalcitonin, and C-reactive protein can be used, along with maternal and infant history and infant clinical examination, to diagnose sepsis." These are "likely adjuncts for the confirmation of neonatal sepsis."

Watch for

(C) is the more dangerous of the two wrong readings, because it would stop a workup in an infant whose only reliable evidence is the clinical picture. Cultures of blood, cerebrospinal fluid, and urine are obtained.

13. What is systemic inflammatory response syndrome as the chapter defines it for the newborn?
  • ✓A syndrome recognized by the presence of two or more of the following: hypothermia or fever, tachypnea or hyperventilation, tachycardia, bradycardia, proven or suspected sepsis, or a high or low white blood cell count
  • BA syndrome requiring a positive blood culture
  • CA syndrome defined by fever alone
  • DA syndrome seen only in preterm infants
Rationale

Neonatal sepsis "can be defined as a systemic inflammatory response syndrome that may occur secondary to infection," and SIRS is "recognized by the presence of two or more of the following signs and symptoms: hypothermia or fever, tachypnea or hyperventilation, tachycardia, bradycardia, proven or suspected sepsis, or a high or low white blood cell count."

Watch for

(B) requires a confirmation that often arrives after treatment must begin, and the definition deliberately includes "proven or suspected sepsis" as one criterion among several.

17. Why is breastfeeding or human milk feeding encouraged in an infant at risk for infection?
  • ✓Colostrum contains IgA, which offers protection against infection in the gastrointestinal tract; human milk contains an iron-binding protein that exerts a bacteriostatic effect on E. coli, as well as macrophages and lymphocytes, and passive transfer of maternal immunity reduces vulnerability to pathogens such as respiratory syncytial virus
  • BBecause human milk is easier to digest, with no immune effect
  • CBecause breastfeeding prevents all neonatal infections
  • DBecause breast milk is sterile
Rationale

"Colostrum contains IgA, which offers protection against infection in the GI tract. Human milk contains iron-binding protein that exerts a bacteriostatic effect on E. coli. Human milk also contains macrophages and lymphocytes. The vulnerability of infants to common mucosal pathogens such as respiratory syncytial virus can be reduced by passive transfer of maternal immunity in the colostrum and breast milk."

Watch for

(B) treats milk as nutrition only, and the IgA point is exactly what fills the gap left by the immunoglobulin that does not cross the placenta.

  • BEarly-onset occurs at 3 to 4 weeks; late-onset within 24 hours
  • CBoth present identically as meningitis
  • DEarly-onset presents only as a skin infection
Rationale

Early-onset GBS "develops during the first 7 days, most often within 12 to 24 hours after birth" and "usually results from vertical transmission from the birth canal," presenting as "systemic infection or respiratory illness that mimics the symptoms of severe respiratory distress," with rapid development of "pneumonia, shock, and, less often, meningitis."   • ✓Ampicillin in combination with an aminoglycoside for suspected infection; if GBS is identified as the cause, penicillin G alone may be given, with dosage and length of treatment depending on whether bacteremia or meningitis is being treated
  • BVancomycin alone throughout
  • CNo antibiotics until cultures return

Rationale

"The usual treatment for newborns with suspected GBS infection is ampicillin in combination with an aminoglycoside.
