1. A woman with mitral stenosis is described as symptomatic with marked limitation of activity. Which New York Heart Association class does this describe, and what does the classification measure?
  • AClass II; it measures the mortality rate associated with her lesion
  • ✓Class III; it measures the degree of disability — what she can physically do
  • CClass III; it measures the mortality rate associated with her lesion
  • DClass IV; it measures disability
Rationale

The NYHA classification is by symptoms and activity limitation: class I asymptomatic without limitation, class II symptomatic with slight limitation, class III symptomatic with marked limitation, and class IV symptomatic with inability to carry on any physical activity without discomfort.

Watch for

(D) is the class for a woman who cannot carry on any activity without discomfort — marked limitation is not the same as no activity at all. And the chapter notes that "the degree of disability experienced by the woman ... is often more important in the treatment and prognosis ... than the diagnosis."

2. A woman has Marfan syndrome with aortic involvement. Which Box 30.1 risk group does this fall into, and what does the group number indicate?
  • AGroup I, indicating a mortality rate under 1%
  • BGroup II, indicating a mortality rate of 5% to 15%
  • ✓Group III, indicating a mortality rate of 25% to 50%
  • DGroup III, indicating marked limitation of activity
Rationale

Box 30.1 sorts lesions by maternal mortality rate: group I under 1%, group II 5% to 15%, and group III 25% to 50%. Group III contains pulmonary hypertension, complicated coarctation of the aorta, endocarditis, and Marfan syndrome with aortic involvement.

Watch for

(D) reads the group as though it were an NYHA class. Group numbers describe how likely the lesion is to kill her; class numbers describe what she can do. Marfan syndrome with a normal aorta sits in group II, so the same named syndrome appears in two groups depending on severity.

4. Why does the chapter identify fever as a particular danger for a woman with cardiac disease?
  • ✓Fever is the major cause of cardiac decompensation during pregnancy
  • BFever causes hypotension and reduced preload
  • CFever is unrelated to cardiac workload
  • DFever indicates endocarditis in every case
Rationale

"Fever is the major cause of cardiac decompensation during pregnancy." Infections are treated promptly "because they can complicate the condition by accelerating the heart rate and by direct spread of organisms (e.g., streptococci) to the heart structures," and the woman is told to notify her provider at the first sign of an upper respiratory infection, especially if fever is present.

Watch for

(D) names a real complication and makes it the only explanation, which would delay treatment of the far more common causes. Perinatal mortality of up to 50% is anticipated with persistent cardiac decompensation.

6. A woman at 26 weeks with cardiac disease has a pulse of 104 beats/min, a respiratory rate of 26 breaths/min, and pale, cool, clammy skin. How should the nurse interpret these findings?
  • AAs expected pregnancy changes at the peak of cardiac output
  • ✓As concerning per the Nursing Alert thresholds, with pale, cool, clammy skin possibly indicating cardiac shock
  • CAs signs of infection only
  • DAs within normal limits since the pulse is under 120
Rationale

The Nursing Alert states that "a pulse rate of 100 beats/min or greater or a respiratory rate of 25 breaths/min or greater is a concern," and that "pale, cool, clammy skin may indicate cardiac shock." The nurse checks respiratory status frequently for dyspnea, coughing, or crackles at the lung bases.

Watch for

(A) uses a true fact — cardiac output peaks between 20 and 26 weeks, or 25 and 30 weeks by the management page — to normalize findings the chapter sets explicit thresholds for.

9. What is the governing principle of antepartum therapy for a pregnant woman with heart disease, and which coexisting conditions are treated because of it?
  • ✓Minimizing stress on the heart, which intensifies as cardiac output increases; emotional stress, hypertension, anemia, hyperthyroidism, and obesity are treated because each adds workload
  • BMaximizing cardiac output to perfuse the placenta
  • CRestricting all activity until birth
  • DPreventing weight gain below 15 pounds
Rationale

"Therapy for the pregnant woman with heart disease is focused on minimizing stress on the heart, which intensifies as cardiac output increases," and "the workload of the cardiovascular system is reduced by appropriate treatment of any coexisting emotional stress, hypertension, anemia, hyperthyroidism, or obesity."

Watch for

(B) has the physiology backwards for this population. Cardiac output rises 30% to 45% in normal pregnancy, and that rise is precisely the stress being managed.

10. A woman with cardiac disease is prescribed warfarin. What nutritional teaching does the chapter specify, and which anticoagulant does Table 30.1 list as having no reported fetal side effects?
  • ✓Avoid foods high in vitamin K such as raw dark green leafy vegetables, which counteract warfarin; heparin's side-effect column reads "None reported"
  • BIncrease vitamin K intake to prevent hemorrhage; heparin crosses the placenta
  • CAvoid vitamin C; warfarin has no reported fetal effects
  • DNo dietary teaching is needed; both drugs are equivalent in pregnancy
Rationale

A woman taking warfarin needs teaching "to avoid foods high in vitamin K, such as raw, dark green leafy vegetables, which counteract the effects of warfarin," plus a folic acid supplement. Table 30.1 lists warfarin as crossing the placenta with fetal hemorrhage, embryopathy, and CNS abnormalities, while heparin's potential side effects are "None reported."

Watch for

(B) reverses the vitamin K relationship. Vitamin K is the antidote to warfarin's effect, so loading up on it defeats the anticoagulation the woman needs.

11. Which drug classes in Table 30.1 carry fetal structural harm in their side-effect column?
  • ADigoxin and lidocaine
  • ✓Warfarin and ACE inhibitors
  • CHeparin and procainamide
  • DVerapamil and quinidine
Rationale

Warfarin "crosses placenta; fetal hemorrhage, embryopathy, CNS abnormalities," and ACE inhibitors carry "oligohydramnios, IUGR, prematurity, neonatal hypotension, renal failure, anemia, death, skull ossification defect, limb contractures, patent ductus arteriosus." Digoxin has "no evidence for unfavorable side effects on the fetus," and heparin has none reported.

Watch for

(D) names two drugs with real but lesser cautions — one uncertain fetal death with verapamil, and transient neonatal thrombocytopenia with high-dose quinidine — which is different from a list of structural anomalies.

antepartum cardiac management and drugscardiac surgery in pregnancy
12. A woman requires cardiac surgery during pregnancy. What does the chapter say about the risks and timing?
  • ✓Maternal mortality risk does not increase, but fetal mortality risk is 10% to 15%, especially with cardiopulmonary bypass; surgery should be postponed to the third trimester if possible, and hypothermia avoided
  • BBoth maternal and fetal mortality rise sharply, and surgery is deferred until after birth in all cases
  • CSurgery is safest in the first trimester
  • DHypothermia is used deliberately to protect the fetus
Rationale

"The maternal mortality risk does not increase, but there is a fetal mortality risk of 10% to 15% if heart surgery is performed, especially if cardiopulmonary bypass is used." Surgery should be postponed to the third trimester if possible, "when the risk to the fetus is considerably decreased," and "hypothermia should also be avoided because it appears to be especially dangerous for the fetus." Ideally, correction happens before pregnancy.

Watch for

(D) inverts a stated danger into a technique. Cooling is protective in some cardiac surgery and is specifically avoided here because of the fetus.

17. What route of birth does the chapter prefer for women with cardiac disease, and why is cesarean birth not routinely recommended?
  • ✓Spontaneous or induced labor with vaginal birth is preferred; cesarean birth carries risks of dramatic fluid shifts, sustained hemodynamic changes, and increased blood loss
  • BCesarean birth is preferred because it avoids the work of labor
  • CCesarean birth is required for any NYHA class above I
  • DVaginal birth is preferred only for class I women
Rationale

"Spontaneous or induced (with a favorable cervix) labor followed by vaginal birth is preferred for women with cardiac disease," and "cesarean birth is not routinely recommended for women who have cardiovascular disease because of the risks of dramatic fluid shifts, sustained hemodynamic changes, and increased blood loss."

postpartum cardiac care and contraceptionthe danger window
18. When is the most hemodynamically dangerous period for a woman with compromised heart function, and why?
  • ADuring the active phase of labor, from the work of contractions
  • ✓The immediate postbirth period and especially the first 24 to 48 hours, because cardiac output increases rapidly as extravascular fluid is remobilized into the vascular compartment and intraabdominal pressure drops
  • CAt 20 to 26 weeks, when cardiac output peaks
  • DAt the 6-week postpartum visit
Rationale

The Safety Alert states that "the immediate postbirth period is hazardous for a woman whose heart function is compromised. Cardiac output increases rapidly as extravascular fluid is remobilized into the vascular compartment. At the moment of birth, intraabdominal pressure is reduced dramatically; pressure on veins is removed, the splanchnic vessels engorge, and blood flow to the heart is increased." The first 24 to 48 hours after birth are the most hemodynamically difficult.

Watch for

(A) names the period that feels most strenuous. The volume shift at birth, not the muscular work before it, is what the heart has to absorb.

19. A woman with cardiac disease had no evidence of compromise during pregnancy or labor. What should the nurse teach her about the first 2 weeks postpartum?
  • AThat the risk has passed once she is discharged
  • ✓That during the first 2 postpartum weeks extravascular fluid is mobilized, diuresis begins, and vascular resistance increases, and women with no prior evidence of compromise may decompensate during this time
  • CThat cardiac output returns to normal within 24 hours
  • DThat decompensation occurs only in women who had symptoms antepartum
Rationale

"During the first 2 postpartum weeks, extravascular fluid is mobilized, diuresis begins, and vascular resistance increases, as the woman returns to a nonpregnant state," and women "who have demonstrated little or no evidence of cardiac compromise during the antepartum or intrapartum period may do so postpartum." Maternal cardiac output usually returns to normal by 2 weeks postpartum.

Watch for

(D) uses a reassuring history to exclude a risk the chapter specifically attaches to women without one.

22. A woman with congenital heart disease asks about her children's risk. What does the chapter say?

anemia in pregnancydefining and treating iron deficiency
23. At what hemoglobin values is anemia diagnosed in pregnancy, and why does anemia matter for cardiac function?
  • ✓Less than 11 g/dL in the first and third trimesters and less than 10.5 g/dL in the second; anemia reduces oxygen-carrying capacity, so the heart compensates by increasing cardiac output, increasing its workload
  • BLess than 12 g/dL throughout pregnancy; anemia has no cardiac implications
  • CLess than 10 g/dL throughout pregnancy; the heart compensates by slowing
  • DLess than 11 g/dL only in the second trimester
Rationale

"Anemia in pregnancy is defined as hemoglobin less than 11 g/dL in the first and third trimesters and less than 10.5 g/dL in the second trimester." It "results in a reduction of the oxygen-carrying capacity of the blood; the heart tries to compensate by increasing the cardiac output," which "increases the workload of the heart and stresses ventricular function."

Watch for

(B) applies the nonpregnant threshold and misses the physiologic dilution of pregnancy — the mean hematocrit at term is 33.8%, against 37% to 47% in nonpregnant women.

anemia in pregnancydefining and treating iron deficiency
24. A woman's serum ferritin is 9 mcg/L with a low hemoglobin. What does this indicate, and what is the usual treatment?
  • ✓Iron deficiency anemia, treated with one 325-mg tablet of ferrous sulfate twice each day
  • BFolate deficiency, treated with 600 mcg of folic acid
  • CSickle cell anemia, treated with transfusion
  • DA normal value requiring no treatment
Rationale

"The serum ferritin level reflects iron reserves," and a level "less than 12 mcg/L along with a low hemoglobin value" is diagnostic of iron deficiency. "Most women with iron deficiency anemia can absorb as much iron as they need by taking one 325-mg tablet of ferrous sulfate twice each day." Iron deficiency anemia accounts for approximately 75% of anemia in pregnancy.

Watch for

(C) is the disorder where iron is specifically withheld, which makes it worth keeping straight from the one where iron is the treatment.

  • BTrait and anemia are the same condition with different names
  • CTrait carries no increased pregnancy risk of any kind

"Sickle cell trait (SA hemoglobin pattern) is sickling of the RBCs but with a normal RBC life span," and "most people with sickle cell trait are asymptomatic."
