classifying hypertensive disorders of pregnancythe twenty-week line
1. A woman at her first prenatal visit at 15 weeks has a blood pressure of 150/95 mm Hg on two readings. She has never had her blood pressure checked before. How is this classified?
  • AGestational hypertension, because the hypertension is newly discovered
  • ✓Chronic hypertension, because it is present before 20 weeks of gestation
  • CPreeclampsia, pending a protein measurement
  • DSuperimposed preeclampsia
Rationale

"Chronic hypertension is defined as hypertension that is present before the pregnancy or diagnosed before 20 weeks of gestation." The 20-week line does the sorting, regardless of how new the finding is to the chart.

Watch for

(A) treats "new to us" as "new-onset," which is the error this date exists to prevent. The chapter adds a second route to the diagnosis: women may be diagnosed with chronic hypertension once it is evident that hypertension persists after the postpartum period.

2. A previously normotensive woman develops a blood pressure of 146/92 mm Hg at 32 weeks, confirmed 4 hours later, with no proteinuria and no other findings. How is this classified?
  • ✓Gestational hypertension
  • BPreeclampsia
  • CChronic hypertension
  • DPreeclampsia with severe features
Rationale

Gestational hypertension is "development of hypertension after week 20 of pregnancy in a woman with a previously normal BP," recorded "on two occasions at least 4 hours apart." Hypertension alone, after 20 weeks, with nothing accompanying it, is gestational hypertension.

Watch for

(B) is what the same reading becomes the moment proteinuria or any severe-feature finding appears. The blood pressure definitions are identical between the two diagnoses; what accompanies the reading is what separates them.

3. A woman with chronic hypertension that has been well controlled on medication has a sudden increase in blood pressure and new-onset proteinuria at 30 weeks. What is this called, and why does it matter?
  • AGestational hypertension, which resolves after birth
  • ✓Superimposed preeclampsia, which increases morbidity for both the pregnant woman and the fetus
  • CEclampsia
  • DPreeclampsia with severe features, by definition
Rationale

Superimposed preeclampsia is "chronic hypertension in association with preeclampsia," diagnosed on "(1) a sudden increase in BP that was previously well controlled or (2) new-onset proteinuria or a sudden and sustained increase in proteinuria," and it "increases morbidity for both the pregnant woman and the fetus."

Watch for

(D) jumps to the severe-features label without the blood pressure that defines it. Severe features require readings of 160/110 or the severe qualifiers, not simply a change from baseline.

  • BIt resolves within 48 hours of birth in all cases

"Gestational hypertension resolves after giving birth, although it may require 6 to 12 months to do so." 8. A woman at 35 weeks has a blood pressure of 148/94 mm Hg twice, 4 hours apart, with no protein on dipstick but a platelet count of 88,000 and liver enzymes twice the upper limit of normal. How should the nurse interpret this?
  • AShe does not have preeclampsia, because proteinuria is required for the diagnosis
  • ✓She meets criteria for preeclampsia, because in the absence of proteinuria new-onset hypertension with thrombocytopenia or impaired liver function establishes the diagnosis
  • CShe has gestational hypertension with an unrelated hematologic problem
  • DShe has HELLP syndrome by definition
Rationale

"In the absence of proteinuria, preeclampsia may be defined as hypertension along with thrombocytopenia, impaired liver function, or persistent right upper quadrant or epigastric pain ..., progressive renal insufficiency, pulmonary edema, new-onset cerebral symptoms ... not responding to analgesia, or visual disturbances." Thrombocytopenia is a platelet count under 100,000.

Watch for

(A) applies the pre-2013 definition. The Task Force eliminated the amount of proteinuria, the presence of oliguria, and intrauterine growth restriction as diagnostic requirements.

9. A woman with preeclampsia has 6 g of protein in a 24-hour specimen. How does this affect the classification of severity?
  • AIt establishes preeclampsia with severe features
  • ✓It does not, because massive proteinuria of more than 5 g in a 24-hour specimen is no longer used as a diagnostic criterion
  • CIt rules out preeclampsia and suggests primary renal disease
  • DIt doubles the magnesium sulfate dose required
Rationale

Table 27.2 states directly: "Massive proteinuria (>5 g in a 24-h specimen) is no longer used as a diagnostic criterion." Proteinuria of 300 mg or more in 24 hours, a protein/creatinine ratio of 0.3 or more, or 2+ on dipstick establishes the finding, but the quantity does not grade the disease.

Watch for

(A) grades severity by grams of protein, which is exactly the practice the 2013 changes removed — and the deeper reason is that proteinuria was never the disease.

11. A woman with preeclampsia reports a severe headache that has not responded to acetaminophen, along with spots in her visual field. How should the nurse classify and act on these findings?
  • AAs expected discomforts of late pregnancy, to be monitored at the next visit
  • ✓As cerebral and visual disturbances meeting criteria for preeclampsia and reportable immediately
  • CAs side effects of magnesium sulfate
  • DAs evidence of migraine, unrelated to the preeclampsia
Rationale

New-onset headache unresponsive to medication and not accounted for by alternative diagnoses, and visual disturbances such as scotoma — "dim vision or blind or dark spots in the visual field" — are diagnostic findings and are on Box 27.3's reportable list. 12. A student asks why a woman with preeclampsia can be seriously ill at a blood pressure of 150/95. Which response reflects the chapter?
  • ✓The main pathogenic factor is not an increase in blood pressure but poor perfusion as a result of vasospasm and reduced plasma volume
  • BThe blood pressure reading is inaccurate in preeclampsia
  • CThe severity is determined by the amount of proteinuria rather than the pressure
  • DIllness at that pressure indicates a second, unrelated disease
Rationale

The chapter states it plainly: "The main pathogenic factor is not an increase in BP but poor perfusion as a result of vasospasm and reduced plasma volume." Every organ finding in preeclampsia is that one lesion appearing in a different system.

(C) resurrects proteinuria as the severity measure, which the same chapter removed. The pressure is a marker of the vasospasm, not the injury itself.

13. What placental change does the chapter identify as the root of preeclampsia, and when does it occur?
  • APremature separation of the placenta in the third trimester
  • ✓Failure of the spiral arteries to remodel into thinner, larger-diameter vessels, developing early in pregnancy long before signs appear
  • CPlacental calcification after 34 weeks
  • DImplantation in the lower uterine segment
Rationale

Normally the spiral arteries "widen from thick-walled muscular vessels to thinner, saclike vessels with much larger diameters," and "because this vascular remodeling does not occur or only partially develops in women with preeclampsia, decreased placental perfusion and endothelial dysfunction result." These changes "develop early in pregnancy, long before the signs and symptoms of preeclampsia become evident."

Watch for

(A) names placental abruption, which is a complication of preeclampsia rather than its cause. The chapter is explicit that "the placenta is the root cause" and that "the disease begins to resolve after the placenta has been expelled."

14. A woman with preeclampsia has a rising hematocrit and worsening edema at the same time. How does the chapter explain this combination?
  • AShe is dehydrated and needs aggressive fluid replacement
  • ✓Endothelial permeability lets fluid leave the intravascular compartment, producing tissue edema and hemoconcentration at once
  • CThe hematocrit rise indicates hemolysis
  • DThe findings are unrelated to preeclampsia
Rationale

"Plasma colloid osmotic pressure decreases as serum albumin levels decrease. Intravascular volume is reduced as fluid moves out of the intravascular compartment, resulting in hemoconcentration, increased blood viscosity, and tissue edema. The hematocrit value increases as fluid leaves the intravascular space."

Watch for

(A) is the intuitive response to a high hematocrit and is dangerous here, because arteriolar vasospasm with increased capillary permeability predisposes these women to pulmonary edema — which is why total fluids are capped at 125 mL/h.

15. A woman with preeclampsia reports severe right upper quadrant pain. What does this finding represent?

  • ✓Hepatic involvement from decreased liver perfusion with hepatic edema and subcapsular hemorrhage, which is a reportable finding
  • CGallbladder disease unrelated to the preeclampsia

"Decreased liver perfusion can lead to impaired liver function and elevated liver enzyme levels. 18. A woman asks whether her new partner changes her risk of preeclampsia, since her partner's previous partner had it. What does the chapter say about paternal contribution?
  • APaternal factors have no bearing on preeclampsia risk
  • ✓Men who have fathered a preeclamptic pregnancy are nearly twice as likely to father another preeclamptic pregnancy with a different woman, regardless of the new partner's history
  • CRisk is determined entirely by the woman's previous pregnancies
  • DRisk is lower with a new partner because of prior sperm exposure
Rationale

"Men who have fathered a preeclamptic pregnancy are nearly twice as likely to father another preeclamptic pregnancy with a different woman, regardless of whether the new partner has a history of a preeclamptic pregnancy." The chapter also notes that "women with limited sperm exposure with the same partner before conception also have a greater risk."

(D) inverts the sperm-exposure finding. Limited exposure before conception raises risk; a new partner means less exposure, not more.

19. A woman with chronic hypertension and a prior preeclamptic pregnancy is seen at 13 weeks. What preventive therapy does the chapter recommend?
  • ✓Daily low-dose aspirin, 81 mg/day, begun between 12 and 28 weeks and ideally before 16 weeks
  • BSalt and protein restriction
  • CVitamin C and E supplementation
  • DProphylactic magnesium sulfate in the second trimester
Rationale

"Low-dose aspirin has been found to reduce preeclampsia and adverse outcomes in selected high-risk women," with the recommendation that high-risk women "begin daily low-dose (81 mg/day) aspirin therapy between 12 and 28 weeks of gestation (ideally before 16 weeks)." One high-risk factor is enough; moderate-risk factors prompt consideration when more than one is present.

Watch for

(B) and (C) are on the list of interventions that "demonstrated minimal to no benefit," along with zinc, magnesium, fish oil, diuretics, other antihypertensives, and heparin.

20. A woman asks whether a blood test early in pregnancy can tell her whether she will develop preeclampsia. What does the chapter say?
  • AFirst-trimester screening tests are recommended in routine practice
  • ✓No reliable routine screening test has been developed; biomarkers such as PlGF and sFlt-1 precede the disease by weeks to months but remain investigational
  • CDoppler studies reliably predict preeclampsia and are recommended for screening
  • DA 24-hour urine protein at 12 weeks predicts later preeclampsia
Rationale

"No reliable test that can be used as a routine screening tool for predicting preeclampsia has yet been developed." Decreased VEGF and PlGF and increased sFlt-1 and sEng "have been found to precede the development of preeclampsia by weeks or months," but adding them to risk assessment "remains investigational," and "the use of first-trimester screening tests for predicting preeclampsia in clinical practice is not recommended."

Watch for

(C) is specifically rejected: "data do not support the use of Doppler studies for routine screening."

21. What does the acronym HELLP stand for, and what kind of diagnosis is it?
  • ✓Hemolysis, elevated liver enzymes, and low platelet count; a laboratory diagnosis for a variant of preeclampsia involving hepatic dysfunction
  • BHypertension, edema, liver failure, and low pressure; a clinical diagnosis
  • CHemorrhage, eclampsia, low lipids, and proteinuria; a radiologic diagnosis
  • DHemolysis, edema, low liver perfusion, and pain; a diagnosis of exclusion
Rationale

"HELLP syndrome is a laboratory diagnosis for a variant of preeclampsia that involves hepatic dysfunction, characterized by hemolysis, elevated liver enzymes, and low platelet count." The chapter stresses that "specific laboratory findings are needed to diagnose HELLP syndrome and distinguish it from other serious diseases that share the same signs and symptoms."

Watch for

(D) calls it a diagnosis of exclusion. It is the opposite: a set of specific laboratory findings is what establishes it, which is why a woman with the symptoms and no labs drawn gets misdiagnosed.

22. A woman at 34 weeks reports nausea, malaise, and epigastric pain. Her blood pressure is 138/88 mm Hg and her urine dipstick shows trace protein. Why should the nurse still consider HELLP syndrome?
  • ABecause HELLP cannot occur without severe hypertension

  • CBecause trace proteinuria is diagnostic of HELLP
  • DBecause epigastric pain alone establishes the diagnosis
Rationale

The Nursing Alert states that "many women with HELLP syndrome may not have signs or symptoms of preeclampsia with severe features," that blood pressure "may be only mildly elevated in 15% to 50% of cases," and that proteinuria "has been reported to be absent in more than 10% of cases." (A) is the assumption that produces those misdiagnoses, and this stem is built exactly as an exam would build it — near-normal pressure, vague symptoms, late third trimester.

25. Which outcomes does the chapter associate with HELLP syndrome?
  • ✓A maternal mortality rate of approximately 1% and a preterm birth rate of approximately 70%, with most perinatal deaths before 28 weeks in association with placental abruption or severe fetal growth restriction
  • BNo increase in maternal mortality
  • CA perinatal mortality rate under 1%
  • DTerm birth in most cases
Rationale

HELLP carries an increased risk for maternal death, a maternal mortality rate of approximately 1%, a perinatal mortality rate ranging from 7.4% to 34%, and a preterm birth rate of approximately 70% with 15% of those births before 28 weeks. (D) contradicts the 70% preterm rate, which is what makes HELLP a neonatal problem as much as a maternal one.

26. A woman with preeclampsia with severe features is started on magnesium sulfate, and her blood pressure remains 162/108 mm Hg an hour later. How should the nurse interpret this?
  • AThe magnesium is failing and the dose should be increased
  • ✓The magnesium is working as intended, because it prevents seizures rather than lowering blood pressure, and an antihypertensive is what is indicated
  • CThe blood pressure should be rechecked in 4 hours before acting
  • DThe magnesium should be discontinued as ineffective
Rationale

"Magnesium sulfate is the medication of choice for preventing and treating seizure activity (eclampsia)," and "contrary to popular belief, magnesium sulfate has little effect on maternal BP when administered in this manner." Box 27.3's teaching point says it directly: magnesium "is used to prevent seizures, not to decrease blood pressure."

Watch for

(A) treats an unchanged pressure as treatment failure, which is the misconception the chapter names twice. A pressure at or above 160/110 calls for hydralazine, labetalol, or nifedipine.

magnesium sulfate administrationpurpose and dosing
27. What is the standard intravenous regimen for magnesium sulfate in preeclampsia, and what is the therapeutic serum level?
  • ✓A loading dose of 4 to 6 g over 15 to 30 minutes, then 1 to 2 g/h, with a therapeutic level of 4 to 7 mEq/L
  • BA loading dose of 10 g intramuscularly, then 1 g/h, with a therapeutic level of 10 mEq/L
  • CA loading dose of 2 g over 5 minutes, then 4 g/h, with a therapeutic level of 1 to 3 mEq/L
  • DA continuous infusion of 1 g/h with no loading dose, titrated to blood pressure
Rationale

Magnesium is given "as a secondary infusion (piggyback) by a volumetric infusion pump," with "an initial loading dose of 4 to 6 g ... infused over 15 to 30 minutes" and maintenance at 1 to 2 g/h from 40 g in 1000 mL of lactated Ringer's solution. The therapeutic serum level is 4 to 7 mEq/L.

Watch for

(B) describes the intramuscular route, which is "rarely given ... because the absorption rate cannot be controlled, injections are painful, and tissue necrosis may occur" — reserved for low-resource settings or transport.

magnesium toxicity and monitoringside effects versus toxicity
30. A woman receiving magnesium sulfate reports feeling warm and flushed, is sweating, and complains of burning at the IV site. What should the nurse do?
  • AStop the infusion and notify the provider
  • ✓Recognize these as expected side effects and continue the infusion while continuing to monitor
  • CAdminister calcium gluconate
  • DSlow the infusion by half
Rationale

The expected side effects are "a feeling of warmth, flushing, diaphoresis, and burning at the IV site," along with sedation and nausea, which Box 27.3 tells the nurse to describe to the woman in advance. The signs of toxicity are different: "absent deep tendon reflexes, decreased respiratory rate, and decreased level of consciousness."

Watch for

(A) treats discomfort as danger and stops a seizure-prevention drug for a predicted effect. The teaching in Box 27.3 exists precisely so these sensations are not mistaken for an emergency.

31. A woman receiving magnesium sulfate has a respiratory rate of 10 breaths/min, absent deep tendon reflexes, and is difficult to rouse. What is the nurse's priority action?
  • ANotify the provider and continue the infusion while awaiting orders
  • ✓Discontinue the magnesium infusion immediately, then administer calcium gluconate intravenously
  • CAdminister calcium gluconate and continue the infusion
  • DIncrease the IV fluid rate to promote magnesium excretion
Rationale

The Medication Alert states: "If magnesium toxicity is suspected, prompt actions are needed to prevent respiratory or cardiac arrest. The magnesium infusion should be discontinued immediately. Calcium gluconate (antidote for magnesium sulfate) can be given intravenously."

(C) gives the antidote while the cause keeps infusing, and (A) is too slow for a drug whose next step is respiratory or cardiac arrest. Both halves of the action are required, in that order.

32. Why is urine output a central assessment during magnesium sulfate therapy, and what is the floor?

  • BBecause magnesium causes diuresis; output above 200 mL/h is expected
  • CBecause urine output measures blood pressure control; output should exceed 100 mL/h

Rationale

"Because magnesium is excreted in the urine, accurate measurements of maternal urine output must be obtained. If renal function declines, excretion of magnesium sulfate is inadequate, resulting in magnesium toxicity." Box 27.3 sets output at "at least 25–30 mL/h" and makes less than that reportable.

Watch for

(B) reverses the relationship. Oliguria in a woman on magnesium is both a preeclampsia finding and the mechanism by which she becomes toxic.
