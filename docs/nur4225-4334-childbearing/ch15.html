folic acid dosing and neural tube defectsgeneral
1. A 24-year-old with no history of a pregnancy affected by a neural tube defect asks how much folic acid she should take while she is trying to conceive. What is the recommended daily amount?
  • ✓0.4 mg, which is 400 mcg, along with dietary sources of folate
  • B4 mg daily, beginning 1 month before conception
  • C0.6 mg daily, which is the pregnancy requirement
  • DNo supplement is needed until pregnancy is confirmed
Rationale

All adolescents and women capable of becoming pregnant are advised to take "0.4 mg (400 mcg) of folic acid every day" and to "consume dietary sources of folate" (p. 297).

Watch for

(B) is a tenfold overdose, and it is chosen by anyone who remembers the higher number without the condition attached to it. The 4 mg dose belongs only to a woman with a previous neural-tube-defect-affected pregnancy, and the units switch between milligrams and micrograms in the source, which is what makes these doses easy to trade.

folic acid dosing and neural tube defectsgeneral
2. A patient whose first child was born with spina bifida is planning another pregnancy. What folic acid regimen should the nurse expect to be prescribed?
  • ✓4 mg daily, beginning at least 1 month before attempting to conceive and continuing throughout the first trimester
  • B0.4 mg daily, beginning when pregnancy is confirmed
  • C0.6 mg daily throughout pregnancy only
  • D4 mg daily, beginning in the second trimester when fetal growth accelerates
Rationale

A woman who has had a pregnancy "that resulted in the birth of a child with an NTD" should take "4 mg of folic acid daily," beginning "at least 1 month before attempting to conceive and continuing throughout the first trimester" (p. 297).

Watch for

(B) is the mirror error of the previous item and delivers a tenfold underdose to the one patient who needs the high dose. (D) gets the dose right and the timing catastrophically wrong: the neural tube has closed long before the second trimester, so a regimen starting then cannot prevent anything.

folic acid dosing and neural tube defectsgeneral
3. A patient at her first prenatal visit at 10 weeks says she began taking folic acid last week. How should the nurse respond?
  • ✓Continuing is still worthwhile, but the neural tube begins to close within the first month of gestation, often before a woman knows she is pregnant, which is why supplementation is recommended before conception
  • BStarting at 10 weeks provides full protection against neural tube defects
  • CFolic acid is only useful in the third trimester, so she has started too early
  • DShe should stop the supplement now that pregnancy is confirmed
Rationale

"Proper closure of the neural tube is required for normal formation of the spinal cord, and the neural tube begins to close within the first month of gestation, often before the woman realizes that she is pregnant" (p. 297). Folate remains needed throughout pregnancy for increased red blood cell production and for the rapidly growing cells of the fetus and placenta.

Watch for

(B) is the reassuring answer that is not true, and the timing is the whole reason the recommendation targets all women capable of becoming pregnant rather than pregnant women. Folate is still needed for other purposes, so the honest response keeps the supplement and corrects the expectation.

4. A patient asks about the difference between the folate on a food label and the folic acid in her prenatal vitamin. Which explanation is accurate?
  • ✓Folate is the form found naturally in foods, and folic acid is the form used in fortification of grain products and in vitamin supplements
  • BFolic acid is the natural food form, and folate is the synthetic supplement form
  • CThe two are unrelated nutrients with different functions
  • DFolate is active only in pregnancy, and folic acid only before conception
Rationale

"Folate (vitamin B9) is the form in which this vitamin is found naturally in foods, and folic acid is the form used in fortification of grain products and other foods and in vitamin supplements" (p. 297).

Watch for

(B) reverses the two terms, which is the mirror of the same distinction. It matters at the counseling level because a patient told to "get more folate" from food and a patient told to "take folic acid" are being told two different things, and only one of them is a supplement instruction.

folic acid dosing and neural tube defectsgeneral
5. What is the recommended daily folate intake during pregnancy itself?
  • ✓600 mcg, which is 0.6 mg
  • B400 mcg, the same as before conception
  • C4 mg, the same as for a woman with a previous affected pregnancy
  • D1000 mcg, double the preconception amount
Rationale

Table 15.3 gives a pregnancy folate value of 600 mcg against a nonpregnant 400 mcg and a lactation value of 500 mcg, and the water-soluble vitamins section states the pregnancy recommendation as "0.6 mg (600 mcg) of folic acid daily" (p. 306). The increase reflects "the increase in RBC production during pregnancy as well as the nutritional requirements of the rapidly growing cells in the fetus and placenta."

Watch for

(B) carries the preconception dose into pregnancy unchanged, which is the middle error of a three-value set. Holding all three together is what prevents it: 400 mcg before, 600 mcg during, and 4 mg only with a previous affected pregnancy.

iron requirements and supplementation teachinggeneral
6. What is the recommended daily iron intake during pregnancy, and how does it compare with lactation?
  • ✓27 mg of elemental iron during pregnancy, which falls to 10 or 9 mg during lactation
  • B27 mg during pregnancy, rising to 40 mg during lactation
  • C18 mg during pregnancy, the same as for nonpregnant adult women
  • D15 mg during pregnancy, rising through lactation as milk production increases
Rationale

"The recommended daily allowance for iron during pregnancy is 27 mg; this is the amount of elemental iron in most supplements" (p. 306). Table 15.3 gives nonpregnant values of 15 mg for adolescents and 18 mg for adult women, a pregnancy value of 27 mg, and lactation values of 10 and 9 mg. The pregnancy increase allows "transfer of adequate iron to the fetus" and "expansion of the maternal RBC mass."

Watch for

(B) assumes that if pregnancy raises a requirement, breastfeeding must raise it further. Iron is the counterexample: the requirement is highest in pregnancy and falls below the nonpregnant value in lactation, so it is one of the few nutrients whose curve goes back down.

iron requirements and supplementation teachinggeneral
7. A patient is starting an iron supplement. Select all the instructions that are accurate.
Select all that apply · scored all or nothing
  • ✓A diet rich in vitamin C and in heme iron from meats increases absorption of the supplement
  • ✓Bran, tea, coffee, milk, oxalates in spinach and Swiss chard, and egg yolk decrease absorption and should not be consumed at the same time as the supplement
  • ✓Iron is absorbed best on an empty stomach, taken between meals with a beverage other than tea, coffee, or milk
  • ✓Black or dark green stools are an expected effect of the supplement
  • ETaking the supplement with a glass of milk improves both absorption and tolerance
Rationale

The Teaching for Self-Management box states that vitamin C and heme iron increase absorption; that "bran, tea, coffee, milk, oxalates (in spinach and Swiss chard) and egg yolk decrease iron absorption"; that "iron is absorbed best if it is taken when the stomach is empty; that is, take it between meals with a beverage other than tea, coffee, or milk"; and that "iron supplements can cause stools to become black or dark green" (p. 306).

Watch for

(E) is the single wrong option and it is the one most likely to be checked, because milk is the beverage people reach for to settle a stomach. Milk is on the inhibitor list twice over — named directly and named again as a beverage to avoid — so it is exactly backwards. Spinach is the other counterintuitive entry: it is a folate source and an iron-absorption inhibitor at the same time.

8. A patient reports abdominal discomfort when she takes her iron supplement between meals. What should the nurse advise?
  • ✓Take the iron at bedtime, since it can be taken then if abdominal discomfort occurs when it is taken between meals
  • BTake the iron with milk to buffer the stomach
  • CStop the iron until the discomfort resolves
  • DTake a double dose every other day to reduce the frequency of discomfort
Rationale

"Iron is absorbed best if it is taken when the stomach is empty; that is, take it between meals ... Iron can be taken at bedtime if abdominal discomfort occurs when it is taken between meals" (p. 306).

Watch for

(B) sacrifices the whole point of the dose to comfort, since milk is a named absorption inhibitor. The teaching box supplies a solution that preserves absorption, which is why bedtime dosing is the answer rather than any strategy that pairs iron with food that blocks it.

9. A patient realizes 10 hours after her scheduled dose that she forgot her iron. What should she do?
  • ✓Take it now, since a missed dose should be taken as soon as it is remembered if that is within 13 hours of the scheduled dose, and doses should not be doubled
  • BSkip it and take two tomorrow
  • CSkip it entirely, since a missed dose cannot be made up
  • DTake it now and also take tomorrow's dose early to catch up
Rationale

"If an iron dose is missed, take it as soon as it is remembered if that is within 13 hours of the scheduled dose. Do not double up on the dose" (p. 306).

Watch for

(B) and (D) both double a dose, which the box prohibits outright. The 13-hour figure is unusual enough to be worth memorizing as printed, and the prohibition on doubling connects to the box's other safety line — iron is kept in a childproof container because it is a common pediatric poisoning.

10. A pregnant patient taking iron reports constipation. What should the nurse recommend?
  • ✓A diet high in fiber with adequate fluid intake, since constipation is common with iron supplementation
  • BDiscontinuing the iron supplement
  • CA stimulant laxative daily for the remainder of the pregnancy
  • DSwitching the iron to the morning with coffee

iron requirements and supplementation teachinggeneral
11. A patient's hemoglobin is low and she is told she has iron deficiency anemia. She asks whether this is the same normal anemia of pregnancy she read about. Which response is accurate?
  • ✓They are different; physiologic anemia of pregnancy is a normal dilutional change as blood volume increases, while iron deficiency anemia reflects poor iron status
  • BThey are the same condition described in two ways
  • CPhysiologic anemia is the more serious of the two and requires supplementation
  • DIron deficiency anemia occurs only after birth, so the diagnosis is likely an error
Rationale

"Poor iron status, which can result in iron deficiency anemia, is relatively common among women in the childbearing years. This is not the same as physiologic anemia of pregnancy, which is a normal occurrence as the maternal blood volume increases" (p. 306). If a woman is already anemic, "increased dosages may be required and must be discussed with the woman's health care provider."

Watch for

(B) is the conflation the chapter explicitly forecloses, and the consequence runs in both directions: treating a dilutional finding as a deficiency leads to unnecessary supplementation, and dismissing a real deficiency as physiologic leaves it untreated. The two are distinguished by the trimester-specific thresholds from Chapter 13, not by the hemoglobin value alone.

weight gain by body mass indexgeneral
12. A patient with a prepregnancy body mass index of 32 asks how much weight she should gain. What is the recommended total gain?
  • ✓5.0 to 9.1 kg, or 11 to 20 lb
  • B11.5 to 16 kg, or 25 to 35 lb
  • C12.7 to 18.1 kg, or 28 to 40 lb
  • DShe should aim to lose weight during the pregnancy
Rationale

Table 15.1 gives recommended total gain by prepregnancy category: underweight, 12.7 to 18.1 kg (28 to 40 lb); normal, 11.5 to 16 kg (25 to 35 lb); overweight, 6.8 to 11.3 kg (15 to 25 lb); and obese, 5.0 to 9.1 kg (11 to 20 lb). The obese category is a body mass index of 30 or greater (p. 298).

Watch for

(D) is the intuitive recommendation and the chapter forbids it in as many words. The four ranges run inversely to body mass index, so the heavier the starting point the smaller the recommended gain, but the recommendation never becomes a loss.

13. An obese patient asks why she cannot simply diet during pregnancy, since she has weight to lose. Which explanation reflects the chapter?
  • ✓Even overweight or obese pregnant women need to gain at least enough weight to equal the weight of the products of conception, and dietary restriction causes catabolism of fat stores that augments production of ketones
  • BDieting is acceptable in pregnancy as long as protein intake is maintained
  • CDieting is discouraged only because the patient will feel hungry
  • DDieting is acceptable in the first trimester, when the fetus is small
Rationale

"Pregnancy is not a time for a diet for weight loss. Even overweight or obese pregnant women need to gain at least enough weight to equal the weight of the products of conception (fetus, placenta, and amniotic fluid)." Limiting energy intake may also "excessively limit their intake of important nutrients. Moreover, dietary restriction results in catabolism of fat stores, which in turn augments the production of ketones" (pp. 298–299).

Watch for

(C) is the incomplete answer that a nurse gives when the mechanism has not been learned. The ketone production is the reason the source supplies, and it is what makes the prohibition physiologic rather than merely a matter of comfort or adherence.

14. A patient of normal prepregnancy weight is at 10 weeks and has gained 3 lb. How should the nurse interpret this?
  • ✓It is appropriate; recommended total first-trimester gain is 2 to 4 lb, after which about 1 lb per week is recommended
  • BIt is excessive; no weight gain is expected in the first trimester
  • CIt is inadequate; 1 lb per week is expected from conception onward
  • DIt cannot be assessed without a body mass index recalculation
Rationale

"During the first trimester of singleton pregnancy, the recommended total weight gain is 2 to 4 lb. (0.9 to 1.8 kg) for a woman with normal prepregnancy weight. Thereafter the recommended weight gain increases to approximately 1 lb. (0.45 kg) per week for an underweight woman and a woman of normal weight" (p. 298).

Watch for

(C) applies the weekly rate to the first trimester, which would set an expectation more than three times the recommended total. The rate changes at the end of the first trimester because growth changes location: in the first and second trimesters growth is primarily in maternal tissues, and in the third it is primarily fetal.

15. A patient at 26 weeks has gained 8 lb since her visit 4 weeks ago. Her weight was recorded on the same scale in similar clothing. What should the nurse do first?
  • ✓Assess for signs of preeclampsia, since a gain of more than 6.6 lb in a month, especially after the 20th week, can be associated with its development
  • BProvide dietary counseling about portion sizes
  • CReassure her that weight gain accelerates in the third trimester
  • DRecommend that she restrict sodium to limit fluid retention
Rationale

"An exceptionally high gain is likely to be caused by an accumulation of fluids," and "a gain of more than 6.6 lb. (3 kg) in a month, especially after the 20th week of gestation, can be associated with the development of preeclampsia" (p. 298). The source also names benign explanations to exclude first, including "measurement or recording errors or differences in weight of clothing or time of day," which this stem has already addressed.

Watch for

(B) treats a possible complication as a dietary problem, which is the error the fact bank names: sudden excessive gain after 20 weeks is a preeclampsia flag, not a counseling opportunity. (D) is separately wrong, since sodium is not routinely restricted in pregnancy and restriction has not proved effective in preventing hypertensive disorders.

16. A severely underweight patient is not gaining adequately. Which outcomes are most associated with this pattern, as distinct from those associated with excessive gain?
  • ✓Preterm labor, low birth weight, intrauterine growth restriction, small-for-gestational-age infants, and increased perinatal mortality
  • BGestational diabetes, hypertensive disorders, and cesarean birth
  • CStillbirth, birth defects, and abnormal fetal growth
  • DSurgical site infection and venous thromboembolism
Rationale

"Severely underweight women are more likely to have preterm labor and to give birth to LBW infants," and both normal-weight and underweight women with inadequate gain have increased risk of intrauterine growth restriction. Low prepregnancy weight and inadequate gain "increase the risk of preterm birth and small-for-gestational-age (SGA) infants. Perinatal mortality is also increased" (pp. 298–299).

Watch for

(B), (C), and (D) all list real risks that belong to obesity and excessive gain rather than to inadequate gain. Note that preterm birth appears on both lists, so it is not a discriminator between them; the growth-restriction and small-for-gestational-age outcomes are what mark the underweight pattern. Inadequate gain should also prompt screening for an eating disorder and for lack of financial resources to buy food.

17. A patient in her first trimester says she has been "eating for two." What should the nurse teach about caloric needs?
  • ✓During the first trimester the recommended daily caloric intake is the same as before pregnancy; an increase of 340 kcal per day begins in the second trimester
  • BCaloric needs double from the moment of conception
  • CAn additional 340 kcal per day is needed from the first trimester onward
  • DCaloric needs decrease in the first trimester because of nausea
Rationale

"During the first trimester the recommended daily caloric intake is the same as in the nonpregnant state. An increase of 340 kcal/day during the second trimester and 452 kcal/day during the third trimester is recommended" (pp. 300–301).

Watch for

(B) is the belief this teaching exists to correct, and it is wrong by an order of magnitude. The source quantifies how small the real increase is: the second-trimester addition is "one additional serving" from a single food group, and the third-trimester addition is about one-third of a serving beyond that.

18. A patient in her third trimester asks how much extra food the additional calories represent. Which response is accurate?
  • ✓About 452 kcal per day above nonpregnant needs, which is roughly one additional serving plus one-third of a serving from a basic food group
  • BAbout 1000 kcal per day, roughly one additional meal
  • CAbout 100 kcal per day, roughly one piece of fruit
  • DNo increase is needed in the third trimester, since activity typically decreases
Rationale

The third-trimester increase is 452 kcal per day. "The additional kilocalories needed during the second trimester can be provided by one additional serving" from milk, yogurt or cheese, fruits, vegetables, or grains, and "in the third trimester, an additional one-third of a serving will provide the needed kilocalories" (p. 301). "Longitudinal assessment of weight gain during pregnancy is the best way to determine whether the kilocalorie intake is adequate."

Watch for

(B) overshoots by more than double, which is how the "eating for two" idea survives even after the number is learned. The check the source recommends is not calorie counting at all but the weight-gain trend over time, which is what a prenatal visit actually measures.

19. What is the recommended protein intake during pregnancy, and when does the increase begin?
  • ✓71 g per day, up from 46 g, with the increase applying to the second and third trimesters
  • B71 g per day beginning at conception
  • C46 g per day throughout, since American protein intake is already adequate
  • D96 g per day, double the nonpregnant requirement
Rationale

"The recommended protein intake during pregnancy is 71 g/day; this is an increase over the 46 g/day recommended for nonpregnant adults" (p. 301). Table 15.3 specifies "first trimester, same as nonpregnant; second and third trimesters, nonpregnant needs + 25 g," with a footnote to "add an additional 25 g in twin pregnancies."

Watch for

(B) starts the increase a trimester early, which mirrors the same error in the energy row — both nutrients hold at nonpregnant levels through the first trimester. (C) misuses a true framing sentence: the source does say many women may not need to increase protein at all because American intake is relatively high, but that is an observation about actual diets, not a lowering of the recommendation.

20. A pregnant patient following a macrobiotic diet asks about taking a high-protein supplement to be safe. Which response is accurate?
  • ✓High-protein supplements are not recommended because of potentially harmful effects on the fetus; her intake should be addressed through food sources
  • BHigh-protein supplements are recommended for all women on restricted diets
  • CHigh-protein supplements are safe but unnecessary, since protein needs do not rise in pregnancy
  • DShe should double the recommended protein intake to compensate for her diet
Rationale

"High-protein supplements are not recommended because of potentially harmful effects on the fetus" (p. 301). Women adhering to a macrobiotic, or highly restricted vegetarian, diet are named alongside pregnant adolescents and women from impoverished backgrounds as those whose protein intake is most likely to be inadequate.

Watch for

(B) is the reflex response to a patient in a named risk group — the risk is real, and the proposed remedy is the one the source rules out. This patient needs attention to food sources and dietitian involvement, which is also the chapter's guidance for vegan patients generally.

21. A patient asks what combination of foods would supply most of her recommended protein. Which response reflects the chapter?
  • ✓Three servings of milk, yogurt, or cheese, and two servings of 5 to 6 oz of meat, poultry, or fish
  • BOne serving of dairy and one 3-oz serving of meat daily
  • CProtein powder added to two meals daily
  • DSix servings of whole grains daily, which are complete protein foods
Rationale

"Three servings of milk, yogurt, or cheese (four for adolescents) and two servings (5 to 6 oz. [140 to 168 g]) of meat, poultry, or fish would supply most of the recommended protein" (p. 301). Milk, meat, eggs, and cheese are complete protein foods of high biologic value; legumes, whole grains, and nuts are also valuable sources.

Watch for

(D) promotes whole grains from valuable sources to complete proteins, which the source reserves for the animal-derived foods. Note also the parenthetical this item does not test but the adolescent topic does: adolescents need four dairy servings rather than three.

22. A pregnant patient asks whether she should avoid all fish because of mercury. Which response reflects the chapter?
  • ✓She should avoid shark, swordfish, king mackerel, and tilefish, but may eat up to 12 oz per week of low-mercury fish such as shrimp, salmon, pollock, catfish, and canned light tuna
  • BShe should avoid all fish and shellfish throughout pregnancy
  • CShe may eat any fish in unlimited quantities as long as it is thoroughly cooked
  • DShe should eat only albacore tuna, which is lowest in mercury
Rationale

The Safety Alert directs pregnant women to "avoid eating shark, swordfish, king mackerel, and tilefish" and to "eat as much as 12 oz. (340 g) per week of a variety of commercially caught fish and shellfish low in mercury, such as shrimp, salmon, pollock, catfish, and canned light tuna," while limiting "albacore or white tuna and tuna steaks, which contain more mercury, to 6 oz. (170 g) per week" (p. 305).

Watch for

(D) inverts the tuna rule, which is the confusable pair inside this alert: canned light tuna is the permitted one and albacore or white tuna is the limited one. (B) is the overcautious answer that contradicts the chapter's own recommendation of one to two seafood servings per week for docosahexaenoic acid.

23. A patient asks about fish caught by her brother in a local lake, for which she can find no state advisory. What should the nurse advise?
  • ✓Limit intake of that fish to 6 oz and eat no other fish that week
  • BAvoid all locally caught fish under every circumstance
  • CTreat it as a low-mercury fish and eat up to 12 oz that week
  • DNo limit is needed for freshwater fish
Rationale

The Safety Alert directs women to "check local advisories about the safety of fish caught by family and friends in local bodies of water, but if no advisory is available, limit intake of these fish to 6 oz. (170 g), and eat no other fish that week" (p. 305).

Watch for

(C) applies the commercial low-mercury allowance to a fish of unknown mercury content. The clause most often dropped is the second one — the 6-oz limit comes with a prohibition on other fish that week, so it is not simply a smaller portion but a weekly cap.

24. Why is docosahexaenoic acid recommended during pregnancy, and how much?
  • ✓It is essential to fetal brain development and neurologic function; many providers recommend at least 300 mg per day, obtainable from one to two servings of seafood per week
  • BIt prevents preeclampsia; 1000 mg per day is recommended
  • CIt replaces the need for folic acid supplementation in the third trimester
  • DIt is a saturated fat and should be limited to 10% of daily calories
Rationale

The long-chain polyunsaturated fatty acids docosahexaenoic acid and arachidonic acid "are considered essential to fetal brain development and neurologic function," and supplementation with omega-3 forms "has been associated with reduced risk for preterm birth and improved neurologic and visual development in offspring." "Many health care providers recommend at least 300 mg/day of DHA," and adequate amounts come from "one to two servings (8 to 12 oz.) of seafood per week" (pp. 301–305).

Watch for

(D) confuses this fatty acid with the saturated fat limit from the same section. Saturated fat should be no more than 10% of total daily calories and trans fatty acids should be avoided entirely, but docosahexaenoic acid is a fat the chapter wants increased rather than restricted.

25. What are the recommended carbohydrate intake and its proportion of daily calories during pregnancy?
  • ✓175 g per day, constituting no more than 45% to 64% of daily caloric intake, ideally from fresh fruits and vegetables and whole grains
  • B300 g per day, constituting at least 70% of daily caloric intake
  • C100 g per day, with carbohydrates restricted to prevent gestational diabetes
  • DNo specific recommendation exists, since carbohydrate needs do not change in pregnancy
Rationale

"Carbohydrates are the primary source of energy, and carbohydrate needs increase during pregnancy to 175 g/day. Carbohydrates should constitute no more than 45% to 64% of daily caloric intake." The ideal sources "are fresh fruits and vegetables and whole grains instead of processed foods" (p. 305).

Watch for

(C) applies a diabetes-management instinct to a healthy pregnancy, and it collides with the chapter's broader rule that pregnancy is not a time for restriction. Carbohydrate needs rise in pregnancy; what the source constrains is the proportion of calories and the quality of the source.

fat-soluble vitamin safetygeneral
26. A patient asks whether she can take extra vitamin supplements to be thorough. Which principle should guide the nurse's response about vitamins A, D, E, and K?
  • ✓These are fat-soluble and stored in body tissues, so prolonged overdoses can reach toxic levels; supplements should be taken only as prescribed, although toxicity from dietary sources is very unlikely
  • BThese are water-soluble and readily excreted in urine, so excess intake is harmless
  • CThese cannot reach toxic levels in pregnancy because maternal metabolism accelerates
  • DThese should all be supplemented at double the usual dose during pregnancy
Rationale

The fat-soluble vitamins are "stored in the body tissues; in the event of prolonged overdoses, these vitamins can reach toxic levels. Because of the high potential for toxicity, pregnant women are advised to take fat-soluble vitamin supplements only as prescribed. Toxicity from dietary sources is very unlikely" (p. 305).

Watch for

The tempting error is applying the water-soluble rule to this class. Water-soluble vitamins have much smaller body stores, are readily excreted in the urine, and must be consumed frequently, with toxicity less likely — the opposite profile, which is why the class a vitamin belongs to decides the answer to any supplement question about it.

27. A patient asks whether she should take a vitamin A supplement for fetal eye development. Which response is accurate?
  • ✓Vitamin A in excessive amounts is a known teratogen, so supplements are not routinely recommended and it is best obtained from dietary sources
  • BVitamin A supplements are recommended for all pregnant women to support fetal eye development
  • CVitamin A has no role in fetal development, so supplementation is simply unnecessary
  • DVitamin A supplements are safe at any dose because excess is excreted in urine
Rationale

Vitamin A is "essential for cell differentiation and proliferation and for development of the heart, spine, eyes, and ears in the fetus," but "vitamin A in excessive amounts is a known teratogen. Congenital malformations of the heart, lungs, skull, and eye have occurred in infants of mothers who took excessive amounts. Vitamin A supplements are not recommended routinely" and it is "best obtained from dietary sources" (p. 305).

Watch for

The trap here is that the nutrient's importance argues for supplementing it. Both halves are true at once: the vitamin is essential to the very organs that excess damages, so the answer is dietary sources rather than a supplement. Deep yellow and deep green vegetables and fruits are the sources named.

28. A patient taking isotretinoin for cystic acne is planning a pregnancy, and her sister uses topical tretinoin. What should the nurse teach about each?
  • ✓Isotretinoin in early pregnancy is associated with heart malformations, facial abnormalities, cleft palate, hydrocephalus, deafness and blindness, and increased miscarriage risk; topical tretinoin does not appear to enter the circulation in substantial amounts, but its safety in pregnancy has not been confirmed
  • BBoth are equally dangerous and both must be stopped
  • CBoth are safe in pregnancy because they are vitamin A analogs rather than vitamin A
  • DIsotretinoin is safe once pregnancy is confirmed; only preconception use is a concern
Rationale

Isotretinoin use during early pregnancy "has been associated with an increased incidence of heart malformations, facial abnormalities, cleft palate, hydrocephalus, and deafness and blindness in the infant as well as an increased risk for miscarriage. Topical agents such as tretinoin (Retin-A) do not appear to enter the circulation in substantial amounts, but their safety in pregnancy has not been confirmed" (p. 306).

Watch for

The two wrong directions are equally available here, and the source takes neither. The topical agent is not established as safe and is not established as dangerous; the honest answer is that its safety has not been confirmed, which is what the patient should be told rather than a reassurance or a warning the source does not support.

29. A patient asks whether vitamin E supplementation will help prevent preeclampsia. Which response is accurate?
  • ✓There is no evidence that supplementation improves outcomes, it does not prevent preeclampsia, and vitamin E supplementation is not recommended during pregnancy
  • BVitamin E supplementation is recommended specifically to prevent preeclampsia
  • CVitamin E requirements double in pregnancy, so supplementation is necessary
  • DVitamin E is water-soluble, so supplementation carries no risk
Rationale

Vitamin E is an antioxidant that helps protect against oxidative stress, but "there is no evidence that supplementation ... improves outcomes; it does not prevent problems such as preeclampsia. In fact, supplementation with vitamin E is not recommended during pregnancy" (p. 305). Table 15.3 gives the same value of 15 mg for nonpregnant and pregnant women.

Watch for

(C) assumes a pregnancy increase where the table shows none. (D) gets the direction backwards for the very reason the source gives for the increase — body water is expanding, which raises the sodium requirement rather than lowering it.
