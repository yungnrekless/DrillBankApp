hemolytic disease mechanismhow alloimmune hemolysis works
1. How does alloimmune hemolytic disease of the newborn occur?
  • ✓Antigens expressed by fetal erythrocytes stimulate the maternal immune response, and when maternal IgG antibodies specific to the fetal red cells cross the placenta they are not protective but attack the fetal red cells, causing agglutination, clumping, and lysis
  • BThe fetus makes antibodies against maternal red cells
  • CThe placenta produces antibodies against fetal red cells
  • DMaternal IgM crosses the placenta and destroys fetal red cells
Rationale

"Hemolytic disease of the newborn, associated with alloimmunity, is the result of antigens expressed by fetal erythrocytes stimulating the maternal immune response." Normally "maternal immunoglobulin G (IgG) antibodies cross the placenta and enter the fetal circulation, providing an adaptive measure to protect the infant," but "when maternal IgG antibodies specific to the fetal RBCs cross the placenta, they are not protective, but can instead attack the fetal RBCs."

Watch for

(D) names the immunoglobulin that does not cross the placenta at all, which is exactly why IgG is the one that can do this damage.

hemolytic disease mechanismhow alloimmune hemolysis works
2. What two consequences follow from lysis of fetal red blood cells?
  • ✓Anemia as red cell volume decreases, and hyperbilirubinemia as freed heme is converted to biliverdin and eventually bilirubin
  • BPolycythemia and hypoglycemia
  • CThrombocytopenia and neutropenia
  • DOnly anemia, with no effect on bilirubin
Rationale

"As erythrocytes are lysed, the volume decreases, resulting in anemia," and the freed heme "is converted to biliverdin and eventually bilirubin," producing hyperbilirubinemia. Hemolytic anemia "occurs when there is rapid destruction of red blood cells (RBCs), faster than the body can produce them."

Watch for

(D) drops the half of the problem that drives newborn treatment — phototherapy, exchange transfusion, and the whole bilirubin-monitoring schedule exist because of the second consequence.

3. Which statement captures the relationship between ABO and Rh incompatibility?
  • ✓Hemolytic disease from alloimmunity is most often caused by ABO incompatibility, which is usually a mild disease process, while Rh incompatibility is less common and results in more severe hemolysis
  • BRh incompatibility is more common and milder than ABO
  • CBoth occur with equal frequency and severity
  • DABO incompatibility is rarer and more severe
Rationale

"Hemolytic disease in newborns from alloimmunity is most often caused by ABO incompatibility but can also be the result of rhesus (Rh) incompatibility. ABO incompatibility usually is a mild disease process whereas Rh incompatibility is less common and results in more severe hemolysis."

Watch for

(D) inverts both halves, and the inversion matters clinically: the common cause is the mild one, so a mildly jaundiced first-born with a positive antiglobulin test is far more likely ABO than Rh.

4. Besides alloimmunity, what other causes of newborn hemolysis does the chapter name?
  • ✓Nonimmune or acquired hemolysis such as with infection, and inherited erythrocyte defects such as thalassemia, glucose-6-phosphate dehydrogenase deficiency, and pyruvate kinase deficiency
  • BOnly infection
  • COnly inherited defects
  • DThere are no other causes
Rationale

The three broad causes are "alloimmune hemolysis (e.g., Rh incompatibility), nonimmune or acquired hemolysis (e.g., infection), or inherited erythrocyte defects that result in hemolysis (e.g., thalassemia)." G6PD deficiency "is most common among neonates whose genetic heritage comes from Africa, Asia, the Mediterranean, and the Middle East," is sex-linked so "male offspring are affected more often," and requires a trigger: "a deficiency of the RBC enzyme in combination with exposure to an oxidant stressor (such as sepsis) results in hemolysis."

Watch for

(D) would make every jaundiced newborn an immunologic problem, and the enzyme deficiencies are the ones that need a stressor before they declare themselves.

rh incompatibilitywho is at risk
5. Which maternal-fetal pairing is at risk in Rh incompatibility?
  • ✓Only the Rh-positive fetus of an Rh-negative mother
  • BThe Rh-negative fetus of an Rh-positive mother
  • CAny pairing in which the parents differ
  • DOnly the Rh-negative fetus of an Rh-negative mother
Rationale

"Only the Rh-positive fetus of an Rh-negative mother is at risk." An Rh-negative fetus "is in no danger because, similar to the mother, the fetus does not have the Rh antigen," and "an Rh-negative fetus with a Rh-positive mother is also in no danger of hemolysis."

Watch for

(B) reverses the pair, which is the most common error with this topic — the mother must be the one lacking the antigen for her to make antibodies against it.

rh incompatibilitywho is at risk
6. An Rh-negative woman's partner is heterozygous for the Rh factor. What does this mean for their children?
  • ✓There is a 50% chance that each infant will be Rh positive and a 50% chance that each will be Rh negative
  • BAll the offspring will be Rh positive
  • CAll the offspring will be Rh negative
  • DThe father's zygosity has no bearing
Rationale

"If the mother is Rh-negative and the father is Rh-positive and homozygous for the Rh factor, all the offspring will be Rh-positive. If the father is heterozygous for the factor, there is a 50% chance that each infant born of the union will be Rh-positive and a 50% chance that each infant will be Rh-negative." The Rh-negative blood type occurs in "approximately 15% of Whites, 8% of Blacks, 1% to 2% of Native Americans and Inuit Eskimos, and less than 1% of Asians."

Watch for

(B) applies the homozygous result to a heterozygous father, and paternal zygosity testing is the first assessment precisely because it changes the probability this much.

7. Why is the first pregnancy with an Rh-positive fetus usually spared?
  • ✓Because Rh-negative women usually become sensitized in the first pregnancy but do not produce enough antibodies to cause lysis of fetal blood cells; in subsequent pregnancies, antibodies form in response to repeated contact with the antigen and lysis results
  • BBecause the placenta blocks all antibody transfer in a first pregnancy
  • CBecause fetal red cells do not carry the Rh antigen until the second pregnancy
  • DThe first pregnancy is not spared and is the most severely affected
Rationale

"Usually, Rh-negative women become sensitized in their first pregnancy with an Rh-positive fetus but do not produce enough antibodies to cause lysis (destruction) of fetal blood cells. During subsequent pregnancies, antibodies form in response to repeated contact with the antigen via fetal blood exposure, and lysis of fetal RBCs results."

Watch for

(D) describes ABO incompatibility instead, where first-born infants can be affected because mothers with type O already have the antibodies without prior sensitization.

9. What is hydrops fetalis, and what characterizes it?
  • ✓The most severe result of fetal anemia in Rh incompatibility, marked by anemia, cardiac decompensation, cardiomegaly, hepatosplenomegaly, and hypoxia, with decreased intravascular oncotic pressure causing generalized edema and effusions into the peritoneal, pericardial, and pleural spaces; mortality is as high as 24% if severe and undiagnosed
  • BA mild form of hemolytic disease requiring no treatment
  • CThe compensatory production of immature erythrocytes
  • DFetal hyperbilirubinemia alone
Rationale

"In the most severe cases of Rh incompatibility, fetal anemia results in hydrops fetalis," identified by "marked anemia, cardiac decompensation, cardiomegaly, hepatosplenomegaly, and hypoxia." With progression, "there is decreased intravascular oncotic pressure and fluid leaks out of the intravascular space. This results in generalized edema and effusions into the peritoneal (ascites), pericardial, and pleural (hydrothorax) spaces." Mortality is "as high as 24%."

Watch for

(C) describes erythroblastosis fetalis, the compensatory step that precedes hydrops — "the fetus compensates for the hemolytic anemia by producing large numbers of immature erythrocytes to replace those lysed."

  • BIt occurs when the mother is type AB and the fetus is type O, and all affected infants have clinical effects

Rationale

(B) reverses the pairing. The type O mother is the one with antibodies to both A and B — "those with type AB blood have no antibodies."

11. Why can a first-born infant be affected by ABO incompatibility, and what prophylaxis is available?
  • ✓Because mothers with type O blood already have anti-A and anti-B antibodies in their blood without sensitization first; there is no preventive treatment available for mothers prenatally
  • BBecause sensitization occurs during the first trimester; RhoGAM prevents it
  • CBecause the first pregnancy always sensitizes; an ABO immune globulin is given at 28 weeks
  • DFirst-born infants cannot be affected
Rationale

"In contrast to the situation that pertains to Rh incompatibility, first-born infants can be affected because mothers with type O blood already have anti-A and anti-B antibodies in their blood without sensitization first." And: "There is no preventive treatment available for mothers prenatally."

Watch for

(C) invents an ABO equivalent of Rh immune globulin, which does not exist — the naturally occurring antibodies are already present, so there is nothing to prevent.

abo incompatibilitydiagnosis and treatment
12. How is ABO incompatibility identified in the newborn, and what does a stronger reaction mean?
  • ✓By a Coombs test, also called a direct antiglobulin test; a positive result means there are antibodies attached to the infant's red cells, and the strength of the reaction reflects the number of antibodies present
  • BBy maternal serum antibody titer alone
  • CBy the infant's blood type alone
  • DBy bilirubin level alone
Rationale

"ABO incompatibility is identified by a Coombs test (also referred to as a direct antiglobulin test [DAT]). A positive DAT means there are antibodies attached to the infant's RBCs. The strength of the DAT reaction (i.e., the more positive) is reflective of the number of antibodies present."

Watch for

(C) identifies the setup rather than the disease. Twenty percent of newborns have the incompatibility; the antiglobulin test is what shows whether antibodies have actually attached.

abo incompatibilitydiagnosis and treatment
13. An infant with ABO incompatibility has a positive direct antiglobulin test. What determines treatment, and what is usually used?
  • ✓Treatment depends on serum bilirubin levels and hematocrit and reticulocyte values; hyperbilirubinemia is usually treated with phototherapy, and if hemolysis is severe, exchange transfusion and intravenous immunoglobulin are options
  • BAll infants with a positive test receive exchange transfusion
  • CNo treatment is ever needed for ABO incompatibility
  • DTreatment is determined by the mother's antibody titer
Rationale

"If an infant is born and has a positive DAT, treatment is dependent on serum bilirubin levels and hematocrit and reticulocyte values." Hyperbilirubinemia "is usually treated with phototherapy," and "if the hemolysis is severe, other treatment options include exchange transfusions and intravenous (IV) immunoglobulin." The resulting anemia "is usually mild, and hyperbilirubinemia is the most common complication."

Watch for

(B) escalates every positive test to the most invasive option, when the usual course is mild and phototherapy is usually enough.

14. What does Rh immune globulin do, and how effective is it?
  • ✓It is a commercial preparation of passive antibodies against the Rh factor; the injection destroys any fetal red cells in the maternal circulation and blocks maternal sensitization and antibody production, and it is 90% effective in preventing sensitization
  • BIt treats hemolysis in the fetus
  • CIt reverses sensitization once antibodies have formed
  • DIt is 100% effective
Rationale

"RhoGAM, a commercial preparation of passive antibodies against the Rh factor, is frequently used. This injection of anti-Rh antibodies destroys any fetal RBCs in the maternal circulation and blocks maternal sensitization and antibody production." And: "Rh immune globulin is 90% effective in preventing sensitization."

Watch for

(C) is the one thing it cannot do. It prevents antibody formation; it does nothing for a woman who has already produced antibodies, which is why the postpartum dose is for the unsensitized mother.

Rationale

"An anti-D titer greater than or equal to 1:16 is considered a critical titer, meaning the fetus is at risk for severe hemolytic disease and follow-up is needed," while "a level of 1:8 rarely results in fetal jeopardy." (C) offers prophylaxis to a woman whose titer proves she is already sensitized — the antepartum dose at 28 weeks is given when the screen "remains negative, indicating that sensitization has not occurred."

  • ✓It replaces the red cells that would otherwise be hemolyzed by circulating maternal antibodies, removes the antibodies responsible for hemolysis, and corrects the anemia; type O Rh-negative blood is used, because the negative Rh from the donor blood prevents the maternal antibodies still present in the infant's serum from hemolyzing the transfused cells

Rationale

The negative Rh from the donor blood prevents the maternal antibodies that are still present in the infant serum from hemolyzing the transfused blood RBCs."

(B) names one of three accomplishments and picks the blood type that the maternal antibodies would destroy.

  • CMyelomeningocele is the mildest form
  • DMeningocele always produces paralysis
Rationale

Spina bifida occulta is "a milder form ... The spinal cord or meninges do not herniate or protrude through the defect," most often at L5 and S1, with neurologic deficits "typically absent" and skin findings such as a dimple, nevus, hemangioma, or hair tuft. Meningocele "occurs when meninges and cerebrospinal fluid (CSF) extend through an irregular vertebral opening; the neural elements (spinal cord) are usually not present," and symptoms are rare. Myelomeningocele is "the most common form of spina bifida," with herniation of meninges, CSF, and neural elements, resulting in "partial or complete motor or sensory dysfunction of the body parts below the spinal opening."

Watch for

(C) reverses the severity. What distinguishes the three is how much comes through the defect, and myelomeningocele is the one that brings the spinal cord with it.

33. How does the chapter describe anencephaly and its prognosis?

Anencephaly "is an open defect and is a more serious NTD," in which "the cranial portion of the neural tube fails to close resulting in absence of both cerebral hemispheres and of the overlying skull." (D) names a different finding — microcephaly is "an occipitofrontal head circumference more than 3 standard deviations smaller than the mean for gestational age, weight, and sex," with a brain that is small rather than absent.

35. How does the chapter describe the range of cleft lip and cleft palate?
  • ✓Cleft lip is most often unilateral though it can be bilateral, and ranges from a simple notch in the lip to complete separation extending to the floor of the nose; cleft palate occurs midline in the secondary palate and ranges from a bifid uvula to a complete cleft extending from the soft to the hard palate
  • BBoth are always bilateral and complete
  • CCleft palate is always visible on external examination
  • DThe two always occur together
Rationale

"Cleft lip is most often unilateral, although it can occur bilaterally," and "cleft lip can range from a simple notch in the lip to complete separation of the lip extending to the floor of the nose. Cleft palate occurs midline in the secondary palate; it can range from a bifid uvula to a complete cleft extending from the soft to the hard palate." The most common defect is cleft lip with cleft palate at 6.4 per 10,000 live births, with cleft palate alone at 5.93 and cleft lip alone at 3.56.

Watch for

(C) would mean a cleft palate could not be missed, when a bifid uvula at the mild end requires the newborn oral examination to find it.

(B) is the answer a family may expect, and the multifactorial reality is why the exposure list overlaps so closely with the heart-defect and neural-tube lists earlier in the chapter.

(D) reverses the order, and the reason for the later palate repair is the one detail that makes the sequence memorable — the timing is set by speech development rather than by the size of the defect.

cleft lip and palaterepair and feeding
38. Why do infants with orofacial clefts have feeding difficulty, and which defect causes more trouble?
  • ✓A cleft lip leaves the newborn unable to maintain a seal around a nipple, and a cleft palate leaves the infant unable to form a vacuum to maintain suction; feeding problems are greater with cleft palate than with an isolated cleft lip
  • BBoth defects cause identical difficulty
  • CCleft lip causes more difficulty than cleft palate
  • DNeither affects feeding
Rationale

"The cleft lip renders the newborn unable to maintain a seal around a nipple; a cleft palate leaves the infant unable to form a vacuum to maintain suction when feeding," and "feeding problems are greater in infants with a cleft palate than in infants with an isolated cleft lip." Breastfeeding "is possible if the infant has a cleft lip alone but is more challenging with a cleft palate," and special nipples, bottles, and appliances such as the Haberman or pigeon feeder are used.

Watch for

(C) reverses the severity, and the mechanism explains why: a seal can be compensated for, but the vacuum cannot be generated at all without an intact palate.

39. What is the aspiration risk in an infant with an orofacial cleft, and what support do parents need?
  • ✓The inability to suck and swallow normally allows milk to pool in the nasopharynx, increasing the likelihood of aspiration, and milk often comes out through the cleft and the nares; parents need support, particularly with cleft lip because it is both a cosmetic and a functional defect, and the nurse must recognize that this can interfere with normal parent-infant bonding in the neonatal period and assess and intervene appropriately
  • BThere is no aspiration risk; parents need no additional support
  • CThe risk is limited to the first feeding, and parental concern is purely cosmetic
  • DAspiration occurs only with bottle feeding
Rationale

"The inability to suck and swallow normally allows milk to pool in the nasopharynx, which increases the likelihood of aspiration. As the infant attempts to suck, milk often comes out through the cleft and the nares." And: "Parents of infants with a cleft lip or palate need support, particularly with cleft lip because it is both a cosmetic and a functional defect. Recognizing that this can interfere with normal parent-infant bonding in the neonatal period, the nurse must assess for and intervene appropriately." Associated laryngeal clefts may require airway management.

Watch for

(C) reduces the parents' distress to appearance, and the chapter's point is that the visible defect and the bonding risk are connected — which is why assessment of bonding, not just of feeding, is named.
