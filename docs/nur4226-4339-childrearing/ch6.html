standard and transmission-based precautionsgeneral
1. Which precautions are designed for the care of all pediatric patients to reduce the risk of transmission of microorganisms from both recognized and unrecognized sources of infection?
  • ATransmission-Based Precautions
  • BAirborne Precautions
  • ✓Standard Precautions
  • DDroplet Precautions
Rationale

This is the chapter's own NCLEX question 1, and the stem quotes p. 169 almost verbatim: Standard Precautions "are designed for the care of all patients to reduce the risk of transmission of microorganisms from both recognized and unrecognized sources of infection."

Watch for

(A) is the reflex pick because the phrase sounds comprehensive, and it is exactly backwards — Transmission-Based Precautions are "designed for patients with documented or suspected infection or colonization" with specific pathogens. The two phrases that identify Standard Precautions in a stem are all patients and recognized and unrecognized.

Standard Precautions use "barrier protection (personal protective equipment [PPE]), such as gloves, goggles, gowns, and masks, to prevent contamination from blood; all body fluids, secretions, and excretions, except sweat, regardless of whether they contain visible blood; nonintact skin; and mucous membranes" (p. 169).

standard and transmission-based precautionsgeneral
3. A child is placed on Droplet Precautions. What is the nurse's responsibility regarding Standard Precautions?
  • AStandard Precautions are replaced by Droplet Precautions for the duration
  • ✓Transmission-Based Precautions are used in addition to Standard Precautions, and may be combined for diseases with multiple routes of transmission
  • CStandard Precautions resume once the droplet order is discontinued
  • DStandard Precautions apply only to the patient's roommate
Rationale

Two rules govern their use: "They may be combined for diseases that have multiple routes of transmission," and "They are to be used in addition to Standard Precautions" (p. 169). Box 6.1 repeats "In addition to Standard Precautions" at the head of every category.

Watch for

(A) is the distractor the fact bank flags — replacing Standard Precautions with a transmission-based type. They are additive, never a substitute, which is also why varicella carries three precaution types at once rather than one.

standard and transmission-based precautionsgeneral
4. A nurse must explain why measles requires a negative-pressure room but pertussis does not. What is the mechanism?
  • AMeasles droplets are larger and therefore travel further
  • ✓Airborne transmission involves droplet nuclei of 5 mm or less that remain suspended and can be dispersed widely by air currents, so special air handling and ventilation are required; droplet transmission involves particles larger than 5 mm that do not remain suspended and generally travel 3 feet or less, so special ventilation is not required
  • CMeasles is bacterial and pertussis is viral
  • DThe distinction is based on the severity of the disease, not the particle
Rationale

Airborne transmission is by "small-particle residue [≤5 mm] of evaporated droplets that may remain suspended in the air for long periods" which "can be dispersed widely by air currents"; droplets are "large-particle droplets (>5 mm)" that "generally travel only short distances, usually 3 feet or less" (p. 169).

Watch for

(A) inverts the size relationship, which is the whole mechanism — the smaller particle is the one that stays airborne and needs ventilation. Note that 3 feet appears twice in this chapter: as droplet travel distance and as the cough-etiquette spacing.

standard and transmission-based precautionsgeneral
5. A nurse is asked to name the single most important practice for reducing transmission of infectious disease in a health care setting. What is it?
  • AWearing gloves for all patient contact
  • ✓Hand hygiene, including hand washing with soap and water and the use of alcohol-based products for hand disinfection
  • CPlacing all children with fever in isolation
  • DRoutine environmental disinfection
Rationale

"Hand hygiene continues to be the single most important practice to reduce the transmission of infectious diseases in health care settings," and it is restated for resistant organisms: "Hand washing is the most critical infection control practice" (pp. 169–170). Approximately 2 million patients each year develop hospital-acquired infections, which "are preventable if caregivers practice meticulous hand washing, cleaning, and disposal techniques."

Watch for

(A) is the visible intervention and it is not the ranked one. The fact bank's rule is worth carrying into any priority item in this chapter: hand hygiene is the default answer, and the chapter names it as most important twice.

6. A child is admitted with an undiagnosed rash. Which precautions should be instituted?
  • AStandard Precautions alone until the rash is identified
  • ✓Contact, airborne, and droplet precautions plus Standard Precautions, until a diagnosis is confirmed
  • CAirborne Precautions alone, since most exanthems are airborne
  • DDroplet Precautions alone
Rationale

The Nursing Alert states: "If a child is admitted to the hospital with an undiagnosed exanthema, institute strict Transmission-Based Precautions (contact, airborne, and droplet) and Standard Precautions until a diagnosis is confirmed" (p. 187).

Watch for

(C) applies the precaution for the most feared possibility instead of covering all of them, and the fact bank calls this the single most likely precautions item in the chapter because it inverts the usual logic: you cannot pick the precaution until you know the disease, so you use all three.

7. A child with chickenpox is admitted. Which precautions are required, and when may they be discontinued?
  • AStandard and Droplet, until the fever resolves
  • ✓Standard, Airborne, and Contact, until all lesions are crusted — or for breakthrough varicella, until no new lesions are seen
  • CContact alone, until the rash fades
  • DAirborne alone, until the fifth day of rash
Rationale

Varicella requires Standard plus Airborne plus Contact precautions "until all lesions are crusted"; for breakthrough varicella, "until no new lesions are seen" (p. 179). Scabs are not infectious, which is why crusting ends isolation.

Watch for

(D) attaches the measles endpoint to varicella — measles requires Airborne Precautions "until fifth day of rash." The fact bank warns that a distractor gives the right precaution type with the wrong endpoint, and the "until" column is the second half of every precautions item.

8. A school-age child is diagnosed with scarlet fever and started on antibiotics. When may precautions be discontinued?
  • AImmediately, since treatment has begun
  • ✓24 hours after initiation of treatment
  • CAfter two negative throat cultures
  • DWhen the rash has fully desquamated
Rationale

Scarlet fever requires Standard plus Droplet precautions "until 24 hours after initiation of treatment" (p. 185).

Watch for

(C) is the endpoint for pharyngeal diphtheria, which requires Standard plus Droplet "until two cultures are negative" (p. 179). Both are droplet-spread bacterial throat infections treated with antibiotics, so the endpoint is the only thing separating them.

9. A nurse reviews Table 6.2 and is asked which diseases in it require Airborne Precautions. What is the answer?
  • AAll of the viral exanthems
  • ✓Only varicella and measles
  • CMeasles, rubella, and mumps
  • DPertussis and diphtheria
Rationale

Of the diseases in Table 6.2, only varicella and measles require Airborne Precautions; Box 6.1 names the same two plus tuberculosis (pp. 169, 179, 182). Everything else in the table is Droplet, Contact, or Standard.

(C) groups the three classic childhood viral exanthems together, and two of them are wrong — rubella is Droplet and mumps is Droplet plus Contact. Being viral and rash-producing does not determine the route.

10. A hospitalized child with sickle cell disease develops an aplastic crisis attributed to parvovirus B19. A well sibling at home also has fifth disease. What precautions apply to each?
  • ABoth require Droplet Precautions
  • ✓The hospitalized child with aplastic crisis requires Standard plus Droplet Precautions; the well child at home requires none
  • CNeither requires precautions, since fifth disease is benign
  • DBoth require Airborne Precautions
Rationale

Erythema infectiosum requires no precautions for the well child; the hospitalized immunosuppressed child or the child in aplastic crisis gets Standard plus Droplet Precautions (p. 181). Its period of communicability is "Uncertain but before onset of symptoms in children with aplastic crisis."

Watch for

(A) applies one rule to both children, and the chapter deliberately splits them. The reason is timing: a well child with the classic rash is past the contagious period, while the child in aplastic crisis is not — so the same diagnosis carries different precautions depending on the presentation.

11. A child was exposed to measles 12 days ago and is now well. What should the nurse tell the family about the incubation period?
  • AThe child is past the incubation period and cannot develop measles
  • ✓The incubation period for measles is 10 to 20 days, so the child remains within it
  • CThe incubation period is 2 to 5 days, so the child is clear
  • DThe incubation period is 14 to 21 days, so the child is clear
Rationale

Measles has an incubation of 10 to 20 days, with a period of communicability "From 4 days before to 5 days after rash appears" (p. 182).

Watch for

(D) is the incubation for mumps and rubella, both 14 to 21 days, and chickenpox overlaps at 14 to 16 days — the three are not separable by incubation alone. Only measles runs from 10 days.

12. A parent asks when a child with measles was most contagious. What is the accurate answer?
  • AOnly while the rash is visible
  • ✓From 4 days before the rash appears to 5 days after — so the child was contagious before any rash was seen
  • CBeginning 24 hours after the rash appears
  • DOnly after the fever resolves
Rationale

Measles is communicable "From 4 days before to 5 days after rash appears" (p. 182). Koplik spots appear on the buccal mucosa opposite the molars 2 days before the rash, while the child is most infectious.

Watch for

(A) is the intuition the fact bank names as an inversion — isolate a rash once it appears. Five diseases in this chapter are contagious before any sign shows: chickenpox 1 day before, measles 4 days before, rubella 7 days before, mumps immediately before swelling, and pertussis during the catarrhal stage. Rubella has the longest presymptomatic contagious window.

13. A 6-year-old develops a sore throat and fever with an incubation history of about 3 days since exposure. Which two diseases in this chapter have the shortest incubation periods, and what is the nursing priority?
  • AMeasles and rubella; begin airborne precautions
  • ✓Diphtheria and scarlet fever, both 2 to 5 days — and the priority is a throat culture, since a sore throat with fever may be diphtheria or streptococcal
  • CMumps and chickenpox; observe at home
  • DPertussis and polio; begin droplet precautions only
Rationale

Diphtheria incubates in "Usually 2-5 days, possibly longer" and scarlet fever in "2-5 days, with range of 1-7 days" (pp. 179, 185) — the two bacterial diseases in the table, and the two shortest incubations. The chapter's priority default for a sore throat with fever is a culture, because it may be diphtheria or streptococcal (p. 186).

Watch for

(C) names diseases whose incubations run two to three weeks, which does not fit a 3-day history. The general pattern is worth holding: the viral exanthems in this chapter all run a week or more, so a short incubation points bacterial.

14. A child with pertussis is admitted during the paroxysmal stage. When was the period of greatest communicability, and what is the immediate nursing priority during a paroxysm?
  • ACommunicability is greatest during the paroxysmal stage; the priority is to sit the child upright
  • ✓Communicability is greatest during the catarrhal stage, before the onset of paroxysms; during a paroxysm, position the child on the side, ensure oxygenation, and suction
  • CCommunicability is greatest during convalescence; the priority is fluid replacement
  • DPertussis is not communicable once coughing begins
Rationale

Pertussis communicability is "Greatest during catarrhal stage before onset of paroxysms," and Droplet Precautions are maintained "during catarrhal stage" (p. 182). The chapter's priority default for an infant with pertussis paroxysms is to position on the side, ensure oxygenation, and suction.

Watch for

(A) assumes the loudest phase is the most infectious phase, which is the reverse of the truth and is why pertussis spreads before anyone suspects it — during the catarrhal stage it looks like a cold. For scale, adolescents cough at least 21 days and up to half cough for 10 weeks.

immunity terminology and vaccine typesgeneral
15. A newborn is protected from several infections by antibodies received across the placenta. What type of immunity is this?
  • ANatural active immunity
  • ✓Natural passive immunity
  • CArtificial active immunity
  • DHerd immunity
Rationale

Active immunity is formed by the person's own immune bodies; passive immunity is "Temporary immunity obtained by transfusing immunoglobulins or antitoxins," and it can be acquired naturally from the mother to the fetus via the placenta or artificially by transfusion (p. 171).

Watch for

(A) is the reflex pick because the antibody arrived by a natural route, and "natural" gets attached to the wrong axis. The two axes are independent: natural versus artificial describes how it was acquired, active versus passive describes who made the antibodies.

immunity terminology and vaccine typesgeneral
16. A nurse must distinguish a vaccine, a toxoid, and an antitoxin. Which set of definitions is correct?
  • AVaccine: a modified bacterial toxin; toxoid: live or inactivated organisms; antitoxin: a conjugated polysaccharide
  • ✓Vaccine: a suspension of live or inactivated organisms; toxoid: a modified bacterial toxin made nontoxic; antitoxin: ready-made antibodies from the serum of an immunized animal, conferring passive immunity
  • CAll three produce active immunity
  • DVaccine and toxoid both confer passive immunity
Rationale

Box 6.2 defines each of these on p. 171. An antitoxin confers passive immunity because the antibodies are transferred rather than formed, while vaccines and toxoids produce active immunity.

Watch for

(C) is the reflex assumption that anything given to prevent disease works the same way. It matters clinically: a child receiving equine antitoxin needs a sensitivity test first with epinephrine available, a precaution that has no counterpart for a vaccine.

17. An unvaccinated child in a highly vaccinated community is protected from a disease he was never immunized against. What concept describes this?
  • AActive immunity
  • ✓Herd immunity — the population that has been vaccinated protects those in the same population who are unvaccinated
  • CCocooning
  • DConjugate protection
Rationale

The definition names the unvaccinated explicitly: "the population that has been vaccinated protects those in the same population who are unvaccinated" (p. 171).

Watch for

(C) is the nearest relative and is narrower and deliberate. Cocooning means "protecting infants from pertussis by vaccinating all persons who come in close contact with the infant, including the mother, grandparents, and health care workers" — a targeted ring around one infant, not a population effect, and pertussis-specific.

immunity terminology and vaccine typesgeneral
18. A nurse documents that a child was "immunized" after administering a vaccine. What distinction does the chapter draw?
  • AThe terms are synonymous in all contexts
  • ✓They are not synonymous: vaccination is the physical act of administering, while immunization is the process of inducing or providing immunity — the administration of a vaccine cannot automatically be equated with the development of adequate immunity
  • CImmunization refers only to live vaccines
  • DVaccination refers only to inactivated vaccines
Rationale

"Although in this discussion the terms vaccination and immunization are used interchangeably in reference to active immunization, they are not synonymous because the administration of an immunobiologic such as a vaccine cannot automatically be equated with the development of adequate immunity" (p. 171).

Watch for

(A) is what the chapter itself does in casual use and then explicitly disavows, which is what makes this testable. Giving the vaccine is an act; producing immunity is an outcome, and the two can come apart.

vaccine contraindications and precautionsgeneral
19. A nurse is deciding whether to withhold a vaccine. What is the difference between a contraindication and a precaution?
  • AThey are equivalent; both bar administration
  • ✓A contraindication is a condition that increases the risk for a serious adverse reaction, and the vaccine is not given; a precaution might increase that risk or compromise the vaccine's ability to produce immunity, and the vaccine is given if the benefit outweighs the risk
  • CA precaution bars administration and a contraindication permits it
  • DA precaution applies only to live vaccines
Rationale

A contraindication is "a condition in an individual that increases the risk for a serious adverse reaction," and "one would not administer a vaccine when a contraindication is present." A precaution "might increase the risk... or that might compromise the ability of the vaccine to produce immunity," and "If conditions are such that the benefit of receiving the vaccine would outweigh the risk of an adverse event or incomplete response, a precaution would not prevent vaccine administration" (p. 177).

Watch for

(A) treats both as absolute bars, and the chapter warns what that costs: "Unfounded fears and lack of knowledge regarding contraindications can needlessly prevent a child from having protection from life-threatening diseases."

  • ✓Altered immune function, for live virus vaccines
  • ✓Recently acquired passive immunity through blood transfusion or immunoglobulin, for live virus vaccines
  • ✓Anaphylactic reaction to a previously administered vaccine or one of its components

21. A parent reports that her child had contact dermatitis after neomycin-containing ointment and asks whether MMR can be given. What should the nurse do?
  • AWithhold MMR permanently
  • ✓Administer MMR — contact dermatitis in reaction to neomycin is not a contraindication; only a history of anaphylactic reaction to neomycin, gelatin, or the vaccine itself is
  • CAdminister half the dose and observe
  • DSubstitute individual measles, mumps, and rubella vaccines
Rationale

"Contact dermatitis in reaction to neomycin is not considered a contraindication to immunization," while "only a history of anaphylactic reaction to neomycin, gelatin, or the vaccine itself is considered a contraindication to their use" (p. 177).

Watch for

(A) is the cautious-sounding answer and it withholds protection on the basis of a reaction type the chapter specifically clears. The fact bank calls this the sharpest pair in the section: anaphylaxis to neomycin is a contraindication, contact dermatitis to neomycin is not.

22. A 4-year-old received immunoglobulin two weeks ago and is due for MMR and varicella vaccines. What should the nurse do?
  • AGive both vaccines today
  • ✓Postpone MMR and varicella for a minimum of 3 months after passive immunization with immunoglobulin or blood transfusion — except after washed red blood cells, which do not interfere with the immune response
  • CPostpone for 1 month
  • DPostpone for 28 days
Rationale

"Administration of MMR and varicella should be postponed for a minimum of 3 months after passive immunization with immunoglobulins and blood transfusions (except washed red blood cells, which do not interfere with the immune response)" (p. 177).

Watch for

(C) and (D) are two other real intervals from this chapter attached to the wrong situation — 1 month is the minimum between two live vaccines not given on the same day, and 28 days is the pregnancy-avoidance interval after rubella vaccine. Three intervals, three situations; match the interval to the situation rather than to a general rule.

vaccine contraindications and precautionsgeneral
23. A child arrives for a well visit with a runny nose and a temperature of 37.4° C, and is breastfeeding. Which of these is a reason to withhold today's vaccines?
  • AThe minor upper respiratory infection
  • BBreastfeeding
  • ✓Neither; minor illnesses such as the common cold are not a contraindication, and breastfeeding is not a contraindication for any vaccine
  • DBoth, until the child is well and weaned
Rationale

"The presence of minor illnesses, such as the common cold, is not a contraindication," and "Breastfeeding is not a contraindication for any vaccine" (p. 177). The general contraindication is a severe febrile illness, which avoids "adding the risk of adverse side effects from the vaccine to an already ill child or mistakenly identifying a symptom of the disease as having been caused by the vaccine."

Watch for

(A) is the most common real-world deferral and it is one of the nine non-contraindications the chapter lists — alongside contact dermatitis to neomycin, egg sensitivity for MMR, family history of seizures, family history of a vaccine adverse event, penicillin allergy, duck allergy, and family history of sudden infant death syndrome.

pertussis diphtheria and tetanus vaccinesgeneral
24. A 4-year-old needs a vaccine containing diphtheria, tetanus, and pertussis components. Which preparation is correct?
  • ATdap
  • ✓DTaP
  • CTd
  • DDT
Rationale

DTaP is the pediatric formulation for children 6 weeks through 6 years; Tdap is for those 11 and older; Td contains tetanus and diphtheria only; and DT is for a child under 7 who has a contraindication to the pertussis component (pp. 172–173).

Watch for

(D) is the correct choice only when pertussis is contraindicated, and the stem specifies that the pertussis component is wanted. The capitalization convention carries the meaning: uppercase letters are full-strength antigens and lowercase are reduced, so DTaP and Tdap differ by which components are reduced, not by which diseases they cover.

26. The parents of a 2-week-old ask how to protect their newborn from pertussis before the infant can be vaccinated. What strategy does the chapter describe?
  • AGive the infant a dose of DTaP now
  • ✓Cocooning — vaccinating all persons who come in close contact with the infant, including the mother, grandparents, and health care workers
  • CGive the infant immunoglobulin monthly
  • DIsolate the infant at home for 6 months

(A) misses the reason the strategy exists — the infant is not the one vaccinated, because the vaccine cannot be given early enough to protect during the period of highest risk. 30. An infant is born to a mother who is hepatitis B surface antigen positive. What is required, and in what time frame?
  • AHepatitis B vaccine alone, within 24 hours
  • ✓Hepatitis B vaccine plus hepatitis B immune globulin 0.5 ml within 12 hours of birth, given at two separate sites
  • CHepatitis B immune globulin alone, at 1 month
  • DHepatitis B vaccine at 2 months with the routine series
Rationale

The newborn of an HBsAg-positive mother receives HepB vaccine plus HBIG 0.5 ml within 12 hours of birth, at two sites (p. 172). The stakes are in the epidemiology: perinatal infection becomes chronic in up to 90% of cases, compared with 25% to 50% for infection acquired before age 5.

Watch for

(A) gives half the regimen. Passive and active immunization are both required here and do different jobs — the immune globulin provides antibodies now, the vaccine provokes the infant's own. Note the separate weight rule for the routine series: HepB is deferred below 2000 g (4.4 lb).

31. A 4-month-old has not yet received any rotavirus vaccine. What limits the nurse's ability to start the series now, and what is the general rule for a lapsed series?
  • AThe series may not be started after 32 weeks of age; and a lapsed series of any vaccine must be restarted from the beginning
  • ✓The rotavirus series may not be given after 32 weeks of age; and for a lapsed series, only the missed doses are given rather than restarting
  • CThere is no upper age limit for rotavirus; a lapsed series is restarted
  • DRotavirus may be given at any age, and a lapsed series continues where it left off
Rationale

The rotavirus vaccine is "not [given] after 32 weeks" (p. 176), and the chapter's catch-up rule is that a lapsed series is not restarted — only the missed doses are given (p. 172).

Watch for

(A) pairs the correct age limit with the wrong catch-up rule, which is the fact bank's flagged inversion: "A lapsed vaccine series must be restarted" is the intuition, and the chapter says give only the missed doses. Note the related preterm rule that runs the same way: preterm infants receive the full dose at the appropriate chronologic age, not a delayed or reduced one.

  • ✓⅝ inch, 22 to 25 gauge, vastus lateralis
  • C1.5 inch, 20 gauge, dorsogluteal
  • D⅝ inch, 22 to 25 gauge, deltoid

33. A nurse is giving an intramuscular vaccine to a toddler. Which actions are correct? (Select all that apply.)
Select all that apply · scored all or nothing
  • ✓Give the vaccine deep into the muscle rather than into subcutaneous tissue
  • ✓Do not aspirate for blood before injecting
  • ✓Do not manually stimulate the site by rubbing or applying pressure afterward
  • ✓Have the parent hold the child during the injection
  • EAspirate slowly for 5 seconds to confirm placement before injecting
Rationale

"Fewer reactions to immunizations are observed when the vaccine is given deep into the muscle rather than into subcutaneous tissue." Aspiration "is no longer recommended for an intramuscular injection because large blood vessels are not present at recommended injection sites and slow aspiration causes more pain than injection without aspiration." Beneficial positioning means "being held by a parent or caregiver for infants and young children, and sitting upright for older children and adolescents," and item 7 directs: "Do not manually stimulate (i.e., rubbing or applying pressure) the injection site" (p. 176).

Watch for

(E) is the one to reject, and it is what many nurses were originally taught — which is exactly why the chapter states both the new rule and its two reasons. Aspiration is not merely unnecessary here; slow aspiration actively hurts more.

  • DBoth for at least 1 hour

The discriminator is the relationship between fever and rash: in roseola the fever breaks as the rash appears and the child looks well; in measles the fever peaks with the rash and the child looks ill (pp. 181–182).

(C) reverses the pair, and the general appearance is as diagnostic as the timing here. The precautions differ accordingly — roseola requires Standard Precautions only, while measles requires Airborne Precautions until the fifth day of rash.

  • CPediculosis capitis
  • DEnterobiasis

43. A parent reports that her 6-year-old has intense perianal itching, worse at night. How is the diagnosis confirmed?
  • AA stool specimen collected at any time of day
  • ✓A tape test: transparent tape, sticky side out on a tongue depressor, pressed against the perianal area in the morning as soon as the child awakens, before a bowel movement or bathing, on 3 consecutive days
  • CA serum antibody titer
  • DA perianal swab collected in the evening
Rationale

"A loop of transparent (not frosted or magic) tape, sticky side out, is placed around the end of a tongue depressor, which is then firmly pressed against the child's perianal area." Specimens "are collected in the morning as soon as the child awakens and before the child has a bowel movement or bathes," and the procedure "may need to be performed on 3 or more consecutive days" (p. 193).

Watch for

(D) breaks the timing conditions, and an evening or post-bath specimen is worthless — the eggs are laid overnight and are removed by washing or defecation. Note two further specifics: the tape must be transparent, not frosted, because frosted tape is opaque under a microscope; and it goes sticky side out on the depressor but sticky side down on the slide.
