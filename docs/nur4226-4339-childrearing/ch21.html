1. A nurse is orienting a new graduate to the pediatric unit and explains that nearly every psychosocial intervention on the unit traces back to a short list of stressors. Which are the major stressors of hospitalization named by the chapter? Select all that apply.
Select all that apply · scored all or nothing
  • ✓Separation
  • ✓Loss of control
  • ✓Bodily injury
  • ✓Pain
  • ELoss of privacy
Rationale

The chapter names the major stressors of hospitalization as "separation, loss of control, bodily injury, and pain" (p. 658), and organizes both the developmental reactions and the nursing interventions around them.

Watch for

(E) is the plausible non-member — privacy is genuinely addressed in the intensive care measures, but it is not one of the four; the chapter's stated reason children are vulnerable at all is that they "have a limited number of coping mechanisms to resolve stressors."

2. A 3-year-old is admitted after a previous hospitalization at age 2. The nurse assumes she will settle in easily because she has been through this before. How should this assumption be evaluated?
  • ✓It is unwarranted; the chapter directs nurses to avoid assuming a child has learned to cope from prior medical experiences
  • BIt is reasonable, since previous experience is listed as a factor that shapes a child's reaction
  • CIt is reasonable for children over 2, who retain procedural memories of hospitalization
  • DIt is unwarranted only if the prior admission involved painful procedures
Rationale

The chapter states explicitly that "Nurses should avoid assuming the child has learned how to cope from prior medical experiences" (p. 661), and adds that "all children, even those who have been hospitalized before, benefit from an introduction to the environment and routine of the hospital unit" (p. 662).

Watch for

(B) is true as far as it goes — previous experience with illness and hospitalization is listed among the factors shaping a reaction — and then draws exactly the wrong inference; multiple and continuing stresses such as frequent hospitalizations are a *risk* factor, not a protective one.

3. A nurse is reviewing two children admitted the same morning. One protests loudly, refuses to cooperate, and demands his parents. The other accepts every change quietly and does whatever she is asked. Which child does the chapter say may need more support?
  • ✓The quiet, compliant child, because children who are active and strong willed tend to fare better when hospitalized
  • BThe protesting child, because his distress is unmistakable and immediate
  • CBoth equally, since outward behavior does not predict adjustment in either direction
  • DNeither, since the response to admission does not predict the response to hospitalization
Rationale

The chapter states that "children who are active and strong willed tend to fare better when hospitalized than those who are passive," and directs that "nurses should be alert to children who passively accept all changes and requests; these children may need more support than" oppositional "children" (p. 661).

Watch for

(B) is the intuitive triage, and it is the same reflex that makes staff misread despair as settling in and detachment as good adjustment; in this chapter, quiet compliance is a finding to investigate rather than a sign of coping.

4. The parents of a 7-year-old ask whether hospitalization will leave lasting harm. Which response reflects the chapter's position?
  • ✓Children are remarkably adaptable and permanent ill effects are rare, and hospitalization can even be an opportunity to master stress and feel competent
  • BLasting effects are common, so the stay should be shortened as much as possible
  • CPermanent effects depend entirely on the length of stay rather than on the family's involvement
  • DLasting harm is unlikely because school-age children do not experience separation anxiety
Rationale

The chapter states that "even with stressors, children are remarkably adaptable, and permanent ill effects are rare" (p. 658), and lists among the beneficial effects that hospitalization "can present an opportunity for children to master stress and feel competent in their coping abilities" (p. 661).

Watch for

(C) names the wrong variable, since what lessens adverse responses is "family-centered care and frequent family visiting"; (D) is simply false, as school-age children's separation concerns are described in detail, and they may want parental support but be unwilling to ask for it.

5. A nurse is designing a unit survey and wants to ask about what children themselves say frightens them most about being in the hospital. Which fears does the chapter report children naming?
  • ✓Being separated from family and friends, being in an unfamiliar environment, receiving treatments, and losing self-determination or choices
  • BNeedle sticks, surgery, and the possibility of dying
  • CMissing school, falling behind classmates, and losing contact with teachers
  • DUnfamiliar food, uncomfortable beds, and being woken at night
Rationale

The chapter reports that children name "being separated from family and friends, being in an unfamiliar environment, receiving treatments, and losing self-determination or choices," and that children 5 to 9 years old described hospitalization in stories focused on "being alone and feeling scared, angry, or sad" (p. 660).

Watch for

(B) lists the fears adults expect children to name, and the chapter's own list leads with separation instead; note that three of the four map directly onto the four major stressors, which is why those four organize the whole chapter.

the three stages of separation anxietygeneral
6. An 18-month-old admitted this morning screams when his mother steps out, refuses to let the nurse touch him, and tries to climb out of the crib to follow her. Which stage of separation anxiety does this describe?
  • ✓Protest
  • BDespair
  • CDetachment
  • DRegression
Rationale

In the protest stage "children react aggressively to the separation from the parent. They cry and scream for their parents, refuse the attention of anyone else, and are inconsolable in their grief" (p. 658), and Box 21.1 adds the toddler behaviors of attempting to escape to find the parent and physically forcing the parent to stay.

Watch for

(D) names a real behavior from a different stage — regression to thumb sucking, bedwetting, or bottle use belongs to despair; protest behaviors "may last from hours to days" and may cease "only with physical exhaustion," which is what makes the next stage look like improvement.

7. On the third hospital day, the same toddler has stopped crying. He lies quietly, will not play, refuses meals, and has begun sucking his thumb again. A staff member remarks that he has finally settled in. How should the nurse interpret this?
  • ✓These are despair behaviors, and the child's physical condition may deteriorate from refusal to eat, drink, or move
  • BThese are detachment behaviors, indicating the child has formed new attachments on the unit
  • CThe child has adjusted, and parental visits can now be reduced to avoid re-triggering protest
  • DThe quiet behavior indicates effective coping and requires no additional intervention
Rationale

In despair "the crying stops and depression is evident. The child is much less active, is uninterested in play or food, and withdraws from others," with regression to earlier behavior, and Box 21.1 notes the child's "physical condition may deteriorate from refusal to eat, drink, or move" (p. 658).

Watch for

(C) and (D) act on the misreading the chapter names outright — staff "may think that the child is finally settling in to the new surroundings" (p. 658) — and (C) compounds it by reducing the very parental contact that is the intervention; this is the mirror of the previous item, where the same child's loud protest was misread as bad behavior.

8. A hospitalized 2-year-old now smiles at every staff member, plays readily with whoever enters, and appears happy. A nurse documents that she has adjusted well. What should a more experienced nurse point out?
  • ✓Behaviors like these can represent detachment, which is resignation rather than contentment, though detachment is rarely seen in hospitalized children
  • BThese behaviors confirm adjustment, since forming relationships with staff is the goal of the stay
  • CThese behaviors indicate the child has moved backward into protest and needs her parents recalled
  • DThese behaviors are diagnostic of detachment and indicate permanent harm has occurred
Rationale

In detachment the child "superficially appears to adjust to the loss" and forms "new but superficial relationships," but "this behavior is the result of resignation and is not a sign of contentment"; the chapter adds that detachment "usually occurs after prolonged separation from parent" and "is rarely seen in hospitalized children" (p. 658).

Watch for

(D) overreaches in the opposite direction from (B), and the qualifier is what this item turns on — a briefly hospitalized child should not be labeled detached, even though the chapter warns that the faster a child reaches this appearance, "the more likely it is that the child will be regarded as the ideal patient."

9. A 20-month-old's protest intensifies each time the nurse approaches the bedside. What should the nurse do?
  • ✓Remain present and provide support through a quiet tone of voice, eye contact, and touch that communicates empathy
  • BStay away from the bedside, since the approach of a stranger clearly worsens the child's distress
  • CAsk the parents to remain outside the room until the child's protest subsides
  • DDocument that the child is not yet ready for nursing interaction and reattempt in several hours
Rationale

The chapter directs that protest and despair are normal and that the nurse provides support "via their physical presence, using a quiet tone of voice, maintaining eye contact, and using touch in a way that establishes rapport and communicates empathy" (p. 665).

Watch for

(B) is named by the chapter as a misreading: "Because the protests increase when a stranger approaches the child, they may interpret that reaction as meaning they should stay away" (p. 658); (C) inverts the primary goal, which is preventing separation, particularly in children under 5.

11. A 4-year-old admitted two days ago is refusing to eat, has trouble falling asleep, cries quietly, and repeatedly asks when her parents will come. The nurse considers whether this is separation anxiety. What is accurate?
  • ✓It is separation anxiety; in preschoolers the protest behaviors are more subtle and passive than in toddlers
  • BIt is not separation anxiety, since preschoolers tolerate brief separations and develop substitute trust in other adults
  • CIt is not separation anxiety, since the behaviors are passive rather than the protest behaviors that define it
  • DIt is separation anxiety only if the child also displays overt aggression toward staff
Rationale

The chapter states that in preschoolers "the protest behaviors are more subtle and passive": "refusing to eat, experiencing difficulty in sleeping, crying quietly for their parents, continually asking when the parents will visit, or withdrawing from others," and adds that "Nurses need to be sensitive to these less obvious signs" (p. 660).

Watch for

(B) uses a true sentence from the same paragraph — preschoolers are "more secure interpersonally than toddlers" — to reach the wrong conclusion, since illness renders them less able to cope; (C) applies the toddler's loud template and misses the age-specific presentation entirely.

12. A 9-year-old admitted for a femur fracture becomes irritable with his parents and withdraws from the staff. He tells the nurse he is fine and does not need anything. What does the chapter suggest is happening?
  • ✓School-age children may need and desire parental guidance or support but be unable or unwilling to ask for it, fearing they will appear weak or childish
  • BThe behavior indicates the child has entered the despair stage of separation anxiety
  • CThe behavior indicates that school-age children are largely unaffected by hospitalization
  • DThe behavior is best addressed by increasing the child's independence and reducing adult involvement
Rationale

The chapter states that school-age children "may need and desire parental guidance or support from adults but may be unable or unwilling to ask for it," fearing they "will appear weak, childish, or dependent," and that negative feelings may surface as "irritability and aggression toward parents, withdrawal from hospital personnel" (p. 660).

Watch for

(D) takes the child's stated self-sufficiency at face value and withdraws the support the chapter says he wants but cannot request; cultural expectations to "be brave and strong" push toward stoicism, which is why the stated request is not the reliable signal here.

13. A 15-year-old scheduled for a two-week admission is most likely to identify which loss as the severest emotional threat?
  • ✓Loss of peer-group contact, with its loss of group status, group control, and group acceptance
  • BSeparation from her parents, which peaks in adolescence as autonomy develops
  • CLoss of privacy during physical examinations and procedures
  • DFalling behind in schoolwork and being unable to compete with classmates
Rationale

The chapter states that for adolescents "Loss of peer-group contact may pose a severe emotional threat" because of "loss of group status, inability to exert group control or leadership, and loss of group acceptance," and notes that although the group expresses concern, "they continue their group activities, quickly filling the gap of the absent member" (p. 660).

Watch for

(D) is the school-age concern — worrying "that they will not be able to compete or fit in with their classmates" — and it is the closest distractor, since both involve peers; the discriminator is that the school-age worry is about keeping up while the adolescent's is about group membership itself.

14. A hospitalized 5-year-old has a dressing changed. Which fear is most consistent with the chapter's account of this age?
  • ✓Fear of bodily injury, which may be addressed by applying a small bandage and leaving dressings in place
  • BFear of the resulting scar, which is the dominant body-image concern at this age
  • CFear of the operation itself and the stitches, more than of the pain
  • DFear of falling behind in kindergarten during the admission
Rationale

The chapter states that "Beyond early infancy, all children fear bodily injury," that young children's body boundaries are poorly defined so "applying a small Band-Aid usually reassures them," and that "Prematurely removing a dressing may cause these children considerable concern" (p. 667).

Watch for

(B) is the adolescent concern — adolescents "may express concern about the actual procedure but be much more anxious over the resulting scar" — and (C) is the school-age concern; all three options are real chapter content attached to the wrong age, which is how the chapter's own review item is built.

15. A 2-year-old admitted for pneumonia is having temper tantrums, refusing meals, and has stopped using the toilet. His father asks whether hospitalization has set him back permanently. What should the nurse explain?
  • ✓These behaviors are the toddler's response to separation, which is the greatest stress of hospitalization in early childhood, and if separation is avoided young children have a tremendous capacity to withstand other stress
  • BThese behaviors indicate the child has entered the detachment stage and needs psychiatric consultation
  • CThese behaviors are unrelated to hospitalization and reflect normal toddler negativism
  • DThese behaviors predict lasting developmental regression and should be addressed with a behavior plan
Rationale

The chapter states that separation anxiety "is the greatest stress imposed by hospitalization during early childhood" and that "If separation is avoided, young children have a tremendous capacity to withstand any other stress," listing tantrums, refusal of usual mealtime and toileting routines, and regression among toddler behaviors (p. 659).

Watch for

(C) dismisses the behaviors as ordinary toddlerhood, and the chapter does note that such behaviors "may also be expressions of anger, a physiologic response to stress, or symptoms of illness" — so the alternatives are real, but the answer is not to stop looking at separation; (D) reads a temporary reaction as a lasting one.

16. The parents of a 6-year-old scheduled for elective surgery in three weeks ask when to tell her. What should the nurse advise?
  • ✓About 1 week in advance, so she can assimilate the information and ask questions
  • BOn the morning of surgery, so she has no time to become anxious
  • CImmediately, so she has the maximum time to prepare
  • DOne to two days in advance, which is the standard for all preschool and school-age children
Rationale

The chapter recommends preparing "Children 4 to 7 years old" about 1 week in advance "so that they can assimilate the information and ask questions," with a longer lead time for older children (p. 662).

Watch for

(D) applies the young-child interval — "1 or 2 days before admission is sufficient" for young children, because they "may begin to wonder about what they observed" — to a 6-year-old; the two lead times are the pair distractors most often swap, and the governing principle throughout is that "a fear of the unknown exceeds fear of the known."

17. A 3-year-old is admitted emergently and there was no opportunity for preparation. What should the nurse do?
  • ✓Orient the child and family to hospital routines, establish expectations, and allow for questions
  • BDefer all orientation until the child is stable, to avoid adding to the stress of the admission
  • CProvide the standard 1-week preparation content in condensed form during the admission
  • DDirect orientation to the parents only, since the child is too young to benefit
Rationale

The chapter directs that when sudden illness makes preparation impossible, the nurse should still "orient the child and family to hospital routines, establish expectations, and allow for questions" (p. 662), and states that all children benefit from an introduction to the environment and routine of the unit.

Watch for

(D) reasons from the child's age to exclude her, but the chapter's emergency guidance is the opposite — unless the situation is life threatening, "children need to participate in their care to maintain a sense of control" (p. 674).

18. A nurse is completing the nursing admission history on a 4-year-old. What is the main purpose of this history?
  • ✓To assess the child's usual health habits at home so a more normal environment can be provided in the hospital
  • BTo obtain a complete medication history so safe orders can be written
  • CTo predict how the hospital stay is likely to proceed and guide roommate selection
  • DTo document baseline physical findings against which improvement can be measured
Rationale

The chapter states that "One of the main purposes of the history is to assess the child's usual health habits at home to promote a more normal environment in the hospital" (p. 662), and notes that the self-perception and role-relationship patterns "offer insight into the child's potential reaction to hospitalization, especially in terms of separation."

Watch for

(B) is the option most people miss, because asking about medications sounds unarguably correct; the admission history in this chapter is a psychosocial instrument, and medication and physical data belong to the physical assessment, which the chapter routes to another chapter entirely.

19. During the admission history, a mother mentions that she gives her son an herbal preparation daily. He is scheduled for surgery tomorrow. What should the nurse do?
  • ✓Document the herbal product in the preoperative assessment, because of possible anesthesia or surgical complications
  • BAdvise the mother to discontinue all herbal products permanently
  • CNote it in the psychosocial history but exclude it from the preoperative record, since it is not a prescribed medication
  • DReassure the mother that herbal products do not interact with anesthesia
Rationale

The chapter states that "It is important that the use of any herbal or complementary therapy be noted in a preoperative assessment because of possible anesthesia or surgical complications related to herbal products" (p. 664).

Watch for

(C) treats complementary therapy as outside the medical record, which is exactly the omission the instruction exists to prevent; (B) overcorrects into a directive the chapter does not give, since it treats complementary practices as something to assess and record rather than to forbid.

20. A nurse is preparing a room for a 14-year-old being admitted this afternoon. Which consideration does the chapter identify as minimum for room assignment?
  • ✓Age, sex, and nature of the illness, with age grouping especially important for adolescents
  • BAcuity level and projected nursing hours per shift
  • CInsurance status and anticipated length of stay
  • DProximity to the nurses' station, regardless of the patient's age
Rationale

The chapter states that "The minimum considerations for room assignment are age, sex, and nature of the illness," expands this in Box 21.7 to developmental age, seriousness of diagnosis, communicability of illness, and projected length of stay, and notes that age grouping "is especially important for adolescents," who are often placed on their own unit or in a separate section (p. 664).

Watch for

(D) is a real safety consideration in practice and is not what this chapter names; note also the preadmission instruction to "Prepare roommate(s) for the arrival of a new patient," which is the step most often forgotten.

21. A 3-year-old's mother must leave for several hours. Which nursing action best supports the child during her absence?
  • ✓Encourage the mother to leave a favorite blanket or toy, and apply an identification band to it
  • BRemove reminders of home so the child is not repeatedly reminded of the separation
  • CAdvise the mother to slip out while the child is distracted to avoid a scene
  • DKeep the child occupied with new toys purchased from the gift shop
Rationale

The chapter directs parents to "leave favorite articles from home with the child, such as a blanket or a toy," explains the child's reasoning — "if the parents left this, the parents will surely return" — and adds that an identification band on the toy prevents loss and "provides a symbol that the toy is experiencing the same needs as the child" (p. 665).

Watch for

(C) is the deception the chapter names as part of a "destructive cycle of misunderstanding and unmet needs"; (D) reverses the chapter's advice that familiar things beat new ones, since "it is often better to wait" on new toys because small children "need the comfort and reassurance of familiar things."

22. An 11-month-old must have a lengthy physical examination. What is the best means of minimizing the need for restraint?
  • ✓Preserve parent-child contact, performing nearly the entire examination in the parent's lap
  • BUse soft wrist restraints from the outset so the examination can proceed without interruption
  • CPerform the examination while the infant sleeps to avoid resistance entirely
  • DHave two staff members hold the infant so the parent is not distressed by participating
Rationale

The chapter states that "For young children, particularly infants and toddlers, preserving parent-child contact is the best means of decreasing the need for restraint," and that nearly the entire physical examination "can be done in a parent's lap" (p. 666).

(D) removes the parent for the parent's sake, which is not the chapter's approach; for painful procedures specifically, "the nurse should assess the parents' preferences for assisting, observing, or waiting outside the room" rather than deciding for them.

23. A hospitalized 9-year-old complains that he never knows what is going to happen next. Which intervention best addresses this?
  • ✓Develop a written daily schedule with the child and parent, post it in the room, and make a clock available
  • BExplain the unit's standing routine to the child at the start of each shift
  • CReduce the number of scheduled activities so the day is simpler to follow
  • DDraw pictures of daily activities beside clock faces showing when each will occur
Rationale

A written daily schedule is "most suitable for non–critically ill school-age and adolescent children who have mastered the concept of time," is planned jointly by nurse, parent, and child, is posted in the room, and is accompanied by "a clock or watch available for the child's use" (p. 666).

Watch for

(D) is the correct technique for a *younger* child — the chapter's Nursing Alert has the child draw or select pictures with a clock face beside each, so that "When the two match, the child knows it is time for a favorite activity"; the two interventions are matched to different ages and are the likeliest swap here.

24. A hospitalized 13-year-old resists every aspect of her care and complains that nothing is up to her. Which approach best addresses the dominant stressor for her age?
  • ✓Allow her to wear street clothes, make choices about food and bedtime, and participate in planning her care
  • BIncrease parental presence at the bedside, since separation is the major stressor of hospitalization
  • CExplain that hospital routines cannot be individualized and that cooperation will shorten the stay
  • DAssign the same nurse each shift and limit the number of staff entering the room
Rationale

The chapter states that "The dependent role of the hospitalized patient imposes tremendous feelings of loss for older children," and lists "jointly planning care, time structuring, wearing street clothes, making choices in food selections and bedtime, continuing school activities, and rooming with an appropriate-age child" as interventions (p. 666).

Watch for

(B) applies the intervention for the wrong age — separation is the dominant stressor for young children, while loss of control dominates for older ones, and matching the intervention to the age's dominant stressor is the organizing rule of this whole chapter.

25. The parents of a hospitalized 6-year-old ask whether they should stop disciplining him while he is ill. What should the nurse advise?
  • ✓Continue discipline, because it provides security and predictability, and removing all limits makes children fear something catastrophic will happen
  • BSuspend discipline during the admission and resume it once the child is fully recovered
  • CTransfer discipline to the nursing staff so the parents can remain a source of comfort
  • DContinue discipline only for behaviors that pose a safety risk on the unit
Rationale

The chapter states that discipline "should be continued to provide a measure of security and predictability," that when ill children know their parents expect certain standards "they feel certain that they will recover," and that "Conversely, when all limits are removed, they fear that something catastrophic will happen" (p. 672).

Watch for

(B) is the compassionate reflex and the one parents most often take, and it produces the outcome the chapter warns about; it also sets up the posthospital problem, since parents who suspend limits then read the child's reactions as evidence of being spoiled.

26. A nurse uses puppets and replicas of hospital equipment to help a 5-year-old work through his fear of an upcoming procedure. How should this be classified?
  • ✓Therapeutic play, a nondirective modality that nurses use to help children deal with concerns and fears
  • BPlay therapy, a psychologic technique for children with emotional disturbance
  • CDiversional activity, since the purpose is to occupy the child before the procedure
  • DDramatic play therapy, a hybrid requiring certification in child life
Rationale

The chapter defines therapeutic play as "an effective, nondirective modality for helping children deal with their concerns and fears, and at the same time, it often helps the nurse gain insights into children's needs and feelings," and says it "should not be confused with play therapy, a psychologic technique reserved for use by trained and qualified therapists as an interpretative method with emotionally disturbed children" (p. 669).

Watch for

(B) is the confusable pair and the one the chapter names explicitly; the discriminators are who does it and for whom — nurses with any child for therapeutic play, trained therapists with emotionally disturbed children for play therapy.

27. A hospitalized 6-year-old is angry and has been hitting the bed rail. Which activity does the chapter identify as best suited to this?
  • ✓Expressive activity such as a pounding board, clay, or beanbags, which permits the safe release of anger and hostility
  • BDiversional activity such as a movie or a board game, chosen for his age and interests
  • CA quiet activity, to avoid reinforcing the aggressive behavior
  • DDeferral of all play until the anger has resolved
Rationale

The chapter describes expressive activities as "one of the best opportunities for encouraging emotional expression, including the safe release of anger and hostility," naming tension release through large-muscle activity, "pounding and throwing games," beanbags, a pounding board, clay, and play dough (p. 668).

Watch for

(B) is the other real category and it does something different — diversional activities are recreation, selected on the basis of "age, interests, and limitations"; (C) treats the anger as misbehavior to be extinguished rather than an emotion needing an outlet.

28. A nurse offers a hospitalized child a choice of two activities, and the child declines both. What has the nurse accomplished?
  • ✓The nurse has offered the child a choice, perhaps one of very few real choices the child has had that day
  • BNothing, since the activity was refused and no therapeutic goal was met
  • CThe nurse has identified that the child is too ill for developmentally appropriate activity
  • DThe nurse has confirmed that the activities selected were above the child's developmental level
Rationale

The chapter states that "In the hospital environment, most decisions are made for the child; play and other expressive activities offer the child much-needed opportunities to make choices for themselves. Even if a child chooses not to participate in a particular activity, the nurse has offered the child a choice, perhaps one of only a few real choices the child has had that day" (p. 668).

Watch for

(B) measures the intervention by participation, which is precisely what this passage rejects; note also that hospitalized children have lower energy than healthy peers, so the chapter advises selecting activities "simpler than would normally be chosen for the child's specific developmental level."

29. A 4-year-old on a sodium-restricted diet asks to play with modeling dough, and another child on the unit has a wheat allergy. What should the nurse know?
  • ✓Modeling dough is contraindicated for the child on salt restriction because salt is one of its major constituents, and flour-based dough is contraindicated for the child with wheat allergy
  • BModeling dough is safe for both, since it is not ingested
  • COnly the wheat-allergic child is at risk, since dietary sodium restriction applies to food alone
  • DBoth children should be offered modeling dough with supervision to ensure it is not eaten
Rationale

The chapter states that "a child who is allergic to wheat should not be given modeling dough made with flour" and that "A child on a restricted salt intake should not play with modeling dough because salt is one of its major constituents" (p. 669).

Watch for

(C) is the more tempting half-right answer, because a skin-contact salt exposure sounds harmless; the chapter treats both as contraindications, and this is a place where a medical limit constrains play rather than the other way round — although the chapter also notes that "a procedure can be postponed briefly to allow the child to complete a special activity."

30. A 5-year-old will not answer the nurse's questions about how he feels regarding his upcoming surgery. Which approach does the chapter identify as most likely to elicit what he is worried about?
  • ✓Offer a puppet, since children see puppets as peers and will tell them feelings they hesitate to express to adults
  • BAsk the parents to relay the child's concerns, since he is most open with them
  • CProvide a detailed explanation of the surgery, since his silence indicates he does not understand
  • DDefer the conversation until after surgery, when he can describe what he experienced
Rationale

The chapter states that in dramatic play "Most children see [puppets] as peers and readily communicate with them. Children will tell the puppet feelings that they hesitate to express to adults" (p. 669), and adds that a drawing before surgery "may reveal unvoiced concerns about mutilation, body changes, and loss of self-control."

Watch for

(B) routes around the child rather than to him; (C) assumes silence means a knowledge gap when the chapter treats it as a disclosure problem, and the same section notes that imagined reasons for illness, "particularly of a sexual nature," are exactly what children will not say aloud but will reveal through drawing or doll play.

31. The mother of a hospitalized infant admits to the nurse that she feels angry and guilty and is ashamed of both feelings. What is the nurse's best response?
  • ✓Emphasize that each of those feelings is a normal, expected, and healthy response to stress
  • BReassure her that most parents do not experience such feelings and that they will pass quickly
  • CRefer her to the hospital chaplain, since guilt is a spiritual rather than a nursing concern
  • DRedirect the conversation to the infant's progress to relieve her distress
Rationale

The chapter notes that parents "resist admitting to such feelings because they expect others to disapprove of behavior that is less than perfect," and directs the nurse to emphasize "that each is a normal, expected, and healthy response to stress" (p. 672).

Watch for

(B) normalizes by denying how common the feelings are, which contradicts the chapter and leaves the shame intact; (C) hands off a conversation the chapter assigns to the nurse, though it notes that parents with deep religious beliefs may appreciate clergy and "may not have sufficient energy to initiate the contact" themselves.

32. The 7-year-old sibling of a hospitalized child tells his parents he does not want to hear anything about his brother's illness. How should the nurse advise the parents?
  • ✓Respect the sibling who avoids information as a means of coping
  • BProvide the full explanation anyway, since avoidance is denial that must be worked through
  • CProvide information only to the older siblings, who can process it
  • DWait until the hospitalized child is discharged before explaining anything
Rationale

The chapter directs the nurse to offer information to young as well as older siblings but to "respect the sibling who avoids information as a means of coping" (p. 672).

(B) reframes a coping style as pathology; (C) and (D) both withhold from a group the chapter says must not be skipped, since "Children in every age group deserve an explanation of the sibling's illness or hospitalization" — the aim being to minimize a sibling's fear of getting sick too or of having caused the illness.

33. A father tells the nurse that his wife is exhausted after five days of staying continuously at their daughter's bedside but feels she cannot leave. What should the nurse do?
  • ✓Encourage brief absences, arrange sleeping quarters on the unit outside the child's room, and plan alternating visits with another family member
  • BReassure them that continuous parental presence is required for family-centered care
  • CSuggest the mother assume more of the direct care, since involvement reduces parental anxiety
  • DAsk the mother to go home for the remainder of the admission so she can recover
Rationale

The chapter names "neglect of the parent's need for sleep, nutrition, and relaxation" as a potential problem of continuous involvement and directs the nurse to encourage brief absences, arrange sleeping quarters, and plan alternating visits (p. 672); it adds that "24-hour responsibility may be too much for some parents."

Watch for

(C) inverts the chapter's finding, since parents "more involved in direct care may feel more anxiety than those less involved"; (B) turns an offer into a requirement, when the chapter's point is that "Not all parents feel equally comfortable assuming responsibility for their child's care."

34. The parents of a 3-year-old being discharged tomorrow ask what to expect at home. What should the nurse prepare them for?
  • ✓A period of initial aloofness toward them, usually lasting minutes, followed by clinging, demands for attention, and possible regression in newly learned skills
  • BImmediate return to baseline behavior, since the admission was brief
  • CEmotional coldness followed by intense demanding dependence and anger directed at them
  • DGradual improvement in behavior over several weeks with no intervening regression
Rationale

Box 21.2 describes young children's posthospital behaviors as "initial aloofness toward parents; this may last from a few minutes (most common) to a few days," frequently followed by dependency behaviors including clinging and demands for attention, plus "Regression in newly learned skills (e.g., self-toileting)" (p. 661).

Watch for

(C) is the *older* child's pattern — "Emotional coldness followed by intense, demanding dependence on parents; Anger toward parents; Jealousy toward others"; preparing parents matters because parents who do not expect these reactions "may misinterpret them as evidence of the child's being spoiled and demand perfect behavior at a time when the child is still reacting to the stress."

37. A nurse is teaching a mother to perform her son's dressing change before discharge. Which approach reflects the chapter's teaching plan?
  • ✓Divide the skill into discrete steps, teach each step until it is learned, and request return demonstration before introducing new skills
  • BDemonstrate the complete procedure twice, then have the mother perform the entire sequence at the end of the teaching

Rationale

The discharge teaching plan "incorporates levels of learning, such as observing, participating with assistance, and finally acting without help or guidance. The skill is divided into discrete steps, and each step is taught to the family member until it is learned. Return demonstration of the skill is requested before new skills are introduced" (p. 672).

Watch for

(B) defers verification to the end, when the chapter checks each skill before moving on; (C) sends the family home to practice unsupervised, whereas the chapter's provision is a supervised "transition or trial period," especially valuable "when the family lives far from the hospital."

The ambulatory discharge instructions direct the nurse to "Request that all prescriptions be filled and given to the family before discharge," and the home-care section states that "All families should receive detailed written instructions about home care, with telephone numbers for assistance, before they leave the hospital" (pp. 672, 674).

(B) is keyed in the chapter's own review item as a statement indicating a need for more teaching, and it is the most reasonable-sounding wrong answer in the section — going to a familiar pharmacy for counseling is sensible, and tomorrow is too late.

41. A 7-year-old arrives in the emergency department with a forearm fracture. The department is busy and the nurse considers proceeding quickly without explanation. What does the chapter advise?
  • ✓Unless the emergency is life threatening, allow the child to participate, because the extra few minutes may save many more minutes of resistance during later procedures
  • BProceed without explanation, since speed is the priority in the emergency department
  • CExplain everything only to the parents, since the child is too distressed to absorb information
  • DPostpone all explanation until the child is admitted to the unit
Rationale

The chapter states that "Unless an emergency is life threatening, children need to participate in their care to maintain a sense of control," and that "the extra few minutes needed to allow children to participate may save many more minutes of useless resistance and uncooperativeness during subsequent procedures" (p. 674).

Watch for

(B) is the time pressure the chapter names and then argues against on its own terms — participation is presented as the faster route overall, not merely the kinder one; other measures named are ensuring privacy, preserving parent-child contact, and personally remaining calm.

42. A child underwent an emergency procedure with no opportunity for preparation. What is the most useful approach afterward?
  • ✓Listen to the explanations the child offers, using drawing, doll play, or storytelling, and then build on what the child already understands
  • BProvide the complete procedural explanation that could not be given beforehand
  • CAvoid revisiting the event, since discussing it may retraumatize the child
  • DAsk the parents to explain the procedure once the child is calm
Rationale

The chapter states that counseling after the fact has therapeutic value and that the direction reverses: "instead of supplying information, the nurse listens to the explanations offered by the child," using "drawing, doll play, or storytelling," and then bases additional information on what the child already understands (p. 674).

Watch for

(B) is the intuitive catch-up and it is backward for this situation; the reason matters, since children are "vulnerable to misinterpreting causes for stress" and the misconception has to be found before it can be corrected.

(B) substitutes a generic list for the individualized assessment the chapter specifically recommends over it; (C) reduces the parental presence that the same section directs the nurse to encourage.
