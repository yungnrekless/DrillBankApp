1. A school nurse finds a 10-year-old unresponsive at the base of a climbing structure after a fall. How should the airway be opened?
  • ✓With a modified jaw thrust, without head tilt or chin lift
  • BWith a head tilt–chin lift, the standard maneuver for any child
  • CWith a head tilt–chin lift, followed by a jaw thrust if it fails
  • DNot at all until emergency medical services arrive
Rationale

"When spinal trauma is a possibility, open the airway using the modified jaw thrust maneuver, which is accomplished by grasping the angles of the victim's lower jaw and lifting with both hands, one on each side, and displacing the mandible upward and outward (without head tilt or chin lift)" (p. 1241). The box restates it: in a child "with head, trunk, or multisystem trauma, modified jaw thrust is preferred method."

Watch for

(B) is the correct maneuver only once trauma has been excluded, and it cannot be excluded here: "Spinal cord injury is always suspected in a patient with head, trunk, or multisystem trauma," and only in a fully equipped trauma center "can spinal cord injury be ruled out or diagnosed. Therefore the patient is treated as if injury were present" (p. 1241). (D) leaves the airway closed while waiting.

2. A bystander finds an unresponsive, pulseless 6-year-old after an unwitnessed collapse. What is the correct sequence?
  • ✓Perform 2 minutes of CPR, then activate emergency medical services and call for a defibrillator
  • BActivate emergency medical services first, then begin CPR
  • CActivate emergency medical services and wait for the defibrillator before starting compressions
  • DGive two rescue breaths, then activate emergency medical services
Rationale

"Activate the EMS system if child appears to be pubertal or older or if witnessed sudden collapse. If child has not reached puberty, perform 2 minutes of cardiopulmonary resuscitation (CPR) (as per assessment); then activate EMS and call for an automatic external defibrillator (AED)" (p. 1241). Compressions run "at a rate of at least 100 compressions per minute," and interruptions are kept under 10 seconds.

Watch for

(B) is the correct sequence for a pubertal child or a witnessed sudden collapse, and this child is neither, which is the entire point of the age split. (C) delays the only intervention maintaining perfusion.

3. A teenager has a deep thigh laceration with arterial bleeding and a piece of metal protruding from the wound. Direct pressure, elevation, and arterial pressure-point compression have failed, and a tourniquet is applied. Which statements are correct? Select all that apply.
Select all that apply · scored all or nothing
  • ✓The tourniquet is a last resort, used only after the other measures fail
  • ✓Once applied, the tourniquet is not loosened
  • ✓The protruding object is left in place
  • ✓Keeping the tourniquet in place gives a better chance of survival, even if it costs the limb
  • EThe tourniquet should be briefly released every 15 minutes to perfuse the limb
Rationale

The hemorrhage sequence is direct pressure, elevation, arterial pressure point, then "Apply tourniquet only as a last resort. Once a tourniquet is applied, it should not be loosened" (p. 1241), and "Do not remove objects protruding from child's body." The reasoning is systemic rather than local: "If the tourniquet is loosened or removed, the toxins can be released into the circulation in high concentrations and may induce a systemic, deadly tourniquet shock. With the tourniquet in place, the patient has a better chance of survival, even though it may mean the loss of a limb" (p. 1242).

Watch for

(E) is the wrong option and it is the intuitive one, since periodic release sounds like limb-sparing care. It is the exact action the chapter forbids, and the harm it names is to the whole patient, not to the leg.

4. A 9-year-old is immobilized for several weeks after significant trauma. Which effects should the nurse anticipate? Select all that apply.
Select all that apply · scored all or nothing
  • ✓Orthostatic intolerance
  • ✓Deep vein thrombosis
  • ✓Pneumonia from pooled secretions
  • ✓Renal calculi
  • EDiarrhea
  • ✓Constipation and fecal impaction
Rationale

"Immobility has three major cardiovascular consequences: orthostatic intolerance, increased workload of the heart, and thrombus formation" (p. 1243). Respirations become "slower and more shallow" and "A weak and ineffectual cough reflex contributes to stasis of secretions," with atelectasis and pneumonia as the complications (p. 1244). "The urinary system is designed to function in an upright posture," and settled particulate matter "may serve as nuclei for calculi formation or as foci for infection." "Slowing of stool in the colon causes the feces to become hard (fecal impaction)."

Watch for

(E) is the wrong option and it inverts the gastrointestinal effect, since immobility slows the gut rather than speeding it. This is the chapter's own NCLEX question 1, where diarrhea and a sense of euphoria are the two excluded options — the documented psychologic response is anxiety, depression, and regression, not elation.

5. A child has been on bed rest for 5 days after a spinal injury, with no range-of-motion exercises. Which statement is accurate?
  • ✓Inactive muscle loses strength at about 3% per day, and contractures develop unless range of motion is reestablished within 3 to 7 days
  • BMuscle strength is preserved for the first 2 weeks of bed rest
  • CContractures take at least 6 weeks of immobility to develop
  • DContractures affect the extensor muscles first, because they are the stronger group
Rationale

"Inactive muscle loses strength at the rate of 3% per day," and "unless range of motion is reestablished within 3 to 7 days, contractures will develop." The frequent sites are "hip flexion, knee flexion, plantar flexion of the feet, and shoulder stiffness" (p. 1243).

Watch for

(D) reverses the muscle balance that produces the deformity, since "body flexor muscles are stronger than extensor muscles" (p. 1243) — which is why every site the chapter lists is a flexion contracture. (B) and (C) both grant the child time the physiology does not give.

6. A nurse turns an immobilized child and finds a red area over the sacrum that is still red 40 minutes later. What does this indicate?
  • ✓A pressure area is developing, and treatment should begin now
  • BNormal reactive hyperemia, which requires no action
  • CAn early sign of deep vein thrombosis
  • DA contact reaction to the bed linen, requiring only a linen change
Rationale

Normal reactive hyperemia "should disappear within 5 to 15 minutes," and "Prolonged redness (>30 minutes) indicates that a pressure area is developing and treatment should begin" (p. 1245). The visible change understates the damage: "The skin changes observed may represent the top of a cone-shaped area with widespread tissue destruction, beneath which tissue rapidly ulcerates and creates a large pressure ulcer that sometimes extends to the bone."

Watch for

(B) is correct for redness that fades within 15 minutes, and the 30-minute mark is the entire discriminator. The other areas to check by name are the occiput, trochanter, and heel.

7. A hospitalized school-age child in traction has become tearful, has begun bed-wetting again, and wants to be fed. What is the most useful nursing response?
  • ✓Accept the regression as expected, and involve the child in planning the daily routine and choosing clothes
  • BTell the child that behaving like a baby will not help him get better
  • CRestrict visitors so the child can rest and settle
  • DTake over all of the child's care to reduce his frustration
Rationale

"Children seek attention by reverting to earlier developmental behaviors, such as wanting to be fed, bed-wetting, and baby talk" (p. 1246), and the chapter names the intervention: "One of the most useful interventions to help children cope with immobility is participation in their own care. Self-care to the maximum extent possible is usually well received by children. They can help plan their daily routine, select their diet (when possible), and choose the clothes they are to wear" (p. 1247).

Watch for

(D) is kindly meant and removes the one thing that helps, because immobilization has already taken the child's normal outlet: "Children respond to anxiety with increased activity. Removal of this power deprives them of necessary input and a natural outlet for their feelings and fantasies" (p. 1246). (B) shames a normal response and adds to the guilt the chapter says to correct with "It just happened—it was an accident."

8. A 12-year-old whose long-leg fiberglass cast was applied this morning reports pain rated 10 out of 10 at 2 AM. The pillows have been pushed off the bed, the toes are edematous, and a finger will not slip under the cast edge. What is the priority action?
  • ✓Notify the surgeon of the findings immediately
  • BGive the ordered opioid, elevate the limb on pillows, and reassess in an hour
  • CReassess in 2 to 3 hours to see whether the pain settles
  • DWithhold analgesia so the pain can be evaluated accurately
Rationale

This is compartment syndrome: pain out of proportion, edema, and a cast acting as a tourniquet. "Permanent muscle and tissue damage can occur within a few hours" (p. 1252), and the Nursing Alert directs the nurse to "Immediately report any observations that include the five Ps of ischemia: pain, especially with passive range of motion; pallor; pulselessness (an ominous and late sign); paresthesia; and paralysis."

Watch for

(B) contains two real interventions and still fails, because it does not report and because "Elevation of the affected extremity is not recommended and may further decrease blood flow to the area" once compartment syndrome is suspected (p. 1265). Elevation is right for a freshly casted limb and wrong here, which is the pairing this item is built on. (C) spends time the tissue does not have.

9. A child with a forearm fracture reports burning pain and numbness in the fingers. The radial pulse is palpable and capillary refill is brisk. What should the nurse conclude?
  • ✓Compartment syndrome is still possible, and sensory deficit is the most reliable finding
  • BCirculation is intact, so compartment syndrome is ruled out
  • CNumbness is expected after any fracture and needs no action
  • DThe finding is reassuring, because pulselessness is an early sign of compartment syndrome
Rationale

"A palpable peripheral pulse and brisk capillary refill may be present despite increasing compartmental pressure," and "Sensory deficit in the affected limb is reported to be the most reliable physical finding of compartment syndrome" (p. 1265). Manifestations "may occur as early as 30 minutes after the ischemia develops."

Watch for

(D) inverts the timing and is the most dangerous option, since pulselessness is named as "an ominous and late sign" (p. 1252). (B) draws a reassuring conclusion from the one finding that cannot support it. The clock is short: ischemia lasting as little as 6 hours can cause contracture, and "A great deal of muscle damage occurs after 12 to 24 hours."

10. A toddler is in a plaster hip spica cast. Which instructions should the nurse give? Select all that apply.
Select all that apply · scored all or nothing
  • ✓Leave the cast uncovered so it dries from the inside out
  • ✓Handle a wet cast with the palms of the hands
  • ✓Turn the child at least every 2 hours so the cast dries evenly
  • DUse a heated fan or hair dryer to speed the drying
  • ✓Report the cast if it becomes too loose
Rationale

"The cast must remain uncovered to allow it to dry from the inside out. A wet plaster cast should be supported by a pillow covered with plastic and handled by the palms of the hands to avoid indenting the cast and creating pressure areas. Turning the child in a plaster cast at least every 2 hours will help dry a body cast evenly" (p. 1252). Parents are also told to seek care "if the cast becomes too loose because a loose cast no longer serves its purpose."

Watch for

(D) is the wrong option and it fails twice over: "Heated fans or dryers are not used because they cause the cast to dry on the outside and remain wet beneath. They may also cause burns from heat conduction by the cast to the underlying tissue" (p. 1252).

11. A cast is being applied to a 5-year-old's forearm. Which statement about the procedure is accurate?
  • ✓Both plaster and synthetic materials generate heat as they set, and warmer water increases the burn risk
  • BOnly plaster generates heat; synthetic material stays cool
  • CWarm water should be used, because it makes application more comfortable
  • DThe cast saw used at removal will cut skin on contact, so the child should be warned
Rationale

"Both types of casting material produce heat from a chemical reaction activated by water immediately after application" (p. 1250), and Table 33.2 states that "the more rapid the drying time, the greater the likelihood of burning; therefore it is recommended to use tepid or cool water to slow drying time and decrease chemical heat produced; the warmer the water, the faster the material dries or hardens" (p. 1251).

Watch for

(D) is the reverse of what a preschooler should be told, and the explanation that defuses the fear is specific: because a cast saw "works by vibration," it "cuts only the hard surface of the cast" and "will not cut when placed lightly on the skin" (p. 1252). Children describe the feeling as tickly. This matters at this age because toddlers and preschoolers "do not have easily defined body boundaries."

12. A child in skeletal traction needs to be repositioned higher in the bed. What should the nurse do?
  • ✓Reposition the child without lifting or releasing the traction weights
  • BLift the weights briefly while a second nurse moves the child
  • CRemove the weights, reposition, and then reapply them
  • DLeave the child as is, since a child in skeletal traction is never repositioned
Rationale

Nursing Alert: "Skeletal traction is never released by the nurse (except under direct supervision by the provider). This precaution includes not lifting the weights that are applying traction (e.g., for moving the child in bed or repositioning)" (p. 1255). The reason is in the physics: "The continuous maintenance of traction is important during this phase because releasing the traction allows the muscle to contract normally and again cause malpositioning of the bone ends" (p. 1254).

Watch for

(B) and (C) both look like reasonable ways to make a routine nursing task easier, and both undo the muscle fatigue traction exists to produce. (D) overcorrects into immobility, which brings its own set of complications.

13. A child is in Russell traction with a padded sling under the knee. Which nursing observation is most specific to this device?
  • ✓Confirming that hip flexion remains at the prescribed angle, and watching for footdrop
  • BConfirming that the legs remain fully extended
  • CConfirming that the halo pin sites are clean and dry
  • DConfirming that the weights rest on the floor between treatments
Rationale

Russell traction "uses skin traction on the lower leg and a padded sling under the knee," and "Hip flexion must remain at the prescribed angle to prevent fracture malalignment." The nurse also watches that "damage to the peroneal nerve under the knee does not cause footdrop" (p. 1254).

Watch for

(B) describes Buck extension, "a type of skin traction applied with the legs in an extended position" (p. 1254) — the two skin tractions differ precisely in whether the hip and knee are flexed. (D) would release the traction altogether; countertraction is adjusted by elevating the foot of the bed, not by resting the weights.

14. A child arrives in the emergency department after a traumatic finger amputation, and the severed part has been brought in. How should it be handled?
  • ✓Rinse gently with normal saline, wrap loosely in sterile gauze, place in a watertight bag, and chill the bag in ice water without packing it in ice
  • BPlace the part directly on ice to keep it as cold as possible
  • CImmerse the part in normal saline in an open container at room temperature
  • DScrub the part clean and wrap it tightly in dry gauze
Rationale

The Nursing Alert gives the sequence: "1. Rinse limb gently with normal saline. 2. Loosely wrap limb in sterile gauze. 3. Place wrapped limb in a watertight bag. 4. Chill bag, without freezing, in ice water. Do not pack in ice because this may harm tissue. 5. Label with child's name, date, and time, and transport with the child to the hospital" (p. 1256).

Watch for

(B) is the instinctive version and the alert excludes it by name, because direct contact with ice damages the very tissue the surgeon needs. (D) reverses both handling instructions at once, since rinsing is gentle and wrapping is loose.

15. A 3-year-old fell from a slide and is walking with a limp, refusing to bear full weight on one leg. A parent says nothing can be broken because she is walking. How should the nurse respond?
  • ✓Walking does not rule out a fracture, and a small child who refuses to walk or crawl should be strongly suspected of having one
  • BWalking on a leg does rule out a fracture in a child this age
  • CRadiographs in young children are highly reliable, so a negative film will settle it
  • DPain would be severe and constant if a fracture were present
Rationale

Nursing Alert: "A fracture should be strongly suspected in a small child who refuses to walk or crawl. However, the fact that a child walks on a suspected fractured extremity does not rule out a fracture" (p. 1264). The anatomy explains it: "the fracture is remarkably stable because of the usually intact periosteum. The child may even be able to use an affected arm or walk on a fractured leg" (p. 1263).

Watch for

(C) overstates the imaging, since much of a young child's skeleton "is composed of radiolucent growth cartilage that does not appear on radiographs," and films "are sometimes less reliable than gross deformity and point tenderness in predicting extremity fractures" (p. 1263).

16. A 2-year-old and a 15-year-old each sustain a femoral shaft fracture. What should the nurse expect?
  • ✓The toddler will heal considerably faster, because healing time is inversely related to age
  • BBoth will heal in about the same time, since bone healing is age independent
  • CThe adolescent will heal faster, because of greater bone mass
  • DNeither will heal without internal fixation, since nonunion is common in children
Rationale

"Healing is more rapid in children, and the rapidity is inversely related to the child's age. The younger the child, the more rapid the healing process. Nonunion of bone fragments is uncommon" (p. 1260). Femoral shaft healing times are given by age as 2 to 3 weeks in the neonatal period, 4 weeks in early childhood, 6 to 8 weeks in later childhood, and 8 to 12 weeks in adolescence (p. 1263).

Watch for

(D) inverts the pediatric advantage, since nonunion is specifically called uncommon. Remodeling runs the same direction: "The younger the child and the closer the proximity of the fracture to the growth plate, the greater the degree of remodeling that is able to take place" (p. 1263).

17. An adolescent's ankle is forcibly inverted during a game. Which statement explains why the injury pattern in a growing child differs from an adult's?
  • ✓Ligaments are stronger than bone in a growing child, and the growth plate is the weakest part, so injury occurs at the physis
  • BLigaments are weaker than bone at every age, so a ligament tear is the expected injury
  • CThe growth plate is the strongest part of a child's bone and protects the joint from injury
  • DChildren's bones are more brittle than adults', so a complete fracture is expected
Rationale

"Ligaments are stronger than bone, and the physis (growth plate) is the weakest part of the bone. The more typical site of injury is at the growth plate" (p. 1269). Before physeal closure, joint injuries are "more likely to cause separation of the epiphysis from the metaphysis (growth plate injury) than dislocation" (p. 1268).

Watch for

(D) reverses the mechanical property that defines pediatric fractures: "The pliable bones of the growing child are more porous than those of the adult, which allows them to bend, buckle, and break in a 'greenstick' manner" (p. 1260). (C) contradicts the sentence that opens the Salter-Harris classification, which names the physis as the weakest point of long bones and therefore "a frequent site of injury during childhood trauma."

18. An infant with osteogenesis imperfecta is admitted. Which nursing actions are appropriate? Select all that apply.
Select all that apply · scored all or nothing
  • ✓Lift gently by the buttocks or support with pillows for diaper changes, never by the ankles
  • ✓Obtain blood pressures with a manual cuff
  • ✓Handle and hold the infant with compassion rather than avoiding contact
  • ✓Screen a child with current or healing fractures for osteogenesis imperfecta before concluding abuse
  • EAnticipate intellectual disability along with the skeletal findings
Rationale

"Even changing a diaper may cause a fracture in severely affected infants. These children should never be held by the ankles when being diapered but should be gently lifted by the buttocks or supported with pillows." "It is recommended that blood pressure be obtained with a manual cuff to prevent fractures." And the counterweight is stated explicitly: "nurses should not be afraid to touch or handle the infant or child with OI. Such children need compassionate handling and care as much as any other patient" (p. 1287). On the differential: "Children with current fractures or healing fractures should be screened for OI; the assumption that abuse or neglect is the cause of fractures in children must be carefully evaluated by a multidisciplinary team."

Watch for

(E) is the wrong option and the chapter says the opposite plainly: these children, "although smaller in stature than same-age children, have normal intelligence and should receive adequate information and instructions regarding their care" (p. 1287).

19. An adolescent felt a pop in the knee, had immediate pain that quickly subsided, has little swelling, and says the joint feels loose. How should the nurse interpret this?
  • ✓A severe (grade III) sprain, because laxity rather than pain indicates severity
  • BA mild (grade I) sprain, because pain and swelling are minimal
  • CA muscle strain, because laxity is a common strain finding
  • DA contusion, because the pain settled quickly
Rationale

A grade III sprain involves "total loss of ligamentous continuity … Pain is immediate but subsides because none of the pain fibers is being stretched. Swelling may be minimal because hemorrhage extravasates outside of the area into soft tissues" (p. 1269). "The presence of joint laxity is the most valid indicator of the severity of a sprain," and the athlete "complains that the joint 'feels loose' or as if 'something is coming apart' … Pain is seldom the principal subjective symptom."

Watch for

(B) is the intuitive reading and it is exactly backward, since less pain and less swelling is worse here rather than better. (C) misapplies laxity, because strains are graded the same way "except that the degree of laxity does not apply," and complaints of laxity are rare even in a severe strain.

20. A child sustains an ankle injury during a soccer game. Which immediate measures are correct? Select all that apply.
Select all that apply · scored all or nothing
  • ✓Apply ice with a layer of cloth between the pack and the skin
  • ✓Compress and elevate the ankle several inches above the level of the heart
  • ✓Avoid any liniment or heat-producing preparation before the ankle is examined
  • ✓Keep each ice application intermittent, never exceeding 30 minutes at a time
  • EApply a heating pad first, to relax the muscle before evaluation
Rationale

"Soft tissue injuries should be iced immediately," with "A single layer of the wrap or cloth … placed over the injured area to protect the skin under the ice pack." "Ice therapy should be intermittent, and ice should never be applied for more than 30 minutes at a time to prevent tissue damage." "The point of injury must be kept several inches above the level of the heart for therapy to be effective." And parents are cautioned "against using any form of liniment or other heat-producing preparation before examination" (p. 1269).

Watch for

(E) is the wrong option, and the reason is concrete: "If the injury requires casting or splinting, the heat generated in the enclosed space can produce extreme discomfort and may even cause tissue damage" (p. 1269). Note that the chapter carries two ice limits — 20 minutes in the fracture Emergency Treatment box (p. 1264) and 30 minutes here — and the emergency figure is the more conservative of the two.

21. A 14-year-old cross-country runner is diagnosed with a tibial stress fracture and told to rest. What does that mean here?
  • ✓Reduced activity with alternative exercise such as pool running, cycling, or swimming, not bed rest or casting
  • BComplete bed rest until the pain resolves
  • CImmobilization in a long-leg cast for 6 weeks
  • DContinuing the same training, since stress fractures heal with use
Rationale

"Rest is the primary therapy, usually interpreted as reduced activity and the use of alternative exercise—not bed rest or immobilization with casting." "It is important to keep the child or adolescent mobile, and training can be continued," with pool running named as the worked example because it "uses the same movements as running but without the weight bearing" (p. 1270).

Watch for

(D) is the version the athlete will prefer, and the chapter forbids it by name: athletes "should seek assistance when an injury is suspected and not 'work through' injuries caused by overuse—for example, shin splints, stress fractures, tendinitis, and apophysitis" (p. 1267). Note too that early films are often unhelpful, since "callus formation is not yet evident."

22. A 13-year-old collapses during football practice. The skin is hot and dry, the core temperature is 105°F, and the athlete is confused. What is the correct management?
  • ✓Move to a cool environment, remove clothing, apply cool water and fans, cool to 102°F, and transport emergently without giving an antipyretic
  • BMove to a cool environment, provide rest and oral fluids; external cooling is not necessary
  • CGive acetaminophen to bring the temperature down, then reassess
  • DGive salt tablets with oral fluids, since this is sodium depletion
Rationale

Heatstroke "represents a failure of normal thermoregulatory mechanisms," with rapid onset, a temperature "typically higher than 104°F (40°C)," disorientation, and sweating that "may not be present." Treatment is "relocation to a cool environment, removal of clothing, application of cool water (wet towels or immersion), and use of fans," and "The child should be emergently transported to the nearest hospital." Cooling continues "until core temperature reaches 102°F (38.9°C) to prevent overcooling. Antipyretics are not used because they are metabolized by the liver, which is already not functioning properly" (p. 1271).

Watch for

(B) is the treatment for heat exhaustion, where the sensorium is clear and "External cooling methods are not necessary" (p. 1271); the altered mentation and the temperature are what move this case into a different syndrome. (C) reaches for the one drug class the chapter excludes, and (D) treats heat cramps, whose mechanism is sodium depletion with normal mentation and usually normal vital signs.

24. After a 14-year-old athlete's sudden death, a school board asks the nurse whether an automatic external defibrillator would help. Which statement is accurate?
  • ✓An automatic external defibrillator can be effective in resuscitating a child or adolescent with a shockable rhythm
  • BAutomatic external defibrillators should be used only by trained health care personnel
  • CAutomatic external defibrillators are not designed for use in children
  • DA school program would benefit adult staff but not student athletes
Rationale

"Appropriate use of an automatic external defibrillator (AED) by civilian bystanders or health care workers may save the life of an athlete," and a school-based program "provides a high survival rate for student athletes and nonathletes" (p. 1274). The trauma box directs bystanders to turn on the defibrillator and follow its directions for children (p. 1241). The most frequent medical cause of sudden death during sport is hypertrophic cardiomyopathy.

(B) and (C) are the two beliefs that keep a defibrillator locked in an office, and the source defeats both in a single sentence by naming civilian bystanders and by giving the survival figure for student athletes specifically. This is the chapter's own NCLEX question 4.

25. A 12-year-old girl who is still growing has a 30-degree idiopathic thoracic curve. What management should the nurse expect?
  • ✓Bracing, with the goal of slowing progression rather than curing the curve
  • BObservation only, since curves under 45 degrees never progress
  • CSpinal fusion, since any curve over 25 degrees is surgical
  • DExercise and chiropractic treatment in place of a brace
Rationale

"For moderate curves (25 to 45 degrees) in the growing child and adolescent, bracing may be the treatment of choice" (p. 1276), while "Surgical intervention may be required for correction of severe curves or those measuring 45 degrees or more" (p. 1277). The honest framing belongs in the teaching: "Historically bracing has not been shown to be curative; the goal is to slow the progression of the curvature to allow skeletal growth and maturity."

Watch for

(D) offers the substitutions families ask about, and the evidence does not support them: evidence for exercise and chiropractic treatment is "very limited," transcutaneous electrical nerve stimulation "has proved to be an ineffective treatment," and "Exercises are of benefit when used in conjunction with bracing" rather than instead of it (p. 1277). (B) collapses three separate degree bands into one.

26. During scoliosis screening a 12-year-old is found to have a left thoracic curve, back pain, and a hairy patch over the sacrum. What do these findings suggest?
  • ✓A possible intraspinal abnormality, for which an MRI is usually obtained
