Select all that apply · scored all or nothing
  • ✓Bradycardia
  • ✓Extension or flexion posturing
  • ✓Cheyne-Stokes respirations
  • ✓Alterations in pupil size and reactivity
  • EForceful vomiting
Rationale

Box 30.1 places bradycardia, decreased motor response to command, decreased sensory response to painful stimuli, alterations in pupil size and reactivity, extension or flexion posturing, Cheyne-Stokes respirations, papilledema, decreased consciousness, and coma in the late column (p. 1111).

Watch for

(E) is an early sign in children, along with headache, nausea, diplopia, seizures, drowsiness, and declining school performance; the whole structure of this box is an early-versus-late sort, and a four-option item usually offers three from one column and one from the other.

2. A child who had a shunt revision this morning has nausea, a headache, irritability, and a heart rate that has fallen from 96 to 62 over the last hour. Which finding is the priority?
  • ✓The decrease in heart rate
  • BThe nausea and refusal to eat
  • CThe headache
  • DThe irritability and sleepiness
Rationale

Bradycardia appears on the late-signs list of Box 30.1, so it signals decompensation rather than early pressure (p. 1111); the other three are real signs of shunt obstruction but sit on the early list (p. 1163).

Watch for

(B), (C), and (D) are all genuinely abnormal and all genuinely common, which is what makes them attractive — a priority question asks for the most ominous finding rather than the most frequent one, and this is how the chapter's own review question is constructed.

3. A nurse is assessing a child with a head injury and wants the earliest indicator of neurologic change. What should the nurse monitor?
  • ✓Level of consciousness, since alterations in consciousness appear earlier than vital sign or focal changes
  • BBlood pressure, since a widening pulse pressure is the first warning in children
  • CPupillary response, since papilledema appears within the first hours
  • DDeep tendon reflexes, which change before consciousness does
Rationale

The chapter states that "Assessment of level of consciousness (LOC) remains the earliest indicator of improvement or deterioration in neurologic status," and that "alterations in consciousness appear earlier than alterations of vital signs or focal neurologic signs" (pp. 1112, 1130).

Watch for

(B) applies the adult Cushing picture, which the chapter says "rarely occur[s] in children or are very late signs"; (C) misplaces papilledema, which "takes 24 to 48 hours to develop" and so "will not be evident early in the course of unconsciousness."

4. A nurse is positioning a child with increased intracranial pressure. Which measures are correct? Select all that apply.
Select all that apply · scored all or nothing
  • ✓Elevate the head of the bed 15 to 30 degrees
  • ✓Maintain the head in midline to facilitate venous drainage
  • ✓Use vibration rather than percussion if airway clearance is needed
  • ✓Hyperventilate with 100% oxygen before suctioning if it is unavoidable
  • ETurn the child side to side every 2 hours
Rationale

The Nursing Alert directs elevating the head 15 to 30 degrees with the head midline "to facilitate venous drainage and avoid jugular compression"; the chapter adds that vibration "does not increase ICP" and "should be tried first," and that unavoidable suctioning "should be used judiciously and preceded by hyperventilation with 100% oxygen" (pp. 1120–1121).

Watch for

(E) is contraindicated in this specific situation — "Turning side to side is contraindicated because of the risk of jugular compression" — even though 2-hourly repositioning is correct for skin and lungs in nearly every other context, which is what makes it the designed error.

5. A child with increased intracranial pressure from head trauma is prescribed a corticosteroid. What should the nurse recognize?
  • ✓Corticosteroids are not recommended for cerebral edema secondary to head trauma
  • BCorticosteroids are first-line for all causes of cerebral edema
  • CCorticosteroids are contraindicated in cerebral edema of any cause
  • DCorticosteroids should be given only after mannitol has failed
Rationale

The chapter states that "Administration of adrenocorticosteroids is not recommended for cerebral edema secondary to head trauma," while they remain useful for edema from inflammatory causes (p. 1120).

(B) generalizes a true statement about inflammatory edema to a cause where the evidence does not support it, and (C) overcorrects in the other direction — the distinction is the cause of the edema, not the drug.

6. A child has a Glasgow Coma Scale score of 7. What does this indicate, and what does it trigger?
  • ✓Coma, which triggers intracranial pressure monitoring
  • BMild impairment requiring hourly reassessment only
  • CNormal function, since 7 is within the normal range
  • DBrain death, since a score below 8 is incompatible with recovery
Rationale

The chapter gives 15 as normal, 8 or below as defining coma and triggering intracranial pressure monitoring, 5 or below as predicting poor outcome, and 3 as deep coma or death (p. 1113).

Watch for

(D) confuses a coma threshold with a death threshold, which are five points apart on this scale; note that for subdural hematoma a different number governs — a score of 12 or less, or a drop of 2 or more points, means calling the neurosurgeon.

consciousness assessment and the unconscious childgeneral
7. An unconscious child is admitted after a fall. What is the first priority of care?
  • ✓Establishing an adequate airway, within a circulation-airway-breathing sequence
  • BObtaining a computed tomography scan before any intervention
  • CAdministering mannitol to reduce pressure
  • DInserting a nasogastric tube to prevent vomiting
Rationale

The chapter directs emergency measures "toward ensuring circulation, airway, and breathing (CAB)" and states that "Respiratory effectiveness is the primary concern in the care of the unconscious child, and establishment of an adequate airway is always the first priority" (p. 1119).

Watch for

(B) delays airway management for imaging, and the reason it matters is stated plainly — "Cerebral hypoxia at normal body temperature that lasts longer than 4 minutes often causes irreversible brain damage." Note also that in a head injury the airway is opened with a jaw thrust rather than a chin lift.

8. An unconscious child has a urine output of 15 ml/hr, a urine specific gravity of 1.030, and a serum sodium of 124 mEq/L. What does this suggest?
  • ✓Syndrome of inappropriate antidiuretic hormone, which is treated with fluid restriction
  • BDiabetes insipidus, which is treated with fluid replacement
  • CAcute kidney injury, which is treated with diuretics
  • DDehydration, which is treated with a fluid bolus
Rationale

Table 30.2 contrasts the two syndromes on urine output, specific gravity, and serum sodium, with the syndrome of inappropriate antidiuretic hormone producing low output, concentrated urine, and low sodium (p. 1121).

Watch for

(D) is the trap the chapter names explicitly — the low urine output "might be erroneously interpreted as a sign of dehydration," which reverses the treatment from fluid restriction to fluid replacement and worsens the hyponatremia.

9. A child with a head injury has an oral temperature of 40.2 degrees Celsius. What does the chapter direct?
  • ✓Vigorous cooling is needed to prevent brain damage above 40 degrees Celsius, with antipyretics for fever and cooling devices for hyperthermia
  • BNo intervention, since fever is protective after brain injury
  • CCooling devices for fever and antipyretics for hyperthermia
  • DTepid sponging as the first measure at any temperature
Rationale

The chapter states that vigorous cooling is "needed to prevent brain damage if the rectal temperature exceeds 104°F (40°C)," and that "Antipyretics are the method of choice for fever reduction; cooling devices are used for hyperthermia" (pp. 1119, 1121).

Watch for

(C) reverses the two methods, which is the swap this pair exists to catch — the set point determines which works, exactly as in the fever chapter earlier in the book.

head injury in childrengeneral
10. A 6-year-old struck his head, was briefly dazed but never lost consciousness, and is confused with no memory of the event. How should the nurse interpret this?
  • ✓A concussion, whose hallmarks are confusion and amnesia rather than loss of consciousness
  • BNot a concussion, since consciousness was never lost
  • CA cerebral contusion, since amnesia indicates structural injury
  • DA normal response to a minor blow requiring no evaluation
Rationale

The chapter defines concussion as "an alteration in mental status with or without loss of consciousness," and names the belief that loss of consciousness is the hallmark "a common misconception" — the hallmarks being confusion and amnesia (p. 1124).

Watch for

(B) is the misconception stated as a rule; (C) overreaches, though the chapter cautions that a contusion's "signs and symptoms may be clinically indistinguishable from those of concussion," so the two cannot always be separated clinically.

head injury in childrengeneral
11. A 4-year-old struck his head, was briefly unconscious, then talked normally for 40 minutes before deteriorating rapidly. Which injury does this suggest?
  • ✓Epidural hematoma
  • BSubdural hematoma
  • CDiffuse axonal injury
  • DCerebral contusion
Rationale

Table 30.3 characterizes epidural hematoma as usually occurring over 2 years of age, arterial or venous, usually unilateral, lenticular on computed tomography, with skull fracture in 70% of cases, and the lucid interval followed by rapid deterioration is its classic story (p. 1126).

Watch for

(B) is the confusable partner and runs the other way on every feature — usually under 1 year, almost always venous, 75% bilateral, crescentic on computed tomography, with seizures in 75% and retinal hemorrhages very frequent.

12. A 5-month-old has bilateral subdural collections and retinal hemorrhages. What should the nurse consider?
  • ✓Inflicted injury, since bilateral collections and retinal hemorrhages point to a subdural hematoma from shaking
  • BAn epidural hematoma from an accidental fall
  • CA normal variant of infant imaging
  • DBacterial meningitis with subdural effusion
Rationale

Table 30.3 lists subdural hematoma as usually under 1 year, 75% bilateral, with retinal hemorrhages "Very frequent," and the fact bank notes that a stem mentioning retinal hemorrhages, bilateral collections, or an infant who was shaken points to a subdural collection (p. 1126).

Watch for

(D) names a real cause of subdural effusion — the meningitis section states that "Subdural effusions often occur" — but it does not explain retinal hemorrhages, which is the finding that shifts the differential toward inflicted trauma.

head injury in childrengeneral
13. A child struck in the occiput has injury to the frontal and temporal regions. What explains this?
  • ✓Contrecoup injury, where the brain collides with surfaces opposite the point of impact
  • BCoup injury, where bruising occurs at the point of impact
  • CDiffuse axonal injury, which is always bilateral
  • DA skull fracture propagating across the vault
Rationale

The chapter describes "bruising at the point of impact (coup) or at a distance as the brain collides with the unyielding surfaces opposite or far removed from the point of impact (contrecoup)," and gives this exact worked example — "a blow to the occipital region can cause severe injury to the frontal and temporal areas of the brain" (p. 1124).

Watch for

(B) names the injury at the site of the blow, which is the other half of the pair; the terms are distinguished by location relative to the impact rather than by severity.

head injury in childrengeneral
14. A child is being discharged after a mild head injury. Which situation would make the nurse recommend hospitalization for observation instead?
  • ✓A language or communication barrier that may hinder the family's learning
  • BA single episode of vomiting at the scene
  • CA brief headache that has resolved
  • DA parent who asks multiple questions about warning signs
Rationale

The chapter states that "language or other communication barriers or even emotional trauma, may hinder learning and make it difficult for families to feel confident caring for their child at home," which is a reason to hospitalize for observation (p. 1129); this is the basis of the chapter's own review question about phone triage.

Watch for

(D) reverses the meaning of an engaged parent, who is better prepared rather than less; the discharge criteria turn on whether the family can reliably observe and act, not on the severity of the initial symptoms alone.

15. A nurse is assessing a 9-month-old for possible bacterial meningitis. Which findings fit this age group? Select all that apply.
Select all that apply · scored all or nothing
  • ✓Fever with poor feeding and vomiting
  • ✓Marked irritability
  • ✓Frequent seizures, often accompanied by a high-pitched cry
  • ✓Bulging fontanel
  • EPositive Kernig and Brudzinski signs, which are reliable at this age
Rationale

Box 30.6 gives the 3-month-to-2-year picture as fever, poor feeding, vomiting, marked irritability, frequent seizures often with a high-pitched cry, bulging fontanel, and possible nuchal rigidity (p. 1135).

bacterial meningitisgeneral
16. Cerebrospinal fluid from a child shows an elevated white cell count with neutrophils, elevated protein, and decreased glucose. What does this indicate?
  • ✓Bacterial meningitis, since decreased glucose is the fastest single discriminator
  • BViral meningitis, since the white cell count is elevated
  • CNormal cerebrospinal fluid for a febrile child
  • DTuberculous meningitis specifically
Rationale

Table 30.4 contrasts bacterial fluid — elevated white cells with neutrophils, elevated protein, decreased glucose, positive Gram stain, turbid, elevated opening pressure — against viral fluid, which shows slightly elevated white cells with lymphocytes, normal or slightly increased protein, and normal glucose (p. 1138).

Watch for

(B) uses the raised cell count, which both share, rather than the values that separate them; glucose is the value the fact bank singles out as the fastest cut, because bacteria consume it and viruses do not.

bacterial meningitisgeneral
17. A child is admitted with suspected bacterial meningitis and antibiotics have just been started. What should the nurse tell the family about isolation?
  • ✓Respiratory isolation remains in place for at least 24 hours after antibiotics are started
  • BNo isolation is needed now that antibiotics have been started
  • CEnteric precautions are needed for 48 hours
  • DIsolation continues until cerebrospinal fluid cultures are negative
Rationale

The chapter directs respiratory isolation for at least 24 hours after antibiotics are started for suspected bacterial meningitis (p. 1137), and this is the keyed answer to the chapter's own review question.

Watch for

(B) treats the antibiotic as ending the requirement when it actually starts the clock, and (C) is wrong on route — the precautions are respiratory rather than enteric. Note that meningococcal meningitis "is the only type readily transmitted by droplet infection from nasopharyngeal secretions," though the isolation rule applies to suspected bacterial meningitis generally.

18. What is the major nursing priority when a child is admitted with suspected bacterial meningitis?
  • ✓Administer antibiotics as soon as they are ordered, with isolation started at the same time
  • BObtain a full neurologic examination before any medication
  • CAwait culture results before beginning antibiotics
  • DPosition the child with the head of the bed flat and a pillow under the head
Rationale

The chapter states that "A major priority of nursing care of a child suspected of having meningitis is to administer antibiotics as soon as they are ordered," with isolation instituted at the same time (p. 1137).

Watch for

(C) delays treatment for a result that takes far longer than the window in which antibiotics change outcome; (D) reverses the comfort positioning, since most children "are more comfortable without a pillow under their head but with the head of the bed slightly elevated."

bacterial meningitisgeneral
19. A child recovering from bacterial meningitis is being discharged. Which follow-up does the chapter specifically direct?
  • ✓Auditory evaluation shortly after discharge, since deafness is the most common permanent sequela
  • BRepeat lumbar puncture at 1 month
  • COphthalmologic evaluation at 6 months
  • DNo specific follow-up is needed once the child is afebrile
Rationale

The chapter states that damage to the eighth cranial nerve "is usually followed by permanent deafness, the most common permanent neurologic sequela of bacterial meningitis," and that "Hearing loss is common. The patient should undergo auditory evaluation shortly after discharge" (pp. 1136–1137).

Watch for

(D) discharges a child without the one test the chapter names by name; note that a petechial or purpuric rash in an ill child may mean meningococcemia and "must receive immediate medical attention," which is a separate teaching point for families.

20. A child is pulled from a pool after an estimated 3 minutes, with sinus rhythm, reactive pupils, and responsiveness at the scene. What does the chapter say about prognosis and disposition?
  • ✓These features predict a good outcome, but the child is still admitted for 24 hours of observation
  • BThe child may be discharged from the scene if fully alert
  • CThe prognosis cannot be estimated from scene findings
  • DThe cold water will have been neuroprotective, improving the outlook further
Rationale

The chapter states that submersion under 5 minutes with sinus rhythm, reactive pupils, and responsiveness at the scene predicts a good outcome, while over 10 minutes with no response to advanced life support within 25 minutes predicts the worst, and it directs 24-hour admission for observation (pp. 1131–1133).

Watch for

(D) states a belief the chapter explicitly retracts — cold water "had previously been thought to be somewhat neuroprotective, but it is not"; the diving reflex is real but does not make the outcome good.

  • CContact precautions for the duration of the illness

23. A nurse is reviewing the mechanisms of cerebral edema after brain injury. Which statement is accurate?
  • ✓Cytotoxic edema results from direct cell injury with intracellular swelling and often irreversible damage, while vasogenic edema results from increased capillary permeability without primary nerve cell injury
  • BCytotoxic edema results from capillary leak and is reversible
  • CVasogenic edema results from direct cell injury and is irreversible
  • DThe two are the same process described at different stages
Rationale

The chapter defines cytotoxic edema as "a result of direct cell injury… caused by intracellular swelling," where "In many cases the brain cells are irreversibly damaged," and vasogenic edema as "due to increased permeability of capillary endothelial cells," where "the nerve cells are not primarily injured" (p. 1127).

Watch for

(B) and (C) swap the two mechanisms, and the practical consequence is prognostic — whether the cells themselves have been injured determines whether resolving the edema restores function.

seizure classification and antiepileptic therapygeneral
24. A 7-year-old has had one unprovoked generalized tonic-clonic seizure. The parents ask whether this means epilepsy and whether daily medication is needed. What should the nurse explain?
  • ✓A single seizure is not classified as epilepsy and is generally not treated with long-term antiepileptic drugs
  • BAny seizure establishes epilepsy and requires lifelong medication
  • CEpilepsy is diagnosed after any two seizures regardless of interval
  • DMedication is started after the first seizure in every case to prevent recurrence
Rationale

The chapter states that "A single seizure is not classified as epilepsy and is generally not treated with long-term antiepileptic drugs," with epilepsy requiring two unprovoked seizures more than 24 hours apart, or one plus a 60% recurrence probability (p. 1142).

Watch for

(C) drops the interval requirement, which is part of the definition; the distinction matters because it determines whether a child is committed to years of daily medication after a single event.

seizure classification and antiepileptic therapygeneral
25. A teacher reports that a 6-year-old has brief staring episodes several times a day. How can an absence seizure be distinguished from daydreaming?
  • ✓A child who is daydreaming responds to touch, while a child having an absence seizure cannot
  • BDaydreaming episodes are shorter than absence seizures
  • CAbsence seizures are followed by postictal sleepiness and daydreaming is not
  • DAbsence seizures are accompanied by incontinence
Rationale

The chapter states that "Children who are daydreaming will respond to touch, whereas those who are having an absence seizure cannot," adding that absence seizures have "no postictal sleepiness," are "not accompanied by incontinence," and produce amnesia for the episode (p. 1146).

Watch for

(C) and (D) each name a feature the chapter assigns to absence seizures in the negative — their absence is what makes the episodes easy to miss, and the interruptibility test is what a teacher or parent can actually perform.

26. A child started on an antiepileptic drug 10 days ago develops a rash. What should the nurse advise?
  • ✓Stop the drug, and have the child evaluated within 24 hours or sooner if edema or respiratory problems develop
  • BContinue the drug and give an antihistamine
  • CHold the drug and call the office in the morning
  • DHalve the dose and continue while monitoring the rash
Rationale

The chapter states that "The drug should be stopped with any signs of a rash," with evaluation "within 24 hours or sooner if the child develops edema or respiratory problems" (p. 1149).

(B) and (D) both continue exposure to a drug that may be producing a hypersensitivity reaction; (C) gets the first half right and adds a delay the instruction does not permit.

27. A child's electroencephalogram is normal, and the parents conclude that the seizures have resolved. How should the nurse respond?
  • ✓A normal recording does not rule out seizures, because it lasts about 1 hour and may show normal activity between events
  • BA normal recording confirms that the seizures have resolved
  • CA normal recording means the original diagnosis was incorrect
  • DA normal recording means medication can be stopped immediately
Rationale

The chapter states that the recording "lasts approximately 1 hour, and therefore may show normal interictal activity," so a normal study does not exclude a seizure disorder (p. 1148).

Watch for

(D) would act on the misreading in the most consequential way, and the chapter separately notes that "The most common cause of status epilepticus in children taking antiepileptic medications is missed medication."

seizure classification and antiepileptic therapygeneral
28. A parent asks whether a child with well-controlled epilepsy may play sports. What should the nurse advise?
  • ✓Sports are encouraged with a helmet for head protection, with water activities the standing exception requiring supervision
  • BAll sports should be avoided because of injury risk
  • COnly non-contact sports are permitted, and swimming is unrestricted
  • DSports are permitted only after 2 years seizure free
Rationale

The chapter states that "Children with epilepsy are not at increased risk for injury with the exception of head injury," so the answer is helmets rather than restriction, and that "Only children with frequent seizures must avoid these activities"; water is the standing exception, requiring eyes-on supervision when swimming and an adult within arm's reach in natural water (pp. 1152–1155).

Watch for

(C) gets the restriction backward by freeing the one activity that needs supervision; showers are preferred to baths, and the bathroom door is not locked.

seizure first aid and status epilepticusgeneral
29. A child begins a tonic-clonic seizure in the classroom. Which actions should the nurse take? Select all that apply.
Select all that apply · scored all or nothing
  • ✓Time the seizure episode
  • ✓Ease the child to the floor and place something soft under the head
  • ✓Turn the child to the side if vomiting occurs
  • ✓Clear the area of hard objects
  • EPlace a padded tongue blade between the teeth
Rationale

The Emergency Treatment box directs remaining calm, timing the episode, easing the child down, placing a pillow or folded blanket under the head, loosening restrictive clothing, removing eyeglasses, clearing hazards, allowing the seizure to end without interference, and turning the child to one side if vomiting occurs (p. 1154).

Watch for

(E) is one of three things the chapter forbids — do not restrain, do not put anything in the mouth, and do not give food or liquids — and a Nursing Alert repeats it: "do not place a solid object between the child's teeth." This is the single most common wrong option in seizure items.

seizure first aid and status epilepticusgeneral
30. Why is a child placed on the side during and after a tonic-clonic seizure?
  • ✓The swallowing reflex is lost and salivation increases, so side-lying facilitates drainage and helps maintain a patent airway
  • BSide-lying shortens the duration of the seizure
  • CSide-lying prevents the tonic phase from progressing to the clonic phase
  • DSide-lying reduces intracranial pressure during the event
Rationale

The chapter states that during and sometimes after a tonic-clonic seizure "the swallowing reflex is lost, salivation increases," and the tongue is hypotonic, so "the child is at risk for aspiration and airway occlusion. Placing the child on the side facilitates drainage and helps maintain a patent airway" (pp. 1152, 1154).

Watch for

(B) and (C) assign the position a therapeutic effect on the seizure itself, and the chapter is explicit that "It is impossible to physically stop a seizure once it has begun, and no attempt should be made to do so."

seizure first aid and status epilepticusgeneral
31. A child has been seizing continuously for 8 minutes. How is this classified, and when should treatment begin?
  • ✓Impending status epilepticus, with treatment beginning within 5 minutes
  • BStatus epilepticus, defined as any seizure over 5 minutes
  • CA simple prolonged seizure, with treatment after 30 minutes
  • DNot yet requiring treatment until 30 minutes have elapsed
Rationale

The chapter defines status epilepticus as "a continuous seizure that lasts more than 30 minutes or a series of seizures from which the child does not regain a premorbid level of consciousness," and impending status epilepticus as "a continuous seizure or series of seizures lasting between 5 and 30 minutes with the designation that treatment should begin within 5 minutes" (p. 1150).

Watch for

(D) waits for the full 30-minute definition before acting, which is exactly what the impending category exists to prevent; the chapter calls status epilepticus "a medical emergency that requires immediate intervention to prevent possible brain injury or death."

seizure first aid and status epilepticusgeneral
32. A child in status epilepticus has intravenous access. Which drug does the chapter name as first line?
  • ✓Intravenous diazepam or lorazepam
  • BIntravenous phenytoin
  • COral levetiracetam
  • DIntravenous phenobarbital
Rationale

The chapter states that with intravenous access, "IV diazepam or lorazepam (Ativan) is the first-line drug of choice," with phenytoin or fosphenytoin followed by phenobarbital as the next line if a benzodiazepine is ineffective (p. 1150).

Watch for

(B) and (D) are the second-line agents presented as first, and the chapter adds the principle that decides several stems: "It appears that the exact medication chosen is less important than choosing the one that can be administered fastest" — which is why buccal or intranasal midazolam and rectal diazepam are used when there is no line.

febrile seizuresgeneral
33. A 20-month-old had a 2-minute generalized seizure with a temperature of 39.4 degrees Celsius and has no history of afebrile seizures. Which criteria define a febrile seizure?
  • ✓No history of afebrile seizures, a temperature of at least 38 degrees Celsius, and an age between 6 and 60 months
  • BAny seizure occurring with a fever at any age
  • CA seizure lasting more than 15 minutes with fever
  • DA seizure with fever in a child with a family history of epilepsy
Rationale

The chapter's three-part definition states that "children who have a febrile seizure cannot have a history of afebrile seizures, must have a temperature of at least 38°C (100.4°F), and must be between the age of 6 and 60 months" (p. 1157).

Watch for

(B) drops both the age band and the prior-seizure exclusion, which are what make this a distinct entity; febrile seizures are "the most common type of seizure, affecting 2% to 4% of children."

febrile seizuresgeneral
34. The parents of a child who had a simple febrile seizure ask how to prevent another one. What should the nurse explain?
  • ✓Antipyretics relieve fever-associated discomfort but appear to be ineffective in preventing seizures
  • BScheduled acetaminophen during every illness will prevent recurrence
  • CDaily antiepileptic medication is recommended after a first febrile seizure
  • DTepid sponging at the first sign of fever prevents recurrence
Rationale

The chapter states that "Antipyretic therapy during febrile illness offers symptomatic relief for fever-associated symptoms but appears to be ineffective in preventing seizures," restating that "Attempts to lower the temperature will not prevent a seizure," and that "There is no benefit of antiepileptic prophylaxis in these children" (p. 1157).

Watch for

(D) names a measure the chapter rejects on three separate grounds — tepid sponging is ineffective at lowering temperature significantly, the shivering increases metabolic output, and the cooling causes discomfort.

febrile seizuresgeneral
35. A child has a febrile seizure that is still ongoing after 5 minutes. What should parents be instructed to do?
  • ✓Call for emergency assistance, and do not place a child who is actively seizing in the car
  • BDrive the child to the emergency department immediately
  • CWait another 10 minutes before seeking help
  • DGive a dose of antipyretic and observe at home
Rationale

The Nursing Alert states that "If a febrile seizure lasts more than 5 minutes, parents should seek medical attention immediately. Instruct parents to call for emergency assistance (911) and not to place the child who is actively having a seizure in the car" (p. 1157).

Watch for

(B) is the instinct of most parents and creates the exact hazard the alert names; for prolonged recurrent febrile seizures over 5 minutes the chapter endorses acute treatment with rectal diazepam, buccal lorazepam, or intranasal midazolam. Reassurance is evidence-based here, since studies show no difference in intelligence, memory, behavior, or academic performance afterward.

36. A child has hydrocephalus caused by obstruction to the flow of cerebrospinal fluid through the ventricular system. How is this classified?
  • ✓Noncommunicating, which is the same as obstructive
  • BCommunicating, since the ventricles remain connected
  • CCommunicating, since absorption is impaired
  • DNeither, since obstruction is a separate category
Rationale

The chapter defines communicating hydrocephalus as "impaired absorption of CSF fluid within the subarachnoid space" and noncommunicating as "obstruction to the flow of CSF through the ventricular system" (p. 1159).

Watch for

(C) attaches the absorption mechanism to the wrong term, and the naming is counterintuitive enough to be worth holding as a pair — noncommunicating equals obstructive.

37. A child has just returned from ventriculoperitoneal shunt placement. How should the nurse position the child?
  • ✓Flat, on the unoperated side, to avoid complications from too-rapid reduction of intracranial fluid
  • BWith the head of the bed elevated 30 degrees to reduce intracranial pressure
  • COn the operated side to promote drainage
  • DIn Trendelenburg position to maintain cerebral perfusion
Rationale

The chapter directs positioning "carefully on the unoperated side to prevent pressure on the shunt valve," with the child remaining flat "to help avert complications resulting from too-rapid reduction of intracranial fluid" (p. 1164).

Watch for

(B) applies the increased-pressure rule to a situation where the risk has reversed — before shunting the danger is too much pressure, and immediately after it is lowering the pressure too fast; this contradiction between the two rules is deliberate and is the point of the item.

38. A nurse is caring for a child after shunt placement. Which assessments are indicated? Select all that apply.
Select all that apply · scored all or nothing
  • ✓Pupil dilation, since pressure compresses or stretches the oculomotor nerve on the same side
  • ✓Abdominal distention and constipation, since cerebrospinal fluid may cause peritonitis or ileus
  • ✓Temperature, poor feeding, vomiting, and decreased responsiveness as signs of infection
  • ✓Testing any suspected incisional drainage for glucose
  • EVigorous range of motion to prevent contractures
Rationale

The chapter directs neurologic assessment including pupil dilation and blood pressure, observation for abdominal distention and constipation "because CSF may cause peritonitis or a postoperative ileus as a complication of distal catheter placement," monitoring for infection signs, and inspecting the incision and testing suspected drainage for glucose "an indication of CSF" (p. 1164).

Watch for

(E) is not among the postoperative directives and runs against the general instruction elsewhere that range of motion in a child with raised pressure is performed "gently but should not be performed vigorously."

39. A school-age child with a shunt has nausea, refusal to eat, headache, and increasing sleepiness. What should the nurse suspect?
  • ✓Shunt obstruction, since these are early signs of increased intracranial pressure in an older child
  • BGastroenteritis, since the symptoms are gastrointestinal
  • CNormal postoperative recovery
  • DShunt infection, which presents only with these findings
Rationale

The chapter lists nausea and refusal to eat, headache, and irritability with sleepiness among the signs of shunt obstruction (p. 1163), and states that in older children "the most valuable indicators of increasing ICP are an alteration in the child's level of consciousness, complaint of headache, and changes in interaction with the environment" (p. 1164).

Watch for

(B) is the natural first reading of vomiting and anorexia in a child, and it is why shunt malfunction is missed; note the earlier priority point that if bradycardia is also present, that late sign outranks all of these.

40. The parents of a child with a ventriculoperitoneal shunt ask about sports. What should the nurse advise?
  • ✓Contact sports such as football, rugby, boxing, and wrestling are prohibited, while swimming, soccer, and track are acceptable and encouraged
  • BAll sports are prohibited because of the risk of shunt damage
  • CAll sports are permitted without restriction
  • DOnly swimming is prohibited, because of infection risk
Rationale

The chapter states that "Contact sports such as football, rugby, boxing, and wrestling are usually prohibited if a person has a VP shunt; other sports such as swimming, soccer, and track are acceptable and even encouraged for the child's physical and emotional health," with helmets required for skating, skiing, biking, and skateboarding (p. 1164).

Watch for

(B) removes activity the chapter actively encourages for physical and emotional health, and (D) restricts the wrong sport; note that most children who require shunting "must depend on the shunt for the remainder of their life," so the guidance is about living with it rather than avoiding activity.
