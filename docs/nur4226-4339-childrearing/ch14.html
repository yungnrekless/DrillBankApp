preschool sleep problems, nightmares, and sleep terrorsgeneral
1. A 4-year-old wakes screaming about 90 minutes after falling asleep, sits up thrashing with eyes bulging and profuse sweating, does not appear to recognize her mother, and pushes her away when held. By morning she remembers nothing. Which problem is this?
  • AA nightmare, occurring during REM sleep
  • ✓A sleep terror, a partial arousal from stage IV non-REM sleep
  • CA seizure requiring immediate evaluation
  • DTrained night crying from an inappropriate sleep association
Rationale

Table 14.1 defines a sleep terror as "A partial arousal from very deep sleep (stage IV, non-REM) sleep," occurring "usually 1-4 hours after falling asleep, when non-REM sleep is deepest." The child "is not very aware" of another's presence, "is not comforted, and may push person away and scream and thrash more if held or restrained," and has "no memory of dream or of yelling or thrashing."

Watch for

(A) is the reflex label for any frightening night waking. A nightmare happens in REM sleep in the second half of the night, the child wakes fully, remembers the dream, and — the discriminator that matters most at the bedside — "is aware of and reassured by another's presence."

2. A 5-year-old wakes at 5 AM crying, tells his father about a frightening dream, clings to him, is visibly comforted by his presence, and then takes a long time to settle back to sleep. Which problem is this, and what is the correct response?
  • AA sleep terror; observe without interfering until the child calms
  • ✓A nightmare; accept the dream as a real fear, sit with the child, and offer comfort, assurance, and a sense of protection
  • CA nightmare; return the child to bed immediately without discussion, to avoid reinforcing the waking
  • DA sleep terror; wake the child fully to break the episode
Rationale

A nightmare is "A scary dream; takes place during REM sleep and is followed by full waking," occurring "in second half of night, when dreams are most intense," with distress "after dream is over." Return to sleep "may be considerably delayed because of persistent fear." The interventions are to "Accept dream as real fear" and "Sit with child; offer comfort, assurance, and sense of protection."

Watch for

(A) is the mirror error of the previous item, and applying the sleep-terror response here would leave a fully awake, frightened child without the comfort that works. The chapter also directs avoiding "forcing child back to his or her own bed," which is what (C) does.

preschool sleep problems, nightmares, and sleep terrorsgeneral
3. During a sleep terror, a 4-year-old is thrashing in bed but is not near the edge and is in no danger of injury. What should the nurse instruct the parents to do?
  • ✓Observe the child for a few minutes without interfering, until the child becomes calm or wakes fully
  • BWake the child fully as quickly as possible to end the episode
  • CHold and restrain the child firmly until the thrashing stops
  • DArrange professional counseling, since sleep terrors indicate an underlying disorder
Rationale

The intervention for sleep terrors is to "Observe child for a few minutes, without interfering, until child becomes calm or wakes fully," and to "Intervene only if necessary to protect child from injury," guiding the child back to bed if needed. Parents should be told that sleep terrors are "a normal, common phenomenon in preschoolers that requires relatively little intervention."

Watch for

(C) is the instinctive parental response and it makes the episode worse — the child "may push person away and scream and thrash more if held or restrained." (D) misroutes the referral: professional counseling belongs to "recurrent nightmares unresponsive to" comfort measures, not to sleep terrors.

preschool sleep problems, nightmares, and sleep terrorsgeneral
4. Parents report that their 4-year-old stalls at bedtime with elaborate requests and comes out of the room repeatedly. Which guidance reflects the chapter?
  • ✓Ignore attention-seeking behavior and do not take the child into the parents' bed or allow the child to stay up past a reasonable hour; a light in the room, a transitional object, and a drink of water by the bed are acceptable
  • BAllow the child into the parents' bed on difficult nights, so that everyone sleeps
  • CRemove all objects from the room, including night-lights, to eliminate stimulation
  • DExtend the bedtime by an hour, since the resistance shows the child is not tired
Rationale

Parents "should ignore attention-seeking behavior, and the child should not be taken into the parents' bed or allowed to stay up past a reasonable hour." Helpful measures are "keeping a light on in the room, providing transitional objects such as a favorite toy, or leaving a drink of water by the bed," along with soothing limited rituals such as a bath or story, and consistently carrying through the routine.

Watch for

(B) is the exhausted-parent answer and the one the chapter names as the thing not to do. Note also the media link: there is "a direct correlation between sleep problems in preschool children and evening media use, as well as daytime exposure to violent media content."

5. A parent telephones after finding their 2-year-old with an open bottle of pills. What should the nurse advise the parent to do first?
  • ✓Call the poison control center before initiating any intervention
  • BInduce vomiting immediately, before the pills are absorbed
  • CGive milk to dilute the medication, then observe at home
  • DDrive to the emergency department without calling anyone
Rationale

Parents are advised "to call the poison control center (PCC) before initiating any intervention," and to post the PCC number, 800-222-1222, near each phone. Based on that telephone assessment, the PCC "counsels the parents to begin treatment at home or to take the child to an emergency facility."

Watch for

(B) is the obsolete reflex — the American Academy of Pediatrics "no longer recommends" syrup of ipecac for routine poison treatment. Read the setting carefully on items like this: for a parent at home the first action is to call the PCC, while for a nurse at the bedside the governing principle is different, as the next item shows.

poisoning emergency management and gastric decontaminationgeneral
6. A 3-year-old arrives in the emergency department after a household poisoning, pale and lethargic with a weak pulse. What is the first and most important principle guiding the nurse's care?
  • AIdentify the poison before initiating any treatment, so the correct antidote is given
  • ✓Treat the child first, not the poison, with immediate concern for life support and the CABs of resuscitation
  • CAdminister activated charcoal as soon as the child arrives
  • DPerform gastric lavage before assessing vital signs
Rationale

"The first and most important principle in dealing with a poisoning is to treat the child first, not the poison." This "requires an immediate concern for life support," with vital signs taken, mental status assessed, and respiratory or circulatory support instituted. Because shock complicates several household poisons, "particularly corrosives," measures begin with circulation, airway, and breathing support — "the CABs of resuscitation."

Watch for

(A) inverts the priority and is attractive because identifying the poison really is one of the four steps in the emergency sequence; it is not the first. The child is stabilized first, and evidence of the poison — "container, vomitus, urine" — is saved along the way.

7. A nurse is preparing to give activated charcoal to a child who ingested a toxic substance 40 minutes ago. Which statements about activated charcoal are correct? Select all that apply.
Select all that apply · scored all or nothing
  • ✓It may be considered when the time to administration is within 1 hour after the ingestion
  • ✓The child must have an intact or protected airway
  • ✓It may be mixed with chocolate milk, fruit syrup, or a cola drink and served through a straw in an opaque covered container
  • DIt effectively absorbs iron, making it the treatment of choice for iron overdose
  • ✓Complications include vomiting with potential aspiration, constipation, and intestinal obstruction with multiple doses
Rationale

Activated charcoal "is an odorless, tasteless, fine black powder that absorbs many compounds, creating a stable complex." It may be considered when the child may have ingested large amounts of carbamazepine, dapsone, phenobarbital, quinine, or theophylline; when administration is "within 1 hour after the poison ingestion"; and when the child "has an intact or protected airway." Acceptance is improved by mixing it with "chocolate milk, fruit syrup, or cola drinks" and serving it "through a straw in an opaque container with a cover."

Watch for

(D) is the one option from the wrong side of the sort, and it is the most commonly swapped fact in this section: "Activated charcoal does not absorb iron." Iron overdose is managed with whole bowel irrigation if tablets are visible on x-ray, and with deferoxamine chelation.

8. A parent asks whether they should keep syrup of ipecac in the medicine cabinet for emergencies. Which response is correct?
  • ✓The American Academy of Pediatrics no longer recommends its use for routine treatment of poison ingestion and suggests disposing of bottles of syrup of ipecac from the home
  • BEvery home with young children should keep a bottle and use it for any ingestion
  • CIt should be kept but used only for corrosive ingestions
  • DIt should be kept for hydrocarbon ingestions, where emptying the stomach is critical
Rationale

The Nursing Alert states that "Syrup of ipecac is not recommended for routine poison treatment intervention in the home." It is an emetic acting "through irritation of the gastric mucosa and by stimulation of the vomiting center," but the AAP "no longer recommends its use for routine treatment of poison ingestion and suggests disposal of bottles of syrup of ipecac from the home."

Watch for

(C) and (D) name the two ingestions in which inducing emesis is most specifically contraindicated — corrosives because vomiting further damages the mucosa, hydrocarbons because of aspiration risk. Both make ipecac worse than useless rather than selectively useful.

poisoning emergency management and gastric decontaminationgeneral
9. A child has ingested an unknown quantity of a toxin. Which statement about gastric lavage reflects current practice?
  • ✓There is no conclusive evidence that gastric lavage decreases morbidity, and it is no longer recommended to be performed routinely, if at all
  • BGastric lavage is the standard first-line intervention for all pediatric ingestions
  • CGastric lavage is most effective when performed more than 4 hours after ingestion
  • DGastric lavage requires no airway protection in a child who is alert
Rationale

Gastric lavage "can be associated with serious complications (gastrointestinal perforation, hypoxia, aspiration)." "There is no conclusive evidence that gastric lavage decreases morbidity, and it is no longer recommended to be performed routinely, if at all." It "may be of little benefit if used later than 1 hour after ingestion," and when used requires "a protected airway, possible sedation, and the largest diameter tube that can be inserted."

Watch for

(C) inverts the timing window, which runs the same direction as activated charcoal's — sooner is better, and after an hour the benefit largely disappears. Gastrointestinal decontamination as a whole "is not routinely recommended for most childhood poisonings."

10. A 2-year-old has swallowed liquid drain cleaner. The mouth and lips are swollen and white, and the child is drooling and unable to clear secretions. Which action is contraindicated, and why?
  • ✓Inducing emesis, because vomiting further damages the mucosa
  • BMaintaining a patent airway, because manipulation increases edema
  • CAdministering analgesics, because they mask the extent of injury
  • DGiving oral fluids once tolerated, because they dilute the gastric contents
Rationale

For corrosives — "strong acids or alkalis" — "Inducing emesis is contraindicated (vomiting further damages the mucosa)." Treatment maintains a patent airway as needed, administers analgesics, and gives oral fluids when tolerated. The swollen white mucous membranes and edema of the lips, tongue, and pharynx in this stem are the chapter's own signs of impending respiratory obstruction.

Watch for

(B), (C), and (D) each name a treatment the chapter actually directs, which is what makes this item turn on knowing the one contraindication rather than on general caution. Note also that "Liquid corrosives are easily ingested and cause more damage than granular/solid preparations."

11. A parent asks whether to give vinegar to neutralize an alkaline cleaner their child has swallowed. Which response is correct?
  • ✓Do not neutralize; neutralization can cause an exothermic reaction, producing heat and adding a thermal burn to the chemical burn
  • BVinegar is appropriate for an alkali, and baking soda for an acid
  • CNeutralization is safe provided the volume is kept under 120 ml
  • DNeutralization should be attempted only after inducing emesis
Rationale

The chapter directs "Do not neutralize," because neutralization "can cause an exothermic reaction (which produces heat and causes increased symptoms or produces a thermal burn in addition to a chemical burn)." If the PCC or medical advice is not immediately available, "it may be appropriate to dilute corrosive with water or milk (usually ≤120 ml [4 oz])."

Watch for

(C) attaches the correct volume figure to the wrong action — 120 ml is the ceiling for *dilution* with water or milk, not a safe dose of a neutralizing agent. (B) is the chemistry-class answer, and it is exactly the exothermic reaction the chapter warns about.

corrosive, hydrocarbon, and mercury exposuregeneral
12. A 3-year-old has swallowed lamp oil and is coughing and gagging. What is the immediate danger, and what does it mean for treatment?
  • ✓Aspiration — even small amounts can cause bronchitis and chemical pneumonia — so inducing emesis is generally contraindicated, and if lavage must be performed a cuffed endotracheal tube should be in place first
  • BCorrosive injury to the esophagus, so immediate dilution with milk is required
  • CSystemic absorption, so emesis should be induced without delay
  • DHypoglycemia, so intravenous dextrose is the priority
Rationale

For hydrocarbons — gasoline, kerosene, lamp oil, mineral seal oil, lighter fluid, turpentine, paint thinner — the "Immediate danger is aspiration (even small amounts can cause bronchitis and chemical pneumonia)." "Inducing emesis is generally contraindicated," and "if gastric lavage must be performed, a cuffed endotracheal tube should be in place before lavage because of a high risk of aspiration." Symptomatic treatment of chemical pneumonia "includes high humidity, oxygen, hydration, and acetaminophen."

Watch for

(B) applies the corrosive picture to a hydrocarbon. Emesis is contraindicated for both, but for opposite reasons — mucosal damage for corrosives, aspiration for hydrocarbons — and items on this pair test the rationale rather than the action.

corrosive, hydrocarbon, and mercury exposuregeneral
13. A child breaks a mercury thermometer and some of the liquid mercury is swallowed; more is spilled on the floor. Which statement is correct?
  • ✓Elemental mercury is nontoxic if ingested and the gastrointestinal tract is healthy, but mercury is volatile at room temperature and enters the bloodstream after it is inhaled, so the spill should be cleaned up quickly with disposable towels and rubber gloves
  • BThe swallowed mercury is the emergency, and emesis should be induced at once
  • CMercury is harmless in both routes and requires no intervention
  • DThe spill may be safely vacuumed to disperse the droplets
Rationale

"Elemental mercury (also called metallic mercury or quicksilver) is nontoxic if ingested and if the gastrointestinal tract is healthy (e.g., has no fistulas). However, mercury is volatile at room temperature and enters the bloodstream after it is inhaled." The Nursing Alert directs: "clean up spilled mercury quickly, using disposable towels and rubber gloves and washing the hands well afterward." The classic form of mercury poisoning is acrodynia, meaning painful extremities.

Watch for

(B) follows the intuition that swallowing a heavy metal must be the dangerous route, and this is one of the few places in the chapter where that intuition inverts. The hazard is the vapor, which is why the cleanup instruction — and not the ingestion — carries the urgency.

acetaminophen, salicylate, and iron poisoninggeneral
14. A 3-year-old ingested a large quantity of acetaminophen 36 hours ago. The child vomited initially but now appears well, and the parents want to go home. How should the nurse interpret this?
  • AThe child has recovered, and discharge is appropriate
  • ✓This is stage 2, in which the patient improves between 24 and 72 hours; treatment with N-acetylcysteine is still indicated
  • CThis is stage 3, and liver failure is already established
  • DThe improvement rules out a toxic ingestion
Rationale

Acetaminophen poisoning is "the most common accidental drug poisoning in children," with a toxic dose of "150 mg/kg or greater in children." Stage 1, from 0 to 24 hours, brings nausea, vomiting, sweating, and pallor; in stage 2, from 24 to 72 hours, "Patient improves," possibly with right upper quadrant pain; stage 3, at 72 to 96 hours, brings jaundice, confusion, coagulation abnormalities, and sometimes renal failure.

Watch for

(A) is the answer the child's appearance invites, and it is why stage 2 exists as a named stage. The antidote is N-acetylcysteine, "equally effective given intravenously or orally," diluted in fruit juice or soda when oral "because of the antidote's offensive odor," and given as one loading dose followed by 17 additional doses.

acetaminophen, salicylate, and iron poisoninggeneral
15. A 2-year-old ingested iron tablets 4 hours ago and has had no vomiting, diarrhea, or abdominal pain. The parents ask whether the child is out of danger. Which response is accurate?
  • ✓If the child does not develop gastrointestinal symptoms in 6 hours, toxicity is unlikely — but the child is not yet at that point and requires continued observation
  • BThe absence of symptoms at 4 hours confirms that no significant amount was ingested
  • CIron toxicity produces no gastrointestinal symptoms at any stage
  • DThe child should be discharged and returned only if symptoms develop
Rationale

In stage 1 of iron poisoning, within 6 hours, the findings are vomiting, hematemesis, diarrhea, bloody stools, and abdominal pain; the chapter states that "if child does not develop gastrointestinal symptoms in 6 hours, toxicity is unlikely." Stage 2 is a "Latency period — up to 24 hours of apparent improvement," after which stage 3 brings metabolic acidosis, bleeding, seizures, and shock.

Watch for

(B) applies the 6-hour rule two hours early, which is the error that sends a child home during the window in which symptoms still declare themselves. Note that iron has the same false-recovery trap as acetaminophen: apparent improvement in stage 2 is a stage, not a resolution.

16. A child with severe iron poisoning is receiving intravenous deferoxamine. The urine turns red-orange, and shortly afterward the child develops facial flushing, urticaria, tachycardia, and hypotension. What should the nurse do?
  • ARecognize both findings as expected and continue the infusion
  • ✓Recognize the red-orange urine as expected, but stop the infusion, maintain the intravenous line with normal saline, and notify the practitioner immediately
  • CStop the infusion because of the urine color change, which indicates hemolysis
  • DSlow the infusion and administer activated charcoal to bind the remaining iron
Rationale

Chelation therapy with deferoxamine "may turn urine red to orange," which is an expected effect. However, if intravenous deferoxamine is given too rapidly, "hypotension, facial flushing, rash, urticaria, tachycardia, and shock may occur; stop the infusion, maintain the IV line with normal saline, and notify the practitioner immediately."

Watch for

(C) reacts to the harmless finding and misses the dangerous one, which is the point of pairing them in one stem. (D) proposes an agent that does not work here at all: "Activated charcoal does not absorb iron."

17. A 4-year-old has been given adult aspirin by a grandparent for several days for a presumed viral illness, and now has subtle confusion and a nosebleed. Which statement reflects the chapter?
  • ✓Chronic salicylate poisoning, more than 100 mg/kg/day for 2 or more days, has a subtle onset with nonspecific symptoms often mistaken for viral illness and can be more serious than acute ingestion
  • BChronic salicylate exposure is always less serious than a single large ingestion
  • CBleeding is not a feature of salicylate toxicity at any dose
  • DThe early findings of acute salicylate poisoning are bradycardia and hypoventilation
Rationale

Chronic salicylate toxicity is ">100 mg/kg/day for ≥2 days" and "can be more serious than acute ingestion," with the "same as listed above but subtle onset and nonspecific symptoms (often mistaken for viral illness)" plus bleeding tendencies. Acute early findings are nausea, hyperventilation, vomiting, and tinnitus.

Watch for

(D) inverts the two most characteristic early acute findings — the child hyperventilates rather than hypoventilates, and tinnitus is the other early cue. (B) is the intuition this fact overturns, and it is why a history of several days of dosing matters more than the size of any single dose.

18. A child with severe salicylate toxicity requires enhanced elimination. Which interventions does the chapter name?
  • ✓Activated charcoal as soon as possible, sodium bicarbonate to correct metabolic acidosis with urinary alkalinization to enhance elimination, external cooling for hyperpyrexia, and hemodialysis rather than peritoneal dialysis in severe cases
  • BGastric lavage, which reliably removes aspirin concretions
  • CPeritoneal dialysis, which is preferred over hemodialysis in children
  • DAspirin reversal with vitamin K alone, which corrects both the acidosis and the bleeding
Rationale

"Activated charcoal is given as soon as possible (unless contraindicated by altered mental status)." "Sodium bicarbonate transfusions are used to correct metabolic acidosis, and urinary alkalinization may be effective in enhancing elimination; hypokalemia may interfere with achieving urinary alkalinization." Use "external cooling for hyperpyrexia," vitamin K for bleeding, and "In severe cases, hemodialysis (not peritoneal dialysis) is used."

Watch for

(B) is explicitly refuted: "Lavage will not remove concretions of ASA." (C) reverses the dialysis preference, which the chapter states in a parenthesis precisely because it is the swappable half.

lead exposure sources and pathophysiologygeneral
19. A parent insists their toddler cannot have lead poisoning because he has never been seen eating paint chips. Which response is correct?
  • ✓A child does not need to eat loose paint chips to be exposed; normal hand-to-mouth behavior, coupled with lead dust in the environment that has settled over decades, is the usual method of poisoning
  • BEating paint chips is the only meaningful route of childhood lead exposure
  • CLead exposure occurs only through inhalation during renovation
  • DLead poisoning cannot occur in a home built after 1950
Rationale

"A child does not need to eat loose paint chips to be exposed to the toxin; normal hand-to-mouth behavior, coupled with the presence of lead dust in the environment that has settled over decades, is the usual method of poisoning." In most cases of acute childhood lead poisoning "the source is nonintact lead-based paint in an older home or lead-contaminated bare soil in the yard." Ingestion "happens during normal day-to-day play and mouthing activities," while inhalation "usually occurs during renovation and remodeling."

Watch for

(D) attaches the wrong date to the wrong rule: lead in paint was *decreased* in 1950 and *banned* in 1978, so a home built in 1960 can still carry lead paint. Those two dates are also the ones the personal risk questions use, which is where the swap does the most damage.

lead exposure sources and pathophysiologygeneral
20. A nurse is teaching about how lead behaves in a child's body. Which statement is accurate?
  • ✓Lead ultimately settles in the bones and teeth, where it remains inert and in storage, making up approximately 75% to 90% of the body burden, and at the cellular level it competes with calcium
  • BLead is stored primarily in the liver and is cleared within weeks
  • CLead affects only the hematologic system
  • DA child's developing nervous system is less vulnerable to lead than an adult's
Rationale

Lead "moves via an equilibration process between the blood, the soft tissues and organs, and the bones and teeth. Lead ultimately settles in the bones and teeth, where it remains inert and in storage. This makes up the largest portion of the body burden, approximately 75% to 90%." At the cellular level "it competes with molecules of calcium, interfering with the regulating action of calcium." Lead "can affect any part of the body, including the renal, hematologic, and neurologic systems."

Watch for

(D) inverts the chapter's central concern: of most concern "is the developing brain and nervous system, which are more vulnerable than those of older children and adults." That vulnerability is the entire reason for screening children rather than adults.

21. A 3-year-old with an elevated blood lead level is also found to be iron deficient. Why does this combination matter, and what does it mean for teaching?
  • ✓Children who are iron deficient absorb lead more readily, and lead can interfere with iron binding onto the heme molecule, sometimes creating a picture of anemia even when the child is not iron deficient; teaching therefore includes adequate calcium and iron and regular meals
  • BIron deficiency protects against lead absorption, so supplementation should be withheld
  • CThe anemia proves the child is iron deficient, so lead can be excluded as a cause
  • DNutrition has no bearing on lead absorption
Rationale

"Children who are iron deficient absorb lead more readily than those with sufficient iron stores. Lead can interfere with the binding of iron onto the heme molecule. This sometimes creates a picture of anemia even though the child is not iron deficient." Education for a child with a blood lead level of 5 mcg/dl or above includes the "Importance of good nutrition in reducing the absorption and effects of lead," with "adequate intake of calcium and iron and importance of regular meals."

Watch for

(C) reads the anemia backward. Lead can produce the appearance of iron-deficiency anemia in a child whose iron stores are adequate, so the blood count cannot be used to rule lead in or out — only a venous blood lead level can.

lead exposure sources and pathophysiologygeneral
22. A child's blood lead level has been elevated for months. The parents ask whether the damage can be undone once the lead is removed. Which response is accurate?
  • ✓Although most of the pathophysiologic effects of lead are reversible, the most serious consequences are on the central nervous system, and low-dose exposure may also cause permanent neurologic deficits
  • BAll effects of lead reverse completely once the blood lead level normalizes
  • COnly high-dose exposure carries any risk of permanent injury
  • DLead poisoning at this age produces no cognitive effects
Rationale

"Although most of the pathophysiologic effects of lead are reversible, the most serious consequences of both high and low lead exposure are the effects on the central nervous system." In lead encephalopathy "permanent brain damage can result in cognitive impairment, behavior changes, possible paralysis, and seizures." "However, low-dose exposure may also cause permanent neurologic deficits." Even mild and moderate poisoning can cause "aggression, hyperactivity, impulsivity, delinquency, disinterest, and withdrawal."

Watch for

(C) is the reassuring half-truth most families hold, and the chapter refuses it in a single sentence. Long-term signs include "developmental delays, lowered intelligence quotient (IQ), reading skill deficits, visual-spatial problems, visual-motor problems, learning disabilities, and lower academic success."

23. A healthy 18-month-old is at a well-child visit and has no symptoms. The parents ask why lead testing is being suggested. Which response is correct?
  • ✓Children with lead poisoning rarely have symptoms even at levels requiring chelation therapy, and universal screening should be done at 1 and 2 years of age
  • BScreening is indicated only for children with behavioral symptoms or pica
  • CScreening begins at 5 years of age, when school entry is required

24. A family cannot be screened under a universal program, so targeted screening is used. Which questions does the chapter give as the personal risk questions?
  • ✓Does the child live in or regularly visit a house built before 1950; a house built before 1978 with recent or ongoing renovations within the past 6 months; or have a sibling or playmate who has or had lead poisoning
  • BDoes the child live in a house built before 1978; a house with well water; or attend day care
  • CDoes the child have pica; a family history of anemia; or a sibling in school
  • DDoes the child live in a house built before 1920; a house painted within the past year; or have a pet
Rationale

Children should be screened if their family cannot answer "no" to three questions: "Does your child live in or regularly visit a house that was built before 1950?"; "Does your child live in or regularly visit a house built before 1978 with recent or ongoing renovations or remodeling within the past 6 months?"; and "Does your child have a sibling or playmate who has or had lead poisoning?"

Watch for

(B) collapses the two dates into one and drops the renovation qualifier, which is what makes the second question different from the first. The 1950 question asks about age of housing alone; the 1978 question asks about age plus recent disturbance of the paint.

25. A 2-year-old's venous blood lead level returns at 52 mcg/dl. Which actions does Table 14.2 direct?
  • ✓Provide lead education, refer to a clinical center specializing in lead poisoning, provide diagnostic testing within 24 to 48 hours, perform clinical evaluation and management within 48 hours, provide appropriate chelation therapy, and ensure aggressive environmental intervention
  • BProvide education and rescreen in 1 year
  • CProvide education and retest within 1 month, with no chelation at this level
  • DWithhold chelation until the child develops symptoms
Rationale

For a blood lead level of 45 to 69 mcg/dl, Table 14.2 directs providing lead education, referral to a clinical center specializing in lead poisoning with coordination of care, "diagnostic testing within 24-48 hours," clinical evaluation and management "within 48 hours," "appropriate chelation therapy," "aggressive environmental intervention," and follow-up testing "at least once per month."

Watch for

(D) is the error the chapter blocks twice over: children with lead poisoning "rarely have symptoms even at levels requiring chelation therapy," and a level of 70 or above requires immediate testing and chelation "whether symptoms are present or not." (B) is the action for a level below 5.

26. A 3-year-old's blood lead level is 8 mcg/dl. Which elements belong in the education and follow-up for this child? Select all that apply.
Select all that apply · scored all or nothing
  • ✓Explanation of the child's blood lead level and what it means, and the potential adverse health effects of an elevated level
  • ✓The importance of wet cleaning to remove lead dust from floors, windowsills, and other surfaces
  • ✓The hazards of improper removal of lead paint, such as dry sanding, scraping, or open-flame burning
  • ✓Follow-up testing within 1 month, and then every 3 to 4 months
  • EErythrocyte protoporphyrin testing as the preferred screening method going forward
Rationale

Education for every child with a blood lead level of 5 mcg/dl or above covers the level and its meaning, potential adverse health effects, sources of exposure and how to reduce it "such as the importance of wet cleaning," the importance of good nutrition, the need for follow-up testing, results of any environmental investigation, and "Hazards of improper removal of lead paint (dry sanding, scraping, or open-flame burning)." For a level of 5 to 14, Table 14.2 directs follow-up testing "within 1 month, and then every 3-4 months."

(E) is the one option from the wrong side of the sort. Erythrocyte protoporphyrin "is no longer used as a screening test" because it "is not sensitive to BLLs of less than about 16 to 25 mcg/dl" — though a level above 35 mcg/dl still indicates toxicity and reflects duration of exposure and body burden.

27. Parents of a child beginning chelation therapy ask whether it will undo the effects of the lead. Which response is accurate?
  • ✓Chelation removes lead from circulating blood and some from organs and tissues, but it does not counteract any effects of the lead, and it is unclear whether it affects lead stores in bones
  • BChelation reverses the neurologic effects of lead exposure
  • CA single course of chelation removes the entire body burden of lead
  • DChelation eliminates the need for environmental remediation
Rationale

Chelation is "the term used for removing lead from circulating blood and, theoretically, some lead from organs and tissues. It is unclear whether chelation affects lead stores in bones." Although it resembles an antidote in purpose, "chelation does not counteract any effects of the lead." Because of the equilibration process "there is often a rebound of the BLL after chelation," and "Multiple chelation treatments may be necessary."

(C) ignores the rebound the chapter describes: the level rises again as lead redistributes from tissue stores, and rebound ceases only once the body burden has fallen enough to stabilize the blood level. (C) inverts the iron rule between the two agents, which is the single most swappable fact in this section — it is British antilewisite that must not be combined with iron, and succimer that may be. Both facts matter because iron supplementation is often indicated in the same child.

43. A nurse is documenting findings in a case of suspected maltreatment. Which approach is correct?
  • ✓Describe behaviors rather than interpret them, record conversations verbatim as much as possible, use a body diagram, and photograph injuries with a measurement tool
  • BRecord the nurse's conclusion that the child appears abused, so the record is unambiguous
  • CSummarize the child's statements in the nurse's own words for clarity
  • DOmit parent-child interaction, since it is subjective
Rationale

"Accurate and factual documentation is essential. Behaviors are described, not interpreted, and are recorded daily to establish a progress record." "Conversations among the nurse, child, and parent are recorded verbatim as much as possible." Recording includes bruise location, size, shape, and color "on drawing of body outline," distinguishing characteristics, symmetry or asymmetry, evidence of past injuries, and "description of parent-child interactions (verbal interactions, eye contact, touching, parental concern)."

Watch for

(B) records a conclusion in place of the evidence supporting it, which weakens the record in precisely the setting — a court proceeding, where "Nurses may be subpoenaed" — that the documentation exists to serve. (C) discards the verbatim quality the chapter requires, and the child's own words are the evidence.
