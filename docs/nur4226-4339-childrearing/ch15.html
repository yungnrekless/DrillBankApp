school-age biologic development and prepubescencegeneral
1. A 9-year-old boy has grown noticeably stronger over the past year and wants to begin an intensive weight-training program. Which consideration should the nurse raise?
  • ✓By the end of this period both boys and girls double their strength and physical capabilities, but this increased strength is often misleading, because muscles are still functionally immature compared with those of the adolescent and are more readily injured by overuse
  • BStrength gains at this age indicate muscular maturity equivalent to an adolescent's
  • CStrength does not increase appreciably during the school-age years
  • DOveruse injury is not a concern until after puberty
Rationale

"By the end of this age period, both boys and girls double their strength and physical capabilities." But "this increased strength is often misleading. Although strength increases, muscles are still functionally immature compared with those of the adolescent, and they are more readily injured by overuse." Bones also continue to ossify, and "mineralization is not completed until maturity."

Watch for

(B) draws the natural inference from visible strength and is exactly the reasoning that produces overuse injuries in this age group. The fact that makes this item worth writing is that capability and tissue maturity diverge here, and only one of them is visible.

2. A 7-year-old is the size of a typical 10-year-old, and his parents have begun expecting him to behave accordingly. Which principle should the nurse teach?
  • ✓Physical maturity is not necessarily correlated with emotional and social maturity; a 7-year-old who looks 10 will think and act like a 7-year-old, and expecting otherwise can be detrimental to competence and self-esteem
  • BPhysical size is a reliable indicator of emotional readiness at this age
  • CChildren who are large for their age mature emotionally faster to match
  • DTreating a 10-year-old as though they were 7 carries no developmental cost
Rationale

"Physical maturity is not necessarily correlated with emotional and social maturity. Seven-year-old children who look like 10-year-old children will think and act like 7-year-olds." Expecting 10-year-old behavior "is unrealistic and can be detrimental to their development of competence and self-esteem."

Watch for

(D) is the other half of the same principle, and the chapter closes it off deliberately: "Conversely, treating 10-year-old children as though they were 7 years old is an equal disservice." The mismatch cuts both ways, and an item can test either direction.

3. A parent asks when to expect pubertal changes in their 9-year-old daughter and 9-year-old son. Which response reflects the chapter?
  • ✓Generally puberty begins at 10 years in girls and 12 years in boys, with onset in either sex after age 8 years considered normal; the average age is 12 years in girls and 14 years in boys, with approximately a 2-year difference between the sexes
  • BPuberty begins at 12 years in girls and 10 years in boys
  • COnset before age 10 is abnormal in both sexes and requires evaluation
  • DBoys and girls begin pubertal changes at the same average age
Rationale

"Generally, puberty begins at 10 years in girls and 12 years in boys, but its onset in either sex after age 8 years is considered normal. The average age of puberty is 12 years in girls and 14 years in boys." On average "there is a difference of approximately 2 years between girls and boys in the age of onset of pubescence," and first signs appear "at about 9 years (particularly in girls)."

Watch for

(B) swaps the two sexes, which is the commonest inversion here because the numbers themselves are easy to hold and their owners are not. (C) would make normal development pathologic — the chapter sets the lower boundary of normal at 8 years, not 10.

4. A 10-year-old girl has developed breasts well ahead of her classmates, and a 12-year-old boy in the same clinic has shown no pubertal changes. What does the chapter say about the psychosocial implications?
  • ✓Early appearance of secondary sex characteristics in girls is often associated with dissatisfaction with physical appearance, greater general unhappiness, and lower self-esteem, while late-developing boys often have a negative self-concept; both have been linked to participation in risk-taking behaviors
  • BOff-schedule development carries psychosocial risk only for girls
  • CEarly development in girls is consistently associated with higher self-esteem
  • DNeither pattern has any documented psychosocial association
Rationale

"Early appearance of secondary sex characteristics in girls is often associated with dissatisfaction with physical appearance, greater general unhappiness, and lower self-esteem. Late-developing boys often have a negative self-concept." Both "have been linked to participation in risk-taking behaviors (e.g., early sexual activity, substance use, and reckless vehicle use)."

Watch for

(C) assumes that developing early confers status, which is the intuition the data overturn for girls. Note the underlying driver the chapter names: preadolescent children "do not want to be different," and the variability at this age is striking in both directions.

industry versus inferiority in the school-age childgeneral
5. An 8-year-old proudly shows the nurse a birdhouse he built and asks whether it is good enough to enter in a competition. Which psychosocial task does this reflect?
  • AInitiative versus guilt
  • ✓Industry versus inferiority, achieving a sense of personal and interpersonal competence through the acquisition of technologic and social skills
  • CIdentity versus role confusion
  • DAutonomy versus shame and doubt
Rationale

"A sense of industry, or a stage of accomplishment, occurs somewhere between age 6 years and adolescence." The goal is "to achieve a sense of personal and interpersonal competence through the acquisition of technologic and social skills." School-age children "are eager to build skills and participate in meaningful and socially useful work," and they "want to engage in tasks that they can complete."

Watch for

(A) belongs to the preschooler and (C) to the adolescent. The discriminator is what the child is seeking: the preschooler is testing whether it is permissible to try, while the school-age child is asking whether the finished product is good — accomplishment rather than permission.

6. A 9-year-old who struggles in athletics tells the nurse she is "bad at everything." Which understanding should guide the nurse's response?
  • ✓No child is able to do well in everything, and all children feel some degree of inferiority regarding a specific skill they cannot master; repeated failures can make a child reluctant to attempt any new task
  • BA child who reports feeling inferior in one area has a pathologic self-concept requiring referral
  • CChildren who fail at one skill readily compensate through any other skill they possess
  • DFeelings of inferiority arise only from the social environment, never from the child
Rationale

"No child is able to do well in everything… All children, even children who usually have positive attitudes toward work and their own capabilities, feel some degree of inferiority in regard to a specific skill that they cannot master." "Repeated failures can generate such strong feelings that eventually the child is reluctant to attempt any new task… Consequently, the child may no longer try."

Watch for

(C) understates a real obstacle the chapter names: "society places a higher value on success in team sports than on success in repairing a bicycle," so compensating through a less valued skill "is difficult for children." (D) drops half the source — feelings of inferiority come "from children themselves or from the social environment."

7. A 10-year-old consistently sets goals far beyond what he can achieve and is repeatedly disappointed. Which principle applies?
  • ✓Children who aspire to more than they are capable of usually experience failure, whereas children who set their aspirations lower than their level of achievement are likely to experience success
  • BSetting the highest possible goals always builds industry
  • CAspiration level has no relationship to experienced success at this age
  • DThe child should be told to stop setting goals until he is older
Rationale

"Children who aspire to more than they are capable of usually experience failure. In contrast, children who set their aspirations lower than their level of achievement are likely to experience success." The chapter also notes that at this age "Most accomplishments during the school years are very public. Family, teachers, and peers are all aware of success or failure in school."

Watch for

(B) is the encouraging advice adults reach for, and in this stage it manufactures the repeated failure that leads to a child refusing to attempt anything new. What builds industry is completing tasks, which requires aspirations the child can actually reach.

8. A slow-to-warm-up 8-year-old becomes tearful and complains of stomachaches before each new after-school activity. What should the nurse advise the parents?
  • ✓Encourage the child to try new experiences but allow adaptation at the child's own speed; pressure to move quickly into new situations only strengthens the tendency to withdraw
  • BInsist the child attend every session without exception, so avoidance is not reinforced
  • CWithdraw the child from all new activities until adolescence
  • DTreat the somatic complaints as a medical problem requiring workup before proceeding
Rationale

Slow-to-warm-up children "exhibit discomfort when introduced to new situations and need time to become accustomed to a new environment, authority figures, and expectations," and may respond "with tears, somatic complaints, or other maneuvers to avoid the event." The guidance is to "Encourage them to try new experiences but allow them to adapt to their surroundings at their own speed," because "Pressure to move quickly into new situations only strengthens the tendency to withdraw." Attending with a friend, or contracting for permission to withdraw after a specified number of tries, may help.

Watch for

(B) is the firm-hand answer and it produces the opposite of the intended effect in this temperament. (C) abandons the encouragement half of the instruction — the child is meant to try new things, just not on someone else's schedule.

concrete operations and conservation in the school-age childgeneral
9. A 9-year-old is shown two identical balls of clay, and one is flattened into a pancake. She says, "It's still the same amount — you could roll it back into a ball." Which concept is she using?
  • AIdentity
  • ✓Reversibility
  • CReciprocity
  • DSeriation
Rationale

Children justify conservation with one of three concepts. Reversibility is that "The clay can be reshaped into its original form (a ball)." Identity is that "Because nothing has been added and nothing has been taken away, the pancake is still the same clay. Nothing has changed but the shape." Reciprocity is that "Although the pancake appears larger in circumference, the ball is much thicker."

Watch for

(A) is the neighbouring justification and the most commonly substituted one, because both amount to "it's still the same clay." The discriminator is the child's reasoning: identity appeals to nothing having been added or removed, while reversibility appeals to the transformation being undoable.

10. A second child, shown the same flattened clay, says, "It's wider, but it's a lot thinner too." Which concept is this, and what ability does it demonstrate?
  • AIdentity, demonstrating that nothing was added or removed
  • ✓Reciprocity, demonstrating the ability to deal with two dimensions at the same time and to comprehend that a change in one dimension compensates for a change in another
  • CReversibility, demonstrating that the action can be undone
  • DClassification, demonstrating grouping by shared attributes
Rationale

Reciprocity is the justification that "Although the pancake appears larger in circumference, the ball is much thicker." The child "demonstrates the ability to deal with two dimensions at the same time and to comprehend that a change in one dimension compensates for a change in another." Once children can use all three concepts, "they can conserve along any physical dimension."

Watch for

(C) is the mirror error of the previous item. Nothing in this child's reasoning refers to undoing the change; the reasoning tracks two dimensions simultaneously, which is the cognitive step reciprocity names.

11. A 7-year-old can conserve number and liquid but insists that a ball of clay weighs more after being flattened. How should the nurse interpret this?
  • ✓Expected; there is a developmental sequence to conservation — number at 5 to 6 years, liquids, mass, and length at about 6 to 7 years, weight at 9 to 10 years, and volume last at 9 to 12 years
  • BDelayed; conservation of weight is expected by 6 years
  • CConcerning; conservation is acquired all at once or not at all
  • DAdvanced; conservation of liquid is not expected until 10 years
Rationale

"There appears to be a developmental sequence in children's capacity to conserve matter": numbers at 5 to 6 years, "liquids, mass, and length" at about 6 to 7 years, weight at 9 to 10 years, and "volume or displacement" at 9 to 12 years.

Watch for

(C) is the assumption that conservation is a single switch, and the staggered sequence is precisely what makes this child's answer normal. A 7-year-old who conserves liquid but not weight is exactly on schedule.

12. An 8-year-old has begun amassing large collections of cards, rocks, and stuffed animals, and ranks her friends as "first best friend" and "second best friend." Which cognitive ability underlies both behaviors?
  • ✓Classification — the ability to group objects according to attributes they have in common, holding a concept in mind while making decisions based on it
  • BConservation of volume
  • CEgocentrism
  • DMetalinguistic awareness
Rationale

Classification is "the ability to group objects according to the attributes that they have in common." School-age children "can place things in a sensible and logical order, group and sort, and hold a concept in their minds while they make decisions based on that concept." This is why they "become occupied with numerous and varied collections," and why "They even begin to order friends and relationships (e.g., first best friend, second best friend)."

Watch for

(C) belongs to the preschooler and is what concrete operations is replacing: "Their rigid, egocentric outlook is replaced by thought processes that allow them to see things from another's point of view."

13. A nurse is explaining what changes cognitively when a child enters concrete operations, between ages 7 and 11. Which statement is accurate?
  • ✓Children progress from making judgments based on what they see, which is perceptual thinking, to judgments based on what they reason, which is conceptual thinking, and they can delay an action until they have evaluated alternative responses
  • BChildren in concrete operations can reason abstractly about hypothetical situations
  • CChildren in concrete operations must still carry out an action before they can anticipate its result
  • DEgocentrism increases during this stage
Rationale

"The concrete operational stage occurs between ages 7 and 11 years." Children "progress from making judgments based on what they see (perceptual thinking) to making judgments based on what they reason (conceptual thinking)," and "are able to delay an action until they have evaluated alternative responses to situations." Their "steady reduction in egocentricity helps form the basis for logical thought and the development and maturation of morality."

Watch for

(C) describes the preschooler and is what reversibility replaces: school-age children "have the ability to think through an action sequence, anticipate the consequences, and, if needed, return to the beginning and rethink the action in a different direction. They no longer need to experience an action before they can anticipate the results." (B) belongs to adolescence — abstract, principled reasoning is "not until adolescence or beyond."

school-age moral, spiritual, and language developmentgeneral
14. A 6-year-old says that a child who accidentally broke five cups is naughtier than one who deliberately broke a single cup. A 10-year-old in the same family disagrees. What explains the difference?
  • ✓Younger school-age children usually judge an act by its consequences, while older school-age children are able to judge an act by the intentions that prompted it rather than just by the consequences
  • BThe 6-year-old is showing delayed moral development
  • CYounger children judge by intentions and older children by consequences
  • DMoral reasoning does not change across the school-age years
Rationale

Younger school-age children, 6 to 7 years, "know the rules and behaviors expected of them, but they do not understand the reasons behind them" and "usually judge an act by its consequences. Rewards and punishment guide their judgment." Older school-age children are "able to judge an act by the intentions that prompted it rather than just by the consequences," and rules become "less absolute and authoritarian."

Watch for

(C) is the straight inversion of the pair. (B) mistakes a normal stage for a delay — this is exactly what a 6-year-old's moral reasoning is expected to look like, and it is the reason the two children in this stem disagree.

15. A hospitalized 8-year-old asks the nurse whether she got sick because she was mean to her brother last week. Which understanding should guide the response?
  • ✓School-age children often view illness or injury as a punishment for a real or imagined misdeed, are developing a conscience, and want and expect to be punished for misbehavior
  • BThis reflects magical thinking that should have resolved by age 5 and warrants referral
  • CChildren this age do not connect behavior with illness
  • DThe child is attempting to avoid a procedure
Rationale

School-age children "are fascinated by heaven and hell and, with a developing conscience and concern about rules, they fear going to hell for misbehavior. School-age children want and expect to be punished for misbehavior and, if given the option, tend to choose a punishment that 'fits the crime.' Often they view illness or injury as a punishment for a real or imagined misdeed."

Watch for

(B) mislabels a normal feature of this stage as a residue of preschool magical thinking. The mechanism differs: the preschooler believes thoughts cause events, while the school-age child believes misdeeds earn punishment — a moral framework rather than a magical one.

school-age moral, spiritual, and language developmentgeneral
16. A 10-year-old is delighted by puns and riddles and can explain what "A stitch in time saves nine" means. Which language development does this demonstrate?
  • ✓Metalinguistic awareness — an ability to think about language and comment on its properties, which enables appreciation of jokes, riddles, and puns and understanding of metaphors and figurative statements
  • BTelegraphic speech
  • CThe minimum-distance principle
  • DEgocentric speech
Rationale

Metalinguistic awareness is "an ability to think about language and to comment on its properties." This "enables them to appreciate jokes, riddles, and puns that involve play on words, sounds, or double meanings," and to begin understanding "metaphors and figurative statements, such as 'A stitch in time saves nine.'" Children become "less likely to engage in literal interpretation of messages."

Watch for

(C) names a real school-age language rule, but a different one — the minimum-distance principle is "the rule that the subject of a verb in an active sentence is the noun or pronoun that immediately precedes it," which is why a 6-year-old understands "Ask Mary her last name" but is confused by "Ask Mary what to bring to the party" until age 9 or 10.

17. A nurse plans religious and spiritual support for a hospitalized 8-year-old. Which approach fits the chapter?
  • ✓Present religious concepts in concrete terms, since children this age have difficulty understanding symbols, and recognize that the beliefs of family and religious personages are more influential than those of peers in matters of faith
  • BUse abstract theological explanations, since concrete operations allow abstract reasoning
  • CRely on peer influence, which predominates over family in matters of faith at this age
  • DAvoid religious ritual, which increases anxiety in hospitalized children
Rationale

"Religious concepts must be presented to them in concrete terms" because children this age "have difficulty understanding symbols." "The beliefs and ideals of family and religious personages are more influential than those of their peers in matters of faith," and religious rituals "can help children cope with threatening situations."

Watch for

(C) generalizes the chapter's emphasis on peer influence into a domain where the chapter says the opposite. Peers dominate standards for dress, behavior, and acceptance; family and religious figures dominate faith.

school-age peer groups, clubs, and team playgeneral
18. A 10-year-old belongs to a club with a secret code, a required style of dress, and elaborate rules for membership. The parents ask whether this rigidity is healthy. Which response reflects the chapter?
  • ✓A prominent feature of middle childhood groups is the code of rigid rules imposed on members, and conforming to the rules provides children with feelings of security and relieves them of the responsibility of making decisions
  • BRigid group rules at this age indicate an unhealthy need for control
  • CConformity to peer rules replaces family values permanently
  • DFormalized clubs are unusual in middle childhood and warrant assessment
Rationale

"One of the outstanding characteristics of middle childhood is the formation of formalized groups or clubs," and "A prominent feature of middle childhood groups is the code of rigid rules imposed on the members." "Conformity is the core of the group structure." Membership is marked by "secret codes, shared interests, special styles of dress, and special words," and "Conforming to the rules provides children with feelings of security and relieves them of the responsibility of making decisions."

Watch for

(C) overstates what conformity displaces, and the chapter is explicit that children "substitute conformity to a peer-group pattern for conformity to a family pattern while they are still too insecure to function independently" — a substitution that is temporary and partial.

19. A parent worries that their 11-year-old is adopting peer values that conflict with the family's. Which response reflects the chapter?
  • ✓Although the peer group is highly influential and necessary to normal development, parents are the primary influence in shaping personality, setting standards, and establishing values, and family values usually predominate when parental and peer value systems come into conflict
  • BPeer values reliably displace family values by this age
  • CChildren who test peer values have rejected family values permanently
  • DPeer influence is not significant until adolescence
Rationale

"Although the peer group is highly influential and necessary to normal child development, parents are the primary influence in shaping children's personalities, setting standards for behavior, and establishing value systems. Family values usually predominate when parental and peer value systems come into conflict." Children "may appear to reject parental values while testing the new values of the peer group," but "ultimately they retain and incorporate many parental values into their own value systems."

Watch for

(B) is the fear that brings parents to this conversation, and the chapter answers it directly. Note the related finding: children "need and want restrictions placed on their behavior" and "are uneasy if they succeed" in breaking down parental limits.

school-age peer groups, clubs, and team playgeneral
20. A 10-year-old on a losing soccer team is distraught about having let the team down. What does the chapter say team play teaches, and what caution applies?
  • ✓Team play teaches subordinating personal goals to group goals and the concept of interdependence, with each member's behavior open to public evaluation; however, winning is not universally given high value, and some cultures emphasize the game and consideration for one's companions rather than the outcome
  • BTeam play teaches that winning is the universal measure of success
  • CChildren this age are unaffected by team loss
  • DTeam play has no cognitive benefit at this age
Rationale

Each child on a team "is accountable to the other team members," each member's behavior "is open to public evaluation," and children "risk ostracism, ridicule, or scapegoating if they contribute to a team loss," learning "the concept of interdependence." Because "losing is often interpreted as failure," children go to great lengths to avoid public embarrassment — "However, winning is not universally given high value. Some cultures and subcultures emphasize the game and consideration for one's companions rather than the outcome."

Watch for

(D) misses a named benefit: team play requires children "to learn many complex rules, make judgments about those rules, plan strategies, and assess the strengths and weaknesses" of both teams, which is cognitive growth as well as social.

21. A nurse is explaining what children gain from peer interaction that they cannot gain from family. Which statement reflects the chapter?
  • ✓Because age-mates are not authorities, other children have a significant influence on decreasing the egocentric outlook, and children learn to argue, persuade, bargain, cooperate, and compromise to maintain friendships
  • BPeer interaction primarily reinforces what the family has already taught
  • CPeer relationships at this age are interchangeable and carry no lasting significance
  • DBest friendships are discouraged at this age in favor of larger group membership
Rationale

Through peers, children "discover that there are many occupations for fathers and mothers, more than one version of the same song, different rules for the same game." Because age-mates are not authorities, "other children have a significant influence on decreasing the egocentric outlook," and children "learn to argue, persuade, bargain, cooperate, and compromise to maintain friendships."

Watch for

(C) dismisses what the chapter treats as formative: school age "is the time when children have 'best friends' with whom they share secrets, private jokes, and adventures," and these dyadic relationships "seem to be important as a foundation for relationships in adulthood." Because "neither child has authority over the other," the children must work through differences themselves.

22. A school nurse is asked to define bullying for a staff in-service. Which definition reflects the chapter?
  • ✓Any recurring activity that intends to cause harm, distress, or control toward another individual where there is a perceived imbalance of power between the aggressor and the victim
  • BAny single act of physical aggression between two children of equal status
  • CAny conflict between children in which one child becomes upset
  • DPhysical aggression only, since verbal acts are classified separately
Rationale

Bullying is "any recurring activity that intends to cause harm, distress, or control toward another individual where there is a perceived imbalance of power between the aggressor(s) and the victim." It "can occur in varying degrees of severity in a physical, social, or emotional context."

Watch for

(B) drops both defining elements — the recurrence and the power imbalance — which is what separates bullying from an ordinary conflict between peers. (C) drops them too and would classify every playground dispute as bullying, which makes the term useless for the interventions that follow from it.

23. A school nurse is advising on where to concentrate supervision to reduce bullying. Which statement reflects the chapter?
  • ✓Although bullying can occur in any setting, it usually takes place in school hallways or on the playground where supervision is minimal but peers are present to witness the attack
  • BBullying occurs most often in the classroom, under direct teacher observation
  • CBullying occurs mainly when no other children are present
  • DLocation has no bearing on where bullying occurs
Rationale

"Although bullying can occur in any setting, it usually takes place in school hallways or on the playground where supervision is minimal but peers are present to witness the attack."

Watch for

(C) misses the audience element, which is part of why location matters: the attack requires witnesses to accomplish what it is for. That combination — minimal supervision plus present peers — is what makes hallways and playgrounds the characteristic setting, and it is why high levels of playground supervision is a named effective intervention.

bullying and cyberbullyinggeneral
24. A nurse compares traditional bullying with cyberbullying for a parent group. Which statement is accurate?
  • ✓Cyberbullying can be more harmful than traditional bullying because the attack can instantly reach a wider audience while allowing the bully to remain anonymous; traditional bullying risk is greater in elementary and middle school, while cyberbullying is more frequent in high school
  • BCyberbullying is less harmful than traditional bullying because there is no physical contact
  • CBoth forms peak in elementary school
  • DCyberbullying always identifies the perpetrator, making intervention easier
Rationale

Cyberbullying "involves an electronic medium to harm or bother another individual and can be more harmful than traditional bullying because the attack can instantly reach a wider audience, while allowing the bully to remain anonymous." Risk of traditional bullying is greater in elementary and middle school than high school, while cyberbullying is more frequent in high school. Up to 30% of school-age children engage in bullying, and 11% to 42% in cyberbullying as bully or victim.

Watch for

(B) is the intuition that physical harm is the measure of severity, and the chapter names the two features that make the electronic form worse instead: reach and anonymity.

27. A nurse is assessing an 11-year-old's self-concept. Which statement reflects the chapter's definitions?
  • ✓Self-concept is a conscious awareness of a variety of self-perceptions including physical characteristics, abilities, values, and self-ideals and one's idea of self in relation to others, and it includes body image, sexuality, and self-esteem
  • BSelf-concept and self-esteem are interchangeable terms
  • CBody image is determined solely by the opinions of significant others
  • DSelf-esteem consists only of positive qualities
Rationale

Self-concept is "a conscious awareness of a variety of self-perceptions, such as one's physical characteristics, abilities, values, and self-ideals, and one's idea of self in relation to others. It also includes one's body image, sexuality, and self-esteem." Children's self-concepts are "composed of their own critical self-assessments plus their interpretations of the opinions of others."

(C) drops the qualifier the chapter puts in the definition: body image is "what children think about their bodies and is influenced, but not solely determined, by significant others." (D) drops the other half of self-esteem, which "consists of both positive and negative qualities."

28. Parents discover their 9-year-old engaging in sex play with a same-age friend and ask the nurse whether this indicates a problem. Which response reflects the chapter?
  • ✓Many children experience some form of sex play during or before preadolescence as a response to normal curiosity, not as a result of love or sexual urge; it is incidental and transitory, and adverse emotional consequences depend largely on how the parents manage the behavior
  • BSex play at this age indicates prior sexual abuse in all cases
  • CExpressions of anger or disgust are the most effective parental response
  • DSex play at this age reflects a precocious sexual urge requiring evaluation
Rationale

"Many children experience some form of sex play during or before preadolescence as a response to normal curiosity, not as a result of love or sexual urge. Children are experimentalists by nature, and this play is incidental and transitory." "Any adverse emotional consequences or guilt feelings depend on how the parents manage the behavior and whether children view their actions as wrong in the eyes of significant persons, particularly their parents."

(C) names a response the chapter specifically identifies as harmful: parents who discourage exploration through "expressions of anger or disgust" communicate that children "should not engage in such activities, discourage questions about sex, and limit the sources of information."

42. A parent asks why a bicycle helmet matters so much when their child only rides on quiet streets. Which response reflects the chapter?
  • ✓Head injury is the major cause of bicycle-related fatalities, so the single most important aspect of bicycle safety is wearing a protective helmet; helmet use reduces head injury by 51%, serious head injury by 69%, and fatal head injury by 65%
  • BHelmets primarily prevent facial injury rather than head injury
  • CHelmets are needed only for riding in traffic
  • DHelmet effectiveness has not been quantified
Rationale

"Because head injury is the major cause of bicycle-related fatalities, the single most important aspect of bicycle safety is to encourage the rider to wear a protective helmet." Helmet use reduces head injury by 51%, serious head injury by 69%, facial injury by 33%, and fatal head injury by 65%. Hard-shelled helmets lined with expanded polystyrene "provide the best head protection," should fit securely without limiting vision or hearing, should carry a Consumer Product Safety Commission seal, and "should be replaced after any damage or crash."

Watch for

(B) names the smallest of the four reductions and misses the one that drives the recommendation. Note the fitting rule too: the helmet "should sit on top of the head in a level position and should not rock back and forth or from side to side," with the strap "fastened securely under the chin."

43. Parents say their 9-year-old refuses to wear a bicycle helmet, and neither parent wears one. Which information is most useful?
  • ✓Ninety percent of children reported they were likely to wear a helmet if their parent used a helmet, compared with only 38% of children reporting a likelihood to wear one if their parent did not

"Ninety percent of children reported they were likely to wear a helmet if their parent used a helmet, compared with only 38% of children reporting a likelihood to wear a helmet if their parent did not use a helmet." (B) reverses the relative size of the two effects. Both matter, but the gap produced by parental modeling — 90% against 38% — is far larger than the gap produced by state law, which makes the parents in this stem the intervention rather than the audience.
