anemia recognition and blood transfusion safetygeneral
1. A nurse is assessing a child with moderate iron deficiency anemia and notes pallor and fatigue but no cyanosis. How should this be interpreted?
  • ✓Expected, because anemia is caused by decreased hemoglobin rather than inadequate oxygen saturation of existing hemoglobin
  • BUnexpected, since cyanosis accompanies all significant anemias
  • CUnexpected, and pulse oximetry should be obtained immediately to confirm hypoxemia
  • DExpected only if the hemoglobin is above 10 g/dl
Rationale

The chapter states that "Anemia is caused by decreased levels of hemoglobin or RBCs, not inadequate oxygen saturation of existing hemoglobin," and that cyanosis "is typically not evident" (p. 1025).

Watch for

(B) is the option the chapter's own review question plants, and it substitutes the wrong mechanism — cyanosis requires a quantity of deoxygenated hemoglobin that an anemic child may not have enough hemoglobin to produce at all.

2. A nurse is sorting the signs of anemia by mechanism. Which findings belong to the increased-destruction group? Select all that apply.
Select all that apply · scored all or nothing
  • ✓Jaundice
  • ✓Icteric sclera
  • ✓Dark urine
  • ✓Splenomegaly
  • ECool skin with decreased peripheral pulses
Rationale

Box 28.2 sorts the signs of anemia into three etiologic groups — decreased production, increased destruction, and increased loss — placing jaundice, icteric sclera, dark urine, and splenomegaly in the destruction column (p. 1025).

Watch for

(E) belongs only to the increased-loss column, where volume depletion produces cool skin and diminished peripheral pulses; sorting each option into its group before answering is what makes an all-or-nothing item survivable here.

anemia recognition and blood transfusion safetygeneral
3. Fifteen minutes into a transfusion, a child develops chills, flank pain, and red urine. What should the nurse do first?
  • ✓Stop the transfusion
  • BSlow the transfusion rate and reassess in 15 minutes
  • CNotify the practitioner and await orders
  • DAdminister acetaminophen and continue the infusion
Rationale

The chapter gives a fixed action order for a suspected reaction: stop the transfusion, take vital signs, maintain a patent intravenous line with normal saline and new tubing, notify the practitioner, and do not restart until the child has been medically evaluated (p. 1030).

Watch for

(B) is the plausible-but-wrong option, because slowing keeps incompatible blood entering the circulation; the findings described — chills, flank pain, and red or black urine — are those of a hemolytic reaction, and ABO incompatibility "remains the most common cause of death from blood transfusion, and human error is usually responsible."

4. A nurse is starting a unit of packed red blood cells on a 4-year-old. Which action is correct?
  • ✓Administer the first 50 ml or initial 20% of the volume slowly and stay with the child
  • BBegin at the full rate and check on the child in 30 minutes
  • CPrime the line with 5% dextrose in water before connecting the blood
  • DReturn unused blood to the unit refrigerator for later use
Rationale

The chapter directs administering "the first 50 ml of blood or initial 20% of volume (whichever is smaller) slowly and stay with the child," with vital signs before, 15 minutes after initiation, hourly while infusing, and on completion (p. 1030).

Watch for

(C) violates the rule against infusing solutions other than normal saline in the line with red cells, and (D) violates the instruction not to store blood in a regular unit refrigerator — blood is used within 30 minutes of arrival and each unit infused within 4 hours.

5. A child receiving a transfusion develops dyspnea, a dry cough, distended neck veins, and hypertension. Which reaction is this, and what positioning helps?
  • ✓Circulatory overload, and the child is placed upright with the feet dependent to increase venous resistance
  • BHemolytic reaction, and the child is placed flat with legs elevated
  • CAllergic reaction, and the child is placed in a side-lying position
  • DAir embolism, and the child is placed in Trendelenburg position
Rationale

Table 28.2 lists precordial pain, dyspnea, rales, cyanosis, dry cough, distended neck veins, and hypertension as circulatory overload, directing the nurse to "Place child upright with feet in dependent position to increase venous resistance" (pp. 1027–1029).

Watch for

(B) names the most feared reaction, whose signs are chills, flank pain, and red or black urine rather than congestion; note that the distinguishing feature here is hypertension with distended neck veins, which points to too much volume rather than to incompatibility.

6. A 14-month-old drinks about 1.5 liters of whole milk daily, eats few solids, is above the ninetieth percentile for weight, and is pale. What should the nurse suspect?
  • ✓Iron deficiency anemia, since "milk baby" infants are overweight yet anemic
  • BIron deficiency is unlikely, since the child is well nourished and gaining weight
  • CLactose intolerance, since milk intake is high
  • DPhysiologic anemia, which resolves without intervention
Rationale

The chapter states that although infants with iron deficiency anemia "tend to be underweight," many "are overweight because of excessive milk ingestion (known as milk baby)," because milk is a poor iron source given almost to the exclusion of solids, and because "Increased fecal loss of blood occurs in 50% of iron deficient infants fed cow's milk" (p. 1031).

Watch for

(B) treats good weight gain as reassurance, which is exactly the bait; (D) confuses two entities the chapter separates explicitly — "Physiologic anemia should not be confused with iron deficiency anemia resulting from nutritional causes."

iron deficiency anemiageneral
7. Which laboratory finding confirms iron deficiency anemia?
  • ✓A total iron-binding capacity elevated above the normal range
  • BA total iron-binding capacity below the normal range
  • CA markedly elevated reticulocyte count
  • DDecreased blood pressure as an early sign
Rationale

The chapter states that "In iron deficiency anemia TIBC is elevated above the normal range," an elevation that "represents the body's compensatory mechanism to absorb more iron" (p. 1031).

Watch for

(B) reverses the compensation, which is the error this pair exists to catch — serum iron falls while binding capacity rises. (C) misstates the reticulocyte count, which is "usually normal or slightly reduced," and (D) misplaces decreased blood pressure, which is a late rather than early sign.

iron deficiency anemiageneral
8. A nurse is teaching a parent to give oral iron to a toddler. Which instruction is correct?
  • ✓Give it between meals with a citrus juice, and expect green-black stools
  • BGive it with milk to reduce stomach upset
  • CGive it with tea to improve absorption
  • DStop the iron as soon as the hemoglobin returns to normal
Rationale

The chapter directs giving iron between meals because free hydrochloric acid is greatest then, notes that ascorbic acid enhances absorption while tannins in tea bind nonheme iron, and describes green-black stool as an expected effect whose absence is a compliance clue (p. 1033).

Watch for

(D) stops too early — iron therapy continues "2 to 3 months after blood values normalize" to replenish stores; (B) and (C) each name a substance that impairs absorption, and milk is already the likely cause of the deficiency.

iron deficiency anemiageneral
9. A nurse is giving anticipatory guidance to the parents of a healthy 5-month-old about preventing iron deficiency. What should be included?
  • ✓Iron supplementation of 1 mg/kg/day by 4 to 6 months, breast milk or iron-fortified formula only for the first 12 months, and no fresh cow's milk before 12 months
  • BIntroduce fresh cow's milk at 6 months to increase calcium and iron intake
  • CNo supplementation is needed, since maternal iron stores last through the first year
  • DLimit all solid foods until 12 months to avoid displacing formula
Rationale

The chapter gives iron supplementation of 1 mg/kg/day by 4 to 6 months in full-term infants, breast milk or iron-fortified formula only for the first 12 months, formula limited to no more than 1 L/day to encourage iron-rich solids, and states that "Infants younger than 12 months old should not be given fresh cow's milk" (p. 1032).

Watch for

(C) overstates the duration of maternal stores, which "are adequate for the first 5 to 6 months in the full-term infant but for only about 2 to 3 months in premature infants or infants of multiple births"; universal screening for anemia occurs at approximately 12 months.

sickle cell disease pathophysiology and crisesgeneral
10. A nurse is teaching the parents of a child with sickle cell anemia what triggers sickling. Which conditions does the chapter name?
  • ✓Dehydration, acidosis, hypoxia, and temperature elevation
  • BDehydration, alkalosis, hyperoxia, and hypothermia
  • CInfection alone, since sickling is otherwise spontaneous
  • DPhysical exertion alone, since sickling is exercise induced
Rationale

The chapter states that "Under conditions of dehydration, acidosis, hypoxia, and temperature elevation, the relatively insoluble HgbS changes its molecular structure to form long, slender crystals" (p. 1034).

Watch for

(B) reverses three of the four, and the reason the list matters is that these four conditions are the whole prevention plan in one sentence; sickling is reversible under adequate oxygenation and hydration until repeated cycles make the cells irreversibly sickled.

sickle cell disease pathophysiology and crisesgeneral
11. Parents ask why their newborn with sickle cell anemia has no symptoms. What should the nurse explain?
  • ✓The newborn is protected by fetal hemoglobin, which is 60% to 80% at birth and falls rapidly during the first year
  • BSymptoms never appear before school age in this disease
  • CThe diagnosis is probably incorrect, since symptoms are present at birth
  • DSymptoms are absent because the spleen is still functioning normally
Rationale

The chapter states that "The newborn with SCD is generally asymptomatic because of the protective effect of HgbF (60% to 80%), but this rapidly decreases during the first year, so the child is at risk for sickle cell-related complications" (p. 1034).

Watch for

(D) names a real fact at the wrong time — the spleen is indeed functioning at birth, but it is progressively infarcted until "by the age of 5 years, the spleen has decreased in size and has been totally replaced by a fibrous mass (functional asplenia)," which is a later development rather than the reason for newborn protection.

sickle cell disease pathophysiology and crisesgeneral
12. A 3-year-old with sickle cell anemia develops sudden profound anemia, rapidly enlarging spleen, and hypotension. Which crisis is this?
  • ✓Splenic sequestration
  • BVasoocclusive crisis
  • CAplastic crisis
  • DHyperhemolytic crisis
Rationale

The chapter describes sequestration as "pooling of large quantities of blood, usually in the spleen... which causes a decrease in blood volume and ultimately shock," occurring "most commonly in children between 2 months and 5 years of age" and potentially resulting in "death from profound anemia and cardiovascular collapse" (p. 1037).

Watch for

(C) also produces profound anemia, but through diminished red cell production "usually triggered by infection with a virus (especially the human parvovirus)" rather than through pooling and shock — the enlarging spleen and hypotension are what separate them.

13. A child with sickle cell disease has a new pulmonary infiltrate on chest radiograph with chest pain, fever, and hypoxia. What is this, and why does it matter?
  • ✓Acute chest syndrome, which is clinically similar to pneumonia and may cause restrictive lung disease and pulmonary hypertension if repeated
  • BVasoocclusive crisis, which is the most common and non-life-threatening type
  • CAplastic crisis, which follows parvovirus infection
  • DSimple pneumonia, which requires no different management than in other children
Rationale

The chapter defines acute chest syndrome as "a new pulmonary infiltrate on chest x-ray of a SCD patient that may be accompanied by chest pain, fever, cough, tachypnea, wheezing, and hypoxia," clinically similar to pneumonia, with repeated episodes causing restrictive lung disease and pulmonary hypertension (p. 1037).

Watch for

(D) is dangerous because exchange transfusion is specifically used in this syndrome and after stroke; note that overwhelming infection with *Streptococcus pneumoniae* and *Haemophilus influenzae* type b "is the major cause of death in children with SCD under the age of 5 years."

14. A screening Sickledex test is positive in a 6-year-old. What does the nurse tell the family?
  • ✓Hemoglobin electrophoresis is needed, because the screening test cannot distinguish trait from disease
  • BThe child has sickle cell anemia, since the test is positive
  • CThe child has sickle cell trait, since disease would have presented earlier
  • DThe test should be repeated in 6 months before further workup
Rationale

The chapter states that the sickle turbidity test "can be performed on blood from a finger or heel stick and yields accurate results in 3 minutes," but that "Hemoglobin electrophoresis is necessary to distinguish between those children with the trait and those with the disease" (p. 1037).

Watch for

(B) and (C) each convert a screen into a diagnosis in opposite directions; note the separate rule that "Whenever sickle cells are found, diagnostic test results are usually positive for SCA, not sickle cell trait," so a smear showing sickled cells points to disease.

  • ✓Antibiotics to treat any existing infection

The chapter lists six objectives: bed rest, hydration through oral and intravenous therapy, electrolyte replacement, analgesia, blood replacement, and antibiotic therapy to treat any existing infection (p. 1039).

16. A child in sickle cell crisis is prescribed meperidine for pain. What should the nurse recognize?
  • ✓Meperidine is contraindicated, because normeperidine accumulates and causes seizures
  • BMeperidine is the preferred opioid in this disease
  • CMeperidine is acceptable if given with an antiemetic
  • DMeperidine is acceptable for doses under 24 hours
Rationale

The chapter states that meperidine "is not recommended" in sickle cell disease because the metabolite normeperidine accumulates and causes seizures (p. 1041).

Watch for

(C) and (D) both look for a safe window that does not exist for this drug in this population; the underlying point is that severe vasoocclusive pain requires adequate opioid analgesia, just not this opioid.

sickle cell disease management and preventiongeneral
17. A nurse is asked to apply oxygen to a child in vasoocclusive crisis who has a saturation of 97%. What should the nurse understand?
  • ✓Oxygen is of little value unless the child is hypoxic, does not reverse sickling, and with prolonged use decreases erythropoiesis
  • BOxygen should be applied to all children in crisis to prevent further sickling
  • COxygen reverses sickling within 30 minutes in most children
  • DOxygen is contraindicated in sickle cell disease at any saturation
Rationale

The chapter states that "Oxygen therapy is of little therapeutic value unless the patient is hypoxic," that it "is usually not effective in reversing sickling or reducing pain because the oxygen is not able to reach the enmeshed sickled RBCs through the clogged vessels," and that prolonged therapy aggravates the anemia (pp. 1039, 1041).

Watch for

(D) overcorrects into a contraindication the chapter does not state — oxygen is appropriate for a hypoxic child; what is wrong is treating it as a routine crisis intervention.

sickle cell disease management and preventiongeneral
18. The parents of an infant newly diagnosed with sickle cell anemia ask what will protect their child from infection. What should the nurse explain?
  • ✓Penicillin prophylaxis started by 2 months of age, plus pneumococcal, *Haemophilus influenzae* type b, and meningococcal vaccines
  • BVaccines alone, since prophylactic antibiotics promote resistance
  • CPenicillin prophylaxis beginning at school entry
  • DNo specific prophylaxis is needed until the spleen is removed
Rationale

The chapter states that "Penicillin prophylaxis is started by 2 months of age" and that pneumococcal, *Haemophilus influenzae* type b, and meningococcal vaccines are recommended because of functional asplenia (p. 1038).

Watch for

(D) waits for a splenectomy that does not occur in this disease, since the spleen is destroyed by repeated infarction rather than removed; parents should seek care for a fever of 101.3 degrees Fahrenheit or higher, an increase in spleen size, or severe pallor, and most experts recommend stopping prophylaxis after age 5.

sickle cell disease management and preventiongeneral
19. A 9-year-old with sickle cell disease wants to join a sports team and take a family trip. What should the nurse advise?
  • ✓Avoid contact sports if the spleen is enlarged, and avoid low-oxygen environments such as high altitudes or nonpressurized flights
  • BAvoid all physical activity permanently
  • CSports and travel carry no additional risk in this disease
  • DAvoid air travel entirely, including commercial pressurized flights
Rationale

The chapter directs avoiding contact sports if the spleen is enlarged "because rupture will cause massive internal hemorrhage," and avoiding "environments with low oxygen concentration, such as high altitudes or nonpressurized airplane flights," along with frequent rest breaks during activity (p. 1041).

Watch for

(B) removes activity the chapter accommodates with rest breaks; (D) overextends the restriction, since the named hazard is the nonpressurized flight. Note also that enuresis in this disease is a complication of impaired urine concentration rather than a behavior problem, so restricting nighttime fluids is the wrong response.

beta-thalassemia and aplastic anemiageneral
20. A nurse suggests checking the iron level of a child with beta-thalassemia "to make sure he is not anemic." How should this be corrected?
  • ✓These children are treated for iron overload with chelation, so the concern is too much iron rather than too little
  • BIron levels are not measurable in thalassemia
  • CThe suggestion is correct, since thalassemia is an iron deficiency disorder
  • DIron supplementation should be started empirically instead
Rationale

The chapter's trap note and its own review question turn on this reversal: children with thalassemia receive repeated transfusions and are managed with chelation for iron overload, so the direction of the problem is excess rather than deficiency (pp. 1045–1046).

Watch for

(C) and (D) both act on the deficiency assumption, and (D) would actively worsen the overload; the anemia in thalassemia arises from defective globin chain synthesis rather than from a lack of available iron.

beta-thalassemia and aplastic anemiageneral
21. A child with beta-thalassemia has excess iron storage without tissue injury. Which term applies?
  • ✓Hemosiderosis
  • BHemochromatosis
  • CHemolysis
  • DHemarthrosis
Rationale

The chapter defines hemosiderosis as excess iron storage "but without associated tissue injury," and hemochromatosis as excess storage "that results in cellular damage" (p. 1046).

Watch for

(B) is the same process one step further along, and the single word separating them is damage; the two are routinely used interchangeably in speech, which is why the chapter distinguishes them.

beta-thalassemia and aplastic anemiageneral
22. A bone marrow aspirate in a child with pancytopenia shows a hypoplastic marrow. Which diagnosis does this support?
  • ✓Aplastic anemia
  • BLeukemia
  • CIron deficiency anemia
  • DImmune thrombocytopenia
Rationale

The chapter contrasts the two directly: "In leukemia the bone marrow is hyperplastic (producing increased numbers of cells), whereas in aplastic anemia the bone marrow is hypoplastic... or aplastic" (p. 1026).

Watch for

(B) produces the same peripheral pancytopenia by a different marrow mechanism, which is why marrow examination rather than the blood count settles it; (D) shows normal marrow with normal or increased immature megakaryocytes.

beta-thalassemia and aplastic anemiageneral
23. A child is being evaluated for severe aplastic anemia. Which findings would meet the criteria the chapter names?
  • ✓Marrow cellularity under 25% plus at least two of neutrophils under 500, platelets under 20,000, and absolute reticulocytes under 40,000
  • BMarrow cellularity under 50% alone
  • CA hemoglobin under 7 g/dl alone
  • DPancytopenia of any degree with a normal marrow
Rationale

Camitta's criteria for severe aplastic anemia require marrow cellularity below 25% plus at least two of an absolute neutrophil count below 500/mm3, platelets below 20,000/mm3, and absolute reticulocytes below 40,000/mm3, with moderate disease defined by cellularity below 50% (p. 1048).

Watch for

(B) names the moderate threshold as though it were the severe one; (D) describes a picture inconsistent with the diagnosis, since a normal marrow points away from marrow failure.

hemophilia and von willebrand diseasegeneral
24. A 2-year-old boy has recurrent bleeding into the knees and ankles but no petechiae. Which disorder does this pattern suggest, and why do petechiae not appear?
  • ✓Hemophilia, because repair of small hemorrhages depends on platelet function rather than on clotting mechanisms
  • BImmune thrombocytopenia, because platelet counts are low
  • CVon Willebrand disease, because mucous membrane bleeding predominates
  • DHenoch-Schönlein purpura, because joints are involved
Rationale

The chapter states that "Petechiae are uncommon in persons with hemophilia because repair of small hemorrhages depends on platelet function, not on blood-clotting mechanisms," and names hemarthrosis "the most frequent form of internal bleeding," especially knees, elbows, and ankles (pp. 1051–1052).

Watch for

(C) names the right family of disorders with the wrong bleeding site, since von Willebrand disease is characterized by mucous membrane bleeding, most commonly nosebleeds; this one sentence about petechiae is the whole hemophilia-versus-thrombocytopenia discriminator.

25. A child has factor VIII activity of 0.5%. What bleeding pattern should the nurse expect?
  • ✓Spontaneous bleeding without trauma
  • BBleeding only with severe trauma or surgery
  • CBleeding only with moderate trauma
  • DNo abnormal bleeding
Rationale

Table 28.6 grades severity by factor activity: below 1% is severe with "Spontaneous bleeding without trauma," 1% to 5% is moderate with bleeding after trauma, and above 5% to 40% is mild with bleeding after severe trauma or surgery (p. 1051).

26. A child with hemophilia has an acute hemarthrosis of the knee. Which measures are appropriate? Select all that apply.
Select all that apply · scored all or nothing
  • ✓Factor replacement
  • ✓Rest, ice, compression, and elevation
  • ✓Active range-of-motion exercises once bleeding is controlled
  • ✓Acetaminophen for pain
  • EPassive range-of-motion exercises to preserve joint mobility
Rationale

The chapter names factor replacement as primary therapy, describes the RICE mnemonic as a supportive measure, directs that "Active range-of-motion exercises are best so that the patient can gauge his or her own pain tolerance," and names acetaminophen as the aspirin substitute (pp. 1052–1054).

(E) is forbidden by a Nursing Alert — "Passive range-of-motion exercises should never be part of an exercise regimen after an acute episode because the joint capsule could easily be stretched and bleeding could recur." 27. An adolescent girl has frequent nosebleeds and heavy menses, and her father has the same bleeding pattern. Which disorder does this suggest?
  • ✓Von Willebrand disease, which is autosomal dominant and affects both males and females
  • BHemophilia A, which is X-linked recessive
  • CHemophilia B, which is X-linked recessive
  • DImmune thrombocytopenia, which is acquired
Rationale

The chapter states that "Unlike hemophilia, vWD affects both males and females because its inheritance shows an autosomal dominant pattern," with mucous membrane bleeding, most commonly nosebleeds, as its characteristic feature (p. 1055).

(B) and (C) are both X-linked recessive, so an affected father transmitting to a daughter in this pattern does not fit; the two clues doing the work here are the sex distribution and the bleeding site.

hemophilia and von willebrand diseasegeneral
28. A child with mild hemophilia A is scheduled for a dental extraction. Which treatment does the chapter identify as the treatment of choice?
  • ✓Desmopressin, if the child responds with a threefold to fourfold rise in factor VIII activity
  • BCryoprecipitate, which remains the standard for factor VIII deficiency
  • CAspirin for procedural pain
  • DFresh frozen plasma as the primary replacement
Rationale

The chapter names desmopressin "the treatment of choice in mild hemophilia and vWD (types I and IIA only)" if the child responds, with a threefold to fourfold rise in factor VIII activity expected, noting that patients with moderate deficiency do not benefit because the goal is to raise the level at least 30% (p. 1052).

(B) is ruled out by a Drug Alert — "Cryoprecipitate is no longer recommended for use in treating factor VIII deficiency" — because it cannot be treated to safely eliminate hepatitis or human immunodeficiency virus; (C) names a drug contraindicated in this disease.

immune thrombocytopenia and henoch-schonlein purpurageneral
29. A 4-year-old develops petechiae and easy bruising 2 weeks after a viral illness, with a platelet count of 18,000/mm3 and a normal bone marrow. What is the likely diagnosis?
  • ✓Immune thrombocytopenia
  • BAplastic anemia
  • CHemophilia
  • DDisseminated intravascular coagulation
Rationale

The chapter describes immune thrombocytopenia as an acquired disorder with thrombocytopenia and "normal bone marrow with a normal or increased number of immature megakaryocytes," usually presenting 1 to 4 weeks after a viral illness with peak incidence between ages 1 and 6, and notes that "Symptomatic bleeding does not usually occur until the platelet count is lower than 20,000/mm3" (p. 1055).

Watch for

(B) shares the low platelet count but has a hypoplastic marrow and involves all three cell lines; the normal marrow in the stem is what separates a destruction problem from a production problem.

immune thrombocytopenia and henoch-schonlein purpurageneral
31. A child with immune thrombocytopenia is actively bleeding. Which treatment is inappropriate at this moment?
  • ✓Anti-D antibody, because the platelet count does not rise until approximately 48 hours after infusion
  • BIntravenous immunoglobulin
  • CPrednisone
  • DPlatelet transfusion for severe hemorrhage
Rationale

The chapter states that with anti-D antibody the platelet count rises "approximately 48 hours after infusion; therefore it is not appropriate therapy for patients who are actively bleeding" (p. 1055).

Watch for

(B) and (C) are the two agents that pull hardest, because both are standard options in this disorder and neither carries the delay that disqualifies the keyed answer here; anti-D antibody otherwise has real advantages, a single dose over 5 to 10 minutes and significantly less expense than immunoglobulin, which is exactly why the timing caveat is worth holding. None of the three "are curative therapies," and the disorder is otherwise managed supportively.

32. A 5-year-old has symmetric purpura on the buttocks and lower extremities, knee and ankle arthritis, colicky abdominal pain, and a normal platelet count. What is the likely diagnosis, and what is the most serious long-term concern?
  • ✓Henoch-Schönlein purpura, with renal involvement occurring in up to 50% of affected children
  • BImmune thrombocytopenia, with intracranial hemorrhage
  • CHemophilia, with joint destruction
  • DDisseminated intravascular coagulation, with organ failure
Rationale

The chapter describes Henoch-Schönlein purpura as a nonthrombocytopenic purpura with arthritis, nephritis, and abdominal pain, with "a symmetric purpura that involves the buttocks and lower extremities," and states that "Renal involvement occurs in up to 50% of affected children and is potentially the most serious long-term complication" (pp. 1057–1059).

Watch for

(B) is the confusable partner, and the normal platelet count is the discriminator — the purpura here comes from vasculitis rather than from too few platelets. Increased immunoglobulin A levels are a frequent finding, and children with nephritis need long-term follow-up.

neutropenia in childrengeneral
33. A 3-year-old has an absolute neutrophil count of 1200/mm3. How should this be classified?
  • ✓Neutropenia, since the threshold for children older than 1 year is below 1500/mm3
  • BSevere neutropenia, since the count is below 1500/mm3
  • CNormal, since the threshold is below 1000/mm3 at all ages
  • DNormal, since only counts below 500/mm3 are abnormal
Rationale

The chapter defines neutropenia as an absolute neutrophil count below 1000/mm3 in infants 2 weeks to 1 year and below 1500/mm3 in children older than 1 year, with severe neutropenia below 500/mm3 "with an increased risk of life-threatening infection" (p. 1057).

Watch for

(B) jumps to the severe tier, which is a threefold lower number and carries a different level of concern; the count is calculated by multiplying the total white blood cell count by the percentage of neutrophils and bands in the differential.

  • DWithhold immunizations permanently
Rationale

The chapter states that children with chronic benign neutropenia "have normal cellular immunity, therefore they should receive their routine childhood immunizations" (p. 1057).

Watch for

(C) applies the live-vaccine caution that belongs to true immunodeficiency; the distinction is that this disorder affects neutrophil numbers without impairing the cellular immunity that determines live vaccine safety.

Rationale

The chapter directs seeking medical attention for a fever of 101.3 degrees Fahrenheit or higher or if skin lesions develop, and notes that "Oral ulcerations and skin infections are the most common manifestation of chronic benign neutropenia" (p. 1057).

Watch for

(B) raises a threshold that recurs throughout this chapter — the same 101.3 degrees appears for postsplenectomy patients, sickle cell patients on penicillin prophylaxis, and neutropenic children; families are also taught to avoid large indoor crowds and ill contacts.

37. A 6-month-old born to a mother with human immunodeficiency virus has a positive antibody test. How should this be interpreted?
  • ✓The result likely reflects maternal antibodies, which may persist up to 18 months, so polymerase chain reaction for proviral DNA is needed
  • BThe infant is infected, since the antibody test is definitive at any age
  • CThe infant is uninfected, since antibody tests are unreliable in infancy
  • DThe test should be repeated at 24 months before any further evaluation
Rationale

The chapter states that in children younger than 18 months antibody tests are positive from maternal antibodies, that "Maternal antibodies may persist in the infant for up to 18 months," and directs using "the HIV polymerase chain reaction (PCR) for detection of proviral DNA" (p. 1062).

Watch for

(B) treats a maternal marker as an infant diagnosis, which would misclassify most exposed but uninfected infants; (D) delays a diagnosis that can be made now with the correct test.

38. A nurse is explaining how the human immunodeficiency virus causes disease. Which statement is accurate?
  • ✓It primarily infects CD4+ T cells, suppressing cell-mediated immunity and producing opportunistic infection, and also causes B-cell dysfunction
  • BIt primarily infects B cells, suppressing humoral immunity alone
  • CIt primarily infects neutrophils, producing bacterial infection
  • DIt destroys red cells, producing hemolytic anemia
Rationale

The chapter states that the virus "primarily infects a specific subset of T lymphocytes, the CD4+ T cells," rendering them dysfunctional, that suppression of cell-mediated immunity brings opportunistic infection, and that the virus also causes B-cell dysfunction suppressing humoral immunity (p. 1061).

Watch for

(B) names only the second and later effect; the ordering matters clinically, because the opportunistic infections that define the illness follow from the cell-mediated arm failing first.

  • ✓Recurrent bacterial infections, parotitis, lymphoid interstitial pneumonitis, and early neurologic deterioration characterize children but are rare in adults
  • BKaposi sarcoma is more common in children than in adults
  • CReceptive language is more impaired than expressive language in children

Rationale

The chapter states that recurrent bacterial infections, parotitis, lymphoid interstitial pneumonitis, and early progressive neurologic deterioration "are characteristic of children with HIV infection but are rarely seen in affected adults" (p. 1061).

Watch for

(B) reverses the finding, since Kaposi sarcoma is "found in fewer than 1% of affected children"; (C) reverses the language pattern, since expressive language is more frequently impaired; and (D) reverses the course, since "A more rapid progression of disease tend to occur in perinatally infected children."

40. A pregnant woman with human immunodeficiency virus asks about the risk to her baby. What should the nurse tell her?
  • ✓With combination antiretroviral therapy, elective cesarean delivery, and avoidance of breastfeeding, perinatal transmission falls to less than 1% to 2%
  • BTransmission is unavoidable and occurs in all pregnancies
  • CTransmission risk is unaffected by treatment during pregnancy
  • DBreastfeeding reduces transmission by providing maternal antibodies
Rationale

The chapter states that untreated transmission rates were 12% to 30% in the United States and Europe, and that with combination antiretroviral therapy, elective cesarean delivery, and avoidance of breastfeeding, perinatal transmission fell to "less than 1% to 2%" (p. 1061).

Watch for

(D) reverses the role of breastfeeding, which is a transmission route rather than a protective one here; note that "Perinatal transmission accounts for almost all HIV infections in young children in the United States," which is why this counseling matters so much.
