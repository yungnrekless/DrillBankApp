1. A 7-year-old boy is referred for short stature. He grew normally through his first year and has since followed a curve below the 3rd percentile. He is chubby, his body proportions are normal for his age, and he looks younger than 7. Intelligence testing is normal. Which feature most helps the nurse distinguish growth hormone deficiency from failure to thrive?
  • ✓His well-nourished, even chubby, appearance
  • BHis height below the 3rd percentile
  • CHis normal intelligence testing
  • DHis normal skeletal proportions
Rationale

"Height may be stunted more than weight because, with good nutrition, these children can become overweight or even obese," and the chapter names the consequence outright: "Their well-nourished appearance is an important diagnostic clue to differentiation from other disorders such as failure to thrive" (p. 1173).

Watch for

(B) is the reflex pick because it is the finding that brought the child in, but a height below the 3rd percentile is where every short-stature workup starts and separates nothing; (D) is equally true of a malnourished child, since "Skeletal proportions are normal for the age" in GH deficiency but proportion is not what undernutrition disturbs first.

2. Two 9-year-old girls are evaluated for short stature. One has a markedly delayed bone age; the other's bone age matches her chronologic age. How should the nurse interpret the two films?
  • ✓The girl whose bone age is not delayed has the more concerning finding
  • BThe girl with the profoundly delayed bone age has a confirmed endocrine diagnosis
  • CNeither film adds anything, because bone age is not part of a growth workup
  • DThe delayed bone age means that girl's short stature is now irreversible
Rationale

"a bone age that is not delayed in a short child is of much greater concern and may, in fact, be of some diagnostic value under certain circumstances" (p. 1175). Most conditions that slow linear growth also retard skeletal maturation, so a delayed film is the expected and nonspecific result.

Watch for

(B) is the intuitive inversion, because an abnormal test feels more diagnostic than a normal one, and Box 31.5 forecloses it: "Observation of even a profoundly delayed bone age is never diagnostic or even indicative of a specific diagnosis" (p. 1175). (D) reverses the meaning entirely, since delay indicates the short stature is to some extent still recoverable "because linear growth will continue until epiphyseal fusion is complete."

3. A 10-year-old is beginning recombinant growth hormone. Which combination of instruction and expectation should the nurse give the family?
  • ✓Give the injection at bedtime, and expect the fastest growth in the first year with the child still remaining shorter than peers
  • BGive the injection at bedtime, and expect the child to reach an average adult height for the family
  • CGive the injection with breakfast, and expect growth to accelerate further with each year of treatment
  • DGive the injection at bedtime, and expect therapy to continue to age 18 regardless of bone age
Rationale

The Drug Alert states that "GH is most effective when it is administered at bedtime," because physiologic release normally follows "during the first 45 to 90 minutes after the onset of sleep" (p. 1176). "Maximum growth velocity occurs in the first year of treatment," after which "growth rates decline" (pp. 1175–1176), and the honest ceiling is that "individuals who receive treatment remain shorter than their peers" (p. 1176).

Watch for

(B) gets the timing right and the promise wrong, which is the harder error to catch, because a family hearing the drug's name expects a normal adult height and the nurse who confirms it has set up the disappointment the chapter warns about; (C) inverts the growth-velocity curve, which is steepest at the start.

4. An adolescent girl has been on growth hormone for 5 years. Her growth rate is now under 1 inch per year and her bone age is 14.5 years. Her 15-year-old brother, also on growth hormone, has a bone age of 14 years. Which statement is correct?
  • ✓The girl meets a criterion to stop therapy that her brother does not yet meet
  • BBoth of them now meet the criteria to stop therapy
  • CNeither meets the criteria, because bone age is not used in this decision
  • DThe brother meets the criterion, since boys finish growing earlier than girls
Rationale

"Growth rates of less than 1 inch per year and a bone age of more than 14 years in girls and more than 16 years in boys are often used as criteria to stop GH therapy" (p. 1176). Her bone age is past 14; his is well short of 16. The decision itself is shared: "The child, family, and health care team make the decision jointly to stop GH therapy."

Watch for

(D) is the reflex error because girls are taught to mature earlier, so a reader who remembers the direction without the numbers attaches the earlier stopping point to the wrong child; (B) applies one threshold to both sexes, which is the same swap in a different shape.

5. Two patients have growth hormone-secreting pituitary adenomas: an 8-year-old and a 30-year-old. The child is growing rapidly in height with proportional weight gain and an enlarging head circumference. The adult has a broadening jaw, enlarged hands, and separating teeth, with no change in height. What accounts for the difference?
  • ✓The epiphyses are still open in the child and have closed in the adult
  • BThe child's tumor secretes a larger quantity of growth hormone
  • CThe adult's tumor is also secreting thyroid-stimulating hormone
  • DThe adult has had the tumor for a longer period
Rationale

"Excess GH before closure of the epiphyseal shafts results in proportional overgrowth of the long bones," while "If oversecretion of GH occurs after epiphyseal closure, growth is in the transverse direction, producing a condition known as acromegaly" (p. 1176). Gigantism also produces "Proportional enlargement of head circumference."

Watch for

(D) pulls hardest because acromegaly genuinely is diagnosed late — patients are identified "as much as 10 years after their symptoms first appear" (p. 1177) — so the duration in the option is a true fact recruited to explain the wrong thing. The single variable is whether the growth plates are open.

6. An adolescent diagnosed with acromegaly after epiphyseal closure asks whether treatment will bring his height back down. Which response is accurate?
  • ✓Treatment will not reduce your height, but it will prevent further excess growth
  • BTreatment will gradually return your height toward your predicted adult height
  • CYour height can be reduced, but only with transsphenoidal resection of the tumor
  • DHeight is never affected in acromegaly, so there is nothing to reduce
Rationale

"Although medical treatment of acromegaly will not reduce a patient's height, it will prevent further excess growth" (p. 1177).

Watch for

(C) is the plausible-but-adjacent version, and it is the most convincing wrong answer because it names the right operation: "Transsphenoid surgery (TSS) is the most common surgical treatment for pituitary adenoma" (p. 1177). Naming a real procedure makes a false promise sound informed.

7. A 7-year-old girl and a 7-year-old boy are each being evaluated for central precocious puberty. Which statement should guide the nurse's expectations for the two workups?
  • ✓The boy is far more likely to have a CNS lesion, but both children still require a brain MRI
  • BThe girl is far more likely to have a CNS lesion, so only she requires a brain MRI
  • CNeither child needs imaging if the rest of the physical examination is normal
  • DThe boy is far more likely to have a CNS lesion, so imaging is reserved for boys
Rationale

"CPP occurs more frequently in girls and is usually idiopathic, with 95% demonstrating no causative factor," whereas "A CNS insult or structural abnormality occurs in more than 75% of boys with CPP" (p. 1178). The imaging rule is nevertheless universal: "In every case, an MRI of the brain should be performed to assess for hypothalamic brain tumor."

Watch for

(D) is more tempting than (B)'s outright inversion, because it gets the epidemiology exactly right and then draws the wrong conclusion from it. Probability shapes the level of suspicion, not the imaging rule, and the phrase the chapter uses is "In every case."

8. The mother of an 8-year-old girl with central precocious puberty asks how to manage her daughter's development. Which statements should the nurse include? Select all that apply.
Select all that apply · scored all or nothing
  • ✓Dress and activities should match her chronologic age rather than her physical development
  • ✓Her sexual interest and mental age are those of an 8-year-old
  • ✓She may be fertile, even at this age
  • DEstrogen-containing birth control pills should be started now as a precaution
  • ✓The hardest period is usually the school years before adolescence
Rationale

"Dress and activities for the physically precocious child should be appropriate to the chronologic age"; "Sexual interest is not usually advanced beyond the child's chronologic age, and parents need to understand that the child's mental age is congruent with the chronologic age"; "the nurse should emphasize to parents that the child may be fertile"; and "The most difficult time for the child is usually the school years before adolescence. After puberty, physical differences from peers are no longer present" (p. 1178).

Watch for

(D) is the one wrong option, and it is wrong for a physiologic reason rather than a moral one: "hormonal forms of birth control, such as estrogen pills, prematurely initiate epiphyseal closure, resulting in stunted linear growth" (p. 1178). The chapter also states that "Usually no form of contraception is necessary unless the child is sexually active," so the option fails twice.

diabetes insipidus and siadh in childrenrecognizing diabetes insipidus
9. A 5-month-old is brought in for persistent irritability and crying. The mother reports the baby quiets when given a bottle of plain water but keeps fussing after formula feedings. The infant has lost weight and soaks through an unusual number of diapers. Which condition does this picture suggest?
  • ✓Diabetes insipidus
  • BSyndrome of inappropriate antidiuretic hormone
  • CType 1 diabetes mellitus
  • DCongenital hypothyroidism
Rationale

"In the infant the initial symptom is irritability that is relieved with feedings of water but not milk" (p. 1178), and the chapter repeats the clue under nursing care management: "persistent irritability and crying in an infant that is relieved only by bottle-feedings of water" (p. 1179). The infant is also "prone to severe dehydration, electrolyte imbalance, hyperthermia, azotemia, and potential circulatory collapse."

Watch for

(C) pulls because polyuria, thirst, and weight loss belong to both diseases, and the water-not-milk detail is the only thing in the stem that separates them. Teaching families that "DI is a different condition from DM" is itself a stated nursing action (p. 1179), which is a fair signal that the confusion is expected.

10. A school-age child is undergoing a water deprivation test. Which finding requires the nurse to terminate the fluid restriction?
  • ✓Weight loss of 4% of body weight
  • BUrine that remains dilute despite the restriction
  • CThe child complaining of intense thirst
  • DA urine output that exceeds the measured intake
Rationale

"A weight loss between 3% and 5% indicates significant dehydration and requires termination of the fluid restriction" (p. 1179). The test requires "strict monitoring of fluid intake and urinary output, measurement of urine concentration (specific gravity or osmolality), and frequent weight checks."

Watch for

(B) and (D) are the abnormal results the test exists to produce, since in DI "fluid restriction has little or no effect on urine formation but causes weight loss from dehydration" (p. 1178) — they are findings to record, not reasons to stop. Thirst is expected throughout, and the Nursing Alert warns that small children need close observation "to prevent them from drinking, even from toilet bowls, flower vases, or other unlikely sources of fluid" (p. 1179).

11. A child with polyuria and polydipsia is given a test dose of aqueous vasopressin, and the urine osmolality does not change. What does this result indicate?
  • ✓Nephrogenic diabetes insipidus, in which the renal tubules do not respond to the hormone
  • BCentral diabetes insipidus, in which too little antidiuretic hormone is produced
  • CSyndrome of inappropriate antidiuretic hormone
  • DCompulsive water drinking from a psychogenic cause
Rationale

"Unresponsiveness to exogenous vasopressin usually indicates nephrogenic DI" (p. 1179), a form described as "a rare hereditary disorder affecting primarily males and caused by unresponsiveness of the renal tubules to the hormone" (p. 1178). The mirror result confirms the other form: "a rise in urine osmolality after vasopressin administration indicates central DI."

Watch for

(B) is the mirror error and names the commoner disease, so the reflex is to answer with the diagnosis rather than with the test result; the test exists precisely because hormone replacement works in one form and is useless in the other. (D) is a genuine differential the chapter raises, but it is settled by psychologic consultation, not by the vasopressin response.

12. A parent of a child taking twice-daily DDAVP calls to report that the child urinates more than usual in the early evening, before the bedtime dose. What should the nurse explain?
  • ✓Some breakthrough urination in the evening is deliberate, guarding against overmedication
  • BThe dose is too low and should be increased tonight
  • CThe child most likely missed the morning dose
  • DThe medication should be changed to the parenteral route
Rationale

Breakthrough "urination is allowed during the evening hours as a precaution against overmedication" (p. 1179). The twice-daily schedule is timed at bedtime "to allow the child to sleep through the night and in the morning to allow fewer interruptions in the school day."

Watch for

(B) is the intuitive answer, because the symptom the drug was given to control has reappeared, and it is the one that causes harm: "The signs of overmedication are similar to manifestations associated with the syndrome of inappropriate ADH (SIADH)" (p. 1179). Over-treated diabetes insipidus becomes the opposite disorder.

13. Two days after a craniotomy, a child develops a serum sodium of 121 mEq/L, rising urine osmolality, weight gain, nausea, and irritability. Which is the immediate management of choice?
  • ✓Fluid restriction
  • BIntranasal DDAVP
  • CFree water boluses through the nasogastric tube
  • DA rapid infusion of hypertonic saline
Rationale

"Fluid restriction is the immediate management of choice" in SIADH (p. 1179). "Excess ADH causes free water to be reabsorbed from the kidneys. As increased free water circulates, serum osmolality goes down, and urine osmolality inappropriately increases," and the danger is cerebral: "When hyponatremia occurs acutely, swelling of the brain occurs."

Watch for

(B) is the mirror error, since DDAVP is the diabetes insipidus treatment (p. 1179) and giving more antidiuretic hormone here deepens the retention; (D) names a real therapy but skips a step, because "Severe SIADH may require hypertonic saline infusion that is only given in the hospital setting under close supervision" — it follows fluid restriction rather than replacing it.

14. The parents of an 11-year-old newly diagnosed with acquired hypothyroidism ask whether their daughter will have lasting intellectual problems. Which response is accurate?
  • ✓Intellectual disability is not associated with hypothyroidism acquired at this age, because brain growth is nearly complete by 2 to 3 years
  • BIntellectual disability is likely unless replacement begins within about two weeks
  • CThe intellectual outcome depends entirely on how high the TSH is at diagnosis
  • DAbout half of children with any form of hypothyroidism have intellectual disability
Rationale

"Because brain growth is nearly complete by 2 to 3 years of age, intellectual disability and neurologic sequelae are not associated with juvenile hypothyroidism" (p. 1180). Box 31.7 supplies the reason: thyroid hormone "Affects central nervous system development and cerebration during first 2 to 3 years," a window that has already closed for this child.

Watch for

(B) moves the consequence of the congenital form onto an acquired case, which is the stage swap this pair is built on: "If left untreated, congenital hypothyroidism causes mental retardation" (p. 1180), and that is why newborn screening exists.

15. A child with severe acquired hypothyroidism is started on L-thyroxine, and the dose is to be increased in steps over 4 to 8 weeks. A parent asks why the full dose is not simply given now. Which explanation is correct?
  • ✓Gradual increases avoid producing symptoms of hyperthyroidism
  • BThe thyroid gland needs time to begin producing its own hormone again
  • CA full dose would suppress the pituitary permanently
  • DThe drug must be titrated to the child's weight, which takes several weeks to establish
Rationale

"In children with severe symptoms, the restoration thyroid function is achieved more gradually with administration of increasing amounts of L-thyroxine over a period of 4 to 8 weeks. This is done to avoid symptoms of hyperthyroidism" (p. 1180).

Watch for

(B) sounds physiologic and describes the wrong organ's recovery — that is the rationale for tapering exogenous steroids so the pituitary can resume secreting ACTH (p. 1188), not for ramping thyroid replacement. (D) invents a titration reason the chapter does not give.

16. A 13-year-old is found on routine examination to have a symmetric, firm, nontender enlargement of the thyroid gland. What can the nurse conclude from the enlargement alone?
  • ✓Nothing about thyroid function, since a goiter occurs with deficient, excessive, or normal hormone secretion
  • BThat the child is hypothyroid
  • CThat the child is hyperthyroid
  • DThat the gland is malignant until proven otherwise
Rationale

"A goiter is an enlargement or hypertrophy of the thyroid gland. It may occur with deficient (hypothyroid), excessive (hyperthyroid), or normal (euthyroid) TH secretion" (p. 1181). In chronic lymphocytic thyroiditis specifically, "Most children are euthyroid, but some display symptoms of hypothyroidism. Others have signs suggestive of hyperthyroidism."

(B) is the reflex pick because goiter is most often taught beside iodine deficiency; (D) takes only half of the nodule rule, which has two halves that must be kept together: "Nodules are rarely caused by a cancerous tumor but always require evaluation" (p. 1181).

18. A 13-year-old started methimazole for Graves disease six weeks ago and calls the clinic with a sore throat and a temperature of 38.9°C. What is the nurse's correct response?
  • ✓Have the child examined now, because sore throat and fever accompany agranulocytosis
  • BAdvise acetaminophen and fluids, and call back if there is no improvement in three days
  • CReassure the family that this is an expected effect of the drug in the first months
  • DInstruct the family to double the next dose, because fever raises thyroid hormone demand
Rationale

"The most serious side effect of ATDs is agranulocytosis (severe leukopenia), which generally occurs within the initial weeks or months of therapy. It is usually accompanied by a sore throat and fever" (p. 1182). The Drug Alert repeats it: because these symptoms "accompany the grave complication of leukopenia, these children should be examined if such symptoms occur" (p. 1183). Treatment is "immediate discontinuation of the drug and the administration of antibiotics and glucocorticoids until symptoms resolve."

Watch for

(B) is the advice a sore throat earns in any other child, which is exactly why the alert is printed twice in two pages; (C) inverts the meaning of the timing, since the first weeks to months are when the risk is highest, not when the symptom is harmless.

graves disease and thyroidectomy care in adolescentsantithyroid drug therapy
19. An adolescent scheduled for subtotal thyroidectomy has been taking oral iodine for several weeks preoperatively and admits she stopped because of the taste. Which risk should the nurse address first?
  • ✓Thyroid crisis, which sudden discontinuation can precipitate
  • BDelayed wound healing after the operation
  • CHypocalcemia before the surgery
  • DLoss of the preparation's antibacterial effect
Rationale

"Compliance with iodine therapy is essential to avoid the danger of thyroid crisis after sudden discontinuation" (p. 1183). Thyroid storm "may be precipitated by acute infection, surgical emergency, or discontinuation of antithyroid therapy," and presents as "acute onset of severe irritability and restlessness, vomiting, diarrhea, hyperthermia, hypertension, severe tachycardia, and prostration," with "rapid progression to delirium, coma, and death" (p. 1182).

Watch for

(C) names the right complication at the wrong moment, since hypoparathyroidism follows removal of the gland rather than preceding it (p. 1183). The palatability problem has its own answer and it is not stopping the drug: mix the preparation "with a strong-tasting fruit juice, such as grape or punch flavors," and give it "through a straw."

21. On the first day after thyroidectomy, a 15-year-old who was cheerful before surgery is now anxious and tearful and says she feels low. Vital signs are stable and the dressing is dry. What should the nurse suspect?
  • ✓Early hypocalcemia from hypoparathyroidism

  • CDamage to the laryngeal nerve
  • DThyroid storm

22. A child's laboratory results show a decreased serum calcium with an increased serum phosphorus. Which group of findings should the nurse expect?
  • ✓Muscle cramps, tingling of the hands and feet, and a positive Trousseau sign
  • BMuscle weakness, constipation, and confusion
  • CPolyuria, renal colic, and hypertension
  • DSpontaneous fractures and vague bone pain
Rationale

"The diagnosis of hypoparathyroidism is made on the basis of clinical manifestations associated with decreased serum calcium and increased serum phosphorus" (p. 1184). "Muscle cramps are an early symptom, progressing to numbness, stiffness, and tingling in the hands and feet. A positive Chvostek or Trousseau sign or laryngeal spasms may be present" (p. 1183).

Watch for

(B), (C), and (D) are all genuine findings of the opposite disorder — Box 31.9 lists weakness and fatigability, constipation, confusion and delusions, polyuria and polydipsia, renal colic, hypertension, and spontaneous fractures under hyperparathyroidism (p. 1184). Low calcium excites the neuromuscular system; high calcium sedates it.

23. A child with chronic renal failure develops secondary hyperparathyroidism. Which treatment should the nurse expect?
  • ✓Oral calcium salts, high-dose vitamin D, and a low-phosphorus diet
  • BMeasures to lower the serum calcium, since hyperparathyroidism causes hypercalcemia
  • CImmediate parathyroidectomy
  • DFluid restriction with a high-phosphorus diet
Rationale

Because the underlying renal disorder is irreversible, "treatment is aimed at raising serum calcium levels to inhibit the stimulatory effect of low levels on the parathyroid. This includes oral administration of calcium salts, high doses of vitamin D to enhance calcium absorption, a low-phosphorus diet, and administration of a phosphorus-mobilizing aluminum hydroxide to reduce phosphate absorption" (p. 1184).

Watch for

(B) is the reflex inversion and the most harmful option, because the name of the disease points at hypercalcemia while the secondary form is being driven by a low calcium the treatment must raise; (C) belongs to the primary form, where "The most common cause of primary hyperparathyroidism is adenoma of the gland."

25. A toddler with meningococcemia develops hypotension, cold clammy skin, and spreading purpura despite adequate antibiotic therapy, and adrenal crisis is suspected. What is the correct first action?
  • ✓Institute cortisol replacement immediately, since improvement with cortisol confirms the diagnosis
  • BDraw plasma cortisol and ACTH levels and wait for the results before giving any steroid
  • CGive a bolus of dextrose-free normal saline and reassess in one hour
  • DObtain an adrenal ultrasound to look for hemorrhage into the gland
Rationale

"There is no rapid, definitive test for confirmation of acute adrenocortical insufficiency," and cortisol and ACTH levels are "too time consuming to be practical for initial diagnosis" (p. 1186). "Because there is no real danger in administering a cortisol preparation for a short period, it is important to institute treatment immediately. Improvement with cortisol therapy confirms the diagnosis."

Watch for

(B) is the option that sounds rigorous and is the one the chapter rules out; the same permission is restated for families as "there is no physical harm in treating for suspected adrenal insufficiency that is not present, but the consequence of not treating acute adrenal insufficiency can be fatal" (p. 1191). (C) gets the fluid right and the composition wrong, because "Normal saline containing 5% glucose is given parenterally to replace lost fluid, electrolytes, and glucose" — cortisol deficiency causes hypoglycemia, so the resuscitation fluid treats two problems at once.

26. A school-age child with Addison disease develops a fever and an ear infection. What should the nurse teach the parents about the hydrocortisone dose?
  • ✓Triple the dose, to meet the body's increased need for glucocorticoids
  • BKeep the dose unchanged, since this is a minor illness
  • CHalve the dose, because fever already raises endogenous cortisol
  • DHold the dose until the fever resolves
Rationale

"During stressful situations (e.g., fever, infection, emotional upset, or surgery), the dosage must be tripled to accommodate the body's increased need for glucocorticoids," and "Failure to meet this requirement will precipitate an acute crisis" (p. 1187).

Watch for

(B) is the quietest wrong answer and the commonest one, because nothing in the stem looks like an emergency yet and an ear infection is an ordinary childhood event. The chapter sets the threshold lower than it feels by naming emotional upset in the same list as surgery, and (C) inverts the physiology entirely: the failing adrenal is what cannot raise cortisol under stress.

27. A parent calls to say their child with chronic adrenocortical insufficiency has vomited the morning hydrocortisone dose twice and is now sleepy. What should the nurse advise?
  • ✓Treat this as an emergency, giving the prefilled hydrocortisone injection if trained and seeking care immediately
  • BWait two hours and retry the oral dose with crackers
  • CSkip today's dose and resume the schedule tomorrow
  • DGive a double oral dose with an antacid to reduce gastric irritation
Rationale

"Sudden termination of the drug because of inadequate supplies or inability to ingest the oral form because of vomiting places the child in danger of an acute adrenal crisis" (pp. 1187–1188). "Ideally, families will have a prefilled syringe of hydrocortisone and have training to administer this drug during a crisis," and "Any evidence of acute insufficiency is reported to the practitioner immediately" (p. 1188).

Watch for

(D) is built from a true fact used in the wrong situation: cortisone does cause "gastric irritation, which is minimized by ingestion with food or the use of an antacid" (p. 1188), but a route that is already failing cannot be rescued by what is mixed into it.
