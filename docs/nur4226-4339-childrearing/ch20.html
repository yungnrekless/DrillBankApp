cognitive impairment definition and diagnosisgeneral
1. A nurse is reviewing the record of a 10-year-old being evaluated for intellectual disability. The child's intelligence testing and adaptive behavior assessment both show significant limitations. What third element must be present for the diagnosis?
  • ✓Onset before 18 years of age
  • BA documented genetic or metabolic cause
  • CA specific intelligence quotient below a stated cutoff
  • DEvidence that the limitations have persisted for at least 2 years
Rationale

The American Association on Intellectual and Developmental Disabilities defines intellectual disability as consisting of "three components: (1) intellectual functioning, (2) adaptive behavior, and (3) age younger than 18 years at time of diagnosis" (p. 632).

Watch for

(B) pulls because the chapter spends most of its length on identifiable syndromes, but the definition is functional rather than etiologic; (C) is the error the next item addresses, since no intelligence quotient cutoff is attached to any level anywhere in this chapter.

2. A student nurse states that intellectual disability is diagnosed on the basis of the intelligence quotient score. How should the nurse correct this?
  • ✓Current criteria move away from relying exclusively on intelligence testing and require deficits in adaptive functioning as well
  • BIntelligence testing is accurate but must be repeated at least twice before the diagnosis is made
  • CIntelligence testing has been replaced entirely by measures of adaptive behavior
  • DThe diagnosis rests on intelligence testing, but adaptive behavior determines the level of support needed
Rationale

The chapter states that DSM-5 criteria "recommend moving away from exclusively relying on IQ testing toward using additional measures of adaptive functioning," and the definition requires "significant limitations in both intellectual functioning and adaptive behavior" (p. 632).

Watch for

(C) overshoots by discarding intelligence testing, which the chapter still names as how intellectual functioning is measured, and (D) demotes adaptive behavior to a support-planning tool when it is one of the two required legs of the diagnosis.

4. The same mother adds, "My sister says I should stop comparing my children." How should the nurse respond?
  • ✓Take the comparison seriously and proceed with developmental assessment of this child
  • BReassure the mother that all children develop differently and comparison can be harmful
  • CArrange to meet the older sibling to identify familial patterns of development
  • DDefer assessment until the child's next well visit to see whether the differences persist
Rationale

The chapter states that "Parental concerns, such as delayed development compared with siblings, need to be taken seriously" (p. 633), and the same rule recurs for hearing and for vision — parental concern is sufficient grounds for evaluation at any age (pp. 642, 647).

(B) is the reflex reassurance and is the one this chapter names and rejects; it sounds supportive and it ends the assessment, which is why it is the keyed distractor in the chapter's own review item.

teaching and discipline for the child with cognitive impairmentgeneral
5. A nurse is teaching a 12-year-old with cognitive impairment to perform a simple dressing change. Which approach is most consistent with how this child learns?
  • ✓Demonstrate the procedure one step at a time and reinforce each step as it is mastered
  • BExplain the reason each step is performed so the child understands why the dressing matters
  • CGive the full sequence of steps at the start so the child can see how they fit together
  • DProvide written instructions the child can refer to between sessions
Rationale

The chapter names four learning deficits and their teaching implications: "demonstration is preferable to verbal explanation," learning should aim at "mastering a skill rather than understanding the scientific principles underlying a procedure," short-term memory limits mean the child needs "simple, one-step directions," and reinforcement is built into the task (p. 633).

Watch for

(B) and (C) are each the direct opposite of a stated rule, and (B) pulls hardest because explaining the rationale is good teaching in nearly every other context; here the chapter says to teach the skill, not the reason.

6. The parents of a 7-year-old with cognitive impairment whose mental age is about 4 years ask how to set limits. What should the nurse advise?
  • ✓Make the limits simple and consistent and match them to the child's mental age
  • BMatch the limits to the child's chronologic age so expectations stay age appropriate
  • CExplain after each incident why the behavior was wrong so the child learns from the mistake
  • DDelay formal limit setting until the child's comprehension improves
Rationale

The chapter directs that limit-setting measures "need to be simple, consistently applied, and appropriate for the child's mental age," and adds that "Discipline must begin early" (p. 636).

Watch for

(C) is the moral-reasoning distractor the chapter names explicitly — "Stressing moral lessons is of little value to a child who lacks the cognitive skills to learn from self-criticism" (p. 636) — and (B) swaps mental age for chronologic age, which is the single most likely discipline error in this chapter.

7. Parents report that their child with cognitive impairment greets visitors politely at home but does not do so at the clinic or at school. What should the nurse explain?
  • ✓There is no automatic transfer of learning from one situation to another, so the skill must be practiced in each setting
  • BThe behavior at home is likely prompted by the parents rather than genuinely learned
  • CInconsistent performance indicates the skill was taught before the child was developmentally ready
  • DSocial behaviors typically generalize over time and no additional teaching is needed
Rationale

The chapter instructs parents to "expose the child to strangers so that he or she can practice manners because there is no automatic transfer of learning from one situation to another" (p. 636).

Watch for

(D) assumes generalization, which is exactly the assumption the chapter corrects; (C) reads inconsistency as a readiness problem and would delay the practice the chapter says is the intervention.

8. A nurse is advising the parents of an 8-year-old with cognitive impairment about clothing and play. Which guidance is correct?
  • ✓Choose clothing appropriate to the child's chronologic age and choose play activities suited to the developmental age
  • BChoose both clothing and play activities suited to the child's developmental age
  • CChoose both clothing and play activities appropriate to the child's chronologic age
  • DChoose clothing suited to the developmental age and play activities appropriate to the chronologic age
Rationale

The chapter says clothes should be "clean, age appropriate, and well fitted with self-adhering fasteners and elastic openings to facilitate self-dressing" (p. 636), while play is "based on the child's developmental age" (p. 634).

Watch for

(D) is the exact inversion and is the reason this item exists — dressing a child in clothing for their mental age undercuts the social acceptance the chapter says grooming is for, while matching play to chronologic age sets the child up to fail.

9. A newborn has low-set ears, upward-slanting palpebral fissures, and marked hypotonia, and the pediatrician states that Down syndrome is very likely. The parents ask why blood is being drawn if the doctor is already sure. What should the nurse explain?
  • ✓The diagnosis can usually be made from the clinical features alone, but chromosome analysis confirms it and identifies which cytogenetic form is present
  • BChromosome analysis is the only reliable means of diagnosis, because clinical features are unreliable in the newborn period
  • CThe chromosome study screens for the leukemia and thyroid disease associated with the condition
  • DConfirmation is deferred until 6 months of age, when the characteristic features become fully apparent
Rationale

The chapter states that Down syndrome "can usually be diagnosed by the clinical manifestations alone, but a chromosome analysis should be done to confirm the genetic abnormality" (p. 637), and the karyotype also distinguishes trisomy 21 from translocation, which is the hereditary form.

Watch for

(B) makes the karyotype diagnostic rather than confirmatory, and (C) misassigns its purpose — leukemia and thyroid dysfunction are real associations but are not what a karyotype detects; the reason the study matters to this family is what it says about recurrence.

10. The parents of an 18-month-old with Down syndrome ask whether it is really necessary to treat every ear infection, since the child seems to recover without antibiotics. How should the nurse respond?
  • ✓Treatment is needed to prevent auditory loss, which can in turn influence cognitive function
  • BTreatment is needed because otitis media in Down syndrome progresses to mastoiditis more often than in other children
  • CTreatment can be withheld for mild episodes, since recurrent otitis media is expected in this population
  • DTreatment is needed primarily to prevent the child from developing chronic pain
Rationale

The chapter directs that "treatment of otitis media is required to prevent auditory loss, which can influence cognitive function" (p. 637), and lists evaluation of sight and hearing among the essential surveillance for these children.

Watch for

(C) is plausible because recurrent otitis media genuinely is common here, and the chapter's answer is that its commonness is the reason to treat it rather than a reason to tolerate it; the chain the chapter gives runs infection to hearing loss to cognition, which is why the stakes are higher than the ear.

11. A nurse is teaching new parents of an infant with Down syndrome about the risks of the first year. What should the nurse identify as the chief cause of death in this period?
  • ✓Respiratory tract infection combined with congenital cardiac anomalies
  • BLeukemia, which occurs with increased incidence in this population
  • CCongenital hypothyroidism if it is not detected on newborn screening
  • DSpinal cord compression from atlantoaxial instability
Rationale

The chapter states that respiratory tract infections "when combined with cardiac anomalies, are the chief causes of death, particularly during the first year of life," attributing the vulnerability to hypotonicity of the chest and abdominal muscles and immune dysfunction (p. 637).

Watch for

(B) and (C) are both real associated conditions the chapter names, which is what makes them plausible, but neither is identified as a leading cause of death; the chapter names the combination and names the first year.

12. The parents of an 8-year-old with Down syndrome are told by the school that a cervical spine x-ray is required before their daughter can compete in Special Olympics, but their pediatrician did not order one at the well visit. How should the nurse explain this?
  • ✓The American Academy of Pediatrics no longer recommends screening asymptomatic children, but the Special Olympics still requires neck x-rays before sports participation
  • BThe pediatrician has made an error, since screening is recommended for all children with Down syndrome
  • CThe school has made an error, since screening is no longer recommended by any organization
  • DScreening is required only for children who have already reported neck pain or weakness
Rationale

The chapter gives both positions in one paragraph — the Academy "no longer recommends screening asymptomatic children with Down syndrome for atlantoaxial instability with cervical spine x-rays," while "the Special Olympics continues to require that all athletes with Down syndrome receive neck x-rays before sports participation" — and adds that recommendations "remain varied" (p. 638).

Watch for

(B) and (C) each take one authority's position and state it as the universal rule, which is exactly the error the chapter's two-sided paragraph guards against; about 15% of these children have the instability and almost all are asymptomatic, which is why the question is contested at all.

13. A 10-year-old with Down syndrome is brought in with persistent neck pain, and his mother reports he has begun wetting himself after years of being continent. What is the nurse's priority action?
  • ✓Report the findings immediately as possible spinal cord compression
  • BObtain a urine specimen to evaluate for urinary tract infection
  • CReassure the mother that regression is common during periods of stress
  • DSchedule a routine follow-up appointment for a cervical spine x-ray
Rationale

The chapter's Nursing Alert directs the nurse to "Immediately report any child with the following signs of spinal cord compression": persistent neck pain, loss of established motor skills and bladder or bowel control, and changes in sensation (p. 638).

Watch for

(B) and (C) each explain the incontinence away, and (C) is especially seductive because regression under stress is real elsewhere in pediatrics; the discriminator is that this is a *loss of an established* skill paired with neck pain, which is the exact pattern the alert exists for.

14. The mother of a newborn with Down syndrome says tearfully that her baby does not cuddle or mold to her the way her other children did, and she wonders whether the baby is rejecting her. What is the nurse's best response?
  • ✓Explain that the lack of molding is a physical characteristic rather than a sign of detachment, and teach her to swaddle the infant snugly before picking him up
  • BReassure her that infants with Down syndrome form attachments more slowly and that molding will develop over the first year
  • CEncourage more frequent skin-to-skin contact so the infant learns to mold to her body
  • DArrange a referral to a lactation consultant, since feeding contact is the usual route to attachment
Rationale

The chapter directs the nurse to emphasize "that the child's lack of clinging or molding is a physical characteristic and not a sign of detachment or rejection," and to "Encourage the parents to swaddle or wrap the infant snugly in a blanket before picking up the child" for security and warmth (p. 639).

Watch for

(B) offers a false timeline for something hypotonia will not resolve, and (C) prescribes more of the very contact the hypotonia makes awkward without giving the mother the technique that solves it.

16. The father of a 9-month-old with Down syndrome says he has stopped offering solids because his son keeps pushing the food back out with his tongue. What should the nurse teach?
  • ✓The tongue thrust is a physiologic response rather than refusal, and extruded food should be refed using a small, long, straight-handled spoon that places food toward the back and side of the mouth
  • BThe tongue thrust indicates the infant is not developmentally ready for solids, and they should be reintroduced in 2 to 3 months
  • CFood should be placed on the tip of the tongue with a wide, shallow spoon so the infant can move it back independently
  • DSolid food should be thinned to a liquid consistency and offered by bottle until the thrust resolves
Rationale

The chapter states that "the tongue thrust is not an indication of refusal to feed but a physiologic response," advises "a small but long, straight-handled spoon to push the food toward the back and side of the mouth," and adds that "If food is thrust out, it should be refed" (p. 639).

Watch for

(B) reads a physical characteristic as a developmental signal and would delay feeding progression; (C) inverts both halves of the technique, since the tip of the tongue is where the thrust is strongest.

  • CGrowth charts are not useful in Down syndrome, so weight alone should be followed

18. A woman is found to carry 120 base-pair repeats at the fragile X site. How should the nurse interpret this result?
  • ✓She has a premutation and is a carrier
  • BShe has a full mutation and will show clinical manifestations
  • CThe result is within the normal range
  • DThe result is indeterminate and must be repeated
Rationale

The chapter gives three bands: normal is between 6 and 50 repeats, 50 to 200 repeats constitutes a premutation and carrier status, and expansion to 200 or more is a full mutation (p. 639).

Watch for

(B) reaches for the full-mutation band, and the boundary is 200 rather than 100, which is where an unanchored memory tends to drift; note the chapter prints permutation for premutation at this point, which is a typographic defect in the source.

19. A man who carries the fragile X premutation asks whether his daughters are at risk of having affected children. What should the nurse explain?
  • ✓Expansion to a full mutation occurs when a carrier mother transmits the gene, not when a carrier father transmits it to his daughters
  • BExpansion occurs regardless of which parent transmits the gene
  • CHis daughters cannot inherit the premutation from him because transmission is maternal
  • DExpansion occurs only when a carrier father transmits the gene
Rationale

The chapter states that "This expansion occurs only when a carrier mother passes the mutation to her offspring; it does not occur when a carrier father passes the mutation to his daughters" (p. 639).

Watch for

(D) is the direct reversal and (B) removes the asymmetry entirely; (C) overshoots by denying transmission altogether, when what the father does not transmit is the *expansion*, not the premutation.

20. A nurse is preparing genetic counseling materials for a family with fragile X syndrome and wants to explain why the counseling differs from that for hemophilia. Which statement is accurate?
  • ✓Fragile X is inherited as X-linked dominant with reduced penetrance, so carrier females may be affected, males may be carriers, and both sexes can transmit it
  • BFragile X follows the classic X-linked recessive pattern, so all carrier females are unaffected and no males are carriers
  • CFragile X is autosomal dominant, so each child of an affected parent has a 50% risk regardless of sex
  • DFragile X is autosomal recessive, so both parents must be carriers for a child to be affected
Rationale

The chapter names the pattern "X-linked dominant with reduced penetrance" and contrasts it with "the classic X-linked recessive pattern in which all carrier females are normal, all affected males have symptoms of the disorder, and no males are carriers," concluding that "Both affected sexes are capable of transmitting the fragile X disorder" (p. 640).

Watch for

(B) applies hemophilia logic to fragile X, which is precisely the comparison the chapter draws to say the two are unlike; fragile X breaks all three clauses of the classic pattern, and that is why counseling is more complex.

21. A nurse is evaluating a 5-year-old boy with unexplained developmental delay for possible fragile X syndrome. Which finding is most likely to suggest the diagnosis at this age?
  • ✓Behavioral manifestations such as hyperactivity and rapid speech with word repetition
  • BA long face with prominent jaw, large protruding ears, and large testes
  • CMacroorchidism, which is present from birth in affected males
  • DA shortened life expectancy compared with the general population
Rationale

The chapter states that in prepubertal children the classic physical findings "may be less obvious, and behavioral manifestations may initially suggest the diagnosis" (p. 640); the physical triad describes adult men, and Box 20.3 places enlarged testicles specifically in postpubertal males.

Watch for

(B) and (C) screen a prepubertal boy on adult findings, which is the error the chapter names; (D) transfers the Down syndrome prognosis, since individuals with fragile X "are expected to live a normal life span" while Down syndrome life expectancy "remains lower than for the general population" (pp. 638, 640).

22. A 4-year-old with a history of recurrent serous otitis media is found to have a hearing loss. Which type is most likely, and what does it do to sound?
  • ✓Conductive loss, which interferes mainly with the loudness of sound
  • BSensorineural loss, which distorts sound and impairs discrimination
  • CCentral auditory imperception, which prevents the message from becoming meaningful communication
  • DMixed loss, which reflects damage confined to the auditory nerve
Rationale

Conductive loss "results from interference of transmission of sound to the middle ear," is "the most common of all types of hearing loss and most frequently a result of recurrent serous otitis media," and "involves mainly interference with loudness of sound" (p. 641); chronic otitis media is also named as the most common cause of impaired hearing overall (p. 644).

Watch for

(D) misdescribes mixed loss, which involves the middle ear *and* the neural pathways; the practical consequence of keying conductive is that amplification helps, which is what the next item turns on.

23. A 6-year-old with sensorineural hearing loss has been fitted with a conventional hearing aid but his parents report he still cannot make out what people say, even when the device is turned up. What explains this?
  • ✓Sensorineural loss distorts sound and impairs discrimination, so amplifying sound into a damaged inner ear does not restore comprehension
  • BThe aid is improperly fitted, which is the usual reason amplification fails in sensorineural loss
  • CSensorineural loss affects loudness, so the aid should be set to a higher gain
  • DComprehension improves only after several months of adjustment to any hearing aid
Rationale

Sensorineural loss "results in distortion of sound and problems in discrimination," and the chapter explains that "conventional hearing aids only amplify sound that may not be processed by a damaged inner ear," so some children "require a referral for a cochlear implant" (pp. 641, 642).

Watch for

(C) is the mirror error of the previous item, applying the conductive rule to a sensorineural loss; (B) names a real problem — improper ear mold fit causes acoustic feedback, the annoying whistling sound — but that is not what this scenario describes.

24. A child can hear sound but cannot interpret what she hears, though she can express ideas normally and discriminate among similar sounds. Which term describes this?
  • ✓Agnosia
  • BAphasia
  • CDysacusis
  • DConversion hysteria
Rationale

Under central auditory imperception of the organic type, the chapter defines agnosia as "the inability to interpret sound correctly," aphasia as "the inability to express ideas in any form, either written or verbal," and dysacusis as "difficulty in processing details or discriminating among sounds" (p. 641).

Watch for

(B) and (C) are each excluded by a clause in the stem, which is how this three-term set has to be read — the discriminating word in each definition is the verb; (D) is the functional-type example, an unconscious withdrawal from hearing to block a traumatic memory.

25. A 7-year-old with a severe hearing loss in the 71 to 90 decibel range pronounces the word spoon as "poon." What explains this pattern?
  • ✓At this level the child can distinguish vowels but not most consonants, which are the higher-frequency sounds
  • BAt this level the child hears only loud sounds, so all speech is reproduced incompletely
  • CThe child has a conductive loss, which typically produces a loud, monotone voice
  • DThe pattern reflects a speech articulation disorder unrelated to the hearing level

27. A nurse is preparing to teach an 11-year-old who relies on lipreading. Which actions facilitate lipreading? Select all that apply.
Select all that apply · scored all or nothing
  • ✓Attract the child's attention before speaking, using light touch to signal your presence
  • ✓Stand still and face the child directly or at a 45-degree angle
  • ✓Ensure good lighting falls on the speaker's face
  • ✓Rephrase the message if the child does not understand the words
  • EExaggerate pronunciation of key words so their shapes are clearer
Rationale

The Nursing Care Guidelines list attracting attention with light touch, standing still and facing directly or at a 45-degree angle, speaking at eye level with good lighting on the speaker's face, speaking clearly at a slow and even rate, keeping sentences short, and rephrasing when the child does not understand (p. 643).

28. The parents of a 5-year-old who uses speech and lipreading ask about a method that would help him tell apart words such as mat and bat, which look identical on the lips. What should the nurse describe?
  • ✓Cued speech, an adjunct to lipreading that uses hand signals to distinguish words that look alike when formed by the lips
  • BAmerican Sign Language, a visual-gestural language that replaces the need for lipreading
  • CSpeech-language therapy, which retrains the child to produce the distinguishing consonants
  • DFinger spelling, in which words are spelled letter by letter into the child's hand
Rationale

Cued speech is "an adjunct to straight lipreading" that "uses hand signals to help the hearing-impaired child distinguish between words that look alike when formed by the lips (e.g., mat, bat)" and is "most commonly employed by hearing-impaired children who are using speech rather than those who are nonverbal" (p. 643).

Watch for

(B) is the most confusable option because both involve the hands, but sign language is a language in its own right rather than an adjunct; (D) is the method taught for combined hearing and visual impairment, not for lipreading support.

29. An 11-month-old with profound sensorineural hearing loss is being evaluated for a cochlear implant. Which teaching point should the nurse include?
  • ✓The implant bypasses the damaged hair cells to stimulate surviving auditory nerve fibers, and the child must receive the pneumococcal polyvalent vaccine
  • BThe implant is a more powerful hearing aid that amplifies sound directly into the inner ear
  • CImplantation is generally deferred until the child is at least 3 years old and can cooperate with programming
  • DThe main risk is device failure, so no additional immunization is required beyond the routine schedule
Rationale

The chapter states that "A cochlear implant bypasses the hair cells to directly stimulate surviving auditory nerve fibers," that the trend is toward implantation "usually by 12 months old," and that because pneumococcal meningitis has been reported, "all children receiving a cochlear implant must be vaccinated with the pneumococcal polyvalent vaccine" (p. 642).

(B) describes an amplifier rather than a bypass, which is the whole distinction between an aid and an implant; (C) inverts the timing trend, and (D) drops the single most actionable safety item in the hearing section.

30. A nurse is caring for a hospitalized 6-year-old with a hearing impairment. Which action is correct?
  • ✓Make certain the child can see the nurse before any procedure, including routine ones such as changing a diaper or adjusting an infusion
  • BReserve the requirement to be seen first for invasive or painful procedures, to avoid disrupting the child unnecessarily
  • CCommunicate procedures through the parents, who understand the child's speech best
  • DAwaken the child at night by turning on the overhead light before approaching the bed
Rationale

The chapter directs the nurse to "always make certain that the child can see him or her before any procedures, even routine ones such as changing a diaper or regulating an infusion," because the child "may not be aware of the nurse's presence until alerted through visual or tactile cues" (p. 644).

Watch for

(C) is the failure the chapter names outright — caregivers "communicate only with the parents" and "the child's needs and feelings remain unrecognized" (p. 644); (D) misses the specific technique, which is to gently shake the child or turn on the hearing aid before arousing them.

31. A school-age child has a corrected visual acuity of 20/100 in the better eye. How should the nurse classify this?
  • ✓Partial sight, and the child can generally use normal-sized print because near vision usually exceeds distance vision
  • BLegal blindness, which requires immediate registration for special educational services
  • CPartial sight, which requires large-print materials as the first educational accommodation
  • DWithin normal limits for school-age children with corrective lenses
Rationale

Partial sight is "a visual acuity between 20/70 and 20/200," and the chapter notes that such a child "can generally use normal-sized print because near vision is almost always better than distance vision"; legal blindness begins at 20/200 or lower, or a visual field of 20 degrees or less in the better eye (p. 645).

Watch for

(C) supplies large print reflexively for any visual impairment, which the chapter does not; note also that legal blindness "is not a medical diagnosis but a legal definition" used to determine tax status, school entrance, and financial aid.

  • DAnisometropia, resulting from different refractive strength in each eye, corrected with contact lenses
Rationale

The chapter states that in refractive disorders light rays "fall in front of the retina (myopia) or beyond it (hyperopia)," that myopia "Results from eyeball that is too long," and that it is "Corrected with biconcave lenses that focus rays on retina" (p. 645).

(B) is the mirror condition with the mirror lens, and it is worth noting that hyperopia in a young child is usually normal — "Most children are normally hyperopic until about 7 years old" — and is treated only when required.

33. A 4-year-old with untreated strabismus is at risk for amblyopia. Which statement about the mechanism and treatment is correct?
  • ✓The brain suppresses the image from the weaker eye until the visual cortex stops responding, and treatment patches the stronger eye, preventing loss if begun before 6 years old
  • BThe weaker eye loses acuity from disuse of the eye muscles, and treatment patches the weaker eye to strengthen it
  • CThe retina of the weaker eye degenerates progressively, and treatment is surgical realignment alone
  • DVision loss is permanent once strabismus is detected, so treatment aims only at cosmetic alignment
Rationale

Box 20.5 describes the chain — one eye receives insufficient stimulation, each retina receives a different image, the "Brain accommodates by suppressing less intense image," and the "Visual cortex eventually does not respond" — and states amblyopia is "Preventable if treatment of primary visual defect, such as anisometropia or strabismus, begins before 6 years old"; treatment "May involve occlusion therapy (patching stronger eye)" (p. 646).

Watch for

(B) patches the affected eye, which is the classic reversal and would accelerate the very suppression the therapy exists to interrupt; the loss in amblyopia is cortical rather than ocular, which is why (C) is wrong and why the developmental window in (A) matters.

The chapter states that autism spectrum disorder "is not related to socioeconomic level, race, or parenting style," that nurses can "alleviate the guilt and shame often associated with this disorder by stressing what is known from a biologic standpoint," and that "It is imperative to help parents understand that they are not the cause of the child's condition" (pp. 650, 653).

(C) is technically hedged and does real harm, because it leaves the parenting hypothesis alive when the chapter has already excluded it; (B) normalizes the guilt without supplying the information that dissolves it, which is the specific method the chapter names.

42. At a 16-month well visit, a mother reports her son babbles but has never used a single word, and adds that he had been saying "mama" at 12 months but stopped. What should the nurse recommend?
  • ✓Immediate hearing and language evaluation, since single words are expected by 16 months and regression is a red-flag event

  • CReassessment at the 18-month visit, when autism screening is due
  • DReassurance that expressive language commonly fluctuates in the second year
Rationale

The chapter recommends "immediate hearing and language evaluation" for any child not displaying "babbling or gesturing by 12 months old, single words by 16 months old, and two-word phrases by 24 months old," and describes autism regression — developing normally and then regressing suddenly — as "a red-flag event that has been frequently displayed in expressive language" (p. 651).

Watch for

(B) is the most common wrong pick because the presenting problem is speech, but the chapter pairs hearing with language every time, since hearing loss is the differential; (D) treats regression as normal variation, which this chapter never does.
