informed consent and assent in pediatric caregeneral
1. A nurse is reviewing what makes consent for a child's procedure valid. Which conditions does the chapter require? Select all that apply.
Select all that apply · scored all or nothing
  • ✓The person must be capable of giving consent, being over the age of majority and competent
  • ✓The person must receive the information needed to make an intelligent decision
  • ✓The person must act voluntarily, without force, fraud, deceit, duress, or coercion
  • DThe person must sign the consent form in the presence of the performing physician
Rationale

The chapter numbers exactly three conditions: capacity, adequate information, and voluntariness, defining the third as acting "voluntarily when exercising freedom of choice without force, fraud, deceit, duress, or other forms of constraint or coercion" (p. 678).

Watch for

(D) confuses the evidence with the act, and the chapter separates them — "A signed consent form is the legal document that signifies that the process of informed consent has occurred" (p. 679); the condition most often dropped from recall is voluntariness, because it reads as too obvious to state.

2. A 9-year-old scheduled for a bone marrow aspiration says he does not want it done. His mother has signed the consent. What is accurate about his role in the decision?
  • ✓His assent should be sought, but assent is an ethical requirement rather than a legal one
  • BHis refusal is legally binding and the procedure cannot proceed
  • CAssent is not sought until adolescence, so his objection has no formal standing
  • DHis silence during the explanation would have constituted assent if he had not spoken
Rationale

The chapter states that assent applies to children "greater than 7 years with appropriate age, maturity, and psychologic state" (p. 678) and that "Assent is not a legal requirement but an ethical one to protect the rights of children" (p. 679).

Watch for

(D) is the misreading the chapter forecloses in one sentence — "If the child or adolescent merely fails to object, this does not equate assent" (p. 678); (C) pushes the age cue to adolescence when the chapter's threshold is greater than 7 years.

3. A surgeon has explained a procedure to a child's parents and left the unit. The mother tells the nurse she is not sure what the alternatives were. What should the nurse do?
  • ✓Notify the surgeon, since explaining the procedure, risks, benefits, and alternatives is the physician's legal responsibility
  • BExplain the alternatives, since reinforcing the explanation is within the nurse's role
  • CWitness the signature and document that the mother had questions
  • DAsk the mother to sign and arrange for the surgeon to answer questions postoperatively
Rationale

The chapter assigns the roles explicitly: "It is the physician's legal responsibility to explain the procedure, risks, benefits, and alternatives," while the nurse "witnesses the patient's, parent's, or legal guardian's signature on the consent form and may reinforce what the patient has been told" (p. 679).

Watch for

(B) stretches "reinforce what the patient has been told" into supplying information that was never given, which is the boundary this item tests; (C) and (D) both proceed to a signature while a condition of valid consent — adequate information — is unmet.

4. A 16-year-old arrives at the clinic alone. Which circumstance would make her an emancipated minor under the chapter's account?
  • ✓She is married
  • BShe is 16, which in a few states meets the mature minor threshold
  • CShe has a part-time job and contributes to household expenses
  • DShe has a signed note from her parents authorizing treatment
Rationale

The chapter names the emancipating circumstances as "pregnancy, marriage, high school graduation, independent living, or military service" (p. 679).

Watch for

(B) is the confusable pair — the mature minor exception is recognized in a few states "for children 14 years and older" and may require legal action, while emancipation follows from a named circumstance and carries no age; (D) describes consent by proxy, which is a different mechanism entirely.

5. A nurse is choosing words to explain an upcoming test to a 4-year-old. Which substitution is consistent with the chapter's guidance?
  • ✓Say "medicine to help the place in your body show up on a picture" instead of "dye"
  • BSay "we will fix your heart" instead of naming the surgery
  • CSay "the cuff will squeeze your arm tightly" instead of "check your pressure"
  • DSay "we will put you to sleep" to describe anesthesia gently
Rationale

Table 22.1 lists "dye" among the words to avoid, with that exact substitution (p. 680); the table's organizing logic is that each avoided word carries a second, frightening meaning.

Watch for

(B) uses a word that is on the avoid list — "fix" implies the child is broken, and it is the entry most often misread as acceptable because it sounds gentle; (D) is also on the list, because it is what happens to a pet, and the substitution is "different kind of sleep so you won't feel anything"; (C) replaces the suggested "hug your arm" with a threat.

6. A nurse plans to demonstrate an upcoming procedure on a doll for a 2-year-old. Which doll should be used?
  • ✓A doll from the unit's supply, because the child may believe her own favorite doll is really feeling the procedure
  • BThe child's own favorite doll, because familiarity makes the demonstration more meaningful
  • CNo doll, because toddlers cannot generalize from a demonstration
  • DA doll chosen by the parents from home, since it will be more comforting
Rationale

The toddler preparation guidance directs the nurse to "Use play; demonstrate on doll but avoid child's favorite doll because child may think doll is really" feeling the procedure (p. 680), which is animism applied to the comfort object.

Watch for

(B) and (D) both reach for the familiar object, which is right for almost every other comfort intervention in this chapter and wrong here; note the toddler's session should also be about 5 to 10 minutes and given shortly or immediately before the procedure, not a day ahead.

7. A nurse has drawn blood from a 4-year-old and the bleeding has stopped. Why does the chapter direct applying an adhesive bandage over the puncture site?
  • ✓Because the preschooler fears that the body's contents will leak out through the opening
  • BBecause it prevents contamination of the site with skin flora
  • CBecause it marks the site for the next shift
  • DBecause bandages are a form of positive reinforcement after a procedure
Rationale

"Apply an adhesive bandage over puncture site" appears under the preschooler's "Fears of Bodily Harm, Intrusion, and Castration" (p. 680); the related instruction in the same block is to "Emphasize that no other body part will be involved."

Watch for

(B) is a true statement about bandages that does not answer a developmental stem, which is what makes it the strongest distractor; note also the chapter's competing caution that adhesive bandages are an aspiration hazard in young children (p. 706), so the resolution is age and supervision rather than one rule for all.

8. A 10-month-old cries and struggles as soon as the nurse carrying a syringe enters the room, before anything has been done. How should the nurse interpret this?
  • ✓Older infants may associate objects, places, or persons with prior painful experiences and resist at the sight of them
  • BThis is stranger anxiety, which is unrelated to any previous procedure
  • CInfants do not retain memories of past experiences, so the reaction must be to the nurse's manner
  • DThis indicates the infant is in pain from an unrecognized source
Rationale

The chapter makes "Memory for Past Experiences" a developmental characteristic of infancy and builds four interventions on it, beginning "Realize that older infants may associate objects, places, or persons with prior painful experiences and will cry and resist at the sight of them" (p. 680).

Watch for

(C) is the claim the chapter's own review item keys as wrong, and it is widely believed; the interventions that follow from the correct reading are to keep frightening objects out of view and to perform painful procedures in a separate room rather than in the crib.

9. A 5-month-old is scheduled for elective surgery at 10:00. Her mother asks how late she may breastfeed and whether formula would be different. What should the nurse tell her?
  • ✓Breast milk requires a 4-hour fast and formula requires 6 hours, because nonhuman milk empties from the stomach like a solid
  • BBoth require a 4-hour fast, since both are liquids
  • CBoth require a 6-hour fast, since both are milk
  • DBreast milk may be given up to 2 hours before, since it is treated as a clear liquid
Rationale

Table 22.2 gives clear liquids greater than 2 hours, breast milk 4 hours, and infant formula, nonhuman milk, and a light meal 6 hours, with the footnote explaining that "Because nonhuman milk is similar to solids in gastric emptying time, the amount ingested must be considered" (p. 686).

Watch for

(B) and (C) collapse the two intervals, which is the most common error; note also that the fasting periods "apply to all ages" and are not scaled for a neonate, and that fasting too long is itself a harm — discomfort, headache, dehydration, hypoglycemia, and delayed discharge.

10. The parents of a child scheduled for surgery mention that an uncle had a dangerous reaction to anesthesia, but say their son had his tonsils out last year with no problem. How should the nurse interpret this?
  • ✓He may still be susceptible to malignant hyperthermia, because a previous uneventful anesthetic does not exclude it
  • BThe uneventful prior surgery effectively rules out susceptibility
  • CThe family history is relevant only if the affected relative was a parent or sibling
  • DSusceptibility is determined by preoperative laboratory screening rather than history
Rationale

The chapter states that a family or previous history of sudden high fever with surgery increases risk and adds directly that "Children who have successfully undergone prior surgery without adverse effects may still be considered susceptible" (p. 687).

Watch for

(B) is the single most inviting wrong answer in this section, because a clean prior anesthetic feels like the strongest possible reassurance; the disorder is described as "a potentially fatal pharmacogenetic disorder of muscle metabolism" triggered by inhaled anesthetics and succinylcholine.

11. During surgery, end-tidal carbon dioxide begins climbing and the child develops tachycardia and muscle rigidity. Which action comes first?
  • ✓Immediate discontinuation of the triggering agent
  • BApplication of ice packs to the groin, axillae, and neck
  • CAdministration of intravenous dantrolene sodium
  • DTransfer to the intensive care unit
Rationale

The chapter orders treatment as discontinuation of the triggering agent, hyperventilation with 100% oxygen, intravenous dantrolene sodium, cooling measures if hyperthermic, and then intensive care for at least 36 hours (p. 687).

Watch for

(C) names the definitive drug and is still not first, which is what this item turns on; (B) leads with cooling, which is step 4 and only if the child is actually hyperthermic — note that the symptom list leads with rising carbon dioxide, so temperature is the later finding despite the name of the disorder.

12. A nurse is preparing three children for surgery: a 6-month-old, a 3-year-old, and a 16-year-old. Which fears does the chapter attach to each?
  • ✓Stranger anxiety in the infant, separation anxiety in the preschooler, and fear of injury or death in the adolescent
  • BSeparation anxiety in the infant, fear of injury in the preschooler, and stranger anxiety in the adolescent
  • CFear of death in all three, differing only in how it is expressed
  • DFear of the mask at induction in all three, which is the chapter's single named stress point
Rationale

The chapter states that children are at higher anesthetic and recovery risk "because of higher anxiety in the preoperative period associated with stranger anxiety (infants), separation anxiety (toddlers and preschoolers), and fear of injury or death (adolescents)" (p. 686).

Watch for

(D) names a real stress point and makes it the only one, when the chapter lists six — admission, blood tests, preoperative medication, transport to the operating room, the mask at induction, and the postanesthesia care unit; note also that underpants or pajama bottoms are worn into the operating room and removed after induction.

fever versus hyperthermia and their managementgeneral
13. A nurse is explaining why the same intervention does not work for every elevated temperature. What distinguishes fever from hyperthermia?
  • ✓Fever raises the hypothalamic set point, so antipyretics work; in hyperthermia the set point is normal, so only cooling works
  • BFever is infectious and hyperthermia is not, which is why only fever responds to antipyretics
  • CFever exceeds 38 degrees Celsius and hyperthermia exceeds 40 degrees Celsius
  • DFever is regulated and self-limiting, so neither antipyretics nor cooling are indicated
Rationale

The chapter defines fever as "An elevation in set point such that body temperature is regulated at a higher level" and hyperthermia as "Body temperature exceeding the set point," and states that "Because the set point is normal in hyperthermia but increased in fever, different approaches must be used" (pp. 693–694).

Watch for

(C) turns a mechanism into a number, and (B) uses etiology where the chapter uses physiology; the practical consequence is stated plainly — "antipyretics are of no value in hyperthermia because the set point is already normal."

14. A 3-year-old has a fever of 39.2 degrees Celsius and received acetaminophen 15 minutes ago. Her father is applying a cool wet washcloth and she has begun shivering. What should the nurse do?
  • ✓Stop the cooling measure, and explain that environmental measures are applied about 1 hour after the antipyretic so the set point has come down
  • BContinue the cooling measure, since shivering indicates the temperature is falling
  • CAdd a tepid sponge bath, which is the most effective measure for a febrile child
  • DRemove all clothing and lower the room temperature until the shivering stops
Rationale

The chapter says traditional cooling measures "are effective if used approximately 1 hour after an antipyretic is given so the set point is lowered," and that shivering "is the body's way of maintaining the elevated set point by producing heat," greatly increasing metabolic requirements (p. 693).

Watch for

(C) is the single most reliable distractor in the chapter, because tepid sponging for fever is what most people were taught — the chapter calls it ineffective for febrile children and adds the parenthesis that "these measures are effective for hyperthermia"; the metabolic cost of shivering is three to five times baseline.

15. The mother of a 2-year-old with a viral illness asks how often she may give acetaminophen and whether aspirin would work better. What should the nurse teach?
  • ✓Acetaminophen may be given every 4 hours but no more than five times in 24 hours because of hepatotoxicity, and aspirin is not given to children because of its association with Reye syndrome
  • BAcetaminophen may be given every 4 hours around the clock, and aspirin is an acceptable alternative if acetaminophen is unavailable
  • CIbuprofen should replace both, since it is approved from birth
  • DAntipyretics should be given on a schedule until the temperature normalizes, whatever the child's comfort
Rationale

The chapter names acetaminophen the preferred drug with that interval and ceiling, states that aspirin "should not be given to children because of its association in children with influenza virus or chickenpox and Reye syndrome," and approves ibuprofen from 6 months (p. 693).

Watch for

(B) is six doses, which exceeds the ceiling the hepatotoxicity warning sets; (D) inverts the chapter's endpoint, which is that "The child's level of discomfort is the best indication for continued treatment" and that the temperature "should not be repeatedly measured."

16. A 2-year-old had a 3-minute generalized seizure with a fever. The parents ask whether he has brain damage and whether scheduled acetaminophen will prevent another one. What should the nurse tell them?
  • ✓A simple febrile seizure lasting less than 10 minutes does not cause brain damage, and evidence does not support antipyretics or anticonvulsants to prevent a second one
  • BBrain damage is unlikely, and scheduled antipyretics are the standard preventive measure
  • CA single febrile seizure predicts epilepsy, so anticonvulsant prophylaxis should be started
  • DRecurrence is rare, occurring in fewer than 5% of children
Rationale

The chapter states that "Simple febrile seizures lasting less than 10 minutes do not cause brain damage or other debilitating effects" and that evidence does not support "antipyretic drugs or anticonvulsants to prevent a second febrile seizure" (p. 694).

Watch for

(B) is the intervention most families expect and most clinicians once gave; (D) misstates the figure, since about 30% to 50% of children have subsequent febrile seizures — the 2% to 5% figure is the incidence of febrile seizures at all, in children 6 months to 5 years.

17. A nurse is assigning precautions for newly admitted children. Which conditions require Airborne Precautions? Select all that apply.
Select all that apply · scored all or nothing
  • ✓Measles
  • ✓Varicella, including disseminated zoster
  • ✓Tuberculosis
  • DInvasive Neisseria meningitidis disease
  • ERespiratory syncytial virus in an infant
Rationale

Box 22.2 places measles, varicella including disseminated zoster, and tuberculosis under Airborne Precautions, which are for "serious illnesses transmitted by airborne droplet nuclei" (p. 696).

Watch for

(D) is Droplet and (E) is Contact, and varicella is the member most often misassigned — chickenpox lesions are also a contact hazard, which is why the box notes that precautions "may be combined for diseases that have multiple routes of transmission"; the same organism can also split by site, as pharyngeal diphtheria is Droplet while cutaneous diphtheria is Contact.

18. A child is placed on Droplet Precautions. What does the nurse need to know about the room?
  • ✓Special air handling and ventilation are not required, because droplets do not remain suspended in the air
  • BA negative-pressure room is required, as with any transmission-based precaution
  • CDroplet Precautions replace Standard Precautions for the duration of the illness
  • DDroplets travel about 20 feet, so visitors must remain outside the room
Rationale

The chapter states that large-particle droplets travel "usually 3 feet or less but up to 10 feet" and that "Because droplets do not remain suspended in the air, special air handling and ventilation are not required to prevent droplet transmission" (p. 696).

Watch for

(C) is the more dangerous error and the chapter forecloses it directly — Transmission-Based Precautions "are to be used in addition to Standard Precautions"; (B) applies the airborne requirement, which exists precisely because those particles do stay suspended.

19. A nurse is completing fall precautions for a hospitalized 6-year-old who walked independently at home. Which action is correct?
  • ✓Assist the child with ambulation anyway, and keep dim lights on while she sleeps
  • BAllow independent ambulation to the bathroom, since she walked well before admission
  • CTurn all lights off at night to support normal sleep rhythms
  • DKeep the door closed at night so hallway noise does not wake her
Rationale

The chapter directs the nurse to "Assist the child with ambulation even though he or she may have ambulated well before hospitalization," to "Keep lights on at all times, including dim lights while sleeping," and to "Keep the patient's door open unless isolation status prohibits" (p. 696).

Watch for

(B) reasons from the child's baseline rather than from her current state, when postoperative status, sedation, and new lines all appear on the risk list; note the chapter also names the family daybed as a fall hazard, and instructs that patients who have been lying down "need to get up slowly, sitting on the side of the bed before standing."

20. A nurse is about to change an infant's wet diaper with ordinary stool. Which action is consistent with the chapter's guidance?
  • ✓Change the diaper without gloves, since the plastic lining is a sufficient barrier, and perform hand hygiene afterward
  • BWear gloves for every diaper change without exception
  • CWear gloves, and skip hand hygiene afterward since the hands stayed covered
  • DWear a gown and gloves, since all diaper changes involve body substances
Rationale

The chapter says that for loose or explosive stools the nurse should "wear gloves and possibly gowns" but that "Otherwise, the plastic lining of disposable diapers provides a sufficient barrier between the hands and body substances" (p. 696), while requiring hand hygiene after glove removal because "gloves fail to provide complete protection."

Watch for

(C) is the substitution the chapter rules out, noting that "The absence of visible leaks does not indicate that gloves are intact"; (B) and (D) are the over-cautious answers, and the discriminating cue the chapter gives is the character of the stool. Note also its Nursing Alert that the stethoscope is itself a vector.

21. A nurse is auditing the unit for restraint use. Which of the following are NOT considered restraints under the chapter's definition? Select all that apply.
Select all that apply · scored all or nothing
  • ✓An armboard securing a peripheral intravenous line, not tied to the bed
  • ✓Hand mitts that are not tied down and not paired with a wrist restraint
  • ✓Crib side rails and a crib dome on an infant's bed
  • ✓A mummy wrap used only for the duration of a throat examination
  • ETight tucking of sheets to prevent a child from moving in bed
Rationale

The chapter excludes an armboard "unless it is tied down to the bed or immobilizes the entire limb," hand mitts "unless tied down to the bed or used in conjunction with a wrist restraint," developmentally age-appropriate safety devices including crib side rails and crib domes, and a mummy wrap "When used only for the duration of the test or procedure" (pp. 698–699).

Watch for

(E) is on the chapter's own list of restraints, alongside limb, elbow, and vest restraints, and it is the one that does not look like a device; each of the four correct options carries a condition that flips it, which is why this list is worth learning as conditions rather than as items.

22. A 7-year-old requires a behavioral restraint for violent behavior. How often must the order be renewed?
  • ✓Every 1 hour
  • BEvery 2 hours
  • CEvery 4 hours
  • DEvery 15 minutes
Rationale

The chapter states that unless state law is more restrictive, behavioral restraints "must be reordered every 15 minutes for a personal restraint, every 1 hour for children under 9 years of age, and every 2 hours for children 9 to 17 years old; orders for adults 18 and older are required every 4 hours" (p. 698).

Watch for

(D) is the interval for a personal restraint, meaning a physical hold, rather than for a mechanical one; the chapter's own review item shifts these intervals up one step and is keyed wrong, and note separately that an in-person evaluation is required within 1 hour and at least every 24 hours.

23. A nurse is applying wrist restraints to a child. Which technique is correct?
  • ✓Secure the ties to the stationary bed frame, leave one finger breadth between the skin and the device, and use knots that allow quick release
  • BSecure the ties to the side rails so the restraint moves with the rail
  • CLeave two finger breadths of slack and use a secure knot that will not loosen
  • DPlace limb restraints above the level of the intravenous site so the line is protected
Rationale

The chapter directs that "Restraints with ties must be secured to the stationary bed or crib frame, not the side rails," and describes "leaving one finger breadth between skin and the device and tying knots that allow for quick release" (p. 699).

Watch for

(B) is the error with the clearest mechanism — lowering the rail then drags the limb; (C) is the adult teaching for some devices and defeats emergency release; (D) reverses the instruction to place limb restraints "distal to the IV."

24. A restraint order for a child expires in 40 minutes, and the child is now calm, oriented, and cooperative. What should the nurse do?
  • ✓Discontinue the restraint now, because release occurs as soon as it is safe even if the order timeframe has not expired
  • BMaintain the restraint until the order expires, then reassess
  • CMaintain the restraint and obtain a new order for the remaining time
  • DLoosen the restraint but leave it applied until the provider evaluates the child
Rationale

The chapter states that "Discontinuation of restraints should occur as soon as it is safe, even if the order timeframe has not expired" (p. 698), and separately that the nurse "is responsible for selecting the least restrictive type of restraint" and must document alternatives considered first.

Watch for

(B) treats the order as a duration rather than a ceiling, which is the most common misreading; the alternatives the chapter names — bringing the child to the nurses' station, diversional activities such as music, and parental participation — are what should have been tried and documented before the restraint went on.

25. A nurse is performing a heelstick on a 3-day-old. Which technique prevents necrotizing osteochondritis of the calcaneus?
  • ✓Puncture no deeper than 2 mm, at the outer aspect of the heel, using an automatic lancet device
  • BPuncture no deeper than 1 mm, at the inner aspect of the heel
  • CPuncture at the center of the heel pad, where the tissue is thickest
  • DUse a surgical blade for a controlled incision depth
Rationale

The chapter states that "To avoid osteochondritis, the puncture should be no deeper than 2 mm and should be made at the outer aspect of the heel," and that an automatic device "delivers a more precise puncture depth and is less painful than using a manual lancet" (p. 706).

Watch for

(B) is the chapter's own review-item distractor, wrong on both numbers; (D) is an absolute contraindication — "A surgical blade of any kind is contraindicated" — and the long-term consequence of repeated trauma to the walking surface is fibrosis and scarring that interferes with locomotion.

26. A nurse is preparing to draw blood from a school-age child and wants to minimize pain. Which measures does the chapter endorse? Select all that apply.
Select all that apply · scored all or nothing
  • ✓Apply EMLA cream if more than 60 minutes are available, or LMX cream if only 30 minutes are available
  • ✓Use the smallest-gauge needle that permits free flow of blood
  • ✓Keep all equipment out of sight until it is used
  • ✓Limit attempts to two operators with two attempts each before escalating
  • EHave the child steady the finger firmly against the bedside table during a fingerstick
Rationale

The Atraumatic Care guidelines specify the EMLA and LMX application times, the smallest-gauge needle, keeping equipment out of sight, and a two-try-only policy meaning two operators with two attempts each, after which alternative access should be considered (p. 706).

(E) reverses an instruction in the same box — press the lancet device lightly against the skin and "avoid steadying the finger against a hard surface"; note also that the whole finger is massaged to maintain flow and the tip is never squeezed, because squeezing hemolyzes the sample and dilutes it with tissue fluid.

27. A nurse plans to apply EMLA cream before a newborn's heelstick. What should the nurse know?
  • ✓EMLA has been found to be no more effective than placebo for heelsticks, while breastfeeding during the procedure has been more effective than sucrose in some studies
  • BEMLA is the most effective option for heelsticks and should be applied 30 minutes beforehand
  • CEMLA is contraindicated in preterm infants at any dose
  • DSucrose is more effective than breastfeeding and should be given 30 minutes before the stick
Rationale

The chapter states that EMLA, "Although safe for use in preterm infants when applied correctly, has been found to be no more effective than placebo in preventing pain during heelsticks," and that "Breastfeeding during a neonatal heelstick is effective in reducing pain and has been found to be more effective than sucrose in some studies" (p. 706).

Watch for

(D) inverts the comparison and the timing, since sucrose is given as 2 ml to the tongue 2 minutes before the procedure; kangaroo care is the other endorsed measure and begins 10 to 15 minutes before the stick rather than afterward as comfort.

28. After an antecubital venipuncture on a 7-year-old, what should the nurse have the child do?
  • ✓Hold pressure with the arm extended, because flexing the elbow increases bruising
  • BFlex the elbow firmly to compress the site
  • CRaise the arm above the head for 2 minutes
  • DApply a cold compress immediately to prevent hematoma
Rationale

The chapter directs applying pressure with the arm extended after an antecubital draw, since flexing increases bruising, and adds that if a hematoma does develop, "applying warm compresses to the ecchymotic area increases circulation, helps remove extravasated blood, and decreases pain" (p. 706).

Watch for

(B) is standard adult practice and is what most learners will do by reflex; (D) reverses the temperature, since the chapter names warm compresses rather than cold for an established bruise. Note also the volume ceiling of 3 ml/kg in 24 hours, and less if the child is ill.

oral and intramuscular medication administrationgeneral
29. A parent asks how to measure 3 ml of an antibiotic suspension at home. What should the nurse recommend?
  • ✓A calibrated oral syringe, which is the most accurate device for small amounts
  • BA household teaspoon, which families find easiest to use consistently
  • CA dropper, emptied into a medicine cup for a more exact reading
  • DA molded plastic medicine cup, which is calibrated for pediatric doses
Rationale

The chapter states that "The most accurate means for measuring small amounts of medication is the plastic disposable calibrated oral syringe," and the American Academy of Pediatrics position that "Syringes are the preferred device for dosing accuracy," with liquid medications "dosed only in milliliters and never in teaspoons or other nonmetric units" (p. 709).

Watch for

(C) compounds two errors the chapter names separately — the dropper "varies to a greater extent than the teaspoon or measuring cup," and "Emptying dropper contents into a medicine cup invites additional error" because liquid clings to the sides; (D) fails because families in one study "could not measure a 5-ml dose within 0.5 ml."

30. A toddler refuses a bitter liquid medication. Which approach does the chapter recommend?
  • ✓Mix it with about 5 ml of a sweet-tasting substance such as jam or applesauce, avoiding essential food items
  • BMix it into the child's full bottle of formula so the whole dose is taken
  • CMix it into the child's usual milk, which masks bitterness best
  • DAdd it to a full serving of the child's favorite food so the taste is diluted
Rationale

The Atraumatic Care guidance is to "Mix the drug with a small amount (about 5 ml) of a sweet-tasting substance, such as flavored syrups, jam, fruit purees, sherbet, or applesauce; avoid essential food items because the child may later refuse to eat them" (p. 709).

Watch for

(B) and (C) both use dairy, which the same box rules out "due to risk of interfering with absorption," and (B) additionally risks an incomplete dose if the bottle is not finished; (D) violates the small-amount rule and the essential-food rule at once.

The chapter states each of the three keyed points directly, and reports that aspiration "is no longer recommended by the Centers for Disease Control and Prevention, World Health Organization, American Academy of Pediatrics, or Immunization Action Coalition" (pp. 711–712).

(D) fails on the word always — aspiration may still be indicated for medications such as penicillin into larger muscle groups, so the absolute is what makes it wrong; (E) doubles the narrative limit, which is "Usually 1 ml is the maximum volume that should be administered in a single IM site to small children and older infants."

34. A vesicant medication has leaked into the tissue around a child's intravenous catheter. How is this classified, and what distinguishes it?
  • ✓Extravasation, because the agent is a vesicant; infiltration is the same event with a nonvesicant solution
  • BInfiltration, because the volume is small; extravasation refers to larger leaks
  • CPhlebitis, because any leakage inflames the vessel wall
  • DExtravasation, because it is defined by the degree of tissue swelling rather than the drug
Rationale

The chapter separates the two by the agent: infiltration is a nonvesicant solution or medication into the tissue, while extravasation involves a vesicant, and "A vesicant or sclerosing agent causes varying degrees of cellular damage when even minute amounts escape into surrounding tissue" (p. 722).

Watch for

(B) and (D) both define extravasation by size or severity, which is the intuitive and wrong reading — even minute amounts of a vesicant cause damage, which is exactly why the drug rather than the volume names the event.
