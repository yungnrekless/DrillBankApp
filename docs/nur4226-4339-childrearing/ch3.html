congenital anomaly classificationgeneral
1. A newborn has a congenital amputation of two fingers caused by amniotic bands that wrapped around the developing hand. How is this anomaly classified?
  • ADeformation
  • ✓Disruption
  • CDysplasia
  • DMalformation
Rationale

"Disruptions result from the breakdown of previously normal tissue," and the chapter's own example is "Congenital amputations caused by amniotic bands (fibrous strands of amnion that wrap around different body parts during development)" (p. 42).

Watch for

(A) is the reflex pick because both involve an outside force acting on the fetus. A deformation is "caused by extrinsic mechanical forces on normally developing tissue" — the tissue is pushed out of shape but intact, as in club foot from uterine constraint. A disruption destroys tissue that had formed normally.

congenital anomaly classificationgeneral
2. The parents of a newborn with cleft lip ask whether they caused it. Which response reflects the chapter's account?
  • ACleft lip is a disruption, caused by tissue that broke down during development
  • ✓Cleft lip is a malformation: at about 5 weeks the embryo normally has two clefts in that area, and cells divide and migrate between 5 and 7 weeks to fill them in; when that process is abnormal, a cleft remains, and the cause is often unknown
  • CCleft lip results from something the mother ate or was exposed to during the first trimester
  • DNothing the parents did caused it, and there is no known mechanism
Rationale

This is the chapter's own NCLEX question 1, keyed to the response that gives the mechanism without blaming. Cleft lip is given as the example of a malformation — "abnormal formations of organs or body parts resulting from an abnormal developmental process" — and most malformations occur before 12 weeks of gestation (p. 42).

Watch for

(A) is the classification trap the book's own item offers, and it is wrong on the definitions: nothing broke down, the tissue never came together. (C) assigns blame, which the chapter's guidance directly opposes — parents' guilt must be "assessed and addressed" and their invented explanations "assessed and dispelled." (D) is a bare denial that gives the family no mechanism, where the chapter asks for "simple and truthful explanations."

congenital anomaly classificationgeneral
3. An infant is born with a recessed mandible; the tongue is abnormally placed, blocking the palatal shelves from meeting, and a cleft palate results. The infant is at risk for obstructive apnea. What is this pattern called, and why?
  • AA syndrome, because the anomalies occur together
  • BAn association, because no cause has been determined
  • ✓A sequence, because a single anomaly led to a cascade of additional anomalies
  • DA dysplasia, because the tissues are abnormally organized
Rationale

"When a single anomaly leads to a cascade of additional anomalies, the pattern of defects is referred to as a sequence" (p. 45). Pierre Robin sequence "begins with the abnormal development of the mandible, resulting in abnormal placement of the tongue," which obstructs "the migration of the palatal shelves toward the midline, and a cleft palate remains."

Watch for

(A) is the reflex pick because any set of anomalies occurring together looks like a syndrome. A syndrome is "a recognized pattern of anomalies resulting from a single specific *cause*"; a sequence has a single first *defect* that produces the rest. The nursing point here is the airway: recessed mandible plus displaced tongue equals obstructive apnea.

congenital anomaly classificationgeneral
4. A nurse examines a newborn and finds a sacral dimple, an extra nipple, and a café-au-lait spot. How should these be characterized, and what do they mean together?
  • AThree major anomalies, requiring immediate genetic referral
  • ✓Minor anomalies or normal variants with no serious consequences — but three or more minor anomalies should raise suspicion of an underlying syndrome
  • CMinor anomalies, which never warrant further evaluation
  • DMajor anomalies, since any structural finding is major by definition
Rationale

The chapter contrasts "major structural abnormalities with serious medical, surgical, or quality-of-life consequences" against "minor anomalies or normal variants with no serious consequences, such as a sacral dimple, an extra nipple, or a café-au-lait spot" (p. 45). The dysmorphology guidance adds: "When three or more minor anomalies are identified, nurses should suspect the possibility of an underlying syndrome" (p. 76).

Watch for

(C) is the reflex pick once the findings are correctly labeled minor, and it drops the counting rule that makes the classification useful. The parallel rule for major anomalies runs the other way: one major anomaly means look for more.

5. A nurse explains the difference between genetics and genomics to a student. Which statement is accurate?
  • AGenetics studies all of a person's genes and their interactions; genomics studies a single gene
  • ✓Genetics studies single genes and their effect on relatively rare disorders; genomics studies all the genes in the genome and their interactions with each other and the environment
  • CThe two terms are interchangeable
  • DGenetics applies to inherited disease and genomics only to cancer
Rationale

The same one-gene-versus-all-of-them split separates the two terms, and it repeats for pharmacogenetics versus pharmacogenomics (pp. 41–43).

Watch for

(A) is the mirror error — a straight swap of the two definitions, which is the only way this pair is ever tested.

6. A parent says her child's heart defect "must be genetic because he was born with it." How should the nurse respond?
  • AShe is correct; present at birth means inherited
  • ✓A congenital disease may or may not be genetic, and a genetic disease may or may not be congenital, although the causative genes are present at birth
  • CShe is incorrect; congenital defects are never genetic
  • DThe distinction has no clinical significance
Rationale

"A congenital disease may or may not be genetic. Likewise, a genetic disease may or may not be congenital, although the causative genes are present at birth" (p. 41).

Watch for

(A) collapses two different claims — present at birth, and inherited — into one. The related error the chapter names is assuming a genetic disorder always shows a positive family history: "It is therefore erroneous to consider all genetic disorders as having a positive family history."

genetics vocabulary and chromosome numbergeneral
7. A karyotype shows 69 chromosomes — three complete sets. How is this classified, and what does it mean for viability?
  • AAneuploid, and lethal
  • ✓Euploid, because 69 is a multiple of 23 — and still lethal
  • CEuploid, and therefore compatible with normal development
  • DTrisomic, because there are three of each chromosome
Rationale

Euploidy means a multiple of 23; aneuploidy means a chromosome number that is not a multiple of 23 (p. 45). Triploidy is euploid and still lethal.

Watch for

(C) is the reflex inference that a "balanced" multiple must be viable, and it is exactly why this pair is tested — euploid describes the arithmetic, not the outcome. Note the related severity rule: "The loss of a chromosome and its related complement of genes is overall more detrimental than the addition of a chromosome," so a monosomy (45) is worse than a trisomy (47).

8. A child has a karyotype of 46,XX/47,XX+21 — two cell lines. When did the error occur, and what is this called?
  • AMeiotic nondisjunction before fertilization, producing whole-body aneuploidy
  • ✓Mitotic nondisjunction after fertilization during early embryonic development of a normal zygote, producing mosaicism
  • CA robertsonian translocation inherited from a carrier parent
  • DA structural deletion arising during gametogenesis
Rationale

Mosaic Down syndrome "results from mitotic nondisjunction during early embryonic development of a normal zygote" (p. 48). The two mechanisms differ by timing: meiotic nondisjunction happens before fertilization and every cell carries the abnormality; mitotic nondisjunction happens after, so only a subset does (p. 46).

Watch for

(A) is the mirror error of this pair, and the tell is in the karyotype itself — two cell lines separated by a slash cannot come from an error that preceded fertilization.

9. The parents of a child with mosaic Down syndrome are told by a relative that the mosaic form is "the mild kind." How should the nurse respond?
  • AThe relative is correct; mosaicism always produces milder disease
  • ✓Children with mosaic Down syndrome do not necessarily have a better developmental outcome than those with free trisomy; the proportion of trisomic cells in various tissues and organs plays a role
  • CMosaic Down syndrome is more severe than free trisomy
  • DThe distinction cannot be made until the child is school age
Rationale

"Contrary to what one might expect, children with mosaic Down syndrome do not necessarily have a better developmental outcome than those with free trisomy type. The proportion of trisomic cells in various tissues and organs play a role in the child's developmental potential and syndrome-associated potential health problems" (p. 48).

Watch for

(A) is a true statement about the wrong condition, which is what makes it dangerous. The chapter does say mosaicism produces milder expression in Klinefelter and Turner syndromes, one page away. Generalizing that to Down syndrome is contradicted by the text, and it is a false reassurance given to a family.

down syndrome configurations and recurrence riskgeneral
10. A newborn has Down syndrome. Which chromosomal configuration is most likely, and what proportion of cases does it account for?
  • ✓Free trisomy 21, about 92% of cases
  • BTranslocation, about 92% of cases
  • CMosaicism, about 60% of cases
  • DFree trisomy 21, about 50% of cases
Rationale

"Trisomy encompasses 92% of all cases of Down syndrome," with the extra chromosome 21 unattached and segregating freely during meiosis; translocation "accounts for approximately 4% of all male and female Down syndrome cases," and the mosaic form is rarer still (pp. 46–48).

Watch for

(B) swaps the two proportions, and the consequence matters: free trisomy is not inherited and its risk rises with maternal age, while translocation may come from a balanced carrier parent and is not age related.

11. A 38-year-old woman is told her risk of having a baby with Down syndrome is higher than a 25-year-old's, but she has read that most babies with Down syndrome are born to younger mothers. How should the nurse explain this?
  • AThe published age-risk figures are outdated
  • ✓Both are true: the risk per pregnancy increases linearly with maternal age, but because young women have more babies, about 75% of babies with trisomy 21 are born to younger mothers
  • CMaternal age affects only the translocation form
  • DThe 75% figure applies only to mosaic Down syndrome
Rationale

"The risk for this type of Down syndrome increases linearly with increasing maternal age," and "however, because young women have more babies, about 75% of babies with trisomy 21 are born to younger mothers" (p. 47).

Watch for

(A) treats an apparent contradiction as an error in the data. Both statements are true at once — one is a rate, the other a count — and a distractor is built by presenting either one alone.

down syndrome configurations and recurrence riskgeneral
12. A woman is found to be a balanced translocation carrier after the birth of a child with Down syndrome. Which recurrence figure should be used in counseling her?
  • A33%, the theoretic risk
  • ✓Approximately 15%, the observed risk when the mother is the carrier
  • CLess than 10%, the observed risk when the father is the carrier
  • D100%, since she is a known carrier
Rationale

The theoretic chance for a carrier parent with a normal partner is 33%, but the observed risks are lower and differ by which parent carries it: approximately 15% when the mother is the carrier and less than 10% when the father is (pp. 47–48). The chance of producing another balanced carrier "approaches 50%."

Watch for

(A) is the wrong number for counseling and the most likely one to be offered, because it is the figure the genetics reasoning produces. Observation gives a lower number, and (C) is the correct observed figure for the *other* parent — sex of the carrier changes the answer.

13. A father is found to carry a 21;21 translocation. What is his chance of producing a pregnancy with Down syndrome?
  • AAbout 15%
  • B33%
  • ✓100%
  • DLess than 10%
Rationale

"A carrier mother [45,XX,t(21;21)] or father [45,XY,t(21;21)] of the translocation would have a 100% chance of producing a pregnancy with Down syndrome because the other parent normally would contribute one chromosome 21." The chapter calls this "one of the rare examples in genetics where an abnormality is passed on to all living progeny" (p. 48).

Watch for

(D) applies the ordinary paternal-carrier figure, which is correct for every translocation except this one. The 21;21 case is a named exception, and it is one of only two 100% situations in the chapter — the other is the affected homozygote in autosomal dominant disease.

sex chromosome aneuploidiesgeneral
14. A nurse is asked which sex chromosome aneuploidy is most common and what cognitive outcome to expect. What is accurate?
  • ATurner syndrome is most common, and moderate cognitive impairment is typical
  • ✓Klinefelter syndrome is the most common of all sex chromosome aneuploidies, and mental development is normal in most cases, with a mean full-scale IQ between 85 and 90
  • CTriple-X is most common, and cognitive impairment is severe
  • DXYY is most common, and cognitive development is normal
Rationale

This is the keyed option in the chapter's own NCLEX question 2. "Klinefelter syndrome is the most common of all sex chromosome aneuploidies," and "Mental development is normal in most cases, with a mean full-scale IQ between 85 and 90," with specific deficits in "expressive language, auditory processing, and auditory memory" (p. 48). More generally, sex chromosome number changes usually do "not produce the profound effects that are associated with the autosomal trisomies."

Watch for

(A) attaches autosomal-trisomy expectations to a sex chromosome aneuploidy, which is the comparison the chapter's general rule is written to settle.

15. A girl with Turner syndrome is 11 years old. Her growth was normal until age 3 and then gradually drifted away from the curve. Her parents ask whether she will have a growth spurt at puberty. What is accurate?
  • AShe will have a prepubertal growth spurt and then mostly stop growing
  • ✓There is no prepubertal growth spurt; treatment for decreased growth velocity includes growth hormone and anabolic steroids, and the female hormones given around the time puberty would occur may further stunt growth and must be used judiciously
  • CGrowth will normalize once estrogen therapy begins
  • DGrowth hormone is contraindicated in Turner syndrome
Rationale

"There is no prepubertal growth spurt"; "The child's growth is usually normal until 3 years of age and then slows, gradually drifting away from the normal growth curve"; and "It is common practice to administer female hormones around the time that puberty would occur to provide the girl with Turner syndrome some secondary sex characteristics; however, the female hormones may further stunt growth and must be used judiciously" (p. 48).

Watch for

(A) is the chapter's own NCLEX question 2 distractor and it reverses the text. (C) misses the trade-off that defines this condition's management: the hormones that give secondary sex characteristics cost height.

16. A nurse is asked about gynecologic findings in triple-X. Which is accurate?
  • APremature menarche and delayed menopause
  • ✓Delayed menarche and premature menopause
  • CNormal menarche with infertility
  • DPrimary amenorrhea
Rationale

"females with triple-X display a normal phenotype, with an increased risk of learning disabilities compared with their euploid sisters. Gynecologic complications include delayed menarche and premature menopause" (p. 49). The offspring of triple-X women are largely normal.

(A) is the chapter's own NCLEX question 2 distractor and reverses both halves of the text — which is why holding the order matters rather than just the two words.

17. A student states that the XYY karyotype is associated with criminal behavior. How should this be corrected?
  • AThe association is well established and should be included in counseling
  • ✓The early studies linking this karyotype with criminal behavior were significantly flawed by selection bias, having been conducted among inmates of penal institutions; the association the chapter names now is increased risk for autism spectrum disorder
  • CXYY is not associated with any clinical finding
  • DThe association applies only to mosaic XYY
Rationale

The genotype "was reported in the early 1960s by Patricia Jacobs, a Scottish cytogeneticist who detected an increased frequency of double-Y men among inmates of penal institutions in Great Britain. This and subsequent early studies that linked this karyotype with criminal behavior were significantly flawed by selection bias." The chapter names autism spectrum disorder risk as the current association (p. 48).

Watch for

(A) repeats a retracted claim, and the chapter includes the history precisely so a nurse can recognize it. (C) overcorrects — there is a named association; it is simply not the one folklore supplies.

18. A man with achondroplasia and his genotypically normal partner ask about the risk to each pregnancy. What should the nurse tell them, and on what assumption does the figure rest?
  • A25%, assuming he is heterozygous
  • ✓50%, assuming he is heterozygous — which the chapter says is much more likely than homozygous
  • C100%, because achondroplasia is 100% penetrant
  • DThe risk cannot be estimated without testing
Rationale

"Children of an affected parent have a 50% chance of inheriting the mutant allele and being affected" (Box 3.2, p. 44). The chapter states the assumption before giving the number: "if one parent is affected, he or she is much more likely to be heterozygous (have one mutant allele) than homozygous," and an individual with two dominant mutant alleles "will experience physical or cognitive abnormalities at a much more severe level" (p. 50).

Watch for

(C) confuses penetrance with transmission. The achondroplasia mutation is 100% penetrant, meaning anyone who inherits it will show it — not that everyone inherits it. Only the rare affected-homozygote mating gives 100% affected offspring.

19. The same couple has three children, all affected, and asks whether the fourth is therefore safe. What is the accurate response?
  • AThe fourth child is very unlikely to be affected, since three of four already are
  • ✓Each pregnancy carries the same 50% chance; 50% of offspring do not necessarily inherit the gene mutation, and chance has no memory
  • CThe risk rises with each affected child
  • DAfter three affected children the couple should be retested for homozygosity
Rationale

The achondroplasia pedigree in the chapter "demonstrates that although an affected parent has a 50% chance with each pregnancy of transmitting the gene mutation associated with achondroplasia, 50% of offspring do not necessarily inherit the gene mutation" (p. 52). The Nursing Alert states it for recessive disease in the same words: "Chance has no memory" (p. 72).

Watch for

(A) is the gambler's-fallacy answer and the most common misconception families bring. Fifty percent is a per-pregnancy probability, not a quota — four children of an affected parent can all be affected or all be unaffected.

20. Two unaffected parents have a second child with the same autosomal dominant disorder. What does the second affected child indicate?
  • AA new de novo mutation occurred twice by coincidence
  • ✓The mutation is present in a subset of the germ cells of one healthy parent — germline mosaicism — since "the birth of other affected children indicates the second possibility"
  • CMisattributed paternity
  • DThe disorder must actually be autosomal recessive
Rationale

A first occurrence in a family "may be due to a new mutation (de novo mutation) in that child or to the presence of the mutation only in a subset of the germ cells of a healthy parent," and "The birth of other affected children indicates the second possibility" (p. 44).

Watch for

(A) is the reflex explanation and it changes the counseling completely — a de novo mutation carries essentially no recurrence risk, while germline mosaicism carries a real one. One affected child is ambiguous; two is not.

21. Two carrier parents ask about the risk in each pregnancy. What are the figures?
  • A50% affected, 25% carrier
  • ✓25% affected, 50% carrier, 25% unaffected noncarrier, in each and every pregnancy
  • C25% affected, 25% carrier
  • D100% carrier
Rationale

"Carrier parents have a 25% chance of producing an affected child and a 50% chance of producing a carrier child in each and every pregnancy" (Box 3.3, p. 53); the remaining 25% follows. Both males and females can be carriers, and males and females are equally likely to be affected.

Watch for

(A) swaps the two figures. The 50% number appears in three different places in this chapter attached to three different things — the dominant transmission risk, the recessive carrier fraction, and the X-linked recessive son's risk — so pairing the number with its pattern is the actual skill.

autosomal inheritance patternsautosomal recessive
22. A man with an autosomal recessive disorder and a partner who is not a carrier ask about their children. What is the answer?
  • A25% of children will be affected
  • B50% of children will be affected
  • ✓No children will be affected, and 100% will be carriers
  • DAll children will be affected
Rationale

The mating of an affected parent with a genotypically normal partner is one "in which 100% of the offspring will be carriers" (p. 57). The affected parent contributes a mutant allele to every child; the noncarrier partner contributes a normal allele to every child.

Watch for

(B) imports the autosomal *dominant* figure, which is the reflex whenever a stem says one parent is affected. The result here is counterintuitive and clean: zero affected, all carriers.

23. An unaffected couple has a child with cystic fibrosis. The mother asks how the genetics laboratory can be sure they are both carriers when neither was tested. What is the basis?
  • ✓They are obligate carriers, because each must have contributed a mutant allele — assuming lack of misattributed paternity
  • BCarrier status is assumed from ethnicity rather than from the child's diagnosis
  • COnly the mother can be presumed a carrier
  • DNothing can be concluded without testing both parents
Rationale

"In the case of an unaffected couple who produce a child with a recessive disease, identification is straightforward, assuming lack of misattributed paternity; because they each must contribute a mutant allele, they are considered obligate carriers, even in the absence of specific carrier testing for that gene" (p. 53). For scale, "1 in 25 persons in the United States and Northern European populations carries a recessive gene mutation for CF."

Watch for

(D) is defensible caution and is not what the chapter says. Note also that the chapter names one alternative explanation for an apparently impossible pedigree: uniparental disomy can produce a recessive disease when only one parent is a carrier, so a single-carrier finding is not proof of misattributed paternity.

24. A couple who are first cousins ask how much their relationship raises the risk of birth defects, and whether more distant relatives should worry. What should the nurse say?
  • ✓The first-cousin risk is about 5% compared with a general population risk of 2% to 3%, and this is usually a concern only for first cousins or closer relatives
  • BThe first-cousin risk is about 25%, and any blood relationship is a concern
  • CConsanguinity raises the risk only for autosomal dominant disorders
  • DThere is no measurable increase in risk for first cousins
Rationale

"The first-cousin risk for birth defects is about 5% compared with the general population risk of 2% to 3%. The primary risk increase is for autosomal recessive disorders, in which the chance that two individuals carry the same rare mutation is increased if they are related," and "contrary to popular belief, this is usually a concern only for first cousins or closer relatives" (p. 74).

Watch for

(B) overstates a real but modest increase — roughly double, not catastrophic — and both halves of that are worth saying to a family. (C) inverts the mechanism: relatedness raises the chance that two people carry the same rare recessive mutation.

x-linked inheritance patternsautosomal recessive
25. A pedigree shows an affected father and an affected son. What does this observation establish?
  • AThe disorder is X-linked recessive
  • BThe disorder is X-linked dominant
  • ✓Male-to-male transmission rules out X-linked inheritance, because men give their X chromosome to their daughters and their Y to their sons
  • DThe disorder must be mitochondrial
Rationale

"It is important to remember that men give their X chromosomes to their daughters (and their Y to their sons), so in analyzing a family pedigree, male-to-male transmission of a gene rules out X-linked inheritance" (p. 57). The bank calls this the single most useful pedigree rule in the chapter.

Watch for

(D) is wrong for the opposite reason and is worth holding alongside: mitochondrial inheritance is maternal only, because "Mitochondria are not contained within the sperm head," so a father transmits neither an X-linked nor a mitochondrial disorder to his son.

26. A man with hemophilia A and a genotypically normal partner ask about their children. What is the risk?
  • A50% of sons affected, 50% of daughters carriers
  • ✓All daughters are carriers and none is affected; no sons are affected
  • CAll daughters are affected and no sons are affected
  • D25% of all children affected
Rationale

Box 3.4 states: "All daughters of an affected male are (heterozygous) carriers; none is affected (homozygous)," and father-to-son transmission of X-linked traits is not possible (p. 58).

27. A woman who carries the Duchenne muscular dystrophy mutation and her genotypically normal partner ask about their children. What is the risk?
  • AAll daughters are carriers and no sons are affected
  • ✓Sons have a 50% chance of being affected, and daughters have a 50% chance of being carriers
  • C25% of sons are affected
  • DAll sons are affected
Rationale

"Sons of carrier females have a 50% chance of inheriting the mutant allele and being affected (hemizygous). Daughters have a 50% risk of inheriting the mutant allele and being (heterozygous) carriers" (Box 3.4, p. 58).

Watch for

(A) is the mirror error of the previous item — the affected-father pattern applied to a carrier mother. Holding the two matings as a pair is what makes an X-linked recessive pedigree readable at a glance.

x-linked inheritance patternsautosomal recessive
28. A man has an X-linked dominant disorder. What is the risk to his daughters?
  • AAll are carriers and none is affected
  • B50% are affected
  • ✓All daughters of an affected man are affected, and each has a 50% chance of passing the mutant allele to her own sons and daughters
  • DNone is affected, because he transmits his Y to his daughters
Rationale

"all daughters of an affected man are affected and have a 50% chance of passing on the mutant allele to their sons and daughters" (p. 59). Affected men do not transmit the mutant allele to their sons, and females "because of the random nature of X inactivation, are usually less severely affected than males."

Watch for

(A) is the X-linked recessive answer for the same mating, and this pair is the sharpest discrimination in the section: an affected father gives carrier daughters in recessive disease and affected daughters in dominant disease.

x-linked inheritance patternsautosomal recessive
29. A girl is a known carrier of an X-linked recessive disorder but has mild symptoms. Her parents were told carriers are unaffected. What explains her symptoms?
  • AShe must actually be homozygous for the mutation
  • ✓X inactivation is random in each cell, and a female who expresses a phenotype may have a greater proportion of the X chromosome carrying the normal allele inactivated within the tissues associated with the disorder
  • CCarriers of X-linked disorders are always symptomatic
  • DShe has a new mutation on her second X chromosome
Rationale

Soon after fertilization one X in each female cell is inactivated by methylation, "whether the paternal or maternally inherited X is inactivated is random in each cell," and once inactivated it stays so through all subsequent divisions. "Females who express a phenotype may have a greater proportion of X chromosome with the normal allele inactivated within the tissues or organs associated with the disorder" (p. 58).

Watch for

(A) is possible but rare, and it is not the explanation the chapter gives for the usual case. This is a mechanism, not an exception to the rule — and note the parallel fact for males: there are no carrier males, because "Hemizygous males are either normal or affected," so on the X chromosome dominant and recessive stop mattering for a man.

30. Two children have deletions of the same region of chromosome 15 but entirely different syndromes. What accounts for this?
  • ✓Genomic imprinting — which parent's copy is expressed differs, so losing the paternal copy gives Prader-Willi syndrome and losing the maternal copy gives Angelman syndrome
  • BVariable expressivity of the same disorder
  • COne child has a deletion and the other a duplication
  • DUniparental disomy in one child only
Rationale

Prader-Willi and Angelman syndromes are the chapter's headline imprinting pair: a paternal deletion versus a maternal deletion of the same chromosome 15 region (p. 60). Lose the father's copy and you get Prader-Willi.

Watch for

(D) names a real and related phenomenon on the same page, and the two are a flagged pair: imprinting is about which parent's copy is expressed; uniparental disomy is about both copies coming from one parent. Uniparental disomy is also the chapter's explanation for a recessive disease appearing when only one parent is a carrier.

31. A family history shows a disorder that appears at progressively earlier ages and greater severity in each successive generation. What is this called, and what is its molecular basis?
  • AReduced penetrance
  • ✓Anticipation, produced by expansion mutations in which a repeated sequence lengthens across generations
  • CLocus heterogeneity
  • DGermline mosaicism
Rationale

Expansion mutations produce anticipation (p. 59). Fragile X gives the repeat ranges: about 5 to 40 repeats is normal, 59 to 200 is a premutation, and hundreds to thousands is a full mutation.

Watch for

(A) is the other half of a flagged pair and describes something different — penetrance is whether a trait shows at all, while expressivity is how much it shows. Neither produces a generation-by-generation shift in age of onset.

32. A student describes an oncogene as a foreign or viral gene introduced into a cell. How should this be corrected?
  • AThe description is accurate
  • ✓Oncogenes are normally involved in cell growth and division; the three gene classes in hereditary cancer are oncogenes that drive growth, tumor suppressor genes that inhibit growth, and mismatch repair genes that repair errors
  • COncogenes are inherited only in the germline
  • DOncogenes and tumor suppressor genes are the same class
Rationale

"In reality, oncogenes are normally involved in cell growth and division" (p. 60), and the chapter separates the three classes by function: drives growth, inhibits growth, repairs errors.

(A) is folklore reinforced by the word itself. Worth holding alongside: a germline p53 mutation gives Li-Fraumeni syndrome while a somatic p53 mutation gives sporadic cancers such as bladder cancer, and BRCA1 is associated with female breast and ovarian cancer while BRCA2 adds male breast and prostate cancer.

33. A child has a mitochondrial disorder. Which statement about its inheritance and prenatal testing is accurate?
  • ABoth parents contribute mitochondrial DNA, so either may be the source
  • ✓It is inherited from the mother only, because mitochondria are not contained within the sperm head; and because of heteroplasmy it is not possible to predict the unborn child's phenotype based on prenatal test results
  • CIt follows an autosomal recessive pattern
