acute diarrhea and oral rehydration in childrengeneral
1. A 16-month-old is being discharged after 2 days of acute diarrhea and is now rehydrated. What diet should the nurse recommend?
  • ✓A regular diet as the child's appetite warrants
  • BThe BRAT diet of bananas, rice, applesauce, and toast
  • CClear liquids for 24 hours, then advance slowly
  • DChicken or beef broth until the stools are formed
Rationale

The chapter states that early reintroduction of a normal diet "has no adverse effects and actually lessens the severity and duration of the illness and improves weight gain when compared with the gradual reintroduction of foods," and that a regular diet including milk can generally be offered after rehydration (pp. 836–837).

Watch for

All three wrong options are named and rejected by the chapter's own Nursing Alert — the BRAT diet "is contraindicated" because it is low in energy, protein, and electrolytes; broth "is not given" because it has excessive sodium and inadequate carbohydrate; and clear fluids "do not help diarrhea" because of high carbohydrate content and high osmolality.

2. A nurse is giving oral rehydration solution to a 2-year-old with moderate dehydration who has vomited twice. What should the nurse do?
  • ✓Continue the solution in 5- to 10-ml increments every 1 to 5 minutes, since vomiting is not a contraindication
  • BStop oral rehydration and request intravenous fluids
  • CWithhold all fluids for 2 hours to rest the stomach, then restart
  • DSwitch to a carbonated beverage, which is better tolerated when vomiting
Rationale

The chapter states that "vomiting is not a contraindication" to oral rehydration therapy and directs 5- to 10-ml increments every 1 to 5 minutes by spoon or small syringe for young children (p. 836).

Watch for

(B) is the reflex escalation the chapter's whole rehydration section exists to prevent, calling oral therapy "more effective, safer, less painful, and less costly than IV rehydration"; note that 10 ml/kg of solution is added for every loose stool or episode of vomiting.

3. A nurse is teaching parents of a child with acute diarrhea. Which instructions are correct? Select all that apply.
Select all that apply · scored all or nothing
  • ✓Continue breastfeeding if it is already established
  • ✓Replace each diarrheal stool with about 10 ml/kg of oral rehydration solution
  • ✓Expect a slightly higher stool output initially when a normal diet is resumed
  • ✓Avoid caffeinated beverages
  • EOffer fruit juice and gelatin, which supply fluid and calories
Rationale

The chapter directs continuing breastfeeding, replacing ongoing stool losses with about 10 ml/kg of solution, warning parents that "a slightly higher stool output initially occurs with continuation of a normal diet," and avoiding caffeinated beverages because caffeine is a mild diuretic (pp. 836–837).

(E) names two of the fluids the Nursing Alert rules out — fruit juices and gelatin have "a high carbohydrate content, a very low electrolyte content, and a high osmolality"; the chapter adds that the nutritional benefit of resuming a normal diet "outweigh[s] the potential increase in stool frequency."

4. A 10-month-old with acute diarrhea has no clinical signs of dehydration. What does the chapter direct?
  • ✓Oral rehydration therapy is not needed, but maintenance fluid and replacement of stool losses continue
  • BOral rehydration therapy at 50 ml/kg over 4 hours is still indicated
  • CIntravenous fluids should be started prophylactically
  • DAll oral intake should be withheld for 12 hours
Rationale

The chapter states that "Infants without clinical signs of dehydration do not need ORT," and Table 25.4's footnote adds that "If no signs of dehydration are present, rehydration therapy is not necessary," while maintenance therapy and stool-loss replacement continue (p. 836).

Watch for

(B) applies the mild-dehydration protocol to a child who is not dehydrated, which over-treats; the 50 ml/kg over 4 hours figure belongs to mild dehydration at 5% to 6% and 100 ml/kg over 4 hours to moderate at 7% to 9%.

5. A child's stool culture grows nontyphoidal *Salmonella*. The parents ask why no antibiotic has been prescribed. What should the nurse explain?
  • ✓Antibiotics are not given in uncomplicated cases because they prolong the carrier state
  • BAntibiotics are withheld only until sensitivity results return
  • CAntibiotics are ineffective against all bacterial causes of diarrhea
  • DAntibiotics are withheld because the organism is resistant to all oral agents
Rationale

The chapter notes that antibiotics "may prolong the carrier period for bacteria such as *Salmonella*," reserving them for children under 3 months, those on immunosuppressive medication, or those with shock, severe malnutrition, dysentery, suspected cholera, or suspected giardiasis (p. 837).

Watch for

(C) generalizes one organism's policy to all, which the chapter contradicts case by case — *Salmonella typhi* must be treated, *Shigella* antibiotics shorten illness and lower mortality, and erythromycin is the drug of choice for *Campylobacter*. Antimotility agents are wrong across essentially all of them.

6. A newborn has not passed meconium by 36 hours of age and has bilious vomiting with abdominal distention. What should the nurse suspect?
  • ✓Hirschsprung disease, since 99% of term infants pass meconium within the first 48 hours
  • BHypertrophic pyloric stenosis, since vomiting and distention are present
  • CNormal transition, since meconium may be delayed up to 5 days
  • DIntussusception, since vomiting and distention are present
Rationale

Box 25.10 gives failure to pass meconium within 24 to 48 hours after birth as the newborn criterion, with refusal to feed, bilious vomiting, and abdominal distention, and the chapter notes that "Typically, 99% of term infants pass meconium in the first 48 hours of life, whereas few infants with HD do so" (p. 844).

Watch for

(B) is excluded by the bilious vomiting, since pyloric stenosis produces nonbilious vomiting and typically begins around 3 weeks rather than at birth; note that this chapter gives several different meconium thresholds for different conditions, so the disease named in the stem determines which number applies.

hirschsprung disease and childhood constipationgeneral
7. A child is undergoing anorectal manometry for suspected Hirschsprung disease. Which finding supports the diagnosis?
  • ✓The external sphincter contracts normally, but the internal sphincter fails to relax
  • BThe internal sphincter relaxes normally, but the external sphincter fails to contract
  • CBoth sphincters fail to contract
  • DBoth sphincters relax simultaneously with rectal distention
Rationale

The chapter states that on anorectal manometry "In HD the external sphincter contracts normally but the internal sphincter fails to relax" (p. 845), which follows from the absence of ganglion cells and the resulting loss of the rectosphincteric reflex.

Watch for

(B) reverses the two sphincters, which is the single likeliest error here; the underlying physiology is that normally "when a stool bolus enters the rectum, the internal sphincter relaxes and the stool is evacuated," and in this disease it does not.

8. A 7-month-old with known Hirschsprung disease develops explosive watery diarrhea and fever and appears significantly ill. What should the nurse recognize?
  • ✓Enterocolitis, which is the most serious complication of the disease
  • BViral gastroenteritis, which is unrelated to the underlying disease
  • CResolution of the obstruction, since stool is now passing
  • DAn expected response to the preoperative low-fiber diet
Rationale

Box 25.10 lists "signs of enterocolitis — explosive watery diarrhea, fever, appears significantly ill" under infancy, and the chapter states that "Enterocolitis is the most serious complication of HD" (pp. 844–845).

Watch for

(C) reads diarrhea as improvement in a disease whose hallmark is obstruction, which is the dangerous misreading; the signs of bowel perforation to watch alongside this are fever, increasing distention, vomiting, increased tenderness, irritability, dyspnea, and cyanosis.

gastroesophageal reflux and its managementgeneral
10. A 5-month-old spits up after most feedings but is gaining weight well and has no respiratory symptoms. How should the nurse classify this, and what management does the chapter direct?
  • ✓Gastroesophageal reflux, which is physiologic and needs no intervention in a thriving infant
  • BGastroesophageal reflux disease, requiring a proton pump inhibitor
  • CGastroesophageal reflux disease, requiring thickened feedings
  • DGastroesophageal reflux, requiring prone positioning for sleep
Rationale

The chapter defines reflux as "the transfer of gastric contents into the esophagus," calls it physiologic, and states that no treatment is needed "for the infant who is thriving and has no respiratory complications"; it becomes disease "when complications such as failure to thrive, respiratory problems, or dysphagia develop" (pp. 845–846).

Watch for

(D) states a true fact and reaches a dangerous conclusion — prone positioning does decrease reflux episodes, but the chapter immediately adds that "due to the risk of sudden infant death syndrome, all infants should sleep in the supine position." Reflux never outranks sudden infant death prevention.

11. An overweight 4-month-old with reflux is being evaluated. The parents ask about adding rice cereal to the formula. What should the nurse explain?
  • ✓Thickened feedings benefit the infant who is underweight from reflux disease, and the additional calories are not beneficial for an infant who is overweight
  • BThickened feedings should be used for every infant with reflux regardless of weight
  • CThickened feedings are contraindicated at any weight because they worsen reflux
  • DThickened feedings should be made with 1 tablespoon of rice cereal per feeding regardless of volume
Rationale

The chapter directs 1 teaspoon to 1 tablespoon of rice cereal per ounce of formula for the infant "who is underweight as a result of GERD," and states that "the additional calories are not beneficial among infants who are overweight," for whom prethickened formulas exist (p. 846).

Watch for

(D) drops the per-ounce basis, which is what makes the amount scale to the feeding; the item turns on the fact that thickening is a calorie intervention as much as a mechanical one.

  • BMetoclopramide is the preferred prokinetic agent in children

13. A child who had a fundoplication now has dysphagia and gas-bloat. What does this suggest?
  • ✓The wrap is too tight
  • BThe wrap is too loose
  • CThe wrap has failed completely and reflux has returned
  • DBarrett esophagus has developed
Rationale

The chapter names the two failure modes of fundoplication — "a wrap that is too tight, causing dysphagia, small bowel obstruction, or gas-bloat; or a wrap that is too loose, causing continuation of symptoms" (p. 846).

Watch for

(B) and (C) both describe the opposite failure, which presents as persistent reflux rather than obstruction; the procedure itself is "passage of the gastric fundus behind the esophagus to encircle the distal esophagus," reserved for recurrent aspiration pneumonia, apnea, severe esophagitis, failure to thrive, or failed medical therapy.

14. A 13-year-old reports periumbilical pain that began this morning, followed by nausea, and now points to the right lower quadrant. How does this sequence fit appendicitis?
  • ✓It fits, because the classic sequence is periumbilical pain first, then nausea, then right lower quadrant pain, and later vomiting with fever
  • BIt does not fit, because vomiting precedes pain in appendicitis
  • CIt does not fit, because appendicitis pain begins in the right lower quadrant
  • DIt fits only if rebound tenderness is also present
Rationale

The chapter states that "Classically, the first symptom of appendicitis is periumbilical pain, followed by nausea, right lower quadrant pain, and, later, vomiting with fever," restating that "Nausea, vomiting, and anorexia typically occur AFTER the pain starts" (p. 848).

Watch for

(D) requires a sign the chapter rules out as a criterion — "Rebound tenderness is not a reliable sign and is extremely painful to the child," and it "is not a finding specific to appendicitis"; the most important physical finding is focal abdominal tenderness, classically at McBurney point.

15. A child with several hours of severe abdominal pain suddenly reports that the pain has stopped. Shortly afterward the pain returns, is diffuse, and the abdomen is rigid. What should the nurse suspect?
  • ✓Perforation with peritonitis, since sudden relief of pain after perforation is followed by diffuse pain with rigid guarding
  • BResolution of the appendicitis, since the pain briefly stopped
  • CIntussusception, since pain is intermittent
  • DGastroenteritis, since the pain is now diffuse
Rationale

The chapter's Nursing Alert lists signs of peritonitis as "sudden relief from pain after perforation; subsequent increase in pain, which is usually diffuse and accompanied by rigid guarding of the abdomen; progressive abdominal distention; tachycardia; rapid, shallow breathing as the child refrains from using abdominal muscles" (p. 849).

Watch for

(B) is the interpretation families and inexperienced clinicians most often make, and it is why the chapter names the transient relief explicitly; perforation can occur within about 48 hours of the initial complaint of pain, and occurs in up to 82% of children under 5.

16. A nurse needs to assess a frightened 5-year-old for abdominal tenderness. Which approach does the chapter recommend?
  • ✓Use the bell of the stethoscope for initial palpation, then follow with gentle manual palpation without lifting the hand
  • BBegin with deep palpation of the right lower quadrant to localize tenderness quickly
  • CTest for rebound tenderness first, since it is the most specific sign
  • DDefer the abdominal examination until imaging results are available
Rationale

The Atraumatic Care box directs the nurse to "use the bell piece for initial palpation of the abdomen for tenderness," since "Children usually endure pressure from the stethoscope that they would not tolerate from a probing hand," followed by manual palpation "using a gentle touch without lifting the hand from the abdomen" (p. 850).

Watch for

(C) leads with the maneuver the chapter calls unreliable and extremely painful; the chapter also offers heel-drop, hopping on one foot, and puffing out or pulling in the abdomen as ways to check for tenderness without probing.

17. A 20-month-old passes a large volume of painless, currant jelly–like stool and becomes pale. Ultrasound of the abdomen is unremarkable. Which diagnosis should the nurse anticipate being investigated?
  • ✓Meckel diverticulum, with a technetium-99 pertechnetate scan
  • BIntussusception, with a repeat ultrasound
  • CAppendicitis, with computed tomography
  • DUlcerative colitis, with colonoscopy
Rationale

The chapter states that "The most common clinical presentation is rectal bleeding caused by ulceration at the junction of the ectopic gastric mucosa and normal ileal mucosa," that the bleeding is "usually painless" and may appear as bright red or currant jelly–like stools, and names the technetium-99 pertechnetate scan as "the most effective diagnostic testing" with sensitivity 80% to 90% and specificity 95% (p. 851).

Watch for

(B) shares the currant jelly stool, which is exactly why this pair is confusable; the discriminator is that intussusception brings sudden colicky pain with the child drawing knees to chest, whereas this bleeding is painless. The rule of 2s places the diverticulum within 2 feet of the ileocecal valve and makes it more common before age 2.

Select all that apply · scored all or nothing
  • ✓Rectal bleeding is common
  • ✓Anal and perianal lesions are rare
  • ✓Fistulas and strictures are rare
  • ✓Colectomy may cure the disease
  • EGrowth retardation is usually severe
Rationale

Table 25.6 assigns common rectal bleeding, rare anal and perianal lesions, and rare fistulas and strictures to ulcerative colitis, and the chapter states that "Because UC is confined to the colon, a colectomy may cure UC" (p. 853).

  • ✓Crohn disease, since growth failure and delayed sexual maturation often precede overt gastrointestinal symptoms by several years
  • BUlcerative colitis, since growth failure is its earliest sign

  • DCeliac disease, since it presents in adolescence with growth failure alone

20. A child with moderate Crohn disease has been on prednisone for 6 weeks and the family asks about continuing it long term. What should the nurse explain?
  • ✓Corticosteroids are used for induction rather than maintenance, because of growth suppression, weight gain, and decreased bone density
  • BCorticosteroids are the preferred maintenance therapy in children with Crohn disease
  • CCorticosteroids may be continued indefinitely if the dose is below 10 mg daily
  • DCorticosteroids are replaced by aminosalicylates, which are equally effective for maintenance in Crohn disease
Rationale

The chapter states that corticosteroids are used for induction in moderate to severe disease and are "not commonly used for maintenance therapy because of their long-term side effects including growth suppression (adrenal suppression), weight gain, and decreased bone density" (pp. 853–855).

Watch for

(D) overstates the role of aminosalicylates, which are induction and maintenance for mild to moderate ulcerative colitis and can induce remission in mild Crohn disease; the agents that have moved front-line for children with severe Crohn disease are the anti-tumor necrosis factor biologics, which have "even improved linear growth in children."

22. A nurse is comparing endoscopic findings in inflammatory bowel disease. Which statement is accurate?
  • ✓Discrete ulcers are commonly seen in Crohn disease, whereas microulcers with diffuse abnormalities are seen in ulcerative colitis
  • BDiscrete ulcers are seen in ulcerative colitis and diffuse inflammation in Crohn disease
  • CBoth diseases produce identical endoscopic appearances
  • DEndoscopy cannot distinguish the two and biopsy is always required

  • BDocument it as a routine finding and continue preparation for the enema

28. A 3-week-old has intermittent bilious vomiting and recurrent abdominal pain. Which diagnosis carries the greatest urgency, and why?
  • ✓Malrotation with volvulus, because it is the most serious type of intestinal obstruction and can cause necrosis, perforation, and death
  • BPyloric stenosis, because dehydration progresses rapidly
  • CHirschsprung disease, because enterocolitis may develop
  • DIntussusception, because the bowel may become ischemic
Rationale

The chapter states that "Malrotation is the most serious type of intestinal obstruction" because complete volvulus compromises blood supply, leading to "intestinal necrosis, peritonitis, perforation, and death," with 80% presenting in the first month and intermittent bilious vomiting as the presentation (p. 862).

Watch for

(B) is excluded by the bilious vomiting, which pyloric stenosis does not produce because the obstruction is proximal to the ampulla; an upper gastrointestinal series is named as the most accurate imaging study, and short-bowel syndrome is a postoperative complication because of the extent of some lesions.
