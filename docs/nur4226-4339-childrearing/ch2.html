family definitions and structure typesgeneral
1. A nursing student asks the instructor for the textbook's definition of family. What is the accurate response?
  • AA family is a married couple and their dependent children
  • ✓There is no universal definition of family; a family is what an individual considers it to be
  • CA family is any group sharing a household and pooling income
  • DThe Census Bureau's four categories are the accepted definition
Rationale

The chapter opens by refusing a single definition: "The term family has been defined in many different ways according to the individual's own frame of reference, values, or discipline. There is no universal definition of family; a family is what an individual considers it to be" (p. 15).

Watch for

(D) is the reflex pick for a learner who has just memorized the four Census definitions. Those four are a classification the Census Bureau uses, not the chapter's definition of the term, and the chapter names numerous other family types outside them.

2. A child lives with his mother, his mother's unmarried partner, and a sibling who is the partner's biologic child. No other relatives live in the home. Which Census Bureau category does this household fit?
  • ATraditional nuclear family
  • ✓Nuclear family
  • CExtended family
  • DBlended family only, since the parents are unmarried
Rationale

The nuclear family is "composed of two parents and their children. The parent-child relationship may be biologic, step, adoptive, or foster. Sibling ties may be biologic, step, half, or adoptive. The parents are not necessarily married. No other relatives or nonrelatives are present in the household" (p. 18).

Watch for

(A) is the pick that misses two requirements at once. The traditional nuclear family requires the couple to be married and all children to be biologic; the plain nuclear family requires neither. A distractor that makes marriage the only difference gets half the discrimination.

3. Two children in a household share the same mother but have different fathers. How are they related?
  • AStepsiblings, because one father is not the biologic parent of both
  • ✓Half-siblings, because they share one biologic parent
  • CStepsiblings, because the household is blended
  • DFull siblings, because they share a residence and a mother
Rationale

"Stepsiblings do not share a common biologic parent; the biologic parent of one child is the stepparent of the other. Half-siblings share only one biologic parent" (p. 18).

Watch for

(A) and (C) are the mirror error of the pair. Stepsiblings share no biologic parent; half-siblings share one. That single count is the whole distinction, and either presence of a stepparent in the home tempts the wrong label.

4. A nurse admits a child whose grandmother brings him in, states that she has reared him since infancy, and answers all history questions while the child's young mother stands quietly beside her. What should the nurse recognize about this extended family?
  • AThe mother is the legal decision-maker and must be addressed directly for all teaching
  • ✓The older relative may hold the authority and make decisions in consultation with the young parents, so identifying the actual decision-maker is part of the assessment
  • CThe grandmother's involvement suggests the mother is neglectful and warrants a referral
  • DExtended family arrangements delay care and should be discouraged
Rationale

"Within the extended family, grandparents often find themselves rearing their grandchildren. Young parents are often considered too young or too inexperienced to make decisions independently. Often, the older relative holds the authority and makes decisions in consultation with the young parents" (p. 18). Sharing a residence also "assists with the management of scarce resources and provides child care for working families."

Watch for

(C) reads a culturally patterned arrangement as a red flag. The chapter presents extended-family authority descriptively and connects it to its later instruction to ask families what the nurse should know about them rather than to apply assumptions.

5. A nurse notes that a father's job loss has changed the mother's work hours, the children's school arrangements, and the family's mealtimes. Which family systems theory assumption does this illustrate?
  • ✓Circular causality — a change in any one part of a family system affects all other parts
  • BDirect causality — each change produces a single predictable effect
  • CMorphostasis — the family resists all change
  • DBoundary maintenance — the family closes itself to the environment
Rationale

Table 2.1 states the assumption as "A change in any one part of a family system affects all other parts of the family system (circular causality)" (p. 15). General systems theory "expanded scientific thought from a simplistic view of direct cause and effect."

Watch for

(B) is the chapter's own NCLEX question 2 distractor, and it is a one-word inversion of the printed line — direct for circular. That question's other distractors work the same way: react for initiate, primarily for as-well-as.

6. A family in crisis begins praising a child's small successes to encourage a change in his behavior, while a grandparent insists the household routine stay exactly as it always has been. Which two processes are at work?
  • APositive feedback in the praise, since praise is by definition positive feedback, and negative feedback in the criticism
  • ✓Positive feedback in the effort to initiate change, and negative feedback in the effort to resist it
  • CMorphogenesis in the grandparent's stance and morphostasis in the parents'
  • DNeither; feedback applies only to communication, not to behavior change
Rationale

"Positive feedback initiates change; negative feedback resists change" (p. 16). Feedback processes "help identify strengths and needs and determine how well goals are accomplished."

Watch for

(A) is the reflex pick and the chapter's flagged inversion: positive and negative here mean change-initiating and change-resisting, not praise and criticism. (C) reverses the other pair — morphogenesis is change, morphostasis is no change, and "a balance between morphogenesis (change) and morphostasis (no change) is necessary."

7. A couple describes the birth of their first child as joyful and also exhausting and destabilizing. Which family stress theory assumption applies?
  • AOnly negative events function as stressors for a family system
  • ✓Stress is an inevitable part of family life, and any event, even if positive, can be stressful for the family
  • CStressors affect individual members but not the family as a unit
  • DNormative transitions are excluded from stress theory, which addresses situational stressors only
Rationale

Table 2.1's first family stress theory assumption is that "Stress is an inevitable part of family life, and any event, even if positive, can be stressful for family," and the family "encounters both normative expected stressors and unexpected situational stressors over the life cycle." Transition to parenthood is listed first among the theory's applications (p. 15).

Watch for

(A) restricts stressors to negative events, which is the intuitive reading and the one the assumption is written to exclude. (D) drops half the framework — both normative and situational stressors are named.

8. A remarried couple has a 14-year-old from one partner's first marriage and a 2-year-old together. A student tries to place them in Duvall's stages and cannot. What explains the difficulty?
  • ADuvall's stages apply only to families with more than two children
  • ✓The stage marker is the age of the oldest child, which makes the model hard to apply to stepfamilies and single-parent families
  • CThe stage marker is the age of the youngest child, and the model has no stage for toddlers
  • DDuvall's model was replaced by family systems theory and no longer applies
Rationale

"This theory is based on the predictable changes in the family's structure, function, and roles, with the age of the oldest child as the marker for stage transition" (p. 17), and Table 2.1 lists as a limitation that "Use of age of oldest child and marital duration as marker of stage transition sometimes problematic (e.g., in stepfamilies, single-parent families)."

(C) inverts the marker, which is the most likely single item from this section — the chapter states "oldest" twice, once in the narrative and once in the table's limitations.

  • ✓They designate how parenting progresses as a child develops
  • ✓They describe the journey a couple will take as their children mature

These are the keyed options in the chapter's own NCLEX question 1 (p. 38). 10. A father states that his household rules are to be followed without discussion, and that when his daughter asks why, he tells her "because I say so." Which parenting style does this describe?
  • AAuthoritative
  • ✓Authoritarian
  • CPermissive
  • DNeglectful
Rationale

Authoritarian parents "try to control their children's behavior and attitudes through unquestioned mandates" and establish standards of conduct "that they expect to be followed rigidly and unquestioningly." The chapter gives the message as "Do it because I say so" (pp. 20–21).

Watch for

(A) is the reflex pick because the two words differ by four letters and both involve firm rules. The authoritative parent's control is firm but explained; the authoritarian parent's is firm and not open to question.

11. A mother says she explains her reasoning for household rules and asks her son's opinion before deciding, but she rarely enforces the rules and does not want to upset the household routine. Which style does this describe, and what distinguishes it?
  • AAuthoritative, because she explains her reasoning and consults the child
  • ✓Permissive, because explaining reasons does not distinguish the styles — control does, and she exerts little or none
  • CAuthoritarian, because she sets the rules unilaterally
  • DIt cannot be classified without knowing whether she uses corporal punishment
Rationale

Permissive parents "exert little or no control over their children's actions," employ "lax, inconsistent discipline; do not set sensible limits," and "rarely punish the children" — and yet, if rules exist, "the parents explain the underlying reason, elicit the children's opinions, and consult them in decision-making processes" (p. 21).

Watch for

(A) is the trap the fact bank flags explicitly: permissive parents do explain reasons, and so do authoritative parents. Explaining is not the discriminator. The authoritative parent explains and then enforces "firm and consistent" control; the permissive parent explains and does not enforce.

12. A nurse describes a child who is self-reliant, assertive, inquisitive, content, and highly interactive with peers, and who behaves well because of an internal sense of right and wrong rather than fear of being caught. Which parenting style does the chapter associate with this outcome?
  • APermissive
  • BAuthoritarian
  • ✓Authoritative
  • DNo style is associated with a predictable outcome
Rationale

Authoritative parenting produces "children with high self-esteem who are self-reliant, assertive, inquisitive, content, and highly interactive with other children," and fosters "inner-directedness, a conscience that regulates behavior based on feelings of guilt or shame for wrongdoing, not on fear of being caught or punished" (p. 21).

Watch for

(B) can produce children who are "courteous, loyal, honest, and dependable, but docile" — and only conditionally, when "close supervision and affection accompany parental authority." Without that affection the same style "may be associated with both defiant and antisocial behaviors," so a distractor stating the authoritarian outcome unconditionally is wrong.

13. A nurse recognizes that a family's parenting style differs from her own upbringing and from what she considers ideal. What does the chapter direct her to do?
  • ATeach the parents that authoritative parenting produces the best outcomes
  • ✓Give differing styles equal respect, since childrearing is a culturally bound phenomenon
  • CDocument the style as a risk factor and refer to social work
  • DWithhold parenting guidance until the family adopts an authoritative approach
Rationale

"There are differing philosophies in regard to parenting. Childrearing is a culturally bound phenomenon, and children are socialized to behave in ways that are important to their family," and "When working with individual families, nurses should give these differing styles equal respect" (p. 21).

Watch for

(A) is the reflex pick because the chapter really does describe the authoritative outcomes most favorably, and a learner converts that into a teaching mandate. The chapter does not rank the styles for the nurse. The teachable content is specific practices — limits, consistency, timing — not the label on a parent's style.

14. A parent asks the nurse to explain the difference between limit setting and discipline. Which response is accurate?
  • AThey are two words for the same childrearing activity
  • ✓Limit setting establishes the rules or guidelines for behavior; discipline, in its narrow sense, is the action taken to enforce the rules after noncompliance
  • CLimit setting is used with young children and discipline with older children
  • DDiscipline establishes the rules and limit setting enforces them
Rationale

Discipline "means to teach or refers to a set of rules governing conduct. In a narrower sense, it refers to the action taken to enforce the rules after noncompliance." Limit setting "refers to establishing the rules or guidelines for behavior" (p. 21). The relationship between them: "The clearer the limits that are set and the more consistently they are enforced, the less need there is for disciplinary action."

Watch for

(D) is the mirror error — a straight swap of the two definitions. (A) is the more common one in practice, and the chapter separates the terms precisely because they are used interchangeably.

15. A mother says her 3-year-old constantly tests the household rules and asks how to stop this. What should the nurse teach?
  • ALimit testing is misbehavior and should be extinguished by ignoring it
  • ✓Children want and need limits, and testing them is how children learn the extent to which they can manipulate their environment and gain reassurance that others will protect them
  • CLimit testing indicates the limits are too strict and should be relaxed
  • DLimit testing at this age suggests a developmental concern warranting referral
Rationale

"Children want and need limits. Unrestricted freedom is a threat to their security and safety. By testing the limits imposed on them, children learn the extent to which they can manipulate their environment and gain reassurance from knowing that others are there to protect them from potential harm." The chapter lists "Test their limits of control" as the first function limits and discipline serve (p. 21).

Watch for

(A) treats limit-testing as a behavior problem when the chapter names it as a function limits are there to serve. This is the inversion the fact bank flags.

17. A mother tells her misbehaving son in the grocery store, "Wait until your father gets home." How should the nurse evaluate this?
  • AAppropriate, because it avoids embarrassing the child in public
  • ✓Problematic on two counts: delaying punishment weakens its intent, and the phrase conveys negative messages about the other parent
  • CAppropriate, because it gives the mother time to calm down before disciplining
  • DProblematic only because it delays the consequence
Rationale

"Delaying punishment weakens its intent, and practices such as telling the child, Wait until your father comes home are not only ineffectual but also convey negative messages about the other parent" (p. 21). It also violates the Unity principle, which exists "to prevent confusion and alliances between child and one parent."

Watch for

(A) is close to a real exception and misapplies it. When delay is unavoidable — to avoid embarrassment, for instance — the Timing principle requires the parent to "verbally disapprove of the behavior and state that disciplinary action will be implemented." Announced deferral is permitted; silent deferral to the other parent is not. (D) catches only half the fault.

18. A parent reports that since she began ignoring her son's tantrums, the tantrums have become louder and more frequent, and she is ready to abandon the plan. What should the nurse tell her?
  • AThe plan has failed and a different strategy should be selected
  • ✓This is a response burst — the undesired behavior increases after ignoring is initiated because the child is testing whether the parents are serious — and the plan should continue
  • CThe tantrums indicate an underlying disorder and warrant evaluation
  • DShe should ignore the tantrums only when they are mild
Rationale

Response burst is "a phenomenon that occurs when the undesired behavior increases after ignoring is initiated because the child is testing the parents to see if they are serious about the plan" (p. 22). Being aware of it is one of the four requirements for ignoring to work. The chapter warns that when parents "give in and resort to previous patterns of discipline... the behavior is actually reinforced because the child learns that persistence gains parental attention."

Watch for

(A) is the intuitive and the harmful reading, and it is the specific error the chapter is written to prevent. Abandoning the plan mid-burst teaches the child that persistence works — the outcome worse than never having started.

19. A parent lists the types of consequences as natural, logical, unrelated, and transforming. Which statement requires correction?
  • ANatural consequences are those that occur without any intervention
  • BLogical consequences are those directly related to the rule
  • ✓Transforming consequences allow the child to come to the conclusion on his or her own
  • DUnrelated consequences are those imposed deliberately
Rationale

"The strategy of consequences involves allowing children to experience the results of their misbehavior. It includes three types" — natural, logical, and unrelated (p. 22). There is no transforming type. This is the chapter's own NCLEX question 3, keyed to the invented fourth category.

Watch for

(A), (B), and (D) are all verbatim-correct definitions, so a learner searching for a factual error finds none and guesses. Knowing the set is closed at three is sufficient to answer. Note also that time-out is an unrelated consequence, stated twice, and that natural consequences are preferred but "effective if they are meaningful to children" — the chapter's own messy-room example shows a natural consequence failing where a logical one works.

20. A parent asks how long her 4-year-old should sit in time-out. What should the nurse advise?
  • ✓Four minutes, following the rule of 1 minute per year of age
  • BTen minutes, a standard duration for preschoolers
  • CThe duration should be scaled to how serious the misbehavior was
  • DUntil the child apologizes
Rationale

"A rule for the length of time-out is 1 minute per year of age; use a kitchen timer with an audible bell to record the time rather than a watch" (p. 23).

(C) is the reflex pick because scaling punishment to severity feels like fairness, and the chapter's Flexibility principle does mention severity — but that principle governs which *strategy* is chosen, not the length of a time-out. The duration rule is fixed to age.

21. The same parent reports that her son screams for the first several minutes of every time-out, and asks whether that time counts. What is the correct guidance?
  • AThe time counts from the moment the child is sent, regardless of behavior
  • ✓The time-out period begins after the child quiets down
  • CThe time-out should be ended early if the child becomes upset
  • DAn additional minute should be added for each outburst
Rationale

The published procedure states: "If they cry, refuse, or display any disruptive behavior, the time-out period will begin after they quiet down," and "When they are quiet for the duration of the time, they can then leave the room" (p. 23).

Watch for

(A) is the mechanic most often gotten wrong, and it is what makes time-out fail for exactly this child — a 4-year-old who screams for five minutes serves no quiet time at all. Note also that the audible kitchen timer is specified rather than a watch, so the endpoint is signalled objectively rather than by the parent.

22. A parent asks where time-out should take place and how many warnings to give. Which response reflects the chapter's procedure?
  • ASend the child to the bedroom immediately, with no warning
  • ✓Give one warning; if the child does not obey, use a safe, convenient, unstimulating area where the child can be monitored, such as a bathroom, hallway, or laundry room
  • CGive three warnings before any consequence, then use the child's bedroom
  • DAny location will work as long as the parent remains present for the entire time
Rationale

The Family-Centered Care box specifies one warning before time-out, and an area "that is safe, convenient, and unstimulating, but where the child can be monitored, such as the bathroom, hallway, or laundry room" (p. 23). Time-out must also "be planned in advance," and one of its named advantages is that "the parent does not need to be present for all of the time-out."

Watch for

(A) and (C) both miss the single warning, which is part of the published procedure — not zero, not repeated. The bedroom is a poor choice by the chapter's own standard because it is stimulating; time-out is described as "a refinement of the common practice of sending the child to his or her room," not a synonym for it.

23. A father says spanking is the only thing that works, and the nurse observes that his son does stop the behavior immediately when spanked. Which response reflects the chapter's argument?
  • ASpanking does not actually change behavior, so another approach is needed
  • ✓Spanking causes a dramatic short-term decrease in the behavior, but it teaches that violence is acceptable, may physically harm the child if it results from parental rage, and requires more severe punishment over time as the child becomes accustomed to it
  • CSpanking is acceptable if it is administered calmly and never leaves a mark
  • DSpanking is appropriate for safety-related behaviors only
Rationale

"Based on the principles of aversive therapy, inflicting pain through spanking causes a dramatic short-term decrease in the behavior," and the chapter numbers three serious flaws: it teaches that violence is acceptable, it may physically harm the child if it results from parental rage, and "children become accustomed to spanking, requiring more severe corporal punishment over time" (p. 23).

Watch for

(A) is the reflex counterargument and is not this chapter's position — it concedes the short-term effect explicitly, and that concession is what makes the flaws the answer. The escalation flaw is the one distractors omit, and it is the mechanism by which spanking becomes physical abuse.

27. Divorcing parents ask the nurse how and when to tell their three children. Which guidance reflects the chapter's protocol?
  • ATell each child separately, at the child's own developmental level, and avoid a group discussion
  • ✓Tell the children together with both parents and all siblings present, followed by individual discussions with each child, during a period of calm rather than after an argument
  • CWait until the separation is complete so the children have concrete information
  • DTell only the school-age children; the preschooler will not understand
Rationale

"If possible, the initial disclosure should include both parents and siblings, followed by individual discussions with each child," with sufficient time set aside, "during a period of calm, not after an argument" (p. 26). The content "should include the reason for the divorce, if age appropriate, and reassurance that the divorce is not the fault of the children."

Watch for

(A) and (C) each capture half a real recommendation and drop the other half. The order is group then individual, not one or the other. (C) is the reluctance the chapter names and rejects: without preparation, children experience "vague, uneasy feelings that are more difficult to cope with than being told the truth."

  • ✓Families who live close to each other and whose occupations permit an active role in the care and rearing of the children
