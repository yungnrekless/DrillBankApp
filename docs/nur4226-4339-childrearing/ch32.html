wound healing phases and factors that delay itthe four phases in order
1. A nurse is teaching a student about wound healing. In what order do the four phases occur?
  • ✓Inflammation, fibroplasia, contraction, maturation
  • BFibroplasia, inflammation, contraction, maturation
  • CInflammation, contraction, fibroplasia, maturation
  • DContraction, inflammation, fibroplasia, maturation
Rationale

The chapter names them once, in order: "The nonspecific repair mechanism of wound healing with scar formation involves the processes of inflammation, fibroplasia, scar contraction, and scar maturation" (p. 1210). Inflammation is "The initial response at the site of injury"; fibroplasia "continues 5 days to 4 weeks"; contraction "helps bring wound edges closer together"; and with maturation "the scar becomes pale, does not tan when exposed to sunlight, will not sweat or produce hair, and may itch" (p. 1211).

Watch for

(C) is the likeliest error, because contraction sounds like something the clot does early, when in fact it is "Fibroblast movement" during and after fibroplasia that draws the edges together (p. 1211). (B) puts repair before the vascular and cellular response that prepares the tissue for it.

2. A child's wound is 10 days old, and shiny granulation tissue is visible as the nurse changes the dressing. What should the nurse anticipate?
  • ✓The wound is in the fibroplasia phase, and granulation tissue bleeds profusely if disturbed
  • BThe wound is in the inflammatory phase and is at its weakest point
  • CThe wound is fully matured and needs no special handling
  • DGranulation tissue indicates infection and should be reported
Rationale

"Fibroplasia (granulation or proliferation), the second phase of healing, continues 5 days to 4 weeks," and the warning attached to it is specific: "The wound is fragile at this time, and granulation tissue bleeds profusely if disturbed" (p. 1211).

Watch for

(B) names a real fragility statement belonging to a different phase — the wound "is weakest" during "the first 3 to 5 days after injury" (pp. 1210–1211) — and a 10-day-old wound is past it. Two fragility warnings, two phases, and the one that answers a dressing-change question is the second. (D) reads healthy repair as pathology.

3. Which factors delay wound healing? Select all that apply.
Select all that apply · scored all or nothing
  • ✓Cleansing the wound with hydrogen peroxide
  • ✓A dry wound environment
  • ✓Vitamin C deficiency
  • ✓Systemic corticosteroid therapy
  • EA moisture-retentive occlusive dressing
Rationale

Table 32.1 pairs each factor with its mechanism: hydrogen peroxide is "Toxic to fibroblasts; can cause subcutaneous gas formation (mimics gas-forming infection)"; a dry wound environment "Allows epithelial cells to dry out and die; impairs migration of epithelial cells across wound surface"; vitamin C deficiency "Inhibits formation of collagen fibers and capillary development"; and corticosteroids "Inhibit fibroblast proliferation" and "Depress formation of granulation tissue" (p. 1211).

Watch for

(E) is the one wrong option, and it is the intervention the whole chapter argues for: "An acute full-thickness wound kept in a moist environment usually reepithelializes in 12 to 15 days, whereas the same wound kept open to the air heals in approximately 25 to 30 days" (p. 1211).

4. A parent says she is keeping her child's abrasion uncovered so a good scab can form. What should the nurse explain?
  • ✓A moist, covered wound resurfaces in roughly half the time of one left open to air
  • BA dry scab is the best protection a wound can have against infection
  • CThe scab should be lifted daily so the wound underneath can be inspected
  • DCovering the wound traps bacteria and causes infection
Rationale

"Emphasis has shifted from interventions directed at maintaining a dry environment that promotes eschar formation to those that promote a moist, crust-free environment that enhances the migration of epithelial cells across the wound and facilitates resurfacing," with 12 to 15 days moist against 25 to 30 days open to air. Eschar "interferes with healing by preventing wound contraction" (p. 1211).

Watch for

(C) adds a second error to the first, since scabs "are allowed to slough off without assistance; picking or early removal may cause scarring and secondary infection" (p. 1215). (D) states the belief the moist-wound evidence overturned — moist wound healing in fact "decreases pain and inflammation" and creates the conditions for autolytic debridement (p. 1212).

5. A contaminated laceration is deliberately left open and then sutured several days later, once granulation tissue is present. How should the nurse describe this?
  • ✓Healing by tertiary intention, in which closure is intentionally delayed
  • BHealing by secondary intention, because the wound edges could not be approximated
  • CWound dehiscence, a complication of the original repair
  • DHealing by primary intention, because the wound was sutured in the end
Rationale

Tertiary intention "takes place when suturing is delayed after injury or the wound later breaks down and is sutured or resutured when granulation is present," and the chapter gives the reason: "Frequently, suturing of a contaminated wound is deliberately delayed to afford better removal of infection before closing" (p. 1211).

Watch for

(C) reads a planned decision as a failure, which is the error this category invites — delayed closure is a choice. (B) describes wounds "in which the edges cannot be approximated, such as an avulsion or a third-degree burn"; here they could be approximated, and were not, on purpose.

6. Parents ask what to use to clean their child's minor wound at home. What should the nurse advise?
  • ✓Normal saline, because nothing should go into a wound that you would not put in the eye
  • BHydrogen peroxide, to bubble debris out of the wound
  • CPovidone-iodine, to prevent infection
  • DRubbing alcohol, to disinfect the surface
Rationale

The Nursing Alert is written as a rule of thumb: "Do not put anything in a wound that you would not put in the eye. The safest solution is normal saline" (p. 1214). Parents are cautioned "to avoid povidone-iodine, alcohol, and hydrogen peroxide because these products are toxic to wounds."

Watch for

(B) and (C) are the two products a family most likely already owns, and both sit in Table 32.1's delay-healing list — hydrogen peroxide is "Toxic to fibroblasts" and povidone-iodine "Toxic to white and red blood cells and fibroblasts" (p. 1211). Povidone-iodine carries an added pediatric caution, since it "may be absorbed through the skin in neonates and young children."

7. A parent calls, alarmed that the gel under her child's hydrocolloid dressing looks like yellow pus and smells faintly fruity. The surrounding skin looks normal and the child is afebrile. What should the nurse say?
  • ✓This is normal leakage under this type of dressing
  • BRemove the dressing at once and bring the child in for a wound culture
  • CStart the leftover antibiotic ointment from the child's last infection
  • DClean the wound with hydrogen peroxide before reapplying the dressing
Rationale

Nursing Alert: "Advise parents that the yellow gel forming under hydrocolloid dressings may look like pus and has a distinct odor (somewhat fruity) but is normal leakage" (p. 1215). The signs that would matter are different ones — increased erythema beyond the wound margin, edema, purulent exudate, odor, pain extending beyond the margin, and increased temperature (p. 1214) — and none is present.

Watch for

(B) is the reasonable-sounding overreaction this alert exists to prevent; the two alerts sit one page apart and are built to be confused. (D) compounds it, since hydrogen peroxide is among the products parents are told to keep out of a wound (p. 1214).

8. Which findings should the nurse teach parents to report as signs of wound infection? Select all that apply.
Select all that apply · scored all or nothing
  • ✓Redness extending beyond the wound margin
  • ✓Purulent exudate
  • ✓Pain extending beyond the wound margin
  • ✓Increased temperature
  • EShiny pink granulation tissue in the wound bed
Rationale

The Nursing Alert lists the signs as "Increased erythema, especially beyond wound margin," edema, purulent exudate, odor, "Pain at the site or extending beyond the wound margin," and "Increased temperature" (p. 1214).

Watch for

(E) is the wrong option and it describes healing rather than infection: granulation tissue is the highly vascular, "shiny connective tissue" of the second phase of repair (p. 1211). Note that two of the four correct options are defined by extension past the margin — redness and pain confined to the wound itself are the expected inflammatory response.

9. A preschooler is discharged with two topical preparations that must be applied in a specific order. Which nursing action best supports correct use at home?
  • ✓Mark the containers 1 and 2, and give written instructions along with a demonstration
  • BTell the parents to apply whichever preparation the child tolerates better first
  • CInstruct the parents to apply a thick layer of each so fewer applications are needed
  • DGive verbal instructions only, to avoid overwhelming the family with paperwork
Rationale

The Nursing Alert is the most concrete teaching instruction in the chapter: "Provide written instructions and demonstrate to parents the correct amount of topical medication to apply (e.g., size of a pea; thin film to cover). If more than one preparation is to be applied, mark the containers 1 and 2 to help parents remember the correct order for application to the skin" (p. 1214).

Watch for

(C) is the assumption most families make unprompted, and it is wrong twice over: "one thick application of topical medication is not equivalent to several thin applications" (p. 1235), and a child's skin is "more absorbent" (p. 1213), so the excess is absorbed rather than wasted. The same alert says that "more medication or increased frequency of applications is not better."

10. A 3-year-old has moist erosions on the face covered with heavy, honey-colored crusts. Which teaching is most important?
  • ✓Keep the child from touching the lesions, because impetigo spreads easily by self-inoculation
  • BIsolate the child from all contact until every lesion has healed completely
  • CScrub the crusts off with a stiff cloth at each dressing change
  • DPrepare the family for permanent scarring
Rationale

"Impetigo contagiosa and MRSA infection can easily spread by self-inoculation; therefore caution the child against touching the involved area" (p. 1216). Table 32.3 describes the lesion exactly — exudate that "dries to form heavy, honey-colored crusts" — and labels it "Autoinoculable and contagious."

Watch for

(D) inverts the prognosis, since impetigo "Tends to heal without scarring unless secondary infection" (p. 1216). (B) overreaches: the child does need separate washcloths and towels and daily clothing changes washed in hot water, but full isolation is reserved for a viral exanthem or an easily spread vector such as lice (p. 1214).

11. An adolescent has several tender, inflamed lesions involving multiple adjacent hair follicles on the back of the neck and asks whether he should squeeze them. How should the nurse respond?
  • ✓These lesions should never be squeezed, because squeezing risks worsening or spreading the infection
  • BSqueezing will speed resolution as long as the hands are washed first
  • CThey can be opened at home with a sterile needle
  • DThey will resolve faster under a high-potency topical steroid
Rationale

The comments column for folliculitis, furuncle, and carbuncle states that "Lesions should never be squeezed" (p. 1216), and the nursing text gives the reasoning: squeezing "will not hasten the resolution of the infection and that there is a risk of making the lesion worse or spreading the infection. No attempt should be made to puncture the surface of the pustule with a needle or sharp instrument."

Watch for

(C) is the half measure a family reaches for once told not to squeeze, and the same sentence refuses it. (D) reaches for the wrong drug class entirely: the management is cleanliness, "Local warm, moist compresses," topical or systemic antibiotics, and incision and drainage of severe lesions — and folliculitis is one of only two conditions in this chapter for which heat is indicated at all (pp. 1213, 1216).

12. A 9-year-old is diagnosed with herpes zoster following a dermatome on the trunk, and the parents ask what to expect. Which response is accurate?
  • ✓Pain in children is usually minimal, and postherpetic pain does not occur in children
  • BHe should be warned to expect months of nerve pain after the rash clears
  • CHe poses no risk to others, since shingles is a reactivation rather than a new infection
  • DThe rash will cross the midline and become generalized within a few days
Rationale

The comments column states "Pain in children usually minimal" and "Postherpetic pain does not occur in children" (p. 1218). The virus "has affinity for posterior root ganglia, posterior horn of spinal cord, and skin," and lesions appear as "crops of vesicles usually confined to dermatome following along course of affected nerve."

Watch for

(B) imports an adult complication into a pediatric case, and it is the warning most people have heard attached to shingles. (C) is right about the mechanism and wrong about the consequence: "Chickenpox may follow exposure; isolate affected child from other children in a hospital or school" (p. 1218), so a susceptible child exposed to these lesions can develop varicella.

14. A 6-year-old has scaly, circumscribed patches of hair loss on the scalp, and tinea capitis is diagnosed. The parent asks whether an antifungal cream will clear it. What should the nurse explain?
  • ✓Scalp ringworm requires an oral antifungal such as griseofulvin or terbinafine
  • BAn over-the-counter antifungal cream applied twice daily is sufficient
  • CA topical antifungal combined with a high-potency steroid is the usual treatment
  • DNo treatment is needed, since the hair loss resolves on its own
Rationale

"Oral antifungal agents are the only effective means for treating systemic fungal infections and tinea capitus" (p. 1214), and Table 32.5 gives management as "Oral griseofulvin or terbinafine," with oral ketoconazole for difficult cases and selenium sulfide shampoo twice weekly to decrease fungal shedding (p. 1218).

Watch for

(C) sounds like it addresses both the fungus and the inflammation, and the chapter forbids it twice: combining a topical antifungal with a high-potency steroid "may lead to further infection" (p. 1218), and over-the-counter hydrocortisone "may further aggravate inflammation caused by fungus or bacteria" (p. 1213). A steroid on a fungal rash is wrong on two separate pages.

Rationale

Families are taught to give "the medication with high-fat foods for best absorption," and the adherence risk is named directly: "because subjective symptoms subside, children or parents may be tempted to decrease or discontinue the drug," even though "the therapy frequently continues for weeks or months" (p. 1218).

16. A child has a round, erythematous, scaling patch on the forearm that is spreading outward with a clearing center. Which combination of actions is correct?
  • ✓Scrape the advancing periphery for microscopic examination, and have the household pets examined
  • BScrape the clear center, where the organism is most concentrated
  • CKeep the child home from school until the lesion has completely cleared
  • DApply the topical antifungal only to the visible ring, stopping when it disappears
Rationale

"Diagnosis is made from microscopic examination of scrapings taken from the advancing periphery of the lesion, which almost always produces a scale" (p. 1218), because the lesion "spreads peripherally and clears centrally." Tinea corporis is "Usually of animal origin from infected pets," so "all household pets should be examined for the disorder."

(C) is stricter than the source, which says "The child may return to school after the therapy is initiated" (p. 1218). (D) stops short in both space and time: the topical is "applied 2.5 cm (1 inch) beyond periphery of lesion," and "application continued 1-2 weeks after no sign of lesion."

17. A school-age child has a widespread, symmetric, itchy rash over most of the trunk and limbs, with no sharply demarcated borders. Which cause is most likely?
  • ✓A generalized irritant such as bubble bath, laundry soap, body soap, or lotion
  • BThe nickel snap on the child's jeans
  • CMascara
  • DUltraviolet injury
Rationale

The chapter turns distribution into a diagnostic method: a generalized eruption is "more likely to be caused by bubble bath, laundry soap, body soap, or lotion," a rash around the eyes suggests mascara, a rash on skin covered by clothing but not by undergarments suggests wool, and a rash on uncovered skin suggests ultraviolet injury (p. 1222).

Watch for

(B) names the offender families never guess, and it is a real one — "nickel found in jewelry and the snaps on sleepers and denim" (p. 1222) — but a point contact produces a localized reaction with "a sharp demarcation between inflamed and normal skin" (p. 1219). This rash has no such border, so the exposure was not a point contact.

18. What is the major goal of treatment in contact dermatitis?
  • ✓Preventing further exposure of the skin to the offending substance
  • BApplying a high-potency topical steroid until the rash clears
  • CStarting a systemic antihistamine and an oral antibiotic
  • DCovering the area so the rash cannot spread to other body sites
Rationale

"The major goal in treatment is to prevent further exposure of the skin to the offending substance," and "Provided there is no further irritation, the skin's normal recuperative powers will often produce healing without treatment" (pp. 1219, 1222).

(B) treats the visible problem while leaving the cause in place, which is why the rash returns, and it runs against the chapter's stated stance that practitioners "aim to avoid overtreatment, particularly in young children" (p. 1212). (D) assumes a dermatitis spreads from the rash itself, which is the misconception the poison ivy section exists to refute.

contact dermatitis and poison ivy in childrenpoison ivy, oak, and sumac
19. A child runs through poison ivy while camping. What should the nurse instruct the family to do first?
  • ✓Flush the skin with cold running water, preferably within 15 minutes, without scrubbing
  • BWash the area vigorously with a harsh soap and a washcloth
  • CApply calamine lotion and wait to see whether a rash develops
  • DCover the area with an occlusive dressing so the oil cannot spread
Rationale

"When it is known that the child has made contact with the plant, the area is immediately flushed (preferably within 15 minutes) with cold running water to neutralize the urushiol not yet bonded to the skin. Once the oil has been removed from the skin, the allergen has been neutralized" (p. 1223). Clothing that touched the plant is removed with care and laundered in hot water and detergent.

Watch for

(B) is the instinctive response, and the chapter names it a contraindication with a mechanism attached: harsh soap and scrubbing "removes protective skin oils and dilutes the urushiol, allowing it to spread" (p. 1223). The window matters because "The earlier the skin is cleansed, the greater the chance of removing the urushiol before it attaches to the skin."

20. A child with weeping poison ivy lesions has been kept home so classmates will not catch it, and the parents are covering the blisters to stop the rash from spreading. How should the nurse respond?
  • ✓The rash cannot be spread to another child, and it does not spread from blister serum or from scratching
  • BThe rash is contagious until every lesion has crusted over
  • CScratching spreads the rash to new sites, so the blisters must stay covered
  • DThe blister fluid contains urushiol and will spread the rash on the child's own skin
Rationale

"The rash that results from poison ivy cannot be spread to another child; only direct contact with the oil can cause the response," and "Although the lesions do not spread by contact with the blister serum or from scratching, they can become secondarily infected" (p. 1223). The lesions "dry and heal spontaneously, and itching stops by 10 to 14 days."

Watch for

(C) and (D) are the two halves of the same near-universal belief, and the chapter denies both in one sentence while keeping the one real risk it leaves standing — scratching does not spread the rash, but it can infect it.

21. A child is stung by a bee and the stinger is visible in the skin. What is the first action?
  • ✓Remove the stinger as quickly as possible
  • BApply a cool compress and observe for 10 minutes before touching the site
  • CCover the stinger with a baking soda paste to neutralize the venom
  • DGive an oral antihistamine and then remove the stinger
Rationale

"As long as the stinger remains in the skin, the muscles push the stinger deeper, and the venom is pumped into the wound. The best approach is to remove the stinger as quickly as possible and to get away from the vicinity of other insects to prevent further injury" (p. 1225). The treatment column leads with "Carefully scrape off stinger or pull out stinger as quickly as possible" (p. 1224).

Watch for

(C) and (D) are both genuine later steps — cleansing, cool compresses, a household paste of lemon juice, aspirin, or baking soda, then antihistamines — and each delays the one action that stops venom from continuing to be injected. The table does not prefer scraping to pulling; it prefers speed.

23. A child is bitten by a spider bearing a fiddle-shaped mark on its head. Three days later the site shows a purple, star-shaped area. What should the nurse expect?
  • ✓A necrotic ulcer over the next one to two weeks, managed with antibiotics and corticosteroids
  • BAntivenin, which is the specific treatment for this spider's necrotoxin
  • CRapid dizziness, weakness, and abdominal pain from a neurotoxin
  • DComplete resolution within 48 hours using cool compresses alone
Rationale

The brown recluse is "recognized by fiddle-shaped mark on head," and its venom "contains powerful necrotoxin." The course is "mild to severe pain in 2-8 hours; purple, star-shaped area in 3-4 days; necrotic ulceration in 7-14 days," treated with cool compresses, "antibiotics, corticosteroids," pain relief, and sometimes a skin graft; "Wounds heal with scar formation" (p. 1225).

Watch for

(B) and (C) both belong to the black widow, which carries "a red or orange hourglass-shaped marking on underside," whose venom "has neurotoxic action," and for whom antivenin is the treatment (p. 1225). Neuro belongs to one spider and necrosis to the other, and the antivenin follows the neurotoxin.

24. A parent finds an attached tick on a child's scalp. What should the nurse instruct?
  • ✓Grasp the tick with tweezers as close to the skin as possible and pull straight up with steady, even pressure
  • BCover the tick with petroleum jelly so that it backs out on its own
  • CTwist the tick counterclockwise until it releases
  • DTake the child to the emergency department, since only a clinician should remove a tick
Rationale

"Grasp tick with tweezers (forceps) as close as possible to point of attachment"; "Pull straight up with steady, even pressure"; and if bare hands must be used, "use a tissue to touch tick during removal; wash hands thoroughly with soap and water" (p. 1225). Afterward, "wash the bite area with an iodine scrub, rubbing alcohol, or plain soap and water" (p. 1229).

(B) is the best-known home method, and the chapter rejects it by name: "The application of nail polish or petroleum jelly is not recommended and does not appear to have an effect on tick withdrawal as has been hypothesized" (p. 1229). Speed is what matters, because a tick "Must attach and feed for 1-2 hours to transmit disease" (p. 1225).

27. The parents of a child with scabies ask why the entire household needs treatment when no one else has a rash. What is the best explanation?
  • ✓It takes 30 to 60 days for symptoms to appear, so close contacts may already be infested without knowing it
  • BThe mite survives in bedding for months, so everyone must be treated to clear the house
  • CThe medication prevents infestation in people who have not been exposed
  • DOnly those with visible burrows need treatment; the others can wait and see

"Because of the length of time between infestation and physical symptoms (30 to 60 days), all persons who were in close contact with the affected child need treatment," which "may include boyfriends or girlfriends, babysitters, grandparents, and immediate family members." (D) is the reasonable-sounding half measure that guarantees reinfestation, since the people who look clear today are the ones who will break out next month. (B) misplaces the mite, which burrows "into the stratum corneum of the epidermis (never into living tissue)" on a host to lay its eggs.
