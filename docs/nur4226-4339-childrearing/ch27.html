cardiac catheterization nursing caregeneral
1. A child has just returned from cardiac catheterization. Which assessments should the nurse perform? Select all that apply.
Select all that apply · scored all or nothing
  • ✓Check pulses below the catheterization site for equality and symmetry
  • ✓Check the dressing for bleeding or hematoma in the femoral or antecubital area
  • ✓Assess temperature and color of the affected extremity
  • ✓Monitor blood pressure, especially for hypotension
  • ETake vital signs every 30 to 45 minutes
Rationale

The chapter lists six postprocedural assessments: pulses especially below the site, temperature and color of the affected extremity because "coolness or blanching may indicate arterial obstruction," vital signs as often as every 15 minutes, blood pressure "especially for hypotension," the dressing, and fluid intake (p. 966).

Watch for

(E) understates the frequency — the text says as often as every 15 minutes, and this is one of the two distractors the chapter's own review question plants; the other is ambulating the child, when the extremity is kept straight for 4 to 6 hours after venous and 6 to 8 hours after arterial catheterization.

2. Bleeding begins at a child's femoral catheterization site. Where should the nurse apply pressure?
  • ✓2.5 cm above the percutaneous skin site, to localize pressure over the vessel puncture
  • BDirectly over the skin puncture site
  • C2.5 cm below the skin site, along the line of venous return
  • DOver the femoral pulse distal to the site
Rationale

The Nursing Alert states that "If bleeding occurs, direct, continuous pressure is applied 2.5 cm (1 inch) above the percutaneous skin site to localize pressure over the vessel puncture" (p. 966).

Watch for

(B) is the intuitive location and misses the vessel entry point, which sits proximal to the skin entry because the catheter travels at an angle; the concern that makes this urgent is that hypotension may indicate hemorrhage from cardiac perforation or bleeding at the site.

3. A nurse is preparing a 10-month-old for cardiac catheterization. Which finding may be a reason to cancel the procedure?
  • ✓Severe diaper rash, if femoral access is required
  • BA resting heart rate of 130 beats per minute
  • CA hemoglobin of 14 g/dl
  • DCrying during the preoperative assessment
Rationale

The chapter states that "Severe diaper rash may be a reason to cancel the procedure if femoral access is required," and directs obtaining an accurate height, which is essential for correct catheter selection, plus a history of allergic reactions because some contrast agents are iodine based (p. 965).

Watch for

(B) is a normal infant rate, and (C) is a normal hemoglobin; the other preprocedural step most often missed is assessing and marking the pedal pulses before the child leaves for the catheterization room, so the postprocedural comparison is meaningful.

4. A family is being discharged the day after cardiac catheterization. Which instruction is correct?
  • ✓Avoid tub baths for the first 3 days, and avoid strenuous exercise for several days, but the child may attend school
  • BKeep the child on bed rest at home for 1 week
  • CKeep the pressure dressing in place for 5 days
  • DRestrict the diet to clear liquids for 24 hours
Rationale

The Family-Centered Care box directs removing the pressure dressing the day after, covering with an adhesive bandage for several days, avoiding tub baths for the first 3 days, avoiding strenuous exercise for several days while allowing school attendance, and a regular diet without restrictions (p. 966).

Watch for

(B) and (D) both over-restrict a child the chapter returns to normal activity quickly; families are also told to watch for redness, swelling, drainage, bleeding, fever, and coolness of the catheterized leg.

5. A nurse is assessing a 3-month-old for heart failure. Which findings indicate it? Select all that apply.
Select all that apply · scored all or nothing
  • ✓A sleeping heart rate above 160 beats per minute
  • ✓A respiratory rate above 60 breaths per minute
  • ✓Weight gain
  • ✓Periorbital edema
  • EDistended neck veins
Rationale

The chapter names tachycardia with a sleeping heart rate above 160 in infants as "One of the earliest signs of CHF," tachypnea above 60 breaths per minute, weight gain as "The earliest sign" of edema, and periorbital edema among the systemic venous congestion findings (pp. 969–970).

Watch for

(E) is a real manifestation in the wrong age group — "Distended neck veins are difficult to detect in the short, fat necks of infants and are usually observed only in older children." Box 27.3 sorts every manifestation into impaired myocardial function, pulmonary congestion, or systemic venous congestion, and sorting first is what makes an all-or-nothing item survivable.

6. A child with heart failure is receiving furosemide. Which nursing instruction is correct?
  • ✓Encourage high-potassium foods, because furosemide causes potassium excretion and hypokalemia may precipitate digitalis toxicity
  • BAvoid potassium supplements, because furosemide is potassium sparing
  • CRestrict fluids to 50% of maintenance in all cases
  • DGive the drug at bedtime to minimize daytime urination
Rationale

Table 27.3 calls furosemide the "Drug of choice in severe heart failure," notes that it causes excretion of chloride and potassium and that "hypokalemia may precipitate digitalis toxicity," and directs encouraging high-potassium foods and observing for signs of digoxin toxicity (p. 970).

Watch for

(B) describes spironolactone, whose instruction is the opposite — "Do not administer potassium supplements" — and giving potassium alongside spironolactone or an angiotensin-converting enzyme inhibitor is the hyperkalemia trap this pair exists to catch.

7. A nurse is reviewing the goals of heart failure management. Which set does the chapter state?
  • ✓Improve cardiac function, remove accumulated fluid and sodium, decrease cardiac demands, and improve tissue oxygenation
  • BIncrease preload, increase afterload, increase contractility, and increase heart rate
  • CRestrict all fluid, restrict all sodium, restrict all activity, and sedate the child
  • DCorrect the underlying defect surgically before any medical management
Rationale

The chapter numbers four goals: "(1) improve cardiac function (increase contractility and decrease afterload), (2) remove accumulated fluid and sodium (decrease preload and minimize fluid overload), (3) decrease cardiac demands, and (4) improve tissue oxygenation and decrease oxygen consumption" (p. 970).

Watch for

(B) inverts the hemodynamic direction on afterload and preload, which is the axis the whole treatment plan turns on; note that fluid restriction "may be required in the acute stages" but infants rarely need it, because heart failure already makes feeding hard enough that they struggle to take maintenance volumes.

8. An infant with heart failure is being fed every 2 hours around the clock, and the parents wake the baby for each feeding. What should the nurse advise?
  • ✓This does not provide enough rest between feedings, and care should be organized to allow uninterrupted periods of sleep
  • BContinue, since frequent small feedings are the goal in heart failure
  • CIncrease to hourly feedings to reduce the work of each feeding
  • DSwitch to a low-sodium formula to reduce fluid retention
Rationale

The chapter states that feeding every 2 hours "does not provide enough rest between feedings," and that nursing care is organized "to allow for uninterrupted periods of sleep" — this is the keyed statement in the chapter's own review question on reducing cardiac demands (p. 975).

Watch for

(D) reaches for a measure the chapter uses infrequently in infants, because "infants need a normal sodium source to offset the sodium depletion of chronic diuretic therapy"; the underlying principle is that rest is itself a treatment when the goal is decreasing cardiac demand.

9. A nurse is explaining why the body's compensation for heart failure eventually fails. Which statement is accurate?
  • ✓Sympathetic stimulation raises heart rate, but tachycardia shortens diastole, increases myocardial oxygen consumption, and impairs coronary artery perfusion
  • BVentricular dilation increases contractile force without limit
  • CHypertrophy improves oxygenation of the heart muscle
  • DRenin-angiotensin activation reduces preload over time
Rationale

The chapter describes sympathetic stimulation as increasing force and rate in the short term while tachycardia "shortens diastole, increases myocardial oxygen consumption, impairs coronary artery perfusion," and increased systemic vascular resistance increases afterload (pp. 968–969).

Watch for

(B) omits the limit the chapter states — "Beyond a certain amount of dilation, the force of contraction decreases and the heart fails"; (C) reverses the cost of hypertrophy, where increased muscle mass impairs oxygenation of the heart muscle itself.

digoxin administration and toxicitygeneral
10. A nurse is about to give digoxin to a 5-month-old. The apical pulse is 96 beats per minute. What should the nurse do?
  • ✓Hold the dose and notify the practitioner, since the drug is withheld in infants and young children when the apical pulse is below 90 to 110
  • BGive the dose, since 96 is above 90
  • CGive the dose and recheck the pulse in 1 hour
  • DHold the dose only if the rate is below 60
Rationale

The chapter gives age-tiered hold parameters: below 90 to 110 beats per minute in infants and young children, below 70 in older children, and below 60 in adults, with the apical rate counted for 1 full minute (p. 974).

Watch for

(D) applies the adult threshold to an infant, which is the level-swap this table exists to prevent; the apical rate is used "because a pulse deficit (radial pulse rate lower than apical) may be present with decreased cardiac output."

digoxin administration and toxicitygeneral
11. A child receiving digoxin has a serum potassium of 3.1 mmol/L. What is the significance?
  • ✓A fall in serum potassium enhances the effects of digoxin and increases the risk of toxicity
  • BA fall in serum potassium diminishes the effects of digoxin and reduces the risk of toxicity
  • CSerum potassium does not interact with digoxin
  • DThe finding is expected and requires no action
Rationale

A Nursing Alert states that "A fall in the serum potassium level enhances the effects of digoxin, increasing the risk of digoxin toxicity. Increased serum potassium levels diminish digoxin's effect. Therefore serum potassium levels (normal range, 3.5 to 5.5 mmol/L) must be carefully monitored" (p. 971).

Watch for

(B) is the exact reversal and reads plausibly, which is why the chapter states both directions in one alert; the practical consequence is that a child on furosemide and digoxin together needs potassium watched closely.

digoxin administration and toxicitygeneral
12. An infant on digoxin has vomited twice today, is refusing feeds, and has a heart rate of 88. What should the nurse suspect?
  • ✓Digoxin toxicity, since bradycardia, anorexia, nausea, and vomiting are its most common signs in infants and children
  • BGastroenteritis, since vomiting in infants is usually gastrointestinal
  • CHeart failure worsening, since anorexia accompanies it
  • DA normal response to the drug that requires no action
Rationale

The chapter states that "The most common signs of digoxin toxicity in infants and children are bradycardia (although other dysrhythmias may occur), anorexia, nausea, and vomiting," and notes that vomiting from toxicity "is often unrelated to feedings" with the infant "usually less interested in feeding" (p. 974).

Watch for

(B) is the competing explanation the chapter anticipates, which is why one episode of vomiting alone does not warrant stopping the drug; what shifts the reading here is the combination of repeated vomiting, decreased intake, and a low heart rate. The therapeutic serum range is 0.8 to 2 mcg/L.

  • ✓Direct it slowly to the side and back of the mouth

14. A parent asks whether the digoxin can be mixed into the baby's bottle to make it easier to give. What should the nurse advise?
  • ✓Do not mix the drug with foods or other fluids, because refusal to consume them would result in inaccurate intake
  • BMixing it into formula is acceptable if the full bottle is taken
  • CMixing it into a small amount of juice is preferred to reduce tooth decay
  • DMixing it into cereal is acceptable once solids are started
Rationale

The chapter directs parents not to mix the drug with foods or other fluids, "because refusal to consume them would result in inaccurate intake," and to give water afterward and brush the teeth "to prevent tooth decay from the sweetened liquid" (p. 975).

Watch for

(B) is the most reasonable-sounding version of the error and still fails whenever the bottle is not finished, which is common in an infant with heart failure; the drug should also be kept in a safe place, preferably a locked cabinet.

hypoxemia, cyanosis, and hypercyanotic spellsgeneral
15. A child with severe anemia has a PaO2 of 45 mm Hg but does not appear cyanotic. How should the nurse interpret this?
  • ✓Cyanosis requires 5 g/dl or more of deoxygenated hemoglobin, so severe anemia can mask it despite significant hypoxemia
  • BThe absence of cyanosis means oxygenation is adequate
  • CAnemia intensifies cyanosis, so another explanation should be sought
  • DCyanosis and hypoxemia always appear together
Rationale

The chapter states that cyanosis "results from the presence of deoxygenated hemoglobin... at a concentration of 5 g/dl of blood or more," is usually apparent when arterial saturation is 85% or lower, and that "Children with severe anemia may not be cyanotic despite severe hypoxemia" (p. 976).

Watch for

(C) reverses the relationship; the mirror case is that "Patients with polycythemia may appear cyanotic despite a near-normal PaO2," so appearance is unreliable in both directions — hypoxemia is a blood value, hypoxia is a tissue state, and cyanosis is only what you see.

16. An infant with tetralogy of Fallot becomes acutely cyanotic and hyperpneic while crying. What should the nurse do first?
  • ✓Place the infant in the knee-chest position
  • BAdminister 100% oxygen by face mask
  • CGive morphine through the existing intravenous line
  • DBegin intravenous fluid replacement
Rationale

The Applying Evidence to Practice guidelines order the response: place the infant in knee-chest position, employ a calm comforting approach, administer 100% oxygen by face mask, give morphine, begin fluid replacement if needed, and repeat morphine (p. 977).

Watch for

(B) and (C) are both correct steps out of order, which is what this item tests; the knee-chest position works because it "reduces the venous return from the legs (which is desaturated) and increases systemic vascular resistance, which diverts more blood into the pulmonary artery," and morphine reduces the infundibular spasm that started the spell.

17. A nurse is starting an intravenous infusion on a child with a right-to-left intracardiac shunt. Which precaution is essential?
  • ✓Use filters on all intravenous lines, because air in the venous system can travel directly to the brain
  • BUse only large-bore catheters to reduce turbulence
  • CWarm all fluids to body temperature before infusion
  • DAvoid dextrose-containing solutions
Rationale

A Nursing Alert states that intracardiac right-to-left shunting "allows air in the venous system to go directly to the brain, which results in an air embolism. Therefore all IV lines should have filters in place to prevent air from entering the system," with tubing and flush syringes checked for air (p. 984).

Watch for

(D) is the opposite of what these children often need, since infants and patients with polycythemia may need dextrose-containing fluids to prevent hypoglycemia; the embolism risk here is mechanical rather than metabolic.

hypoxemia, cyanosis, and hypercyanotic spellsgeneral
18. A child with chronic hypoxemia has a hematocrit of 62%. What does the chapter identify as the risks?
  • ✓Increased blood viscosity with thromboembolic risk, crowding out of platelets and clotting factors, and increased postoperative bleeding
  • BImproved oxygen delivery with no associated risk
  • CA reduced risk of stroke because of the higher oxygen-carrying capacity
  • DDecreased risk of postoperative bleeding because of the higher cell mass
Rationale

The chapter states that persistent hypoxemia stimulates erythropoiesis, producing polycythemia that "increases blood viscosity" and crowds out platelets and coagulation factors, so it "increases the likelihood of postoperative bleeding," with small venous clots able to cross a right-to-left shunt and reach the brain (pp. 976–977).

Watch for

(C) and (D) both read a higher red cell mass as protective; the chapter's practical countermeasure is to keep the child well hydrated, treating fever, vomiting, and diarrhea promptly and keeping an oral electrolyte solution at home.

congenital heart defect classificationgeneral
19. A nurse is classifying congenital heart defects by hemodynamics. Which pairing is correct?
  • ✓Aortic stenosis and obstruction to blood flow out of the heart
  • BVentricular septal defect and decreased pulmonary blood flow
  • CTricuspid atresia and increased pulmonary blood flow
  • DAtrioventricular canal defect and obstruction to blood flow
Rationale

The chapter classifies defects into four hemodynamic groups — increased pulmonary blood flow, decreased pulmonary blood flow, obstructive, and mixed — placing aortic stenosis among the obstructive defects, ventricular septal defect and atrioventricular canal among those with increased pulmonary blood flow, and tricuspid atresia among those with decreased flow (pp. 979–987).

Watch for

(B) and (C) each reverse the flow direction, and these four pairings are exactly the ones the chapter's own review question tests; note that the book calls the older cyanotic-versus-acyanotic split "inadequate and misleading," so the hemodynamic axis is the testable one.

20. A newborn has high blood pressure and bounding pulses in the arms, weak femoral pulses, and cool lower extremities. Which defect does this describe?
  • ✓Coarctation of the aorta
  • BPatent ductus arteriosus
  • CTetralogy of Fallot
  • DTransposition of the great arteries
Rationale

The chapter describes coarctation as localized narrowing near the ductal insertion producing increased pressure proximally and decreased pressure distally, with "high blood pressure and bounding pulses in the arms, weak or absent femoral pulses, and cool lower extremities with lower blood pressure" (p. 979).

Watch for

(B) is characterized instead by a machinery-like murmur; note that coarctation repair needs no cardiopulmonary bypass because the defect lies outside the heart and pericardium, and elective surgery is advised within the first 2 years to prevent later hypertension.

  • ✓Down syndrome
  • BTurner syndrome

The chapter states that atrioventricular canal defect "is the most common cardiac defect in children with Down syndrome," and describes the anatomy exactly as the stem gives it, with moderate to severe heart failure usual (p. 979).

22. A preterm infant has a patent ductus arteriosus. Which medical treatment does the chapter name?
  • ✓Indomethacin, a prostaglandin inhibitor
  • BProstaglandin E1 infusion
  • CDigoxin and furosemide alone
  • DBalloon atrial septostomy
Rationale

The chapter states that "Administration of indomethacin (prostaglandin inhibitor) has proved successful in closing a patent ductus in premature infants and some newborns" (p. 979).

23. A nurse is reviewing tetralogy of Fallot with a student. Which four defects make up the classic form?
  • ✓Ventricular septal defect, pulmonic stenosis, overriding aorta, and right ventricular hypertrophy
  • BAtrial septal defect, pulmonic stenosis, overriding aorta, and left ventricular hypertrophy
  • CVentricular septal defect, aortic stenosis, patent ductus arteriosus, and right ventricular hypertrophy
  • DAtrial septal defect, ventricular septal defect, patent ductus arteriosus, and pulmonic stenosis
Rationale

The chapter states that "The classic form includes four defects: (1) ventricular septal defect (VSD), (2) pulmonic stenosis, (3) overriding aorta, and (4) right ventricular hypertrophy" (p. 981).

Watch for

(B) swaps two of the four, and both swaps are internally consistent-sounding — an atrial rather than ventricular defect, and left rather than right hypertrophy; the hypertrophy is right-sided precisely because the pulmonic stenosis obstructs right ventricular outflow.

24. A nurse is explaining the staged repair of hypoplastic left heart syndrome. Which sequence is correct?
  • ✓Norwood in the first week, bidirectional Glenn at 3 to 8 months, Fontan at 2 to 4 years
  • BGlenn in the first week, Norwood at 3 to 8 months, Fontan at 2 to 4 years
  • CFontan in the first week, Norwood at 6 months, Glenn at 2 years
  • DNorwood at 6 months, Glenn at 2 years, Fontan at 5 years
Rationale

The chapter gives stage I Norwood in "first week of life," stage II bidirectional Glenn at "3 to 8 months of age," and stage III Fontan at "2 to 4 years of age," the last directing systemic venous return to the lungs "without a ventricular pump" (p. 987).

(B) reverses the first two stages, which cannot work because the Glenn depends on the low pulmonary vascular resistance the Norwood establishes; note also that the modified Fontan "does not restore normal anatomy or hemodynamics."

25. An infant with a modified Blalock-Taussig shunt has an oxygen saturation of 66%. How should the nurse interpret this?
  • ✓The shunt may be too small or narrowed, and surgical revision or an additional shunt may be needed
  • BThe shunt is too large, and diuretics should be started
  • CThis is the expected saturation after a shunt
  • DThe pulse oximeter is malfunctioning, since saturation cannot fall this low
Rationale

The chapter states that saturations below 70% indicate a shunt that is too small or narrowed, with the newborn remaining severely hypoxemic, while saturations above 85% with heart failure signs indicate a shunt that is too large (p. 984).

Watch for

(B) names the management for the opposite problem; both figures mark abnormalities in opposite directions, and acute cyanosis with signs of tissue hypoxia means shunt occlusion, which is a medical emergency.

26. A child's chest tube drainage stops abruptly 6 hours after cardiac surgery, and the pulse pressure is narrowing. What should the nurse do?
  • ✓Report it immediately, since these findings suggest cardiac tamponade
  • BDocument that the chest tube is functioning and drainage has resolved
  • CMilk the tube and continue routine monitoring
  • DClamp the tube to prevent air entry
Rationale

27. A child is 36 hours post cardiac surgery with a temperature of 100.2 degrees Fahrenheit. How should the nurse interpret this?
  • ✓Likely the inflammatory response to tissue trauma, which is expected in the first 24 to 48 hours
  • BCertain evidence of surgical site infection requiring cultures now
  • CEvidence of a transfusion reaction
  • DNormal at any point in the postoperative course
Rationale

The chapter states that a temperature to about 100 degrees Fahrenheit in the first 24 to 48 hours is the inflammatory response to tissue trauma, and that after that period fever "is most likely infection" (p. 990).

infective endocarditis and its prophylaxisgeneral
28. A child has an insidious onset of unexplained low-grade intermittent fever, malaise, anorexia, and a new murmur. What should the nurse suspect?
  • ✓Infective endocarditis
  • BAcute rheumatic fever
  • CKawasaki disease
  • DViral myocarditis
Rationale

The chapter describes the onset of infective endocarditis as "usually insidious, with unexplained low-grade, intermittent fever," plus malaise, anorexia, heart failure symptoms, arthralgias, and weight loss, with "A new murmur or a change in an existing one" frequently found (p. 994).

Watch for

(B) is the confusable partner and differs on mechanism and timing — rheumatic fever is an abnormal immune response 2 to 6 weeks after streptococcal pharyngitis, diagnosed by Jones criteria and antistreptolysin O titers, while endocarditis is a direct infection diagnosed by blood culture plus echocardiography.

29. Which children should receive antibiotic prophylaxis before dental procedures? Select all that apply.
Select all that apply · scored all or nothing
  • ✓A child with an artificial heart valve
  • ✓A child with a previous diagnosis of infective endocarditis
  • ✓A child with unrepaired cyanotic congenital heart disease including palliative shunts
  • ✓A child with a ventricular septal defect repaired with prosthetic material 3 months ago
  • EA child with a ventricular septal defect repaired without prosthetic material 4 years ago

Box 27.9 limits prophylaxis to artificial heart valves, previous infective endocarditis, unrepaired cyanotic disease including palliative shunts and conduits, repaired disease using prosthetic material "during the first 6 months after the procedure," residual defects adjacent to prosthetic material, and transplant recipients with valvulopathy (p. 995).

30. How many blood cultures does the chapter recommend when infective endocarditis is suspected, and why?
  • ✓Three, drawn with strict sterile technique because the common causative organisms live on the skin
  • BOne, since a single positive culture is diagnostic
  • CTwo, drawn at least 24 hours apart
  • DCultures are not useful, and diagnosis rests on echocardiography alone
Rationale

The chapter directs several blood specimens, "three recommended," drawn with strict sterile technique because the common organisms live on the skin, and gives the Duke criteria as two major, or one major and three minor, or five minor criteria — the two major being positive blood cultures and echocardiographic evidence (pp. 994–995).

Watch for

(D) discards half the major criteria; note that culture-negative endocarditis is reported in 5% to 10% of cases, which is another reason multiple specimens matter, and the most common organisms are *Streptococcus viridans* and *Staphylococcus aureus*.

31. The parent of a child at high risk for infective endocarditis asks what to watch for at home. What should the nurse emphasize?
  • ✓Any unexplained fever, weight loss, or change in behavior such as lethargy, malaise, or anorexia must be reported
  • BOnly chest pain or palpitations need to be reported
  • CSymptoms will always begin abruptly and be obvious
  • DRoutine monitoring is unnecessary once prophylaxis is prescribed
Rationale

The chapter directs that "Any unexplained fever, weight loss, or change in behavior (lethargy, malaise, anorexia) must be brought to the practitioner's attention," and that children at risk "should have blood drawn for culture if they have a fever without an obvious source" (p. 995).

Watch for

(C) contradicts the insidious onset the chapter describes, which is exactly why the vague symptoms in the correct option are the ones families must be taught to take seriously; note that endocarditis may follow routine bacteremia from ordinary activities such as brushing teeth.

32. A 9-year-old had streptococcal pharyngitis 3 weeks ago and now has migratory joint pain, a new murmur, and a fever of 38.8 degrees Celsius. How many Jones criteria does this satisfy, and is the diagnosis supported?
  • ✓Two major manifestations, carditis and polyarthritis, which satisfies the requirement for diagnosis
  • BOne major and one minor, which does not satisfy the requirement
  • CThree minor manifestations, which satisfies the requirement in all populations
  • DNo criteria, since fever alone is not diagnostic
Rationale

The chapter states that "Patients must have two major manifestations or one major and two minor manifestations for diagnosis," listing carditis and polyarthritis among the five major manifestations; the arthritis is characteristically migratory, affecting "one joint for 1 to 2 days, then another" (pp. 996–997).

Watch for

(B) miscounts the murmur, which represents carditis and is major rather than minor; the timing fits, since the disease develops after streptococcal infection "usually within 2 to 6 weeks," and the age of 5 to 15 years with a preceding sore throat is itself a diagnostic clue.

acute rheumatic fever and rheumatic heart diseasegeneral
33. Which manifestation of acute rheumatic fever leads to permanent damage?
  • ✓Carditis
  • BPolyarthritis
  • CChorea
  • DErythema marginatum
Rationale

The chapter states that "Carditis is the only manifestation of ARF that leads to permanent damage," and that rheumatic heart disease is "the most significant complication of ARF," occurring in more than half the cases, with the mitral valve most often affected (p. 996).

Watch for

(B) is the most frequent major manifestation at 35% to 66% and leaves no permanent injury, which is what makes it a tempting choice; echocardiography is now recommended in all suspected and confirmed cases because it is more sensitive than auscultation for valve dysfunction.

34. A child is diagnosed with acute rheumatic fever with carditis. How long should secondary prophylaxis continue?
  • ✓10 years or until age 21, whichever is longer
  • B5 years or until age 21, whichever is longer
  • CUntil age 40 and possibly indefinitely
  • DUntil two negative throat cultures are obtained
Rationale

The chapter tiers the duration by cardiac involvement: without carditis, 5 years or until age 21; with carditis, 10 years or until age 21; with established rheumatic heart disease, until age 40 and possibly indefinitely (p. 997).
