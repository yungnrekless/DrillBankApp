1. A nurse tells a mother, "I know exactly how you feel — I was terrified when my own son was admitted." How should this response be classified?
  • AEmpathy, because the nurse is sharing a comparable experience
  • ✓Sympathy — having feelings or emotions similar to those of another person, rather than understanding those feelings
  • CA facilitative response
  • DAnticipatory guidance
Rationale

Sympathy is "having feelings or emotions similar to those of another person, rather than understanding those feelings," while empathy is "the capacity to understand what another person is experiencing from within that person's frame of reference; it is often described as the ability to put oneself in another's shoes" (p. 82).

Watch for

(A) is the reflex pick, and the book defines the two terms against each other on the same page, which is the shape of the item. The discriminator is whose frame of reference is being used: empathy enters the parent's, while sympathy substitutes the nurse's own.

3. A mother falls silent partway through describing her son's behavior problems. What should the nurse do?
  • AIntroduce a new topic to relieve the discomfort
  • ✓Allow the silence, which permits the interviewee to sort out thoughts and feelings and search for responses, and can also be a cue for the interviewer to go more slowly
  • CFill the pause by summarizing what has been discussed so far
  • DEnd the interview, since the mother has finished
Rationale

Silence "permits the interviewee to sort out thoughts and feelings and search for responses to questions. Silence can also be a cue for the interviewer to go more slowly," and "Breaking a silence by introducing a new topic or by prolonged talking essentially terminates the interviewee's opportunity to use the silence" (p. 82).

Watch for

(A) and (C) both fill the pause, which is the instinct the chapter names and rejects. When a silence does need breaking, the chapter's example is a question that keeps the floor with the parent: "Is there anything else you wish to say?"

4. A nurse is interviewing a family through a hospital interpreter. Which actions are correct? (Select all that apply.)
Select all that apply · scored all or nothing
  • ✓Communicate directly with family members when asking questions, without ignoring the interpreter
  • ✓Pose questions to elicit only one answer at a time
  • ✓Refrain from interrupting family members and the interpreter while they are conversing
  • ✓Arrange for the family to speak with the same interpreter on subsequent visits whenever possible
  • EAsk the interpreter privately what she thinks of the family's understanding while the family is present
Rationale

The Nursing Care Guidelines direct the nurse to explain the purpose to the interpreter beforehand, introduce the interpreter and allow time for acquaintance, communicate directly with family members, ask one-answer questions ("Do you have pain?" rather than "Do you have any pain, tiredness, or loss of appetite?"), avoid interrupting, avoid medical jargon, and use the same interpreter on later visits (p. 83).

(E) is the one to reject, and the chapter gives the reason plainly: "Avoid commenting to the interpreter about family members because they may understand some English."

5. A 12-year-old is fluent in English and offers to interpret for her non-English-speaking mother during a history. What should the nurse do?
  • AAccept, since the child is willing and fluent
  • ✓Decline; the use of children in health care interpreting is strongly discouraged, and use a professional interpreter or a telephonic language line instead
  • CAccept for the history but not for the physical examination
  • DAccept if the mother consents
Rationale

"the use of children in health care interpreting is strongly discouraged because they are often not mature enough to understand health care questions, answers, or messages." Children "may inadvertently commit interpretive errors, such as inaccuracies, omissions, or substitutions," "can be adversely affected by serious or sensitive information," and in some cultures using a child "is considered an insult to an adult because children are expected to show respect by not questioning their elders." Some institutions prohibit it outright; the alternative named is "a language line using a telephonic interpreter" (p. 83).

Watch for

(D) is the most defensible-sounding wrong answer, and it misplaces the objection. The problem is not consent — it is accuracy, the burden placed on the child, and in some cultures the insult to the adult.

6. A nurse enters an examination room and immediately kneels in front of a shy 3-year-old, smiles broadly, makes eye contact, and asks him about his toy. What is the likely effect?
  • AThe child will feel welcomed and relax
  • ✓Anxiety will increase — active attempts to make friends with children before they have had an opportunity to evaluate an unfamiliar person tend to increase their anxiety
  • CThe approach is correct because the nurse is at the child's level
  • DThe effect depends entirely on the child's temperament
Rationale

"Active attempts to make friends with children before they have had an opportunity to evaluate an unfamiliar person tend to increase their anxiety," and the guidelines direct the nurse to "Avoid sudden or rapid advances, broad smiles, extended eye contact, or other gestures that may be seen as threatening" (pp. 83–84). The move is to keep talking to the parent while doing something that does not involve the child.

Watch for

(C) names a real guideline — "Assume a position that is at the same level as the child" — and applies it at the wrong moment. Being at eye level is right; arriving there before the child has had time to evaluate you is not. Broad smiles and extended eye contact appear on the chapter's list of potentially threatening gestures, not reassuring ones.

7. A 4-year-old is told she will feel "a little stick in the arm." She becomes distressed and looks for a stick. What accounts for this?
  • AShe is developmentally delayed
  • ✓Children in early childhood interpret words literally, and the chapter uses this exact example
  • CShe is exhibiting regression from the stress of hospitalization
  • DThe explanation was given too far in advance
Rationale

Children younger than 5 "interpret words literally" and attach literal meaning to phrases such as "two-faced," "sticky fingers," or "coughing your head off"; a child told they will get "a little stick in the arm" may not envision an injection (p. 84). The related instruction under vital signs is to "avoid using the word 'take' when measuring vital signs because young children interpret words literally and may think that their temperature or other function will be taken away."

Watch for

(A) reads normal preoperational thinking as a deficit. The same egocentrism means the chapter says it is "futile to use another child's experience in an attempt to gain the cooperation of small children" — focus communication on the child in front of you.

8. A 9-year-old is scheduled for a procedure and keeps asking why it is being done and how the equipment works. How should the nurse respond?
  • ARedirect the questions, since detailed explanations increase anxiety at this age
  • ✓Give explanations and reasons, since school-age children want them and are interested in the functional aspect of all procedures — and they need to know what is going to take place and why it is being done to them specifically
  • CAnswer only through a doll or puppet
  • DDefer all explanations to the parent
Rationale

School-age children "rely less on what they see and more on what they know," "want explanations and reasons for everything but require no verification beyond that," "are interested in the functional aspect of all procedures," and "need to know what is going to take place and why it is being done to them specifically" (p. 85).

Watch for

(C) is the technique for a *young* child — communicating through transition objects such as dolls, puppets, and stuffed animals before questioning directly — applied several stages too late. Note the school-age child's other feature: "a heightened concern about body integrity" that extends to possessions, so they "may appear to overreact to loss or threatened loss of treasured objects."

9. A 15-year-old who has been managing her own diabetes for years becomes tearful and asks her mother to stay during a painful procedure. How should the nurse interpret this?
  • AAs regression requiring referral
  • ✓As normal: adolescents fluctuate between child and adult thinking and behavior, and when tensions rise they may seek the security of the more familiar and comfortable expectations of childhood
  • CAs evidence she is not ready to self-manage her condition
  • DAs manipulation to avoid the procedure
Rationale

Adolescents "fluctuate between child and adult thinking and behavior," and "when tensions rise, they may seek the security of the more familiar and comfortable expectations of childhood." The chapter states the balance directly: "It is as much a mistake to regard an adolescent as an adult with an adult's wisdom and control as it is to assume that the teenager has the concerns and expectations of a child" (p. 85).

Watch for

(C) draws a durable conclusion from a momentary shift, which is exactly the error the chapter's two-sided warning is written against. Note also that privacy and confidentiality are "of great importance" at this age "because it is consistent with developmental maturity and autonomy."

10. A nurse is beginning a visit with a 16-year-old and his mother, and time is limited. What should the nurse do?
  • AInterview the parent first in every case, since the parent is the informant
  • BInterview the adolescent first in every case
  • ✓Either order is defensible — talking with the adolescent first immediately identifies with the young person, and talking with the parents first may provide insight into the family relationship — but give both parties an opportunity to be included, and state at the onset that time is limited to avoid appearing to take sides
  • DInterview them together only, so nothing is concealed
Rationale

Talking with the adolescent first "has the advantage of immediately identifying with the young person"; talking with the parents first "may provide insight into the family relationship." Either way, "give both parties an opportunity to be included," and if time is limited say so at the onset "to avoid appearing to 'take sides'" (p. 85).

Watch for

(D) forecloses the private conversation the chapter treats as essential — older children should be given "the opportunity to talk without parents present," and nurses must know state and federal consent and confidentiality laws for suspected abuse, substance use, suicidal or homicidal ideation, contraceptive care, pregnancy, sexually transmitted infections, and sexual assault.

11. A nurse says to an adolescent, "You are being uncooperative about doing your treatments." Which technique should replace this, and why?
  • AA third-person statement, because it is less threatening
  • ✓An I-message: "I am concerned about how the treatments are going because I want to see you get better" — because you-messages are judgmental and provoke defensiveness
  • CA facilitative response
  • DMutual storytelling
Rationale

The technique is to "Relate a feeling about a behavior in terms of 'I.'... Avoid use of 'you.' 'You' messages are judgmental and provoke defensiveness," and the chapter gives this exact contrast (p. 85).

Watch for

(A) names a different real technique for a different purpose. The third-person technique expresses a feeling as "he," "she," or "they," which "allows children three choices: (1) to agree and, one hopes, express how they feel; (2) to disagree; or (3) to remain silent" — useful for eliciting, not for correcting a behavior.

12. A hospitalized child says she is scared the other children will laugh at her scar. The nurse replies, "You feel worried because you think the other kids will notice." Which technique is this?
  • ✓Facilitative response
  • BBibliotherapy
  • CStorytelling
  • DSentence completion
Rationale

A facilitative response reflects feelings and content back: "Listen carefully and reflect back to patients the feelings and content of their statements," with the formula "You feel _ because _." Such responses "are empathic and nonjudgmental and legitimize the person's feelings" (p. 85).

Watch for

(C) is the nearest relative and works the other direction. Storytelling uses "the language of children to probe into areas of their thinking while bypassing conscious inhibitions or fears"; mutual storytelling goes further, with the nurse retelling the child's story with therapeutic differences, and the chapter calls it the "more therapeutic approach than storytelling."

13. A nurse is evaluating a child's drawing of her family. Which principles apply? (Select all that apply.)
Select all that apply · scored all or nothing
  • ✓Use spontaneous drawings and evaluate more than one drawing whenever possible
  • ✓Interpret the drawing as a whole rather than focusing on specific details
  • ✓Size expresses importance, power, or authority
  • ✓Exclusion of a family member may denote a feeling of not belonging or a desire to eliminate
  • EA single drawing is sufficient to establish a diagnosis of family dysfunction
Rationale

The guidelines for evaluating drawings state each of the keyed items, and add that the order drawn "expresses priority in terms of importance," the child's position relative to others "expresses feelings of status or alliance," and erasures, shading, or cross-hatching "expresses ambivalence, concern, or anxiety" (p. 87).

Watch for

(E) is the one to reject and it contradicts the first two principles at once — more than one drawing, and the whole rather than the detail. Drawing is called "one of the most valuable forms of communication — both nonverbal (from looking at the drawing) and verbal (from the child's story of the picture)," and the verbal half is why a single image is not read as a finding.

15. A nurse begins a visit by asking, "How is your son sick?" How should this opening be evaluated?
  • AAppropriate, because it is open ended
  • ✓It is a labeling-type question and should be avoided; elicit the chief complaint with open-ended, neutral questions such as "What seems to be the matter?" or "Why did you come here today?"
  • CAppropriate only for a well-child visit
  • DIt is too broad and should be replaced with a closed-ended question
Rationale

Elicit the chief complaint with "open-ended, neutral questions (e.g., 'What seems to be the matter?,' 'How may I help you?,' or 'Why did you come here today?'). Avoid labeling-type questions (e.g., 'How are you sick?' or 'What is the problem?')" (p. 88). If the parent names many problems, ask which one prompted them to seek help now.

Watch for

(D) moves in the wrong direction. Closed-ended questions are named among the blocks to communication — "limiting expression of emotion by asking directed, closed-ended questions." The fault in the stem's question is the label it plants, not its breadth.

16. A 7-year-old reports abdominal pain. Which approach best establishes the location?
  • AAccept the description "stomach pains" and document it
  • ✓Have the child point with one finger to where it hurts, or point to where a parent would put a bandage — and for radiation, ask the child to show with a finger where the pain goes
  • CAsk the parent to indicate the location on the child's body
  • DPalpate each quadrant and note where the child withdraws
Rationale

"'Stomach pains' is too general." Have the child "point with one finger to where it hurts" or "point to where mommy or daddy would put a Band-Aid," and for radiation: "Does the pain stay there or move? Show me with your finger where the pain goes" (p. 89). Type, location, severity, duration, and influencing factors are the five components of symptom analysis; for type, "Record whatever words they use in quotes."

Watch for

(A) is the failure the chapter names by name, and it is what makes location a technique rather than a question. Note that severity is "best determined by finding out how it affects the child's usual behavior" — pain that prevents playing, interacting, sleeping, and eating is most often severe.

17. A parent reports that her child walked without assistance at 6 months. How should the nurse interpret this?
  • AAs evidence of advanced motor development, to be documented as reported
  • ✓As a clue to misunderstanding of the requested activity, prompting a more specific question
  • CAs a reason to refer for developmental evaluation
  • DAs unreliable recall that should be omitted from the record
Rationale

The chapter directs the use of specific questions, because "'sitting up' can mean many different activities," and "A clue to misunderstanding of the requested activity may be an unusually early age of achievement" (p. 90).

18. A nurse is taking a sexual history from a 16-year-old. Which approach does the chapter recommend?
  • AAsk whether the adolescent is "sexually active," since it is the standard clinical term
  • ✓Ask "Are you having sex with anyone?" — probably the most direct and best understood question — and refer to all sexual contacts in nongender terms, such as "partners"
  • CAsk the parent to leave and then ask about girlfriends or boyfriends by name
  • DDefer sexual history to the provider
Rationale

"avoid asking whether the adolescent is 'sexually active' because this term is broadly defined. 'Are you having sex with anyone?' is probably the most direct and best understood question." Because same-sex experimentation may occur, "refer to all sexual contacts in nongender terms, such as 'partners,' rather than 'girlfriends' or 'boyfriends'" (pp. 90–91).

Watch for

(A) is the clinical habit the chapter singles out as too vague to yield an answer. (C) gets the privacy half right and then reintroduces the gendered assumption. Compare the chemical-use phrasing on the same pages, which works the same way: "Many kids your age are experimenting with drugs and alcohol; have you ever had any drugs or alcohol?" yields more reliable data than "How much do you drink?"

19. A nurse examines a quiet 4-month-old in the parent's lap. Which sequence is correct?
  • AProceed strictly head to toe, beginning with the eyes and ears
  • ✓Auscultate heart, lungs, and abdomen while the infant is quiet; then palpate and percuss the same areas; then proceed head to toe, performing traumatic procedures last and eliciting the Moro reflex last of all
  • CElicit all reflexes first while the infant is calm, then examine body systems
  • DBegin with the most painful area so it is not anticipated
Rationale

Table 4.2 gives the infant sequence: "If quiet, auscultate heart, lungs, abdomen. Record heart and respiratory rates. Palpate and percuss same areas. Proceed in usual head-to-toe direction. Perform traumatic procedures last (eyes, ears, mouth [while crying]). Elicit reflexes as body part is examined. Elicit Moro reflex last" (p. 98).

Watch for

(A) confuses the recording order with the examination order. The head-to-toe order is kept for *recording*; the examination itself is reordered by age to minimize stress, preserve the parent-child relationship, and "Maximize the accuracy and reliability of assessment findings." (D) reverses the standing rule that painful areas are examined last — except in an emergency, where airway, breathing, circulation, and the injured area come first.

20. A nurse wants to introduce the stethoscope to a 2-year-old before auscultating. What does Table 4.2 direct?
  • ADemonstrate the equipment's use on a parent, then on the child
  • ✓Allow the toddler to inspect the equipment; demonstrating its use is usually ineffective
  • CExplain the purpose of the equipment and its significance
  • DExplain the findings as they are obtained
Rationale

The toddler column states: "Allow toddler to inspect equipment; demonstrating use of equipment is usually ineffective" (p. 98).

Watch for

(A) and (C) are both correct — for older children. The Preparation column runs a developmental ladder: the toddler is *shown nothing* because demonstration does not work, the preschooler gets a *brief* demonstration, the school-age child gets the *purpose* explained ("otoscope to see eardrum, which is necessary for hearing"), and the adolescent gets the *findings* explained during the examination. Choosing the rung is the item.

  • ✓WHO growth standards for the 18-month-old and CDC growth charts for the 4-year-old
  • CWHO standards for both
  • DCDC charts for the 18-month-old and WHO standards for the 4-year-old
Rationale

The WHO growth standards are used "to monitor growth for infants and children between the ages of 0 and 2 years old," because they "reflect growth patterns among children who were predominately breastfed," and "The Centers for Disease Control and Prevention growth charts... are used for children 2 years old and older" (pp. 98–100).

(D) swaps the two age bands, which is the only way this pair is tested. The rationale for the split is worth holding: the WHO standard is a breastfed reference, which is why it belongs to the years when breastfeeding is most common.

25. A nurse needs to measure a 14-month-old. What is the correct method?
  • AStanding height on a stadiometer, since the child can walk
  • ✓Recumbent length on a length board, holding the head in midline, grasping the knees together gently, and pushing down until the legs are fully extended
  • CA tape measure laid alongside the infant on the examining table
  • DEither method, as long as it is documented
Rationale

Measure recumbent length until 24 months (or 36 months if using the birth-to-36-months chart) using a length board, extending the body by "(1) holding the head in midline, (2) grasping the knees together gently, and (3) pushing down on the knees until the legs are fully extended" (p. 100).

Watch for

(C) is wrong twice over — wrong instrument and wrong position — and the chapter rules the instrument out explicitly: "A tape measure should not be used to measure the length of infants and children because the methods are inaccurate and unreliable." Note the related alert for older children: "Normally height is less if measured in the afternoon than in the morning," so serial measurements are taken at the same time of day.

27. Which growth patterns does the chapter identify as questionable and warranting further investigation? (Select all that apply.)
Select all that apply · scored all or nothing

  • ✓Failure to follow the expected growth velocity in height and weight

28. A nurse measures head circumference on a 15-month-old and finds it equal to the chest circumference. How should this be interpreted, and through what age is head circumference routinely measured?
  • AAbnormal; head circumference should always exceed chest circumference in childhood
  • ✓Expected — head and chest circumferences are equal at about 1 to 2 years of age — and head circumference is measured routinely up to 36 months and in any child whose head size is questionable
  • CAbnormal; chest should already exceed head by 5 to 7 cm at this age
  • DExpected, and head circumference is measured routinely only through 12 months
Rationale

"Generally, head and chest circumferences are equal at about 1 to 2 years of age. During childhood, chest circumference exceeds head size by about 5 to 7 cm (2 to 2.75 inches)." Head circumference is measured "in children up to 36 months of age and in any child whose head size is questionable," at "its greatest frontooccipital circumference" (p. 104).

Watch for

(C) applies the later childhood relationship at the crossover age. Note the technique detail: use paper or nonstretchable tape "because a cloth tape can stretch and give a falsely small measurement."

  • ✓Respirations first (before the infant is disturbed), pulse next, temperature last
  • CPulse, respirations, temperature
  • DAny order, provided all three are obtained

  • ARectal, since it is the clinical gold standard
  • ✓Axillary, because rectal temperature is contraindicated for infants younger than 1 month old due to risk of rectal perforation, and axillary is the recommended screening route at this age
  • COral, since it is the standard for noninvasive measurement
  • DTympanic, since it is fastest
Rationale

Rectal "remains the clinical gold standard" but is "contraindicated for infants younger than 1 month old due to risk of rectal perforation," and the chapter's bottom line states: "For infants younger than 1 month of age, axillary temperatures are recommended for screening" (pp. 105–106).

(A) is true as a general statement and unsafe in this patient, which is the shape of every route question in this chapter. 35. The correctly sized cuff is unavailable and the nurse must choose between one that is too small and one that is too large. Which should be used, and what error will result?
  • AThe undersized cuff, which will read falsely low
  • ✓The oversized cuff, accepting a falsely low reading — the chapter directs using an oversized cuff rather than an undersized one
  • CThe undersized cuff, accepting a falsely high reading
  • DNeither; defer the measurement
Rationale

"If the cuff size is too small, the reading on the device is falsely high. If the cuff size is too large, the reading is falsely low," and the Nursing Alert directs: "When the correct size is not available, use an oversized cuff rather than an undersized one" (p. 108).

Watch for

(A) is the mirror error and the fact bank calls this the single most swapped pair in the chapter.   • ✓Orthostatic hypotension most likely due to an autonomic deficit, because the sustained systolic drop exceeds 20 mm Hg without an increase in heart rate of more than 15 beats/min
  • COrthostatic hypotension from a nonneurogenic cause such as hypovolemia
  • DThe measurement is invalid because 2 minutes is too short
Rationale
