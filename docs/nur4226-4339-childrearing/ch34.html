1. A child is assessed after a traumatic spinal cord injury, once the flaccid phase has resolved. Which findings are expected?
  • ✓Spastic paralysis below the lesion, exaggerated tendon reflexes, a Babinski reflex, and no muscle wasting
  • BFlaccid paralysis, absent reflexes, and marked atrophy
  • CFlaccid paralysis with hyperreflexia
  • DNormal reflexes with preserved strength
Rationale

Box 34.9 gives the upper motor neuron syndrome as "Spastic paralysis in muscle groups below lesion (intact reflex arcs below lesion)," "Hyperreflexia with tendon reflexes exaggerated, Babinski reflex present," "No wasting of muscle mass because of increased muscle tone," and "Flexion contractures and spasms of muscle groups below lesion level common" (p. 1329).

Watch for

(B) is the lower motor neuron picture, and the chapter assigns each to its population in one sentence: "Most SCIs involve upper motor neurons; children born with spinal cord defects have primarily lower motor neuron deficits" (p. 1329). A myelomeningocele stem should read flaccid and a traumatic injury stem spastic. (C) mixes one finding from each column, which is how a half-remembered pair fails.

2. A child is being worked up for progressive weakness, and the serum creatine kinase is markedly elevated. What does this suggest?
  • ✓A muscle disease such as muscular dystrophy, because creatine kinase is not elevated in neurogenic disease
  • BAn anterior horn cell disease such as spinal muscular atrophy
  • CA peripheral nerve disorder such as Guillain-Barre syndrome
  • DNothing, since creatine kinase rises with any cause of weakness
Rationale

Creatine kinase "is released in large amounts in some muscular diseases, such as MD. CK is not elevated in neurogenic disease" (p. 1299). In Duchenne muscular dystrophy the level is dramatic and early: "Serum CK levels are extremely high in the first 2 years of life, before the onset of clinical weakness" (p. 1341).

Watch for

(B) and (C) are both real causes of weakness in this chapter and both are neurogenic, so the enzyme is precisely what sorts them from a myopathy. The chapter's own framework is anatomic: the most useful classification "defines the site of origin of the pathologic lesion: the anterior horn cells of the spinal cord, the peripheral nerves, the neuromuscular junction, and the muscles" (p. 1298).

3. A nurse is reviewing how different disorders block neuromuscular transmission. Which pairing is correct?
  • ✓Botulism blocks the release of acetylcholine, while myasthenia gravis blocks the receptor sites
  • BBotulism blocks the receptor sites, while myasthenia gravis blocks acetylcholine release
  • CBoth act by inhibiting cholinesterase
  • DNeither acts at the neuromuscular junction
Rationale

Transmission happens in three steps — release, diffusion to receptor sites, and removal by cholinesterase — and "Interference at any of these three steps will block transmission of nerve impulses and prevent muscular contraction" (p. 1299). "toxins that prevent the release of acetylcholine are those that produce the paralysis of botulism and tick paralysis," while action at receptor sites "is also blocked by the drug curare" and by the antibodies of myasthenia gravis. Botulism toxin "exerts its effect by inhibiting the release of acetylcholine at the neuromuscular junction" (p. 1325).

Watch for

(B) swaps the two, and the swap matters clinically because the treatments diverge completely: botulism is treated by neutralizing the toxin with immune globulin, while myasthenia gravis is treated with cholinesterase inhibitors that leave more acetylcholine at the receptor. (C) describes the step blocked by organophosphate insecticide poisoning.

4. A nursing student asks what the single most important risk factor for cerebral palsy is. Which answer is correct?
  • ✓Preterm birth of extremely and very low birth weight infants
  • BBirth asphyxia
  • CChorioamnionitis
  • DIntraventricular hemorrhage
Rationale

"Although CP occurs in term births, preterm birth of ELBW and VLBW infants continues to be the single most important risk factor for CP" (p. 1301). The chapter also revises the older teaching: "Although the prevalent traditional hypothesis has been that CP results from perinatal problems, especially birth asphyxia, it is now believed that CP results more often from existing prenatal brain abnormalities."

Watch for

(B) is the retired hypothesis and the one most people were taught first. (C) and (D) are real risk factors, which is what makes them selectable, but chorioamnionitis carries the caveat that "not all term infants exposed to chorioamnionitis develop CP," and intraventricular hemorrhage is named as a risk in low-birth-weight infants specifically, which makes it a consequence of the same prematurity the correct answer names. This is the chapter's own NCLEX question 3.

5. A 3-month-old at risk for cerebral palsy is generally hypotonic, and a parent asks whether this rules out the spastic type. What should the nurse explain?
  • ✓Early neurologic manifestations are usually generalized hypotonia, which may last weeks to as long as a year before spasticity appears
  • BHypotonia rules out spastic cerebral palsy, which is hypertonic from birth
  • CHypotonia at this age is always a normal finding
  • DThe diagnosis can be confirmed now on the basis of tone alone
Rationale

"Early neurologic manifestations are usually generalized hypotonia or decreased tone that lasts for a few weeks or may extend for months or even as long as a year" (p. 1301). The diagnosis takes time: "motor impairment associated with voluntary control is usually not apparent until after 2 to 4 months of age at the earliest. More often the diagnosis cannot be confirmed until the age of 1 or 2 years" (p. 1304).

Watch for

(B) is the stage swap this section is built on, because spastic cerebral palsy does not start out spastic. (D) overstates what tone can settle, and the chapter cuts the other way as well: "some children who show signs consistent with CP before 2 years do not demonstrate such signs after 2 years."

6. Which findings in an infant should raise concern for cerebral palsy? Select all that apply.
Select all that apply · scored all or nothing
  • ✓Preferential use of one hand at about 6 months of age
  • ✓Persistence of primitive infantile reflexes
  • ✓An obligatory tonic neck response
  • ✓Rigidity and extension of the whole body when pulled to a sitting position
  • EAbsence of hand dominance at 4 years of age
Rationale

"An early sign is preferential unilateral hand use that may be apparent at approximately 6 months of age. Hand dominance does not normally develop until the preschool years" (p. 1301). "Persistence of primitive reflexes is one of the earliest clues to CP" (p. 1303), and Box 34.3 lists an "Obligatory tonic neck reflex at any age." (E) is the wrong option and it inverts the hand-preference rule: what concerns the examiner is dominance appearing too early, not too late, because dominance normally does not emerge until the preschool years.

7. A school nurse hears a teacher say that a student with spastic cerebral palsy and dysarthria must be cognitively impaired. How should the nurse respond?
  • ✓Children with cerebral palsy have a wide range of intelligence, and 50% to 60% are within normal limits; speech difficulty is often misread as cognitive impairment
  • BThe teacher is correct, since cerebral palsy always involves cognitive impairment
  • COnly children with athetoid cerebral palsy have normal intelligence
  • DIntelligence cannot be assessed in a child whose speech is unclear
Rationale

"Intellectual impairment is a concern, although children with CP have a wide range of intelligence, and 50% to 60% are within normal limits," and "Speech difficulties are often interpreted as a sign of cognitive impairment" (p. 1303). The chapter adds that "Many persons with CP who have severely limiting physical involvement actually have the least intellectual impairment," and that improved communication devices "have revealed that some people with quadriplegic spastic CP have normal intelligence."

Watch for

(C) takes a true comparative statement — "As a group, children with athetosis and ataxia are intellectually superior to those with other types of CP" — and converts it into an exclusion the same paragraph refutes. (D) restates the assumption that created the problem.

8. A nurse is teaching a parent to feed a toddler with cerebral palsy who has difficulty sucking and swallowing. Which position should be used?
  • ✓A more flexed sitting position, with the arms brought forward
  • BSemireclining, so gravity assists the flow
  • CFully supine with the neck extended
  • DProne across the parent's lap
Rationale

"Children should be fed in the normal eating position. When they have difficulty sucking and swallowing, it is tempting to hold them in a semireclining posture to make use of gravity flow. However, this method does not promote active swallowing, and the neck hyperextension may even interfere with swallowing. A more flexed sitting position, with the arms brought forward to decrease the tendency toward back and neck extension, is more natural during bottle- or spoon-feeding and encourages active swallowing" (p. 1307).

Watch for

(B) is named in the source as the tempting option and refused in the same sentence, and (C) deepens the same problem, since the neck hyperextension is what interferes with the swallow. The stakes are concrete: coughing and choking while eating "may predispose the child with CP to aspiration, which may not be readily apparent" (p. 1303).

9. A child with an intrathecal baclofen pump is admitted with high fever, rebound spasticity, itching, and rapid deterioration, and sepsis is suspected. What else should the nurse consider?
  • ✓Intrathecal baclofen withdrawal, which can mimic sepsis and is treated by reestablishing the dose
  • BAn expected adverse effect of baclofen that will resolve on its own
  • CBotulinum toxin overdose from a recent injection
  • DA seizure disorder unrelated to the pump
Rationale

"Abrupt withdrawal of intrathecal baclofen, especially at high doses, may result in adverse effects such as rebound spasticity, pruritus, hyperthermia, rhabdomyolysis, disseminated intravascular coagulation, multiorgan failure, and death; in some cases intrathecal baclofen withdrawal may mimic sepsis." Treatment "centers on reestablishing the medication dosage, with improvements observed within 1 to 2 hours" (p. 1306).

Watch for

(B) treats a potentially fatal withdrawal as a nuisance effect. (C) reaches for the other spasticity drug in the chapter, whose adverse effects are far milder — "pain at the injection site and a temporary weakness" — and whose action peaks at 2 weeks rather than producing an acute crisis (p. 1305).

10. A school-age child with spastic cerebral palsy is falling below the 5th percentile for weight. Which statement should guide the nurse's plan?
  • ✓Children with spasticity expend more energy and often require more energy intake than same-age peers
  • BCaloric intake should be restricted, since children with cerebral palsy gain weight easily
  • CGrowth below the 5th percentile is expected in this population and needs no intervention
  • DFeeding difficulty is the only possible cause, so a gastrostomy should be placed
Rationale

"Children with spasticity expend more energy and often require more energy intake than same-age counterparts to maintain adequate growth," and "children with CP are at risk for failure to thrive, and the nurse must ensure an adequate caloric intake," with high-calorie supplements sometimes needed after 1 year of age (p. 1309). The balance is named too: monitor growth on a standardized growth chart while "avoiding overfeeding and obesity."

Watch for

(C) is close to a real observation — "Growth is affected in children with spastic tetraplegia, and many children remain below the 5th percentile for age and sex" (p. 1308) — and turns an expected difficulty into a reason not to act, which is the plausible-but-adjacent move. (B) reverses the risk for this subgroup entirely. More frequent rest periods belong in the plan as well, because the effort of daily activities is itself the drain.

11. A child with cerebral palsy is admitted for orthopedic surgery, and the mother says she is exhausted and asks whether she must stay overnight. What is the best response?
  • ✓Respect her preference, since a hospitalization may be the first chance for a parent to defer care and the respite may be crucial
  • BInsist that she room in, since parental presence always improves outcomes
  • CExplain that the nursing staff cannot manage her child's care without her
  • DSuggest she stay but sleep in the waiting area
Rationale

Rooming-in has real benefits, "but it is equally important to remember that a hospitalization may be the first time a parent can defer care to a nurse and not be the primary caregiver. This respite may be crucial to the parent's well-being. Respect the parent's preference in this regard" (p. 1310).

Watch for

(B) states a principle that is true often enough to become a reflex, and this chapter names the exception explicitly, because the family of a child with cerebral palsy has been delivering continuous care at home for years. (C) is untrue and produces guilt on top of exhaustion.

12. A woman whose first child was born with a myelomeningocele asks about folic acid before her next pregnancy. What should the nurse advise?
  • ✓4 mg daily under a practitioner's supervision, beginning at least 1 month before conception and through the first trimester, and not from a multivitamin alone
  • B0.4 mg daily, the same as for all women of childbearing age
  • C4 mg daily, taken as extra multivitamin tablets
  • DNo supplement, since folic acid is already added to cereal grain products
Rationale

All women of childbearing age take "a multivitamin with 0.4 mg folic acid daily," while a woman with a previously affected pregnancy takes 4 mg daily "under a practitioner's supervision, beginning at least 1 month before they plan a pregnancy and through the first trimester because the neural tube closes about 1 month after conception" (p. 1311). And "Supplementation of 4 mg of folate should not be given solely in multivitamin preparations because of the risk for overdose of other vitamins" (p. 1317).

Watch for

(B) applies the general-population dose to a woman whose recurrence risk is 3% to 4% (p. 1313), and the tenfold difference between the two numbers is the entire point of the pair. (C) has the right dose in the wrong vehicle, which is exactly what the overdose caution exists to prevent.

13. A newborn has a saclike protrusion over the lumbar spine. Which finding would distinguish a myelomeningocele from a meningocele?
  • ✓A neurologic deficit below the lesion
  • BThe presence of a visible sac
  • CA location in the lumbosacral region
  • DAn elevated maternal alpha-fetoprotein level
Rationale

A meningocele "encases meninges and spinal fluid but no neural elements," while a myelomeningocele "contains meninges, spinal fluid, and nerves," and the chapter states the consequence directly: "Neurologic deficit is not associated with meningocele but occurs in varying, often serious, degrees in myelomeningocele" (pp. 1312–1313).

Watch for

(B) and (C) belong to both, since each is a form of spina bifida cystica, "a visible defect with an external saclike protrusion," and the lumbar or lumbosacral area is the commonest site for either. What separates them is what is inside the sac and therefore what works below it.

14. A newborn with an unrepaired myelomeningocele is admitted. Which nursing actions are correct? Select all that apply.
Select all that apply · scored all or nothing
  • ✓Cover the sac with a sterile, moist, nonadherent dressing using normal saline
  • ✓Change the dressing every 2 to 4 hours and inspect the sac for leaks and irritation
  • ✓Keep the infant prone
  • ✓Avoid rectal temperatures
  • EApply a diaper over the defect to contain stool
Rationale

"Before surgical closure, the myelomeningocele is kept from drying by the application of a sterile, moist, nonadherent dressing. The moistening solution is usually sterile normal saline. Dressings are changed frequently (every 2 to 4 hours), and the sac is closely inspected for leaks, abrasions, irritation, and signs of infection." "Before surgery the infant remains in the prone position to minimize tension on the sac and the risk of trauma" (p. 1318). The safety rule is separate: "Avoid taking rectal temperatures in affected infants. Because bowel sphincter function is frequently affected, the thermometer can cause irritation and rectal prolapse" (p. 1313).

Watch for

(E) is the wrong option and it is the instinct of anyone caring for a newborn passing stool continuously: "Diapering the infant may be contraindicated until the defect has been repaired and healing is well advanced" (p. 1318). Note that the continual passage of stool is itself "often misinterpreted as diarrhea."

15. A neonate born 6 hours ago has a myelomeningocele that is leaking cerebrospinal fluid. When should surgical closure occur?
  • ✓Within 24 hours
  • BWithin 24 to 72 hours, the usual window
  • CAfter about a week, to allow the infant to stabilize
  • DAfter hydrocephalus has been ruled out
Rationale

"Most authorities believe that early closure, within the first 24 to 72 hours, offers the most favorable outcome. Surgical closure within the first 24 hours is recommended if the sac is leaking CSF" (p. 1314), and earlier still is preferred: "Early closure, preferably in the first 12 to 18 hours, not only prevents local infection and trauma to the exposed tissues but also avoids stretching other nerve roots."

Watch for

(B) is the general rule, and this infant meets the stated exception to it. (D) inverts the sequence, since hydrocephalus commonly declares itself only "after the primary closure of the opening on the back" (p. 1313). Delay also costs the kidneys: "Increased incidence of febrile UTIs, vesicoureteral reflux, and hydronephrosis have been shown when closure is delayed past 72 hours" (p. 1314).

16. An infant has had surgical repair of a myelomeningocele. Which complication should the nurse most anticipate?
  • ✓Hydrocephalus
  • BChiari II malformation
  • CLatex allergy
  • DTethered cord syndrome
Rationale

"approximately 80% to 85% of children with SB develop hydrocephalus," and "Although present at birth, hydrocephalus may not be apparent until shortly thereafter, or after the primary closure of the opening on the back" (p. 1313). Postoperative care therefore includes measuring head circumference daily and examining the fontanels "for signs of tension or bulging" (p. 1318).

Watch for

(B) is a real association in about 80% of these infants, but it is a coexisting malformation rather than a postoperative complication and is treated "only when the child is symptomatic" (p. 1315). (C) develops over years of repeated exposure rather than in the first postoperative week (p. 1319), and (D) is "more common during periods of rapid growth" (p. 1315). All three are true of this population, and none is what the nurse watches for now. This is the chapter's own NCLEX question 1.

17. A 4-year-old with spina bifida is at risk for urinary system distress. What management should the nurse expect?
  • ✓Clean intermittent catheterization, typically combined with an antispasmodic medication
  • BAn indwelling catheter for continuous drainage
  • CDDAVP, to reduce urine output
  • DThe Mitrofanoff procedure as the first-line approach
Rationale

For a child with evidence of, or at risk for, urinary system distress, management is "clean intermittent catheterization (CIC), typically in combination with an antispasmodic medication such as oxybutynin or propantheline" (p. 1316). (D) is a real option and a surgical one, reserved for "When the child cannot attain continence by conservative measures" (p. 1316), so it answers a different question than the one asked. 18. Two children with myelomeningocele are compared: one leaks urine almost constantly, the other stays dry between catheterizations. Which statement is accurate?
  • ✓The child with more severe leakage is less predisposed to serious urinary tract infections, because the detrusor leak point pressure is lower
  • BThe drier child has the safer bladder
  • CLeakage has no relationship to upper urinary tract risk
  • DBoth carry identical risk, determined only by the level of the lesion
Rationale

"When the magnitude of the pressure required to drive urine across the abnormal sphincter is greater than 40 cm H2O (the detrusor leak point pressure) and the compliance of the bladder wall is low, the risk of urinary system distress is high" (p. 1315). "When the detrusor leak point pressure is lower than 40 cm H2O, urinary leakage is more severe, although the risk of urinary system distress is lessened. Thus the child with more severe urinary incontinence is less predisposed than the 'drier' child to serious urinary tract infections" (p. 1316).

Watch for

(B) is what continence intuitively suggests, and the chapter states the reverse in so many words. The pressure the bladder generates, not the dryness of the diaper, is what threatens the kidneys.

20. A nurse is taking a preoperative history. Which approach to latex allergy is correct?
  • ✓Ask every patient about latex sensitivity as a routine part of the preoperative history
  • BAsk only patients with spina bifida or urogenital anomalies
  • CRely on skin prick testing rather than on the history
  • DAsk only if the parent raises the concern
Rationale

"The identification of those sensitive to latex is best accomplished through careful screening of all patients. During the health interview with the parent or child, ask all patients, not only those at risk, about sensitivity to latex. (B) narrows the screen to the population in which the association was first described. 21. A 6-year-old develops weakness that began in the legs about 10 days after a viral illness and is ascending, with depressed reflexes and flaccid paralysis. Which condition is this, and what is the treatment?
  • ✓Guillain-Barre syndrome, treated primarily with intravenous immunoglobulin given within 2 weeks of onset
  • BBotulism, treated with botulism immune globulin
  • CGuillain-Barre syndrome, treated with corticosteroids alone
  • DMyasthenia gravis, treated with a cholinesterase inhibitor
Rationale

Guillain-Barre syndrome is "an uncommon acute demyelinating polyneuropathy with a progressive, usually ascending flaccid paralysis," typically beginning "approximately 10 days after a nonspecific viral infection," with "Proximal muscle weakness progressing to paralysis usually occurs before distal weakness" and tendon reflexes "depressed or absent" (p. 1322). "IVIG is now recommended as the primary treatment of GBS when administered within 2 weeks of disease onset" (p. 1323).

Watch for

(C) attaches a plausible antiinflammatory to a demyelinating disease, and the chapter refuses it: "Corticosteroids alone do not decrease the symptoms or shorten the duration of the disease" (p. 1323). (B) names the disease whose paralysis runs the other way, beginning with cranial nerve signs and descending.

22. An adolescent with Guillain-Barre syndrome is intubated and nearly completely paralyzed. Which nursing considerations are correct? Select all that apply.

  • ✓Recovery of muscle strength progresses in the reverse order of onset

  • ✓The head of the bed is elevated 30 degrees to help prevent ventilator-associated pneumonia

23. A child is admitted with generalized tetanus. Which set of nursing measures is correct?
  • ✓Minimize sound, touch, and light; use standard precautions rather than isolation; and plan active immunization during recovery
  • BPlace the child in isolation, since tetanus is communicable
  • CKeep the room brightly lit and the child stimulated, to assess mentation
  • DOmit immunization after recovery, since the illness confers immunity
Rationale

"every effort should be made to control or eliminate stimulation from sound, light, and touch," because "The slightest noise, a gentle touch, or bright light triggers convulsive muscular contractions that last seconds to minutes" (pp. 1324–1325). "Standard precautions for the child with tetanus are recommended; isolation is not recommended," and "When the child recovers, active immunization should take place because contraction of the disease does not confer a permanent immunity" (p. 1325).

Watch for

(D) is the assumption that holds for many infections and specifically does not hold here. (B) treats a toxin-mediated wound infection as a communicable disease. (C) trades a seizure trigger for information the nurse already has, since mentation is unaffected throughout: "the patient remains alert, and pain and distress are reflected in a rapid pulse, sweating, and an anxious expression."

24. A fully immunized 10-year-old whose last booster was 3 years ago has a deep puncture wound contaminated with soil. What should the nurse anticipate?
  • ✓A tetanus toxoid booster as soon as possible after the injury
  • BTetanus immunoglobulin, because the wound is contaminated
  • CNo intervention, since the child is fully immunized
  • DBoth tetanus immunoglobulin and toxoid, at separate sites
Rationale

For a more serious wound — contaminated, puncture, crush, or burn — in an immunized child, the child is "given a tetanus toxoid booster prophylactically as soon as possible after injury." Watch for

(B) and (D) both give immunoglobulin, which belongs to the unprotected or inadequately immunized child with a tetanus-prone wound, where "Concurrent administration of both TIG and tetanus toxoid at separate sites is recommended." The child's immune status, not the dirt in the wound, decides which column applies, and (C) would be correct only for a clean, minor wound.

25. A 3-month-old presents with constipation, generalized weakness, a weak cry, poor feeding, and diminished reflexes, and infant botulism is suspected. Which statements are correct? Select all that apply.
Select all that apply · scored all or nothing
  • ✓Botulism immune globulin intravenously is given without waiting for laboratory confirmation
  • ✓Antibiotics are not part of management, and aminoglycosides may worsen the condition
  • ✓Honey should not be given to infants younger than 12 months
  • ✓Spinal muscular atrophy type 1 and sepsis are common initial misdiagnoses
  • EEquine antitoxin is the treatment of choice in infants
Rationale

"Treatment consists of immediate administration of botulism immune globulin intravenously (BIG-IV) without delaying for laboratory diagnosis." And "SMA type 1 and metabolic disorders are often mistaken for infant botulism … Presenting clinical signs also often mimic those of sepsis in young infants" (p. 1325).

Watch for

(E) is the wrong option, excluded by age: "Trivalent equine botulinum antitoxin and bivalent antitoxin, used in adults and older children, are not administered to infants" (p. 1326). Constipation as the presenting symptom, and an acute onset in a previously well infant, are what point away from spinal muscular atrophy.
