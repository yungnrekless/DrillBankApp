1. A nurse hesitates to medicate a postoperative child, reasoning that pain is unpleasant but not harmful. How should this be corrected?
  • AThe reasoning is sound if the pain is short lived
  • ✓Pain triggers physiologic stress responses with negative consequences involving multiple systems; long-term amplification of the sympathetic nervous system hinders healing, and unrelieved pain may prolong the stress response and adversely affect recovery
  • CThe concern applies only to neonates
  • DPain becomes harmful only once it becomes chronic
Rationale

"Pain triggers a number of physiologic stress responses in the body, and these may lead to negative consequences involving multiple systems. Long-term amplification of the sympathetic nervous system hinders healing. Unrelieved pain, whether from trauma, surgery, or disease, may prolong stress response and adversely affect a child's recovery" (pp. 137–138).

Watch for

(D) reverses the direction of the relationship the chapter draws. Poorly controlled acute pain "predisposes patients to chronic pain syndromes" — acute pain left untreated is one of the ways chronic pain begins, not a separate and lesser problem.

2. A preterm infant who has had many heel sticks now cries and becomes rigid during routine repositioning and diaper changes. What explains this?
  • AThe infant has become manipulative and is seeking attention
  • ✓The wind-up phenomenon — after noxious stimuli, multiple levels of the spinal cord experience altered excitability, which may cause normal nonpainful stimuli such as routine nursing care and handling to be perceived as noxious
  • CNormal irritability of prematurity, unrelated to prior procedures
  • DAn adverse effect of analgesia
Rationale

The wind-up phenomenon is "attributed to a decreased pain threshold and chronic pain"; after noxious stimuli "multiple levels of the spinal cord experience an altered excitability," which "may cause normal nonpainful stimuli, such as routine nursing care and handling, to be perceived as noxious stimuli" (p. 138). Neonates "have more generalized spinal reflex responses and lower thresholds to stimuli than older children."

Watch for

(C) treats an acquired, mechanistic change as constitutional temperament, and it removes the reason to act. Wind-up is why repeated procedural pain in a preterm infant is a compounding problem rather than a series of isolated events.

3. A nurse describes bandage removal, nasogastric tube placement, and urinary catheter placement as routine rather than painful. How should this be evaluated?
  • AAccurate; these are noninvasive and are not associated with pain
  • ✓Inaccurate; the chapter names each of these explicitly as very painful to children, and warns that nurses may find it easy to overlook such experiences as part of normal care
  • CAccurate for children over 5 years old
  • DInaccurate only for the nasogastric tube
Rationale

"Nurses may find it easy to overlook certain painful experiences as part of normal care and perform invasive procedures without any attempts to minimize pain. Something as minor and ordinary as bandage removal, nasogastric tube placement, or urinary catheter placement is very painful to children" (p. 138). Premature infants "are particularly vulnerable to repeated acute pain episodes from routine and frequent procedures."

Watch for

(A) is the working assumption the chapter is written to dislodge — the risk is not disbelieving a child's pain report but never asking, because the procedure was not categorized as painful in the first place.

4. A child reports pain at a site where no tissue injury can be identified, and a nurse suggests the pain is not real. How should this be addressed?
  • AThe nurse is correct; pain requires an identifiable physical source
  • ✓Once pain tracks are created in the brain, it is possible for pain to exist purely in the brain — and that pain is real, and it feels the way the child says it feels, regardless of the location of origin
  • CThe child should be evaluated for a psychiatric disorder before pain is treated
  • DThe report should be documented but not treated
Rationale

"Once these pain tracks are created in the brain, it is possible for pain to exist purely in the brain. This pain, however, is real and it feels the way the child says it feels — regardless of the location of origin" (p. 138). The brain neuromatrix is "a series of connected neural cells with cognitive, sensory, and affective pathways combined in a feedback loop unique to each individual."

Watch for

(C) makes treatment conditional on ruling out a psychological cause, which reverses the chapter's stance. The origin of the pain does not determine whether it is real or whether it is treated.

5. A verbal 9-year-old rates his pain 8 out of 10 while lying quietly and watching television. His FLACC score is 1. Which assessment should guide management?
  • AThe FLACC score, because behavioral observation is more objective than self-report
  • ✓The child's self-report, because self-report is the single most reliable indicator of pain and must be accepted and respected as the most important pain assessment data available
  • CAn average of the two scores
  • DNeither; repeat both in an hour
Rationale

"Self-report is the single most reliable indicator of pain and must be accepted and respected by nurses as the most important pain assessment data available" (p. 145). Observational measures are the fallback for children who cannot self-report, and "Pain scores on observational measures do not correlate with children's own reports of pain intensity" (p. 144).

Watch for

(A) is the central inversion of this chapter and it is the reason quiet children go untreated. An observational tool measures behavioral and physiologic responses, not perceived intensity — the two are different constructs, so a low behavioral score does not overrule a high self-report. (C) treats two incommensurable measures as averageable.

6. A nurse is deciding whether to use an observational pain measure for a 10-year-old with chronic abdominal pain. What does the chapter say about the tool's validity here?
  • AObservational measures are equally valid at all ages and for all pain types
  • ✓Observational measures are most reliable for short, sharp procedural pain such as heel lance or injection; they are less reliable for acute pain and are not reliable or valid for chronic pain or for pain in older children
  • CObservational measures are preferred for chronic pain because they avoid reporting bias
  • DObservational measures are valid for chronic pain but not procedural pain
Rationale

"Observational pain measures are most reliable when used to measure short, sharp procedural pain, such as pain due to heel lance or injections. They are less reliable when measuring acute pain and not reliable or valid for assessing chronic pain or pain in older children" (p. 144).

Watch for

(C) is the convenience answer for a busy unit and it fails on two counts at once — wrong pain type and wrong age. The related reason acute tools fail in chronic pain: "Tools that measure acute pain intensity may not be sensitive to the subtle changes and slow resolution characteristic of chronic and recurrent pain because pain expression becomes more complex."

7. A child rated 5 on one unit and 5 on another, and a nurse concludes the pain is unchanged. What is the flaw?
  • AThere is no flaw; a 5 is a 5
  • ✓Scores are not interchangeable — a pain score of 5 may mean a lot of pain on a 0 to 5 scale or very little on a 0 to 100 scale — and consistent use of the same measurement tool is necessary to standardize communication and evaluate effectiveness
  • CThe second score should have been doubled
  • DPain scores should never be compared across time
Rationale

"A pain score of 5 may mean a lot of pain (if a 0 to 5 scale is used) or very little (if a 0 to 100 scale is used)," and "Consistent use of the same measurement tools is necessary to standardize communication related to pain and evaluate the effectiveness of pain management strategies" (p. 143).

Watch for

(D) overcorrects into abandoning serial comparison entirely, which is the whole purpose of consistent tool use. Comparison across time is the point; comparison across *tools* is what fails, and the deeper reason is that different tools measure different pain expressions.

principles of pain measurementgeneral
8. A nurse records only a numeric pain rating at each assessment. What else does the chapter require?
  • ANothing further; intensity is the standard measure
  • ✓Location, frequency, duration, and precipitating, aggravating, and alleviating factors, plus pain's effect on sleep, emotional function, physical recovery or functioning, disability, role functioning, and satisfaction with pain treatment
  • COnly the analgesic given and the time
  • DVital signs, which substitute for the additional dimensions
Rationale

"Pain assessment should be more thorough than merely obtaining a pain rating. Understanding the location, frequency, duration, and precipitating, aggravating, and alleviating factors of the pain experienced by the child is also essential," along with pain's effect on the domains listed (p. 143). Other dimensions "may change without a change in pain intensity."

Watch for

(D) substitutes physiologic signs for the assessment, which is a version of the same error as trusting a behavioral score over a self-report — vital signs are a response to pain, not a measure of its dimensions.

9. A nurse is assessing pain in a nonverbal 6-month-old. Which finding is the most consistent and specific indicator?
  • AHeart rate elevation
  • ✓Facial expression, evaluated through features such as eye squeeze, brow bulge, open mouth, and taut tongue
  • COxygen saturation
  • DDuration of crying
Rationale

"Facial expression is the most consistent and specific indicator of pain in infants," evaluated through features such as "eye squeeze, brow bulge, open mouth, and taut tongue" (p. 143).

Watch for

(A) and (C) are physiologic parameters that appear inside several scales as scored variables, which makes them plausible — but neither is singled out as the most consistent and specific indicator, and both change for many reasons other than pain.

10. A 4-year-old is recovering in the postanesthesia care unit and is too drowsy to self-report. Which tool is validated for this situation?
  • ACOMFORT-B
  • ✓FLACC
  • CWong-Baker FACES
  • DCRIES
Rationale

"The only tool found reliable and valid for pain assessment of children 2 months to 7 years of age recovering in the postanesthesia care unit is the FLACC tool" (p. 145). FLACC scores five behaviors — Face, Legs, Activity, Cry, Consolability — each 0 to 2, for a total of 0 to 10.

Watch for

(A) is the right idea in the wrong setting, and it is the item's whole point. COMFORT-B belongs to the critically ill, ventilated, nonverbal child; FLACC is the one with PACU validation. This pair sorts by setting, not by the child's alertness.

11. A 6-year-old is mechanically ventilated and sedated in the PICU. Which tool is most appropriate, and what score suggests pain?
  • AFLACC, with pain considered above 5
  • ✓COMFORT-B, with pain considered above 17 on a 6 to 30 scale
  • CNumeric Rating Scale, with pain considered above 4
  • DNIPS, with pain considered above 4
Rationale

The COMFORT-B is "one of the most commonly used pain scales to assess critically ill ventilated children who are nonverbal due to sedation or their condition" and "is particularly helpful in children who are mechanically ventilated." Its range is 6–30, and the table's note reads "Consider pain >17" (pp. 144–145). It scores alertness, calmness/agitation, respiratory response or crying, physical movement, muscle tone, and facial tension.

Watch for

(A) attaches the FLACC score range to the right kind of child but the wrong tool, and (D) uses an infant scale on a school-age child. Both score ranges and age ranges have to travel with the tool name.

12. A nurse must choose an infant pain scale for a neonate born at 28 weeks of gestation who is being assessed after surgery. Which scale's age range excludes this infant?
  • APIPP-revised, 25–40 weeks
  • BN-PASS, 23–40 weeks
  • ✓CRIES, 32–40 weeks
  • DNIPS, 26–40 weeks
Rationale

Table 5.4's age ranges are in gestational weeks: PIPP-revised 25–40, NIPS 26–40, N-PASS 23–40, COMFORT-neo 24–42, and CRIES 32–40 (p. 144). At 28 weeks this infant falls below the CRIES range.

Watch for

CRIES is otherwise the intuitive pick, because it is the postoperative scale and the stem supplies a postoperative context — which is exactly why the age column has to be read alongside the type-of-pain column. (A) is the reflex rejection, since PIPP-revised sounds like the specialized tool with the narrowest window; its range comfortably includes 28 weeks.

13. Which infant pain scales adjust the score for gestational age?
  • ANIPS and CRIES
  • ✓PIPP-revised and N-PASS
  • CCOMFORT-neo and CRIES
  • DAll five adjust for gestational age
Rationale

Of the five scales in Table 5.4, only PIPP-revised and N-PASS carry a gestational-age adjustment (p. 144). PIPP-revised scores heart rate, oxygen saturation, brow bulge, eye squeeze, nasolabial furrow, and behavioral state 0–3 each, for a range of 0–21; N-PASS scores vital signs, crying/irritability, facial expressions, behavioral state, and extremities/tone 0–2 each, for a pain range of 0–10.

Watch for

(D) is the reasonable assumption that any scale designed for neonates must account for maturity, and it is wrong for three of the five. Note that CRIES scores Crying, requiring Oxygen, Increased vital signs, Expression, and Sleeplessness — its acronym is the mnemonic, and it is a postoperative tool for the 32-to-40-week infant.

self-report pain rating scalesgeneral
14. A 3-year-old needs a self-report pain scale. Which is validated at this age, and what is the caution?
  • AFaces Pain Scale–Revised, which starts at a neutral face
  • ✓Wong-Baker FACES, validated for children as young as 3 years — but smiling faces on pain assessment scales may lead younger children to rate the affective domain of pain rather than its intensity, so simple concrete anchor words should be used
  • CNumeric Rating Scale, since 3-year-olds can count
  • DVisual Analog Scale, since it is nonverbal
Rationale

Wong-Baker FACES uses "six cartoon faces ranging from smiling face for 'no pain' to tearful face for 'worst pain'" and is "For children as young as 3 years old." The caution: "smiling faces on pain assessment scales may lead younger children to rate the affective domain of pain; or, in other words, rate their feelings about having pain rather than report the intensity of their physical pain," mitigated by using "Simple, concrete anchor words, such as the script originally used to validate the tool" (pp. 145–146).

Watch for

(A) is the right instinct about the smiling-face problem applied to the wrong age — the Faces Pain Scale–Revised starts at a neutral face precisely because of that concern, but it is validated for children 5 to 17, not for a 3-year-old.

15. A 6-year-old needs a faces scale, and the nurse wants to avoid the affect problem. Which scale starts with a neutral rather than a smiling face, and what is its age range?
  • AWong-Baker FACES, 3 years and up
  • ✓Faces Pain Scale–Revised, for children 5 to 17
  • COucher, 3 to 13 years
  • DWord-Graphic Rating Scale, 8 to 17 years
Rationale

The Faces Pain Scale–Revised uses "six cartoon faces ranging from neutral face for 'no pain' to grimacing face 'worst possible pain'" and is for children 5–17 (p. 146).

Watch for

(A) is the mirror error of the previous item, and the starting face is the entire discriminator between these two scales — smiling for Wong-Baker, neutral for the revised scale. Both have six faces and both are cartoons, so nothing else separates them at a glance.

16. A nurse plans to use the Oucher photographic scale with a 5-year-old. What must the child be able to do first?
  • ACount off any two numbers or count by 10s
  • ✓Rate six geometric shapes from largest to smallest
  • CRead the descriptive words on the scale
  • DNothing; the photographic scale requires no prerequisite
Rationale

The Oucher provides "six photographs of a white child's face representing 'no hurt' to 'biggest hurt you could ever have;' also includes vertical scale with numbers from 0-100; scales for African American and Hispanic children have been developed," for children 3–13. For the photographic scale the child "should be able to rate six geometric shapes from largest to smallest" (p. 146).

Watch for

(A) is the prerequisite for the Oucher's numeric scale, not its photographic one — the same tool has two halves with two different readiness tests. Note also the chapter's finding on the ethnic versions: an "Ethnically similar scale may not be preferred by child when given choice of ethnically neutral cartoon scale."

17. An 8-year-old is being asked to rate pain. Which scale is most widely used in clinical practice, and which do children least prefer?
  • AThe Visual Analog Scale is most widely used; children least prefer the Numeric Rating Scale
  • ✓The Numeric Rating Scale is most widely used because it is easy to use; research shows children ages 3 to 18 least prefer the Visual Analog Scale
  • CBoth are equally used and equally preferred
  • DThe Word-Graphic Rating Scale is most widely used
Rationale

"The numeric rating scales (NRSs), such as the 0 to 10 scale, are the most widely used in clinical practice because they are easy to use," and of the VAS: "Research shows that children ages 3-18 years old least prefer VAS compared with other scales" (pp. 146–147). Both are used from about 8 years.

Watch for

(A) swaps the two, which is the only way this pair is tested. Both scales share an age floor of about 8 years, so the discriminator is preference and practicality, not developmental readiness.

self-report pain rating scalesgeneral
18. At what age do the cognitive skills required for a numeric rating scale appear, and can a younger child report pain at all?
  • ✓Seriation appears between 7 and 10 years, so most school-age children can use a numeric scale — and even 2-year-olds can report pain
  • BNumeric scales can be used from 3 years, and children under 3 cannot report pain
  • CSeriation appears at 5 years, and children under 5 cannot report pain
  • DNumeric scales require 12 years of age
Rationale

The cognitive skills of "measurement, classification, and seriation (the ability to accurately place items in rank order), become apparent between 7 and 10 years of age. Therefore most school-age children have the cognitive ability required to use a numeric rating scale." Self-report measures "are most often used for children older than 3 to 4 years, but even 2-year-olds can report pain" (p. 145).

Watch for

(B) and (C) both convert a scale's readiness threshold into a claim that younger children cannot report pain. Those are different statements: the age boundary governs which *tool* fits, not whether the child has something to tell you. "Although children at 3 or 4 years of age are able to self-report pain, they do not have the cognitive ability to understand rank order," which is why faces scales exist.

19. A preterm infant lies still and shows no facial grimace during a heel stick. What should the nurse conclude?
  • AThe infant is not experiencing pain
  • ✓Preterm infants' responses to pain may be behaviorally blunted or absent, so it is imperative to presume that pain exists in all situations usually considered painful for adults and children, even in the absence of behavioral or physiologic signs
  • CThe observational score should be recorded as zero and no analgesia given
  • DThe infant has a high pain threshold
Rationale

"Preterm infants' responses to pain may be behaviorally blunted or absent," and the rule that follows is that "it is imperative to presume that pain exists in all situations that are usually considered painful for adults and children, even in the absence of behavioral or physiologic signs" (pp. 144–145).

Watch for

(D) inverts the physiology. Neonates have "lower thresholds to stimuli than older children," not higher — the absence of a visible response reflects an immature or overwhelmed capacity to *show* pain, not an absence of feeling it.

20. A neonate is receiving vecuronium. How should pain be assessed and managed?
  • ABehavioral observation scales remain valid because muscle tone is scored
  • ✓An infant receiving a muscle-paralyzing agent is incapable of a behavioral or visible pain response, yet there is ample evidence such infants are neurologically capable of feeling pain — so pain is presumed and treated
  • CParalysis eliminates pain perception, so analgesia is unnecessary
  • DPain should be assessed by heart rate alone
Rationale

"an infant receiving a muscle-paralyzing agent (vecuronium) is incapable of a behavioral or visible pain response; however, there is ample evidence that such infants are neurologically capable of feeling pain" (p. 145).

Watch for

(C) is the dangerous version of the same misreading, and it is why the chapter states this case separately from the blunted-response one. A paralytic removes the signal, not the sensation. (A) fails because every behavioral variable, muscle tone included, depends on the movement the drug has abolished.

21. A nonverbal 8-year-old with severe cognitive impairment is being assessed for postoperative pain. Which tools are validated, and what does the chapter require when using the revised FLACC?
  • AFLACC, COMFORT-neo, and CRIES; no customization is needed
  • ✓Revised FLACC, the Non-communicating Children's Pain Checklist–Revised, and the Paediatric Pain Profile — and when using the revised FLACC, nurses must include parent-reported pain behaviors to customize the tool to the individual child
  • COnly the Numeric Rating Scale, with parental proxy reporting
  • DWong-Baker FACES, with the parent selecting the face
Rationale

The three validated tools are rFLACC, NCCPC-r, and PPP, and "When using the rFLACC, nurses must include parent-reported pain behaviors to customize the tool to use with individual children" (p. 145). Children with cognitive impairment "may not be able to express pain through behaviors or self-report methods validated for developmentally similar peers," and parents report their pain "was not effectively managed."

Watch for

(A) offers a plain FLACC without the revised descriptors, and the difference is not cosmetic — the customization step is what makes the tool valid for this child, because the behaviors that signal pain differ from child to child.

22. A parent of a child with cerebral palsy reports that physical therapy, assisted stretching, and putting on splints are painful. How should the nurse treat this information?
  • AAs parental over-reporting, since these are therapeutic rather than noxious activities
  • ✓As accurate and expected: parents of children with cognitive impairment report that ordinary daily activities including assisted stretching and walking, independent standing, toileting, putting on splints, occupational therapy, range of motion, and physical therapy are painful
  • CAs a reason to discontinue therapy
  • DAs relevant only if the child also cries
Rationale

Parents reported that these ordinary daily activities were painful, and the most frequent parent-reported pain behaviors include "crying; being less active; seeking comfort; moaning; not cooperating; being irritable; being stiff, spastic, tense, or rigid; sleeping less; being difficult to satisfy or pacify; flinching or moving body part away; and being agitated or fidgety" (p. 145).

Watch for

(D) makes crying the gatekeeper for believing the report, when the parent's list contains eleven behaviors and crying is only one — the child who becomes stiff, less active, or hard to pacify is reporting pain in a form a nurse unfamiliar with that child will miss.

pain assessment in special populationsgeneral
23. A verbal 10-year-old with autism spectrum disorder needs pain assessment. What does the chapter direct?
  • AUse an observational tool, since children with autism cannot reliably self-report
  • ✓Use self-report measures whenever possible — ask the verbal child where the pain is located and to describe how it feels; in nonverbal individuals, watch for escalation of repeated self-stimulating activities such as rocking or hand flapping
  • CRely entirely on parental report
  • DUse the CRIES scale
Rationale

Because cognitive ability varies widely in autism spectrum disorder, "self-report measures should be used whenever possible. Ask the verbal child with ASD where the pain is located and to describe how it feels." Nonverbal individuals "may have an escalation of repeated self-stimulating activities (i.e., rocking or hand flapping) when in pain" (p. 146).

Watch for

(A) generalizes from a diagnosis to a capability, which is exactly what the chapter warns against — ability varies widely, and the default remains self-report. This is the same principle as the chapter's central rule, applied to a population where it is most often set aside.

chronic and recurrent pain assessmentgeneral
24. A child has had daily abdominal pain for four months with significant functional disability and no other explanation. How is this classified?
  • AAcute pain, since each episode is brief
  • ✓Chronic primary pain — pain in one or more anatomic regions persisting or recurring for longer than 3 months, associated with significant emotional distress or functional disability, and not better explained by another chronic pain condition
  • CChronic postsurgical pain
  • DNociceptive pain
Rationale

Chronic pain is "Persistent or recurrent pain lasting longer than 3 months," and chronic primary pain adds the emotional distress or functional disability criterion and the exclusion clause (p. 137). The 3-month cutoff runs through every chronic subtype in the definitions table.

Watch for

(D) names a mechanism rather than a time course — nociceptive pain is "pain related to tissue injury" — and it is available as a distractor whenever a stem describes a bodily location. Duration is what sorts acute from chronic here.

chronic and recurrent pain assessmentgeneral
25. A nurse is selecting a tool for a child with recurrent headaches and wants a measure of how pain affects everyday physical activities. Which tool fits?
  • ✓Functional Disability Inventory, a comprehensive evaluation of the influence of pain on physical functioning
  • BFLACC
  • CCRIES
  • DWong-Baker FACES
Rationale

The Functional Disability Inventory is "a more comprehensive evaluation of the influence of pain on physical functioning" and assesses "the child's ability to perform everyday physical activities" (p. 147). For migraine specifically, PedMIDAS is "specifically designed to evaluate pain caused by migraines," and the PedsQL covers physical, emotional, social, and academic functioning for the child younger than 7 years.

Watch for

(D) measures intensity, which is exactly what acute-pain tools give and what chronic pain assessment needs more than. The goal of a pain treatment program is "to improve function, quality of life, and a return to daily activities" — so function is the outcome being measured, not a secondary detail.

chronic and recurrent pain assessmentgeneral
26. A child is being followed for chronic pain. What else does the chapter direct the nurse to screen for?
  • ANothing beyond pain intensity and function
  • ✓Depression and anxiety, since chronic and recurrent types of pain often occur with some level of both, using measures such as the Children's Depression Inventory and the Revised Child Anxiety and Depression Scale together
  • COnly sleep disturbance
  • DSubstance use exclusively
Rationale

"Chronic and recurrent types of pain often occur with some level of depression and anxiety in children," and the two named instruments "may be used together to better measure negative affect" (p. 148).

Watch for

(C) picks a single real domain out of a broader assessment. Sleep is one of the dimensions the chapter lists for any pain assessment, alongside emotional function, physical functioning, disability, role functioning, and satisfaction with treatment — screening for mood is an addition specific to chronic pain, not a substitute for the rest.

biobehavioral interventionsgeneral
27. A nurse refers to distraction and guided imagery as "nonpharmacologic" interventions. What term does the chapter prefer, and why?
  • ANonpharmacologic is the correct and preferred term
  • ✓Biobehavioral, because it reflects the mechanism of action, whereas "nonpharmacologic" reflects the lack of pharmaceutical treatment as though medications are the gold standard
  • CAlternative, because the interventions replace medication
  • DComplementary, because they are secondary to medication
Rationale

The term biobehavioral intervention "is preferred over nonpharmacologic because it is a term that reflects the mechanism of action. The term nonpharmacologic reflects the lack of pharmaceutical treatment as though medications are the gold standard" (p. 148). And the chapter adds: "Sometimes biobehavioral interventions are as effective as, or even more effective than, pharmacologic interventions."

Watch for

(C) and (D) misuse two terms the chapter defines precisely against each other: "Alternative refers to use of a nonmainstream practice to replace standard medical care. Complementary care is used together with standard medical care," and integrative medicine is "the integration of conventional and biobehavioral approaches."

biobehavioral interventionsgeneral
28. A nurse plans a pain treatment approach for a postoperative child. Which approach does the chapter identify as preferred?
  • AAnalgesics alone, with biobehavioral methods reserved for failures
  • ✓Multimodal — combinations of analgesic and biobehavioral therapies — because by targeting different mechanisms of pain, medications combined with biobehavioral interventions are more effective at relieving pain
  • CBiobehavioral methods alone, to avoid opioid exposure
  • DWhichever the parent prefers, without regard to evidence
Rationale

Multimodal treatment, "also known as combinations of analgesic and biobehavioral therapies, is the preferred pain treatment plan" (p. 149). Biobehavioral interventions work by "interrupting the pain, fear, anxiety, and stress cycle," and they "can help reduce pain perception, make pain more tolerable, decrease anxiety, and enhance the effectiveness of analgesics or reduce the dosage required," with "few if any side effects."

Watch for

(A) treats biobehavioral methods as a fallback, which is the ranking the chapter's terminology argument is written against. The mechanism is complementary rather than duplicative — different targets, additive effect.

biobehavioral interventionsgeneral
29. A nurse wants to teach a school-age child a distraction technique for an upcoming dressing change. When should this be taught?
  • ADuring the procedure, when the child is motivated to use it
  • ✓Before pain occurs or before it becomes severe — and even after learning it, children often need help using it during a painful procedure
  • CAfter the procedure, for use next time
  • DTiming is not important as long as the technique is taught
Rationale

"Children should learn to use a specific strategy before pain occurs or before it becomes severe." Even after learning it, "children often need help using it during a painful procedure," and it can be used afterward so "the child [has] a chance to recover, feel mastery, and cope more effectively" (p. 149).

Watch for

(A) is the practical instinct and it fails for the same reason the chapter gives for severe pain generally: "Severe and sustained pain is more difficult to control." A technique first encountered mid-procedure has to compete with the distress it is meant to interrupt.

biobehavioral interventionsgeneral
30. A child cannot name a coping technique she has used before. What should the nurse do?
  • ASelect a technique for her based on her age
  • ✓Describe several techniques and let the child select one, involving the parents in the selection process because they may be familiar with the child's usual coping style
  • CDefer biobehavioral intervention and rely on medication
  • DUse the technique most recently taught on the unit
Rationale

If the child cannot name a familiar coping technique, describe several and let the child select. "Parents should be involved in the selection process; they may be familiar with the child's usual coping style," and if the parent cannot assist, "other appropriate persons may include a grandparent, older sibling, nurse, or child-life specialist" (p. 149).

Watch for

(A) removes the child from a choice the chapter deliberately gives her, and it discards the parent's knowledge of how this child actually copes. Well-researched, low-risk options the chapter names are biofeedback, guided imagery, hypnosis, mindfulness, and yoga.

31. A 2-year-old has moderate postoperative pain. According to the WHO two-step strategy, what is the first-line approach?
  • AStart morphine, since the pain is postoperative
  • ✓Administer a nonopioid analgesic first; opioids are started when pain is severe or unrelieved by nonopioids, and morphine is the medicine of choice for the second step
  • CStart with codeine and escalate if needed
  • DStart a benzodiazepine to reduce anxiety and reassess
Rationale

"For children older than 3 months of age the first step is to administer a nonopioid analgesic. Opioids are started when pain is severe or unrelieved by nonopioids. The WHO principles are "Using a two-step strategy; Dosing at regular intervals; Using the appropriate route of administration; Adapting treatment to the individual child."

Watch for

(D) substitutes an anxiolytic for an analgesic, which the chapter rules out by name: "Benzodiazepines are frequently used to treat anxiety; however, these drugs do not provide analgesia and should not be used as a substitute for analgesics." 36. A child receiving morphine becomes difficult to arouse. The nurse notes the respiratory rate is slightly increased and concludes the child is comfortable and sleeping. How should this be evaluated?

  • ✓Incorrect and dangerous — respiratory depression progresses predictably: the patient first becomes sedated and unarousable, then depth of respirations decreases while rate increases, then end-tidal carbon dioxide rises, oxygen saturation falls, and respiratory rate decreases and then ceases
  • CCorrect; sedation is the expected therapeutic effect
  • DIncorrect only if the child has an opioid allergy
Rationale

Respiratory depression "is rare, but is the most serious adverse effect from analgesics," and the chapter gives the sequence exactly as stated. The nursing implication: "Sedation from opioids is to be avoided and nurses must increase their monitoring vigilance while patients sleep" (pp. 157–158).

Watch for

(A) and (C) are the two halves of the same reassuring misreading, and both are specifically wrong. Unarousable sedation is the first step of the sequence, not a sign of comfort, and the rate rise is an early compensatory change rather than evidence of adequate ventilation.

  • ✓Withdrawal symptoms can be anticipated and prevented by weaning patients from opioids that were administered for more than 5 days
  • CWithdrawal is a concern only after 30 days of therapy

Rationale

Watch for

(D) confuses physical dependence with addiction.
