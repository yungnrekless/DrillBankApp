infectious diseaseimmunity types
1. A nurse administers a live attenuated vaccine to a healthy toddler. Which type of immunity is this intervention producing?
  • ANatural immunity
  • BPassive immunization
  • ✓Active immunization
  • DHerd immunity
Rationale

Active immunization is the administration of an antigen (infectious agent or vaccine), characterized by an antibody produced by the individual host, as in vaccinating children. It is slower to develop but long lasting.

Watch for

Calling a vaccine "passive" is the classic distractor — passive immunity is a transferred, ready-made antibody, not a host-generated one.

2. A patient is bitten by a stray dog and, along with the rabies vaccine series, receives human rabies immunoglobulin. What type of immunity does the immunoglobulin provide?
  • AActive immunization
  • ✓Passive immunization
  • CNatural immunity
  • DHerd immunity
Rationale

Passive immunization is the transfer of a specific antibody from an immunized individual or source (immunoglobulin/antiserum). It works almost immediately but is short lived, serving as a stopgap until active immunity develops.

Watch for

This is the rabies postexposure prophylaxis scenario, which uses both passive (immunoglobulin, immediate) and active (vaccine, durable) immunity at once — do not assume only one type is present.

4. Which statement correctly distinguishes active from passive immunization?
  • AActive immunization is immediate but short lived; passive immunization is slow but durable
  • ✓Active immunization involves a host-produced antibody after antigen exposure and is slow but long lasting; passive immunization involves a transferred antibody and is immediate but short lived
  • CBoth produce antibody responses of similar speed and duration
  • DPassive immunization always precedes active immunization in a vaccine schedule
Rationale

Active immunization means the host makes its own antibody after receiving an antigen (agent or vaccine); onset is slower but protection is long lasting. Passive immunization transfers a ready-made antibody; protection is almost immediate but short lived, often used as a stopgap.

Option A reverses the two — this exact reversal is the single most likely EAQ distractor pattern for this topic.

6. A woman who had measles as a child is now protected against future measles infection. What type of immunity does this represent?
  • ANatural immunity
  • ✓Acquired immunity
  • CPassive immunization
  • DHerd immunity
Rationale

Acquired immunity is resistance a host acquires as a result of previous natural exposure to an infectious agent (having measles once protects against future infection). It may be induced by either active or passive immunization.

7. What is the basis of herd immunity, and what is it the rationale for?
  • AAn individual's own antibody response after natural infection; the rationale for isolation precautions
  • ✓A high proportion of individuals in a group being immune, which resists invasion and spread of an agent; the rationale for increasing immunization coverage
  • CPassive transfer of maternal antibody across a whole community; the rationale for prenatal screening

Herd immunity is the immunity of a group or community — resistance to invasion and spread of an infectious agent based on a high proportion of individuals being immune. It is the basis for programs that increase immunization coverage.

  • AInfectivity
  • BPathogenicity
  • ✓Virulence
  • DToxicity
Rationale

Virulence is the ability to produce a severe pathologic reaction, distinct from pathogenicity (the ability to produce any specific clinical reaction at all) and from toxicity (the ability to produce a poisonous reaction).

Watch for

Pathogenicity and virulence are frequently confused. Pathogenicity asks whether disease results at all; virulence asks how severe that disease is.

16. A patient is HIV-positive but has no signs or symptoms of illness. How should this be classified?
  • AInfected and diseased
  • ✓Infected but not diseased
  • CDiseased but not infected
  • DNeither infected nor diseased
Rationale

Infection is the entry, development, and multiplication of the infectious agent in the host; disease is a possible outcome of infection, involving physiologic dysfunction or a pathologic reaction. An asymptomatic HIV-positive person is infected but not diseased; once clinical AIDS develops, the person is both infected and diseased.

Watch for

Infection does not automatically equal disease — this exact HIV framing appears in the source and is a likely stem.

19. A patient with hepatitis B is infectious for weeks before symptoms appear and remains infective through the acute phase and possibly for life as a chronic carrier. What does this illustrate about incubation and communicable periods?
  • AThey are synonymous and always overlap exactly
  • BThe incubation period always outlasts the communicable period
  • ✓They are not synonymous and can diverge, as HBV shows by being communicable well before and long after the incubation period ends
  • DThe communicable period only begins after the incubation period is complete
Rationale

The incubation period is the time from invasion by an infectious agent to first appearance of signs and symptoms; the communicable period is the interval during which the agent may be transferred to others. They are not synonymous. HBV-infected persons are infectious many weeks before symptoms and remain infective through the acute phase and possibly for life as chronic carriers.

Watch for

Any option treating incubation period and communicable period as interchangeable is wrong — the chapter explicitly flags them as distinct concepts that can diverge.

36. Which influenza virus type is responsible for large epidemics and is the only type capable of antigenic shift?
  • ✓Type A
  • BType B
  • CType C
  • DType D
Rationale

Influenza type A causes large epidemics and is the only type in which antigenic shift occurs. Type B tends to be more regional, type C is sporadic and mild, and type D infects cattle rather than people.

infectious diseaseinfluenza, antigenic drift and shift
37. How do antigenic drift and antigenic shift differ?
  • ADrift is a major change producing new subtypes; shift is a minor yearly change
  • ✓Drift is a minor antigenic change causing yearly epidemics; shift is a major change producing new subtypes with pandemic potential, occurring only in type A
  • CDrift and shift both occur equally across types A, B, and C
  • DDrift causes pandemics; shift causes only regional outbreaks
Rationale

Antigenic drift refers to minor antigenic changes that drive yearly epidemics and regional outbreaks. Antigenic shift refers to major changes producing new subtypes, which occur only with type A; shift plus drift together drive epidemics every few years and pandemics every 10 to 40 years, such as novel influenza A H1N1 in 2009.

Watch for

Reversing "minor/yearly" and "major/pandemic potential" between drift and shift is the classic inversion distractor. ---
