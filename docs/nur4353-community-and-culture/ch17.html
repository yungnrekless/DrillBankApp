disease surveillancedefinitions and purpose
1. Which four activities are named in the textbook's definition of disease surveillance?
  • AScreening, diagnosis, treatment, and follow-up
  • ✓Collection, analysis, interpretation, and dissemination of specific health data
  • CNotification, investigation, quarantine, and reporting
  • DDetection, confirmation, control, and evaluation
Rationale

Disease surveillance is defined as "the ongoing systematic collection, analysis, interpretation, and dissemination of specific health data for use in public health."

Watch for

Notification/investigation/quarantine/reporting describe outbreak response activities, not the definition of surveillance itself.

  • AAn optional adjunct to clinical practice
  • ✓The foundation of public health practice
  • CA function reserved for federal agencies only

The CDC indicates that public health surveillance is the foundation of public health practice.

  • ✓Process data

Process data focus on what is done — services provided or protocols for care delivery. The proportion of the eligible population vaccinated is the textbook's own example of process data.

4. A health department tracks the incidence rate of new influenza cases in that same population over the same year. What type of data is this?
  • AProcess data
  • ✓Outcome data
  • CRegistry data
  • DAlgorithmic data
Rationale

Outcome data focus on changes in health status; incidence rates of influenza are the textbook's own example of outcome data.

This is the mirror image of the vaccination-proportion item. Process = what is done (an action/service); outcome = what happened to health status as a result. ---

notifiable disease reportinglegal basis and national/state structure
5. A nurse assumes that because a disease is on the CDC's nationally notifiable disease list, every state legally requires it to be reported. Is this assumption correct?
  • AYes, national notifiable status automatically creates a legal reporting mandate in every state
  • ✓No — reporting requirements are mandated by state law or regulation, and states differ in their lists, so not all nationally notifiable diseases are legally mandated for reporting in a given state
  • CYes, but only for diseases added to the list after 1997
  • DNo — only CDC WONDER-listed diseases carry any legal mandate
Rationale

Requirements for reporting diseases are mandated by law or regulation at the state level. Each state and Washington, DC, differs in its list of reportable diseases, so not all nationally notifiable diseases are legally mandated for reporting in a given state.

Watch for

The national list (NNDSS) is a CDC/CSTE reporting arrangement between states and the CDC, not itself a law compelling reporting within a state — that authority is state law.

  • CThe National Vital Statistics System

10. A local health department identifies a possible case that does not yet meet the formal case definition for a reportable disease because lab confirmation is pending. What is the correct action?
  • AWait until the case definition is formally met before taking any public health action
  • ✓Take action to control the disease as soon as the problem is identified, even without enough information to meet the case definition
  • CRefer the decision to the CDC before acting
  • DTake no action unless the patient develops severe symptoms
Rationale

The chapter states plainly that action to control a disease should be taken as soon as a problem is identified, although there may not be enough information to meet the case definition. The case definition should not be used as the only criterion for taking public health action.

Watch for

Waiting for laboratory confirmation before acting is the classic wrong answer — the case definition is a reporting/monitoring tool, not a gate on control action. ---

surveillance systemsthe four types
11. A physician's office sends a case report to the local health department after diagnosing a reportable illness. Which type of surveillance system does this describe?
  • AActive
  • ✓Passive
  • CSentinel
  • DSpecial
Rationale

In a passive system, case reports are sent to local health departments by health care providers, or laboratory reports are sent to the local health department. Reports come in to the health department.

12. Several schoolchildren become ill after eating lunch in the cafeteria. The health department nurse begins contacting local providers, names the suspected illness, and actively gathers data on existing cases to determine the scope of the problem. Which type of surveillance system does this describe?
  • APassive
  • ✓Active
  • CSentinel
  • DSyndromic
Rationale

This is the chapter's own example of an active surveillance system: the nurse, as a health department employee, begins a search for cases through contacts with local providers and agencies, names the disease, and gathers data to determine the magnitude of the problem.

Watch for

A distractor may frame active surveillance as merely "faster" or "automated" reporting. Its defining feature is who initiates the search — the health department goes out looking, rather than waiting for reports to come in.

outbreak and endemic vocabularymagnitude terms
17. Which term describes the persistent, usual presence of a disease at a low to moderate number of cases, and provides the baseline against which increases are measured?
  • ASporadic
  • ✓Endemic
  • CHyperendemic
  • DEpidemic
Rationale

Endemic is a persistent (usual) presence with a low to moderate number of cases of the disease or event in a population, and provides the baseline.

outbreak and endemic vocabularymagnitude terms
18. A disease is highly prevalent and commonly acquired early in life. Counterintuitively, its prevalence decreases as age increases. Which term describes this pattern?
  • AHyperendemic
  • BSporadic
  • ✓Holoendemic
  • DEpidemic
Rationale

Holoendemic describes a highly prevalent problem commonly acquired early in life, in which prevalence decreases as age increases — the reverse of what most conditions do.

Watch for

This is stated as counterintuitive precisely because most health problems become more prevalent with age; holoendemic is the exception, not the rule.

  • AEndemic
  • ✓Hyperendemic
  • CHoloendemic
  • DPandemic
Rationale

Hyperendemic describes a persistently (usually) high number of cases; the chapter's example is the high cholera incidence rate among Asian and Pacific Islanders.

20. Occasional cases of a disease occur at irregular intervals with no consistent pattern. Which term applies?
  • AEndemic
  • BEpidemic
  • ✓Sporadic
  • DMixed
Rationale

Sporadic describes an irregular pattern with occasional cases found at irregular intervals.

outbreak and endemic vocabularymagnitude terms
21. The occurrence of a disease within an area clearly in excess of expected (endemic) levels for a given time period is often called which of the following?
  • AThe pandemic
  • ✓The outbreak
  • CThe sentinel event
  • DThe cluster
Rationale

An epidemic is the occurrence of a disease clearly in excess of expected (endemic) levels for a given time period; this is often called the outbreak. A pandemic is an epidemic spread over several countries or continents.

outbreak and endemic vocabularyoutbreak patterns
22. In an outbreak, all persons exposed become ill at the same time, during one incubation period. Which outbreak pattern does this describe?
  • APropagated
  • ✓Point source
  • CIntermittent source
  • DMixed
Rationale

A point source outbreak is one in which all persons exposed become ill at the same time, during one incubation period.

Watch for

The discriminator to memorize is incubation periods, not severity or case count: point source = one incubation period, everyone at once; propagated = more than one incubation period, no common source.

outbreak and endemic vocabularyoutbreak patterns
23. An outbreak has no common source and spreads gradually from person to person over more than one incubation period, as with tuberculosis. Which outbreak pattern does this describe?
  • APoint source
  • BCommon source
  • ✓Propagated
  • DIntermittent or continuous source
Rationale

A propagated outbreak does not have a common source and spreads gradually from person to person over more than one incubation period; tuberculosis spreading from one person to another is the textbook example.

outbreak and endemic vocabularyoutbreak patterns
24. A group is exposed to a single common noxious influence, such as the release of a toxic gas into a subway or water system. Which outbreak pattern does this describe?
  • ✓Common source
  • BMixed
  • CPropagated
  • DSentinel
Rationale

A common source outbreak is one in which a group is exposed to a common noxious influence; the textbook example is the release of ricin into a subway system and into a US water system.

outbreak and endemic vocabularyoutbreak patterns
25. A restaurant chain's food poisoning cases appear over several days to weeks, traced to a shipment of contaminated green onions purchased at different times. Which outbreak pattern does this describe?
  • APoint source
  • ✓Intermittent or continuous source
  • CPropagated
  • DMixed
Rationale

An intermittent or continuous source outbreak involves cases exposed over a period of days or weeks; the textbook example is food poisonings at a restaurant chain nationwide from contaminated green onions.

outbreak and endemic vocabularyoutbreak patterns
26. A person becomes ill after a common source exposure, then has person-to-person contact with others and spreads the disease further, as occurs with influenza. Which outbreak pattern does this describe?
  • APropagated
  • ✓Mixed
  • CPoint source
  • DCommon source
Rationale

A mixed outbreak occurs when a victim of a common source epidemic has person-to-person contact with others and spreads the disease, further propagating the health problem; influenza is the given example.

Watch for

Mixed starts as common source, then becomes person-to-person — it is not the same as a purely propagated outbreak, which never has a common source at all. ---

27. Of all the people who become infected with an agent, the proportion who go on to develop clinical disease describes which concept?
  • AInfectivity
  • ✓Pathogenicity
  • CVirulence
  • DCommunicability
Rationale

Pathogenicity is the proportion of infected people who develop the disease.

Watch for

Swapping pathogenicity and virulence is the standard error — pathogenicity's denominator is "infected," virulence's denominator is "clinically ill."

Virulence is the proportion of people with clinical disease who become severely ill or die.

outbreak investigationthe six-step process and foodborne definition
33. Which of the following is the FIRST of the CDC's six steps for conducting an outbreak investigation?
  • ADevelop and evaluate a hypothesis
  • ✓Confirm the existence of an outbreak
  • CEstimate the number of cases
  • DInstitute control measures and communicate findings
Rationale

The six CDC steps, in order, are: confirm the existence of an outbreak; verify the diagnosis and/or define a case; estimate the number of cases; orient the data collected to person, place, and time; develop and evaluate a hypothesis; and institute control measures and communicate findings.
