HIVnatural history and window period
1. A patient contracted HIV three weeks ago and now reports fever, sore throat, night sweats, and swollen lymph nodes. An antibody test is drawn today. What is the most likely result and why?
  • APositive, because antibodies form within days of infection
  • ✓Negative, because antibodies have not yet developed even though the virus is present in large amounts
  • CPositive, because the mononucleosis-like syndrome always follows seroconversion
  • DIndeterminate, requiring a Western blot regardless of timing
Rationale

This is the primary infection stage — a mononucleosis-like syndrome within a month of infection when the virus is most plentiful. An antibody test at this stage is usually negative because antibodies have not yet formed.

Watch for

The symptoms are dramatic and the viral burden is high, which tempts a "positive" answer, but antibody tests detect antibodies, not the virus itself.

2. Place the three stages of HIV natural history in order.
  • AClinical latency, primary infection, symptomatic disease/AIDS
  • ✓Primary infection, clinical latency, symptomatic disease/AIDS
  • CSymptomatic disease/AIDS, primary infection, clinical latency
  • DPrimary infection, symptomatic disease/AIDS, clinical latency
Rationale

The sequence is primary infection (mononucleosis-like syndrome), clinical latency (asymptomatic period ending in seroconversion), then symptomatic disease progressing to AIDS.

HIVnatural history and window period
3. What is the HIV "window period," and what is its clinical significance?
  • AThe 20 minutes required for a rapid oral test to return a result
  • ✓The 6 weeks to 3 months after infection before antibodies appear, during which an antibody test can give a false negative
  • CThe interval between a positive EIA and a confirmatory Western blot
  • DThe period after AIDS diagnosis during which opportunistic infections are most likely
Rationale

The window period is the 6-weeks-to-3-months interval after infection but before antibodies are produced, during which the antibody test can give a false negative.

Watch for

An early negative test during this window is not definitive proof of no infection.

4. A patient tests negative for HIV antibodies 5 weeks after a high-risk exposure and asks if this rules out infection. What is the best response?
  • AYes, a negative antibody test at any point rules out infection
  • ✓No, this falls within the window period, and repeat testing is recommended because antibodies may not yet be detectable
  • CYes, because the mononucleosis-like syndrome would already have resolved by 5 weeks
  • DNo, because antibody tests are never accurate for HIV
Rationale

Seroconversion occurs 6 weeks to 3 months after infection. A negative test at 5 weeks falls inside the window period and does not rule out infection; repeat testing is warranted.

  • ASharing needles or drug-preparation equipment
  • ✓Anal or vaginal sex with an HIV-positive person not using a condom or HIV medicines
  • CAccidental needlestick injury

The first most common US transmission route is anal or vaginal sex with an HIV-positive person not using a condom or HIV medicines. Sharing needles/syringes is second most common; needlestick and organ transplant are rare routes.

8. A screening blood donation returns a positive result on the initial HIV test. What should happen next, and why?

  • ✓A Western blot should be performed to confirm the result and minimize false positives
  • CNo further testing is needed until symptoms develop

EIA is the most commonly used screening test. A positive EIA is confirmed with a Western blot, which minimizes false positives.

31. What is the primary stated purpose of directly observed therapy (DOT) for TB?

  • ✓To ensure adherence and prevent drug resistance, functioning as a population-level intervention
