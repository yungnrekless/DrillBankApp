3. A urine culture grows an extended-spectrum beta-lactamase-producing Klebsiella. The nurse anticipates an order for a drug from which class?
  • ✓Carbapenems
  • BPenicillins
  • CCephalosporins
  • DMonobactams such as aztreonam
Rationale

The chapter states that organisms producing ESBL are resistant to all beta-lactam antibiotics, meaning penicillins and cephalosporins, and to aztreonam, and can be treated only with carbapenems or sometimes fluoroquinolones. Box 39.1 gives carbapenems as the ESBL treatment.

Watch for

(D) pulls hardest because aztreonam is a monobactam rather than a penicillin or cephalosporin, so it reads as an escape from beta-lactam resistance. The key term definition explicitly includes aztreonam in what ESBL producers resist.

4. A week later the same patient's culture grows a carbapenem-resistant organism, and meropenem is ordered. Which agents does the chapter name as the remaining options for this infection?
  • ✓colistin and tigecycline
  • Bcarbapenems such as meropenem
  • Cpenicillins and cephalosporins
  • Daztreonam
Rationale

Box 39.1 names colistin and tigecycline as the treatment for carbapenem-resistant organisms. The chapter tells the escalation as cause and effect: heavy carbapenem use against ESBL producers drove bacteria to produce carbapenemase, which renders all carbapenems ineffective, leaving few known antibiotics.

(B) is the mirror error of the previous item — carbapenems are the correct escalation for ESBL, and a learner who banked that rule will apply it one organism too far. A carbapenem for a carbapenem-resistant organism names the exact drug class the organism is defined by resisting.

  • ✓Washing hands before and after every patient contact
  • BWearing a mask whenever entering the room
  • CRestricting the patient's visitors to immediate family
  • DObtaining repeat cultures every 48 hours
Rationale

The chapter states three separate times that these organisms are spread by contact, that all health care professionals must wash their hands before and after all patient contact, and that the importance of handwashing cannot be overemphasized. The vancomycin case study asks directly for the single best action to prevent spread.

(B) is the reflex pick because isolation and masks travel together in the learner's memory, but the chapter pairs multidrug-resistant organisms with contact isolation, not droplet or airborne precautions, and names handwashing rather than any barrier as the action of utmost importance.

aminoglycoside pharmacology and spectrummechanism and route
6. A student notes that aminoglycosides and tetracyclines bind the same ribosomal subunit and asks whether the two classes therefore do the same thing to bacteria. Which statement is accurate?
  • ✓Both bind the 30S ribosome, but aminoglycosides are bactericidal while tetracyclines are bacteriostatic
  • BBoth bind the 30S ribosome and both are bacteriostatic
  • CAminoglycosides bind the 50S ribosome, which is why they kill rather than suppress
  • DBoth bind the 50S ribosome, but only the tetracyclines are bactericidal
Rationale

The chapter states that aminoglycosides work in a way similar to that of the tetracyclines, in that they bind to ribosomes, specifically the 30S ribosome, and thereby prevent protein synthesis in bacteria. It separately classifies aminoglycosides as bactericidal and potent, making them drugs of choice for particularly virulent infections.

Watch for

(B) is the pull, because same binding site reads as same behavior. Shared target does not mean shared outcome here: the 30S site is common ground and the killing behavior is opposite.

7. An oral aminoglycoside is ordered for a patient with hepatic encephalopathy. Which statement explains why this order is not the class exception it first appears to be?
  • ✓neomycin is given orally precisely because it is too toxic for systemic use, and it acts locally on gut bacteria
  • BAll aminoglycosides are well absorbed orally when given with food
  • COral aminoglycosides are absorbed systemically and reach the liver in high concentration
  • Dneomycin is the only aminoglycoside available in an injectable form
Rationale

The chapter states that aminoglycosides are not given orally because of their poor oral absorption, with neomycin as the exception, and that neomycin's toxicity limits its use to the gastrointestinal tract and topical application. In hepatic encephalopathy it reduces the number of ammonia-producing bacteria in the gastrointestinal tract, and the resulting drop in blood ammonia levels sometimes improves neurologic symptoms.

Watch for

(D) inverts the fact that matters most about this drug: neomycin is not available in injectable form at all. A learner who remembers neomycin as the odd one out but not which way it is odd will pick it.

aminoglycoside pharmacology and spectrumcombination therapy and sequence
10. Gentamicin 300 mg IV and a beta-lactam antibiotic are both scheduled at 0900. Which should the nurse infuse first, and why?
  • ✓The beta-lactam, because it breaks down the bacterial cell wall so the aminoglycoside can reach the ribosomes
  • BThe aminoglycoside, because it needs the highest possible peak concentration and should not be delayed
  • CThe beta-lactam, because giving the aminoglycoside first inactivates the beta-lactam in the tubing
  • DEither order is acceptable, because the synergistic effect does not depend on sequence
Rationale

The chapter states that when aminoglycosides are used in combination with beta-lactam antibiotics, the beta-lactam antibiotic is given first, because the beta-lactams break down the cell wall of the bacteria and allow the aminoglycoside to gain access to the ribosomes, where they work. Critical thinking exercise 2 is built on this sequence.

Watch for

(B) is the reflex pick for anyone who has just learned concentration-dependent killing, since it argues from a real property of the class. Concentration matters for dosing interval, not for which bag hangs first; the sequence rule is mechanistic and runs the other way.

aminoglycoside therapeutic drug monitoringconcentration-dependent versus time-dependent killing
11. A pharmacist converts a patient from gentamicin three times daily to a single daily dose and explains that this drug kills best when a high plasma concentration is achieved even briefly. Which property is being described?
  • ✓Concentration-dependent killing
  • BTime-dependent killing
  • CThe postantibiotic effect
  • DThe synergistic effect
Rationale

The key term box defines concentration-dependent killing as a property of some antibiotics, especially aminoglycosides, whereby achieving high plasma drug concentrations, even if briefly, results in the most effective bacterial kill. This is the rationale for moving aminoglycosides toward once-daily dosing.

Watch for

(C) is adjacent and genuinely true of aminoglycosides, which makes it tempting: the postantibiotic effect is continued bacterial suppression after brief exposure. But that names what happens after the drug is gone, not the property that determines how high the level must go.

12. A different antibiotic's monograph states that prolonged high plasma concentrations are required for effective bacterial kill, which is why it is infused frequently rather than once daily. Which property is this, and which classes does the chapter say show it?
  • ✓Time-dependent killing, and it is the property of most antibiotic classes
  • BTime-dependent killing, and it is unique to the aminoglycosides
  • CConcentration-dependent killing, and it is the property of most antibiotic classes
  • DConcentration-dependent killing, and it is unique to the beta-lactams
Rationale

The key term box defines time-dependent killing as a property of most antibiotic classes whereby prolonged high plasma drug concentrations are required for effective bacterial kill, and cross-references it to concentration-dependent killing. The two definitions point at each other in the source text.

Watch for

This is the mirror error of the previous item, and (B) is where it lands: a learner who has just attached a monitoring rule to aminoglycosides tends to attach the second property to them as well. The aminoglycosides are the concentration-dependent exception; most other classes, including the beta-lactams, are time-dependent.

aminoglycoside therapeutic drug monitoringwhen peaks and troughs are drawn
13. A patient is receiving tobramycin 5 mg/kg once daily. When should the nurse expect the laboratory sample for a drug level to be drawn?
  • ✓A trough only, 8 to 12 hours after completion of the dose
  • BA peak only, 30 to 60 minutes after the infusion ends
  • CBoth a peak and a trough, with the trough drawn just before the next dose
  • DA trough only, drawn immediately before the next scheduled dose
Rationale

For once-daily regimens the chapter states that peak levels are usually not measured, because the peak for a single daily dose is assumed to be short-lived, and that trough levels are drawn at least 8 to 12 hours after completion of dose administration, with a therapeutic goal at or below 1 mcg/mL.

Watch for

(D) is the near miss and the most dangerous one, because drawing immediately before the next dose is the correct rule for traditional aminoglycoside dosing and for vancomycin. The sampling rule splits by dosing regimen, not by drug.

aminoglycoside therapeutic drug monitoringwhen peaks and troughs are drawn
14. Another patient receives gentamicin three times daily. Which sampling schedule matches the chapter's rule for traditional dosing?
  • ✓Peak 30 to 60 minutes after a 30-minute infusion, and trough just before the next dose
  • BPeak 8 to 12 hours after the dose, and no trough
  • CTrough only, 8 to 12 hours after completion of the dose
  • DPeak just before the next dose, and trough 30 minutes after the infusion
Rationale

With traditional three-times-daily dosing both peak and trough levels are measured. Samples for peak levels are drawn 30 to 60 minutes after a 30-minute infusion, and samples for trough levels are drawn just before the next dose.

Watch for

This is the mirror error of the previous item, and (D) is its full inversion — a peak drawn just before the next dose has the two definitions reversed. A trough is the lowest level and therefore comes at the end of the interval; a peak is the highest and comes shortly after the infusion.

15. A patient on once-daily tobramycin has a reported trough of 3 mcg/mL, and the next dose is due now. What does the nurse do?
  • ✓Hold the dose and notify the prescriber, because this trough is above the level associated with toxicity
  • BGive the dose, because troughs up to 5 mcg/mL are therapeutic for tobramycin
  • CRepeat the level in 24 hours and give the dose now
  • DSkip one dose only and resume the schedule without notifying anyone
Rationale

Table 39.1 gives a once-daily trough goal of less than 1 mcg/mL for gentamicin and tobramycin, and the text states that trough levels above 2 mcg/mL are associated with a greater risk for both ototoxicity and nephrotoxicity. The chapter also states that alteration in auditory, vestibular, or renal function may indicate the need for a possible dosage adjustment or withdrawal of the drug.

Watch for

(C) and (D) both delay a decision the number already supports, which is what makes them attractive to a learner who is unsure of the threshold. Repeating a level or skipping a single dose without notification leaves the prescriber unaware that the patient is in the toxic range.

16. An amikacin trough returns at 8 mcg/mL on once-daily dosing, and a new nurse says this must be toxic because gentamicin troughs are kept below 1. Which response is accurate?
  • ✓Amikacin's therapeutic range runs an order of magnitude higher, and less than 10 mcg/mL is the once-daily trough goal
  • BThe nurse is correct; all aminoglycoside troughs share the same goal of less than 1 mcg/mL
  • CAmikacin troughs should be reported in mg/mL rather than mcg/mL, so the value must be converted
  • DAmikacin peaks rather than troughs are the monitored value on once-daily dosing
Rationale

Table 39.1 gives amikacin a once-daily trough goal of less than 10 mcg/mL and a traditional-dosing trough range of 5 to 10 mcg/mL, against 1 to 2 mcg/mL for gentamicin and tobramycin. The chapter states that peak levels are usually not measured on once-daily regimens for any of these drugs.

Watch for

(C) is the unit trap the chapter gives its own key term for, warning that confusion of microgram and milligram sometimes results in drug dosage errors. Every level in this chapter is in mcg/mL, and a value reported in mg/mL is off by a factor of 1000.

17. A patient has been on IV gentamicin for six days. Which findings would the nurse interpret as evidence of nephrotoxicity? Select all that apply.
Select all that apply · scored all or nothing
  • ✓Urinary casts
  • ✓Proteinuria
  • ✓Rising BUN and serum creatinine levels
  • DTinnitus
  • EA sense of fullness in the ears
Rationale

The chapter lists urinary casts, described as visible remnants of destroyed renal cells, along with proteinuria and increased BUN and serum creatinine levels, as the manifestations of aminoglycoside nephrotoxicity. Serum creatinine is measured at least every 3 days as an index of renal function.

Watch for

(D) and (E) are the wrong side of the same toxicity table. Tinnitus and a sense of fullness in the ears are ototoxicity symptoms, and they pull because both toxicities are aminoglycoside toxicities monitored by the same drug levels. The distinction that matters clinically is that nephrotoxicity is usually reversible and ototoxicity likely is not.

aminoglycoside toxicity and monitoring prioritiesnephrotoxicity versus ototoxicity
18. A patient receiving an IV aminoglycoside reports new ringing in the ears partway through the infusion. Which action is the priority?
  • ✓Stop the infusion and notify the prescriber
  • BReassure the patient that ringing is expected and will resolve when therapy ends
  • CSlow the infusion rate and continue the dose
  • DIncrease the infusion rate so the dose finishes sooner
Rationale

Tinnitus is a symptom of ototoxicity, which the chapter says often is not reversible and can result in varying degrees of permanent hearing loss from injury to the eighth cranial nerve. Alteration in auditory, vestibular, or renal function may indicate the need for a possible dosage adjustment or withdrawal of the drug, and patient teaching states that hearing loss may occur even after completion of therapy.

Watch for

(C) is the mirror error of a different drug's rule — slowing the rate is the correct response to vancomycin red man syndrome, where rate is the actual mechanism. Ototoxicity is not rate-related, so the rate-adjustment options are answering the wrong drug's problem. (B) is wrong because the loss is likely permanent, not self-limiting.

20. A patient stabilized on warfarin is started on IV gentamicin. Which effect does the chapter predict, and by what mechanism?
  • ✓Potentiated warfarin toxicity, because the aminoglycoside kills gut flora and reduces vitamin K production
  • BReduced warfarin effect, because the aminoglycoside induces hepatic metabolism of warfarin
  • CNo interaction, because aminoglycosides are not absorbed from the gut
  • DPotentiated warfarin toxicity, because the aminoglycoside displaces warfarin from albumin
Rationale

The chapter states that aminoglycosides kill intestinal bacterial flora and so reduce the amount of vitamin K produced by these gut bacteria, and therefore can potentiate warfarin toxicity. The quinolone section gives the same mechanism for that class, describing antibiotic-induced alteration of the intestinal flora, which affects vitamin K synthesis.

Watch for

(C) borrows a true fact and draws the wrong conclusion from it. Poor oral absorption is why aminoglycosides are not given by mouth, but a systemically administered aminoglycoside still reaches and kills gut flora, which is exactly why the warfarin interaction exists.

21. A patient asks whether an antibiotic that works by destroying bacterial DNA will also damage human DNA. Which response reflects the chapter's description of the quinolones?
  • ✓They interfere with the bacterial enzymes DNA gyrase and topoisomerase IV and do not inhibit the production of human DNA
  • BThey bind the 30S ribosome, so human DNA is not involved at all
  • CThey inhibit both bacterial and human DNA synthesis, which is why courses are kept short
  • DThey disrupt the bacterial cell membrane rather than acting on DNA
Rationale

The chapter states that quinolones destroy bacteria by altering their deoxyribonucleic acid, accomplishing this by interfering with the bacterial enzymes DNA gyrase and topoisomerase IV, and that quinolones do not inhibit the production of human DNA.

Watch for

(B) is the aminoglycoside and tetracycline mechanism imported into the wrong class, and it pulls because both answers sound like plausible bacteria-specific targets. Ribosomal binding belongs to the protein-synthesis inhibitors; the quinolones act on the bacterial DNA enzymes.

quinolone mechanism and drug selectionmechanism and oral absorption
22. A hospitalized patient on IV levofloxacin is ready for discharge, and the prescriber changes the order to the oral form at the same dose. The patient's family questions whether pills can work as well as an IV drug. Which statement is accurate?
  • ✓Quinolones other than norfloxacin have excellent oral absorption, comparable in most cases to intravenous injection
  • BOral quinolones reach roughly half the blood level of the IV form, so the dose is usually doubled
  • CAll quinolones including norfloxacin are equally well absorbed orally
  • DOral quinolones are absorbed well only when taken with milk or an antacid
Rationale

The chapter states that with the exception of norfloxacin these antibiotics have excellent oral absorption, and that in most cases the extent of oral absorption is comparable to that of intravenous injection. Ciprofloxacin is described as working orally as well as many intravenous antibiotics.

Watch for

(D) inverts the class's central interaction. Dairy products and antacids reduce quinolone absorption and must be separated by at least 1 hour on either side, so the option names the exact pairing that defeats the oral route.

quinolone safety warnings and interactionscardiac effect versus interaction
27. A patient on amiodarone is prescribed a quinolone. Which cardiac concern does the chapter attach to the quinolone itself, and which to this combination?
  • ✓The drug prolongs the QT interval; the combination with a class Ia or class III antidysrhythmic makes dangerous dysrhythmias more likely
  • BThe drug causes dysrhythmias; the combination prolongs the QT interval
  • CThe drug causes bradycardia; the combination causes tachycardia
  • DThe drug has no cardiac effect; only the combination is a concern
Rationale

The chapter names prolongation of the QT interval on the electrocardiogram as the quinolone cardiac effect, and states that dangerous cardiac dysrhythmias are more likely to occur when quinolones are taken by patients also receiving class Ia and class III antidysrhythmic drugs such as disopyramide and amiodarone, so such combinations are best avoided.

Watch for

(B) is the direct swap and the one review question 1 is built around, offering bradycardia, dysrhythmias, tachycardia, and prolonged QT interval side by side. The effect of the drug is the electrocardiogram finding; the dysrhythmia is what the interaction produces.

quinolone safety warnings and interactionsabsorption interactions and teaching
28. A patient taking ciprofloxacin also takes calcium carbonate antacids and an iron supplement. What spacing does the chapter direct?
  • ✓Take the interacting products at least 1 hour before or after the quinolone
  • BTake the interacting products 2 hours before or 3 hours after the quinolone
  • CTake everything together to improve tolerance
  • DDiscontinue the antacid and iron for the full course of therapy
Rationale

The chapter states that antacids, calcium, magnesium, iron, zinc preparations, and sucralfate cause a reduction in the oral absorption of the quinolone, and that patients need to take the interacting drugs at least 1 hour before or after taking quinolones. Dairy products and enteral tube feedings reduce absorption in the same way and are separated the same way.

Watch for

(B) is the tetracycline interval from Chapter 38 applied to the wrong class. Both classes are blocked by antacids, calcium, magnesium, and iron, and both are fixed by spacing, which makes borrowing the wrong interval the most economical distractor available. Quinolone is 1 hour either side; tetracycline is 2 before and 3 after.

31. A prescriber orders clindamycin for a patient with a history of ulcerative colitis. Why does the nurse question the order?
  • ✓Ulcerative colitis is a listed contraindication, and clindamycin is the antibiotic most associated with pseudomembranous colitis
  • BClindamycin has no activity against the anaerobes that colonize an inflamed bowel
  • CUlcerative colitis increases clindamycin absorption to toxic levels
  • DClindamycin is contraindicated only in patients older than 65 years
Rationale

Clindamycin is contraindicated in known hypersensitivity, in those with ulcerative colitis or enteritis, and in infants younger than 1 month of age. Its most common adverse effects are gastrointestinal, including pseudomembranous colitis, which the chapter says is often associated with antibiotic therapy, especially clindamycin therapy.

(B) states the opposite of the drug's main strength, which is that it is active against several anaerobic organisms and is most often used for this purpose. A learner who knows the bowel is the problem may reach for the wrong reason and land on a false pharmacologic claim.

32. A patient is starting linezolid. Which concurrent therapies does the chapter name as interacting with it? Select all that apply.
Select all that apply · scored all or nothing
  • ✓A selective serotonin reuptake inhibitor antidepressant
  • ✓A vasopressor such as dopamine
  • CAn oral anticoagulant
  • DThyroid replacement hormone
  • EA beta blocker
Rationale
