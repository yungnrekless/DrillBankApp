nausea pathway and antiemetic terminologychemoreceptor trigger zone versus vomiting center
1. A nursing student asks how a chemotherapy drug circulating in the bloodstream ends up producing vomiting. Which sequence should the nurse describe?
  • ✓The chemoreceptor trigger zone detects the drug and sends neurotransmitter signals to the vomiting center
  • BThe vomiting center detects the drug and sends neurotransmitter signals to the chemoreceptor trigger zone
  • CThe drug acts directly on the vomiting center, with no involvement of the chemoreceptor trigger zone
  • DThe drug acts on the vestibular apparatus, which then signals the chemoreceptor trigger zone
Rationale

The chapter states that neurotransmitter signals are sent to the vomiting center from the chemoreceptor trigger zone. The CTZ is the area of the brain involved in the sensation of nausea and the action of vomiting, and Fig. 52.1 shows the labyrinth, GI tract, and cerebral cortex feeding the CTZ, with the CTZ feeding the vomiting center in the medulla.

Watch for

(B) reverses the direction of the signal, and it is the reflex pick because the key terms box defines both structures with the same opening words, "the area of the brain that is involved in." The signal runs CTZ to VC, never the reverse.

2. A patient asks which part of the brain actually sets off the retching and vomiting once the signal has arrived. Which structure should the nurse name?
  • AThe chemoreceptor trigger zone, because it senses emetogenic substances in the blood
  • ✓The vomiting center in the medulla, because it initiates the physiologic events that lead to nausea and vomiting
  • CThe vestibular nuclei, because they receive input from the inner ear
  • DThe reticular formation, because it is where the tetrahydrocannabinoids act
Rationale

The vomiting center is defined as the area of the brain involved in stimulating the physiologic events that lead to nausea and vomiting, and the body text says it is responsible for initiating those events. Fig. 52.1 places it in the medulla.

Watch for

(A) is the mirror error of the previous item. The CTZ senses and signals; the VC initiates. Because both key-term definitions begin identically, the discriminator has to come from the verbs.

nausea pathway and antiemetic terminologynausea versus emesis
3. A postoperative patient reports feeling queasy and says the urge to vomit keeps coming and going, but nothing has come up. How should the nurse document this?
  • AEmesis, because the urge to vomit is itself the defining feature of emesis
  • ✓Nausea, because nausea is the sensation that often leads to the urge to vomit
  • CNeither term applies until gastric contents are actually expelled
  • DEither term is correct, because the chapter uses nausea and emesis interchangeably
Rationale

Nausea is the sensation often leading to the urge to vomit, described elsewhere in the chapter as an unpleasant feeling that often precedes vomiting. Emesis is the forcible emptying or expulsion of gastric and occasionally intestinal contents through the mouth, which has not occurred here.

Watch for

(A) is the reflex pick for students who treat the two words as interchangeable. Nausea is the feeling; emesis is the event, and only emesis requires forcible expulsion. An antiemetic is defined against both, as a drug given to relieve nausea and vomiting, not vomiting alone.

Table 52.1 lists six neurotransmitters in the vomiting pathway, but only five map to a blocking class in Tables 52.2 and 52.3: acetylcholine to the anticholinergics, histamine to the antihistamines, dopamine to the antidopaminergics and the prokinetics, serotonin to the serotonin blockers, and substance P or neurokinin 1 to the neurokinin antagonists.

5. A patient taking scopolamine for motion sickness and a patient taking meclizine for motion sickness both report dry mouth and blurred vision. How should the nurse explain the difference in mechanism?
  • ✓Scopolamine blocks acetylcholine receptors directly, while meclizine blocks histamine 1 receptors and thereby prevents acetylcholine from binding
  • BScopolamine blocks histamine 1 receptors, while meclizine blocks acetylcholine receptors directly
  • CScopolamine acts at the chemoreceptor trigger zone, while meclizine acts at the vestibular nuclei
  • DOnly scopolamine has anticholinergic activity; meclizine's effects have nothing to do with acetylcholine
Rationale

Table 52.3 says anticholinergics block ACh receptors in the vestibular nuclei and reticular formation, while antihistamines block histamine 1 receptors, thereby preventing ACh from binding to receptors in the vestibular nuclei. The chapter adds that antihistamines also have potent anticholinergic activity, which is why the dry mouth and blurred vision set appears under both classes in Table 52.4.

Watch for

(D) is the reflex pick because the class name antihistamine says nothing about acetylcholine. Table 52.1 gives acetylcholine and histamine 1 the identical site entry and the shared adverse effect profile cannot discriminate these two classes, so only the receptor actually bound can.

6. A patient is started on dimenhydrinate and the nurse anticipates dry mouth and urinary retention. Which explanation is correct?

  • ✓Dimenhydrinate blocks histamine 1 receptors and also has potent anticholinergic activity

  • DDimenhydrinate blocks serotonin receptors in the GI tract

Dimenhydrinate is one of the antihistamine antiemetics along with meclizine and diphenhydramine. (A) is the mirror error of the previous item, handing the anticholinergic's direct receptor blockade to the antihistamine. Same destination, different door: anticholinergics block acetylcholine directly, antihistamines get there through histamine 1.

7. A patient says a friend told her that "a histamine blocker" will prevent her motion sickness, and she already has a histamine 2 receptor blocker at home for gastric acid control. What should the nurse explain?
  • AThe histamine 2 blocker will work, because all histamine blockers act on the vestibular pathways
  • ✓Only histamine 1 blockers are antiemetics; histamine 2 blockers act at the parietal cell for gastric acid control and are a different drug group
  • CThe histamine 2 blocker will work, but only if it is taken 1 hour before travel
  • DNeither histamine 1 nor histamine 2 blockers are used for motion sickness
Rationale

The chapter warns directly that the antihistamine antiemetics are not to be confused with histamine 2 receptor blockers, used for gastric acid control, which are covered in Chapter 50. Histamine 1 blockers act on the vestibular and labyrinthine pathways of the inner ear; histamine 2 blockers control gastric acid.

Watch for

(A) is the reflex pick because both drug groups are called histamine blockers in ordinary speech. The subscript is the entire distinction, and this is a confusion the chapter flags itself rather than one a test writer invented.

antiemetic classes and receptor targetsantidopaminergics versus prokinetics
8. A patient with delayed gastric emptying reports nausea after every meal. Which antiemetic class is aimed at that problem?
  • AAntidopaminergics, because they block dopamine in the chemoreceptor trigger zone
  • ✓Prokinetics, because along with blocking dopamine in the chemoreceptor trigger zone their primary action is to stimulate peristalsis and empty stomach contents into the duodenum
  • CSerotonin blockers, because they act at the GI tract, chemoreceptor trigger zone, and vomiting center
  • DAnticholinergics, because they reduce gastrointestinal secretions
Rationale

Both antidopaminergics and prokinetics block dopamine in the chemoreceptor trigger zone, and the key points say so outright. What separates the prokinetics is that their primary action is to stimulate peristalsis in the GI tract, promoting emptying of stomach contents into the duodenum. Table 52.2 gives prokinetics the indications delayed gastric emptying, gastroesophageal reflux, and nausea and vomiting.

Watch for

(A) is the reflex pick precisely because it is true as far as it goes; both classes do block dopamine at the CTZ, so a partially correct mechanism feels like the answer. The prokinetic row of Table 52.3 is the only line in the chapter where a class stimulates a receptor rather than blocking one, and motility is the discriminator.

9. A hospitalized patient has intractable hiccups and also needs an injectable antiemetic. Which class and drug fit both needs?
  • AProkinetics, such as metoclopramide, because they empty the stomach
  • ✓Antidopaminergics, such as prochlorperazine, because the class also treats psychotic disorders and intractable hiccups
  • CSerotonin blockers, such as ondansetron, because the class has very few adverse effects
  • DNeurokinin antagonists, such as aprepitant, because the class covers acute and delayed vomiting
Rationale

Table 52.2 gives the antidopaminergics the indications psychotic disorders including mania, schizophrenia, and anxiety, intractable hiccups, and nausea and vomiting. Prochlorperazine, especially in the injectable form, is used frequently in the hospital setting.

(A) is the mirror error of the previous item. Since both classes block dopamine in the chemoreceptor trigger zone, the stem's cue decides: gastric emptying, reflux, or motility points to the prokinetic, while psychosis, hiccups, or an injectable hospital antiemetic points to the antidopaminergic.

antiemetic drug selection by indicationmotion sickness versus vertigo
12. A patient is leaving on a 5-day cruise and wants something for motion sickness that will not have to be taken over and over. Which drug fits?
  • Ameclizine, taken 1 hour before departure and repeated daily during travel
  • ✓scopolamine transdermal patch, applied behind one ear every 3 days
  • Cprochlorperazine by injection each morning
  • Dondansetron 30 minutes before each meal
Rationale

Scopolamine is the primary anticholinergic drug used as an antiemetic, and its transdermal patch is applied to a hairless area behind one ear every 3 days, releasing a total of 1 mg over 72 hours. That 72-hour duration is what makes it the travel option.

Watch for

(A) is a genuine motion-sickness option and not a throwaway, which is what makes it pull: meclizine is dosed 25 to 50 mg 1 hour before travel and repeated daily during travel. The cue in the stem is the patch and the multi-day duration, and that points to scopolamine.

15. A patient is admitted for a first cycle of highly emetogenic chemotherapy and has orders for both ondansetron and dronabinol. Which should the nurse expect to give?
  • Adronabinol, because it also stimulates appetite in patients with cancer
  • ✓ondansetron, because dronabinol is generally used as a second-line drug after treatment with other antiemetics has failed
  • Cdronabinol, because tetrahydrocannabinoids act on the cortex where anticipatory nausea begins
  • DNeither, until the patient actually reports nausea
Rationale

Dronabinol is generally used as a second-line drug after treatment with other antiemetics has failed, and the chapter's case study models exactly that sequence, adding dronabinol only after 2 weeks of failed ondansetron. (D) fails the chapter's core principle, since antiemetics for chemotherapy are prophylactic and given 30 to 60 minutes before the drug rather than held for symptoms. antiemetic route safety and administrationpromethazine and hydroxyzine route rules
23. A nurse is preparing to give intravenous promethazine to a patient with a peripheral line. Which action is correct?
  • AGive it undiluted as a rapid intravenous push through the port closest to the patient's vein
  • ✓Dilute it in at least 10 mL of fluid and give it in a running intravenous line at the port farthest from the patient's vein, using a large-bore vein
  • CGive it subcutaneously if intravenous access cannot be obtained
  • DGive it into a hand or wrist vein so that any reaction is easy to see
Rationale

Promethazine is the chapter's only high-alert drug. The intravenous route is not the preferred route but is commonly used, and when it is used the drug is best diluted in at least 10 mL of fluid, the more dilute the better, and given in a running intravenous line at the port farthest from the patient's vein or through a large-bore vein, not a hand or wrist vein. Therapy must be discontinued immediately if burning or pain occurs.

Watch for

(D) names the exact vein the chapter forbids, and it pulls because a small visible vein feels easier to monitor. (C) is forbidden outright: promethazine is not to be given subcutaneously. If promethazine is inadvertently given intraarterially instead of intravenously, severe tissue damage often requiring amputation can occur.

27. A patient is starting aprepitant with a chemotherapy regimen. Which of the patient's current drugs or drug groups does this chapter identify as interacting with aprepitant? Select all that apply.

  • Adigoxin
  • ✓warfarin

  • Dnonsteroidal antiinflammatory drugs

Neurokinin antagonists may induce the metabolism of warfarin, so the INR must be checked before each cycle, and they may reduce the effectiveness of oral contraceptives. antiemetic interactions and contraindicationsaprepitant as the interaction-heavy drug
28. A prescriber asks whether aprepitant can simply replace ondansetron for a patient on highly emetogenic chemotherapy. What should the nurse know about aprepitant?
  • AIt blocks the same serotonin receptors as ondansetron, so it is a direct substitute
  • ✓In contrast to other antiemetics it has little affinity for serotonin and dopamine receptors, and it is used in conjunction with serotonin blockers and glucocorticoids to augment them
  • CIt blocks dopamine in the chemoreceptor trigger zone, so it also treats intractable hiccups
  • DIt has no significant drug interactions, so it is the safer of the two drugs
Rationale

The aprepitant profile states that in contrast to other antiemetics this drug has little affinity for serotonin and dopamine receptors; it inhibits the substance P and neurokinin receptors in the brain stem. Neurokinin blockers are used in conjunction with serotonin blockers and glucocorticoids, and their use augments those drugs, because combining categories blocks more than one pathway.

Watch for

(D) is exactly backwards, and it is the reflex pick because the two drugs are given together and their profiles blur. Aprepitant is the most interaction-heavy drug in the chapter; the serotonin blockers are the class with no significant drug interactions. Aprepitant adds a pathway rather than replacing one.

antiemetic interactions and contraindicationsthe classes that stay clean, and one that loses effect
29. A patient taking ondansetron is worried about interactions because he takes several other medications. What does the chapter say about this class?
  • ASerotonin blockers are major inhibitors of cytochrome P-450 and interact with many drugs
  • ✓Serotonin blockers have no significant known drug interactions, and the tetrahydrocannabinoids are the only other class the chapter describes the same way
  • CSerotonin blockers may induce the metabolism of warfarin and reduce the effectiveness of oral contraceptives
  • DSerotonin blockers must not be combined with any other antiemetic class
Rationale

The chapter states that serotonin blockers and tetrahydrocannabinoids have no significant drug interactions, and the ondansetron profile repeats it: very specific actions, very few adverse effects, and no significant drug interactions known to occur. Ondansetron's only listed contraindication is known drug allergy.

30. A postoperative patient receiving opioids has metoclopramide ordered for delayed gastric emptying, and the nurse notes that an anticholinergic is also on the medication list. What effect should the nurse anticipate?
  • AAdditive central nervous system depression
  • ✓The motility effects of metoclopramide will be blocked, decreasing its action
  • CAn increased risk of extrapyramidal reactions
  • DProlongation of the QT interval
Rationale

The chapter states that anticholinergics and analgesics can block the motility effects of metoclopramide, and that its action is decreased when it is taken with anticholinergics or opiates. This is the only interaction in the chapter that reduces an antiemetic's effect rather than compounding a toxicity, and it is clinically likely, because opioid patients are exactly the ones receiving antiemetics.

Watch for

(A) substitutes the far more common interaction type, and it pulls because metoclopramide with alcohol genuinely does produce additive central nervous system depression. (C) names a real metoclopramide danger with the wrong partner: it is the phenothiazines, prochlorperazine and promethazine, that would lead to exacerbation of extrapyramidal reactions.
