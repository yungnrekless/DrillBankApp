branches of pharmacologykinetics versus dynamics
1. A drug monograph states that only 35% of an oral dose reaches the systemic circulation, because the liver inactivates the rest before it gets there. Which branch of pharmacology does that statement belong to?
  • APharmacodynamics
  • BPharmaceutics
  • ✓Pharmacokinetics
  • DPharmacotherapeutics
Rationale

Pharmacokinetics is the study of what happens to a drug from the time it is put into the body until the parent drug and all metabolites have left it — absorption into, distribution and metabolism within, and excretion from the body. How much drug survives hepatic metabolism is a kinetic fact.

Watch for

(A) is the reflex pick because the two words differ by four letters. Pharmacokinetics is what the body does to the drug; pharmacodynamics is what the drug does to the body.

branches of pharmacologykinetics versus dynamics
2. The same monograph states that the drug slows heart rate by binding beta-1 receptors and reducing the rate at which cardiac cells contract. Which branch of pharmacology does that statement belong to?
  • APharmacokinetics
  • ✓Pharmacodynamics
  • CPharmaceutics
  • DPharmacognosy
Rationale

Pharmacodynamics is the study of the biochemical and physiologic interactions of drugs at their sites of activity; it examines the effect of the drug on the body. Receptor binding at the site of action is the definition case.

Watch for

(A) is the mirror error of the previous item. Nothing here describes the drug's journey through the body — it describes what the drug does once it has arrived.

branches of pharmacologypharmaceutics as its own phase
3. A pharmacist explains that micronized glyburide reaches its maximum concentration peak faster than nonmicronized glyburide, because the smaller particle size dissolves more quickly. Which science does this observation belong to?
  • ✓Pharmaceutics
  • BPharmacokinetics
  • CPharmacotherapeutics
  • DPharmacoeconomics
Rationale

Pharmaceutics is the study of how various dosage forms influence the way in which the drug affects the body, and dosage form determines the rate at which dissolution occurs. Micronized versus nonmicronized glyburide is the chapter's own example.

Watch for

(B) sounds defensible because dissolution feeds absorption, but Fig. 2.2 orders the three phases pharmaceutical, then pharmacokinetic, then pharmacodynamic. Anything decided by the dosage form before the body gets involved is pharmaceutic.

drug names and approvalchemical, generic, and trade names
4. A discharge list reads "ibuprofen (Motrin) 400 mg," and the package insert gives the same drug as 2-p-isobutylphenyl propionic acid. Which of these names is the registered trademark?
  • Aibuprofen, because it is the name used in most official drug compendiums
  • ✓Motrin, because it is the commercial name given to the product by its manufacturer
  • C2-p-isobutylphenyl propionic acid, because it uniquely identifies the molecule
  • DBoth ibuprofen and Motrin, because both are proprietary
Rationale

The trade or proprietary name is the drug's registered trademark, indicating that its commercial use is restricted to the owner of the patent. Trade names are created with marketability in mind, which is why they are shorter and easier to pronounce than generic names.

Watch for

(A) is chosen by students who assume the name printed first is the official, protected one. The generic name is the official compendium name and is explicitly not protected by trademark.

drug names and approvalchemical, generic, and trade names
5. The same patient asks who decides what a drug's generic name will be. Which response is accurate?
  • AThe manufacturer chooses it, with marketability in mind
  • ✓The United States Adopted Names Council assigns it, and it is not protected by trademark
  • CThe FDA assigns it at approval and protects it for the life of the patent
  • DIt is derived from the drug's chemical composition and molecular structure
Rationale

The generic or nonproprietary name is given to a drug by the United States Adopted Names Council. It is much shorter and simpler than the chemical name and is not protected by trademark.

Watch for

(A) is the mirror error of the previous item — marketability drives the trade name, not the generic one. (D) describes the chemical name, which is the one that states composition and molecular structure.

drug names and approvalbioequivalence versus therapeutic equivalence
6. A formulary committee designates one drug in a class as the preferred agent and substitutes it for the others, even though the drugs do not contain the same active ingredient, on the ground that all have been proven to produce the same therapeutic effect. Which term names this practice?
  • ABioequivalence
  • ✓Therapeutic equivalence
  • CBiosimilarity
  • DOff-label substitution
Rationale

Therapeutic equivalence is the practice of choosing one drug in a class of several as the preferred agent even though the drugs do not have the same active ingredients; the drugs must have been proven to have the same therapeutic effect before one can be substituted for another.

Watch for

(A) is the reflex answer because the two terms differ by one word. Bioequivalence requires the same active ingredient at the same concentration with the same bioavailability. The phrase "do not contain the same active ingredient" is what rules it out here.

drug names and approvalbioequivalence versus therapeutic equivalence
7. A pharmacist substitutes a generic tablet for the brand-name product, noting that the two contain the same active ingredient at the same concentration and have the same bioavailability. Which term names this relationship?
  • ATherapeutic equivalence
  • ✓Bioequivalence
  • CPrototypical substitution
  • DOff-label substitution
Rationale

If two drug products have the same bioavailability and the same concentration of active ingredient, they are said to be bioequivalent — the chapter's own example is a brand-name drug and the same generic drug.

Watch for

(A) is the mirror error. Therapeutic equivalence is the looser, formulary-level standard that spans drugs with different active ingredients; bioequivalence is the strict same-ingredient, same-bioavailability match.

drug names and approvalpatents and off-label use
8. A patient asks why a newly approved drug costs so much more than an older one in the same class. Which statements about drug patents are accurate? Select all that apply.
Select all that apply · scored all or nothing
  • ✓The patent life of a newly discovered drug molecule is normally 17 years
  • ✓The research process for new drug development normally requires about 10 of those years
  • ✓The manufacturer generally has the remaining 7 years for sales profits
  • ✓After the patent expires, other manufacturers may make generics with the same active ingredient and the price usually decreases substantially
  • EThe 17 years begins at FDA approval, giving the manufacturer 17 years of marketing exclusivity
Rationale

Patent life is normally 17 years, development consumes about 10, roughly 7 remain for sales profits, and generic competition after expiration usually drops the price substantially.

(E) confuses patent life with the post-approval remainder, and it is the version that sounds most generous to the manufacturer. The clock runs on the molecule from discovery, which is exactly why only about 7 of the 17 years earn anything.

drug names and approvalpatents and off-label use
9. A prescriber orders a drug for an indication that does not appear in the manufacturer's labeling. Which statement is accurate?
  • AThis is illegal, and the nurse should refuse to administer the drug
  • ✓This is off-label prescribing; only the manufacturer's written labeling is restricted to FDA-approved indications
  • CThis is off-label prescribing, which the FDA prohibits once a drug has an approved indication
  • DThis means the drug has not completed FDA approval for any indication
Rationale

The FDA permits only FDA-approved indications to be described in the manufacturer's written information, or labeling. Prescribers may at times choose to use drugs for non-FDA-approved indications, which is known as off-label prescribing, and previously off-label indications often become approved over time.

Watch for

(A) reads the labeling restriction as a restriction on practice. What is regulated is what the manufacturer may claim in print, not what the prescriber may judge appropriate.

dosage forms and dissolutionoral absorption ranking
10. Four oral formulations of the same drug are stocked. Which will be absorbed most rapidly?
  • AEnteric-coated tablet
  • ✓Oral soluble wafer
  • CSuspension
  • DCapsule
Rationale

Table 2.1 ranks oral disintegration forms, buccal tablets, and oral soluble wafers as the fastest-absorbed oral preparations, ahead of liquids and elixirs, suspensions, powders, capsules, tablets, coated tablets, and enteric-coated tablets.

Watch for

(C) is the strong second choice, because liquids are already dissolved and genuinely do beat every solid form. The wafer beats the liquid because nothing has to dissolve at all.

dosage forms and dissolutionoral absorption ranking
11. Of those same four formulations, which will be absorbed most slowly?
  • ✓Enteric-coated tablet
  • BOral soluble wafer
  • CSuspension
  • DCapsule
Rationale

An enteric coating prevents the tablet from being broken down in the acidic pH of the stomach, so the drug is not absorbed until it reaches the higher, more alkaline pH of the intestines. That property produces slower dissolution and therefore slower absorption.

Watch for

(D) is the reflex pick for anyone ranking by how solid the form looks. A plain capsule sits in the middle of Table 2.1; the enteric-coated tablet is last because the delay is engineered, not incidental.

dosage forms and dissolutioncrushing, chewing, and extended release
12. A patient who has trouble swallowing is prescribed a tablet whose name ends in XL. What is the nurse's best action?
  • ACrush the tablet and mix it into applesauce
  • BInstruct the patient to chew the tablet thoroughly before swallowing
  • ✓Consult the pharmacist about an alternative dosage form
  • DDissolve the tablet in 3 ounces of orange juice
Rationale

SR, SA, CR, XL, and XT all mark extended-release formulations, which must not be crushed, because crushing could cause accelerated release of drug from the dosage form and possible toxicity. The chapter directs consultation with a pharmacist or other suitable source whenever a question exists.

Watch for

(A) is the reflex answer because the chapter does endorse applesauce and pudding — but as a vehicle for capsule contents and granules that are sprinkled, not as permission to crush an extended-release tablet. The soft food is the delivery method, not the authorization.

dosage forms and dissolutioncrushing, chewing, and extended release
13. A patient asks why an enteric-coated tablet cannot be chewed. Which explanation is correct?
  • AChewing releases the whole dose at once, which can cause toxicity
  • ✓Chewing destroys a coating that protects the stomach lining from the drug and protects the drug from stomach acid
  • CChewing makes the drug dissolve too slowly to be absorbed at all
  • DChewing converts the drug into an inactive metabolite before absorption
Rationale

The chapter names both purposes of an enteric coat in one sentence — protecting the stomach lining from the local effects of the drug, and protecting the drug from being prematurely disrupted by stomach acid. A stem may want either half.

Watch for

(A) is the extended-release rationale attached to the wrong dosage form. One prohibited action, two different harms: crushing an extended-release form dumps the dose and risks toxicity, while destroying an enteric coat removes acid protection.

absorption and bioavailabilityfirst-pass versus non-first-pass routes
14. A patient with acute chest pain receives sublingual nitroglycerin rather than an oral tablet. Which statement best explains the choice?
  • ASublingual tissue absorbs slowly, which prolongs the drug's duration of action
  • ✓The area under the tongue is highly vascular, so absorption is rapid, and the drug bypasses the liver while remaining systemically bioavailable
  • CSublingual administration is a topical route and therefore produces only local effects
  • DSublingual drugs undergo a first-pass effect that converts them to their active form
Rationale

Sublingual drugs are absorbed into the highly vascularized tissue under the tongue, absorbed rapidly because the area has a large blood supply, and bypass the liver while still being systemically bioavailable.

Watch for

(D) turns the first-pass effect into an activation step. The first pass inactivates, which is why it reduces bioavailability to less than 100%. Metabolism that activates a drug is the separate case of a prodrug.

absorption and bioavailabilityfirst-pass versus non-first-pass routes
15. Which routes deliver drug to the systemic circulation without a first pass through the liver? Select all that apply.
Select all that apply · scored all or nothing
  • AOral
  • ✓Sublingual
  • ✓Intravenous
  • ✓Transdermal
  • ✓Intramuscular
Rationale

Box 2.2 lists hepatic arterial, oral, portal venous, and rectal as first-pass routes. Sublingual, buccal, intravenous, intramuscular, subcutaneous, transdermal, inhaled, intranasal, intraarterial, intraocular, intravaginal, and aural are all non-first-pass.

Watch for

(A) is the single first-pass route in this list, and it is the one most often let through, because oral feels like the default rather than a special case. Anything draining into the portal circulation is first-passed.

absorption and bioavailabilityfirst-pass versus non-first-pass routes
16. A patient receives an acetaminophen suppository. Which statement about the rectal route is accurate?
  • ARectal administration is topical and therefore completely avoids the first-pass effect
  • ✓Rectal administration leads to both first-pass and non-first-pass effects, because some drug reaches the portal vein while some is absorbed into perirectal tissue
  • CRectal administration is handled identically to oral administration
  • DRectal administration produces 100% bioavailability
Rationale

All topical routes avoid first-pass effects with the exception of rectal administration. Because the rectum is part of the GI tract, some drug is absorbed into capillaries feeding the portal vein, while some is absorbed locally into perirectal tissues. Box 2.2 footnotes rectal on both lists.

Watch for

(A) states the general topical rule and stops one clause early. The chapter names the exception in the same sentence as the rule, and rectal is the one route that appears on both sides of Box 2.2.

absorption and bioavailabilityroute choice and conditions that alter absorption
17. A trauma patient is in shock with decreased cardiac output and urine output under 30 mL/hr, and needs a drug to take effect immediately. Which route should the nurse anticipate?
  • AIntramuscular
  • BSubcutaneous
  • ✓Intravenous
  • DOral
Rationale

The parenteral route is the fastest route by which a drug can be absorbed, and drugs injected intravenously are immediately placed into solution in the bloodstream, so 100% absorption is assumed to occur immediately. Intramuscular and subcutaneous doses are absorbed over a period of several hours.

Watch for

(A) is the common miss, because intramuscular is also parenteral and feels fast. Intramuscular and subcutaneous absorption both depend on blood flow to the tissue, and perfusion is precisely what has failed in this patient — decreased blood flow decreases absorption.

absorption and bioavailabilityroute choice and conditions that alter absorption
18. A nurse reviews the timing of a patient's medications with respect to food. Which statements are accurate? Select all that apply.
Select all that apply · scored all or nothing
  • ✓Alendronate is taken on an empty stomach with at least 6 ounces of water
  • ✓Taking an enteric-coated medication with a large amount of food may cause it to be dissolved by acidic stomach contents
  • ✓The presence of food may enhance the absorption of some fat-soluble drugs
  • ✓Macrolides and oral opioids are often taken with food to minimize gastrointestinal irritation, even though they are specified for an empty stomach
  • EAnticholinergic drugs speed gastrointestinal transit time and increase the absorption of acid-susceptible drugs
Rationale

Box 2.1 places alendronate and risedronate on an empty stomach with at least 6 ounces of water, and carbamazepine, iron, hydralazine, lithium, propranolol, spironolactone, NSAIDs, and theophylline with food. Macrolides and oral opioids are its third category — specified for an empty stomach but often given with food for tolerability.

(E) reverses the anticholinergic effect in both halves of the sentence. Anticholinergic drugs slow GI transit time, which may reduce the amount absorbed for acid-susceptible drugs.

absorption and bioavailabilityroute choice and conditions that alter absorption
19. A patient has had a large portion of the small intestine resected and now has short bowel syndrome. Which effect on oral drug therapy should the nurse anticipate?
  • AIncreased absorption, because transit time is shortened
  • ✓Decreased absorption, because the surface available for absorption is reduced
  • CNo change, because absorption occurs primarily in the stomach
  • DIncreased first-pass effect, because the liver receives more drug
Rationale

The chapter lists surgical alteration of GI anatomy among the factors that alter absorption, naming short bowel syndrome from small-intestine resection and bariatric surgery, where stomach contents are delivered to the intestines more rapidly than usual in what is called gastric dumping.

Watch for

(C) mislocates absorption. Orally administered drugs are absorbed through the mucosa of the stomach and the small or large intestine, and the small intestine is the highly vascularized surface that resection removes.

distribution and protein bindingfree versus bound drug
20. A patient with extensive burns and a low serum albumin is receiving a highly protein-bound drug at a standard dose and develops signs of toxicity. Which explanation is correct?
  • ✓Low albumin leaves a larger fraction of free, pharmacologically active drug
  • BLow albumin leaves more drug bound to protein and therefore more active
  • CLow albumin slows hepatic metabolism of the drug
  • DLow albumin increases the drug's distribution into adipose tissue
Rationale

Only drug molecules not bound to plasma proteins can freely distribute to extravascular tissue and reach the site of action, and the unbound portion is the pharmacologically active one. Conditions causing low albumin, such as extensive burns and malnourished states, result in a larger fraction of free drug and raise the risk for toxicity.

Watch for

(B) inverts the rule, and it is the intuitive inversion, because "bound to a carrier" sounds like "being delivered." Bound drug is stuck in the bloodstream in a complex too large to cross the capillary wall, and it is doing nothing.

distribution and protein bindingfree versus bound drug
21. A patient stabilized on warfarin, which is highly protein bound, is started on amiodarone, which is also highly protein bound. Which effect should the nurse anticipate, and in which pharmacokinetic phase does it occur?
  • ADecreased effect of both drugs, in the absorption phase
  • ✓Higher levels of free warfarin and amiodarone with increased actions of both, in the distribution phase
  • CDecreased metabolism of warfarin with toxic accumulation, in the metabolism phase
  • DProlonged plasma levels of both drugs, in the excretion phase
Rationale

Table 2.10 gives warfarin with amiodarone as its distribution-phase example: both drugs compete for protein-binding sites, producing higher levels of free, unbound drug and increasing the actions of both, which is harmful.

Watch for

(C) names a plausible outcome with the wrong mechanism, and it is the reflex answer because most interactions in this chapter turn out to be enzyme interactions. Competition for albumin is displacement, not enzyme competition, and the phase label is what this item is testing.

distribution and protein bindingvolume of distribution
22. A drug is described as highly water-soluble, or hydrophilic. What should the nurse expect?
  • AA large volume of distribution and low blood concentrations
  • ✓A small volume of distribution and high blood concentrations
  • CA large volume of distribution and high blood concentrations
  • DExtensive distribution into adipose tissue
Rationale

Highly water-soluble drugs have a smaller volume of distribution and high blood concentrations. Volume of distribution is a theoretical volume describing the compartments — blood, total body water, body fat, other tissues and organs — into which a drug may distribute.

Watch for

(A) is the lipophilic pattern, and it is the more commonly chosen inversion, because "distributes widely" sounds like it should mean higher levels. Water-soluble drugs stay in the water compartment, which is small, so the concentration there is high.

distribution and protein bindingvolume of distribution
23. A drug is described as highly fat-soluble, or lipophilic. What should the nurse expect?
  • AA small volume of distribution and high blood concentrations
  • ✓A large volume of distribution and low blood concentrations
  • CA small volume of distribution and low blood concentrations
  • DDistribution confined to the intravascular space
Rationale

Fat-soluble drugs have a larger volume of distribution and low blood concentrations, because they disperse out of the blood into fat and other tissues.

Watch for

(A) is the mirror error of the previous item. The relationship is inverse in both directions, and the practical danger is reading a low blood level on a lipophilic drug as "not enough drug on board."

distribution and protein bindingsites that are hard to reach
24. An antibiotic reaches therapeutic serum concentrations but fails to clear an infection in bone. Which explanation does the chapter give?
  • ABone is protected by a physiologic barrier similar to the blood-brain barrier
  • ✓Bone has a poor blood supply, which makes drug distribution to it difficult
  • CBone tissue metabolizes drugs faster than other tissues
  • DBone binds drug to albumin more tightly than plasma does
Rationale

The chapter names two kinds of hard-to-reach site and gives one example of each: sites with a poor blood supply, such as bone, and sites with physiologic barriers, such as the brain with its blood-brain barrier. Heart, liver, kidneys, and brain receive drug rapidly, while muscle, skin, and fat receive it more slowly.

Watch for

(A) transfers the brain's reason onto bone. There is no bone-blood barrier in this chapter; the obstacle is perfusion, which is a different problem with a different set of solutions.

metabolismenzyme inducers versus inhibitors
25. A patient stable on a maintenance drug is started on rifampin, and within two weeks the original drug is no longer controlling their symptoms. What has most likely happened?
  • ARifampin inhibited the cytochrome P-450 enzymes, so the drug accumulated
  • ✓Rifampin induced the cytochrome P-450 enzymes, speeding metabolism and lowering drug levels
  • CRifampin displaced the drug from albumin, increasing the free fraction
  • DRifampin blocked renal tubular secretion of the drug
Rationale

Rifampin and phenytoin are the chapter's named P-450 inducers, and barbiturates also increase metabolism. Enzyme inducers stimulate drug metabolism, often by stimulating the formation of new microsomal enzymes, which causes decreased pharmacologic effects.

Watch for

(A) is the reflex answer for anyone who hears "enzyme interaction" and reaches for accumulation. An inducer speeds metabolism, so there is less drug present and the failure is therapeutic, not toxic.

metabolismenzyme inducers versus inhibitors
26. A patient stable on a maintenance drug is started on ketoconazole, and within a week shows signs of toxicity from the original drug. What has most likely happened?
  • AKetoconazole induced the cytochrome P-450 enzymes, speeding metabolism
  • ✓Ketoconazole inhibited the cytochrome P-450 enzymes, so the drug accumulated and its effects were prolonged
  • CKetoconazole increased the drug's volume of distribution
  • DKetoconazole increased renal excretion of the drug
Rationale

Ketoconazole is the chapter's named P-450 inhibitor. Decreases in drug metabolism result in accumulation of the drug and prolongation of its effects, which can lead to drug toxicity.

Watch for

(A) is the mirror error of the previous item. The word "inhibitor" misleads because it sounds like it should reduce the drug's effect — it reduces enzyme activity, which raises drug levels.

28. A patient is described as a slow acetylator. What is the clinical consequence?
  • AMetabolism of certain drugs is increased, risking therapeutic failure
  • ✓Metabolism of certain drugs is reduced, so the drug accumulates and toxicity is more likely
  • CRed blood cells hemolyze when the patient is exposed to oxidizing drugs
  • DRenal excretion of the drug is accelerated

Table 2.6 lists slow acetylator status under genetic constitution with decreased metabolism, and fast acetylator status with increased metabolism. Reduced metabolism means the drug accumulates.

(C) is the other genetic host factor this course teaches, and it is the substitution students make under time pressure. G6PD deficiency makes red cells break down; slow acetylation makes drug pile up. They are not interchangeable.

metabolismprodrugs and active metabolites
29. A prodrug is ordered for a patient with severe hepatic impairment. Why is the nurse concerned?
  • AThe prodrug is already active and will accumulate to toxic levels
  • ✓The prodrug is inactive until biochemical reactions convert it to an active metabolite, and impaired metabolism may leave it ineffective
  • CThe prodrug will bypass the first-pass effect entirely
  • DThe prodrug will be excreted unchanged by the kidney before it can act
Rationale

A prodrug is an inactive drug dosage form that is converted to an active metabolite by various biochemical reactions once it is inside the body, and the liver is the organ most responsible for drug metabolism. Cefuroxime axetil is the course's worked example, hydrolyzed in the liver to its active cefuroxime form.

Watch for

(A) treats every metabolic problem as an accumulation problem, which is the correct instinct for most drugs and exactly backwards here. With a prodrug the failure runs the other way: no metabolism means no active drug.

excretion and half-lifehalf-life and steady state
30. A drug's peak level is 100 mg/L, and the level measured 8 hours later is 50 mg/L. Approximately how long after the peak will this drug be considered effectively removed from the body?
  • A16 hours
  • B24 hours
  • ✓40 hours
  • D80 hours
Rationale

A fall from 100 to 50 mg/L over 8 hours makes the half-life 8 hours. After about five half-lives most drugs are considered effectively removed, with approximately 97% eliminated — five times 8 hours is 40 hours.

Watch for

(B) is what you get by stopping at three half-lives, where Table 2.7 shows 88% removed. Eighty-eight percent feels like "most of it is gone," but the chapter's threshold is five half-lives and 97%.

excretion and half-lifehalf-life and steady state
31. A drug with a half-life of 24 hours is started at a fixed dose on a fixed schedule. When should the nurse expect steady-state blood levels?
  • AAfter the first dose, once absorption is complete
  • ✓After about four to five half-lives, so roughly 4 to 5 days
  • CAfter one half-life, so roughly 24 hours
  • DNever, because drugs with long half-lives do not reach steady state
Rationale

Steady state is the physiologic state in which the amount of drug removed via elimination equals the amount absorbed with each dose, and this plateau typically occurs after four to five half-lives. A drug with an extremely long half-life takes much longer to reach steady-state blood levels.

Watch for

(C) collapses the plateau into a single half-life. Both of this chapter's milestones are counted in half-lives — about five to clear a drug out, four to five to build up to steady state — and the shared unit is what makes them swappable.

excretion and half-lifepeak and trough
32. A patient's therapeutic drug monitoring returns a peak level above the reference range and a trough level below it. Which interpretation is correct?
  • ✓The high peak risks toxicity and the low trough risks a period below therapeutic levels
  • BThe high peak risks a period below therapeutic levels and the low trough risks toxicity
  • CBoth values indicate toxicity
  • DPeak and trough describe drug effects only, never drug concentrations
Rationale

If the peak blood level is too high, drug toxicity may occur, ranging from intensified drug effects such as excessive sedation to damage to vital organs. If the trough blood level is too low, the drug may not be at therapeutic levels to produce a response.

Watch for

(B) inverts the two ends of the same curve, which is the whole reason monitoring measures both. (D) is half true and therefore tempting: onset, peak, duration, and trough all describe drug effects, but peak and trough are also used for concentrations measured from blood samples.

excretion and half-liferenal handling and recirculation
33. A drug undergoes active tubular reabsorption in the nephron. What is the effect on that drug?
  • ✓It is taken back up into the systemic circulation rather than eliminated
  • BIt is moved from the surrounding vasculature into the nephron for elimination
  • CIt is passively filtered at the glomerulus
  • DIt is conjugated into a more water-soluble metabolite
Rationale

In active tubular reabsorption, substances present in the nephron are taken back up into the systemic circulation and transported away from the kidney, in what the chapter calls an attempt by the body to retain needed substances.

Watch for

(B) is the definition of active tubular secretion, and the two are swapped constantly because both are "active tubular" processes. Reabsorption returns drug to the body; secretion pushes more into the nephron. This is the transporter probenecid blocks, which is why it keeps penicillin in the blood.

excretion and half-liferenal handling and recirculation
34. A fat-soluble drug persists in a patient far longer than its elimination rate alone would predict. It is taken up by the liver, released into bile, reabsorbed from the intestine, returned to the liver, and secreted into bile again. Which process is this?
  • AFirst-pass effect
  • ✓Enterohepatic recirculation
  • CActive tubular reabsorption
  • DProtein binding
Rationale

Drugs eliminated by biliary excretion are taken up by the liver, released into the bile, and eliminated in the feces, but fat-soluble drugs in the bile may be reabsorbed into the bloodstream, returned to the liver, and again secreted into the bile. Enterohepatically recirculated drugs persist in the body for much longer periods.

Watch for

(A) is chosen because both processes involve the liver and the gut. The first-pass effect happens once, on the way in, and reduces bioavailability; recirculation happens repeatedly, on the way out, and prolongs the drug.

pharmacodynamicsagonists and antagonists
35. A patient receives naloxone after an opioid overdose. Naloxone binds the opioid receptor, produces no response, and prevents the opioid from binding. How is naloxone classified?
  • AAgonist
  • BPartial agonist
  • ✓Antagonist
  • DSubstrate
Rationale

Table 2.8 defines an antagonist as a drug that binds to the receptor with no response and prevents the binding of agonists. Antagonists are also called inhibitors.

Watch for

(B) is picked by students who reason that some effect must be occurring, since the patient visibly improves. A partial agonist binds and produces a diminished response; naloxone's benefit comes from producing no response at all while blocking the opioid.

pharmacodynamicsagonists and antagonists
36. A drug binds its receptor and produces the full expected cellular response. How is it classified?
  • AAntagonist
  • ✓Agonist
  • CCompetitive antagonist
  • DNoncompetitive antagonist
Rationale

An agonist binds to and stimulates the activity of one or more receptors in the body, and Table 2.8 states simply that the drug binds the receptor and there is a response.

Watch for

(A) is the inversion this chapter warns about most directly, because both agonists and antagonists bind. Fig. 2.7 makes the point visually: the antagonist occupies the site and produces a blocked response. The distinction is what happens after binding, not whether attachment occurs.

pharmacodynamicsagonists and antagonists
37. A patient who took a long-acting opioid is given naloxone, improves, then deteriorates again 45 minutes later. Which property of naloxone explains why repeat dosing is required?
  • AIt is a noncompetitive antagonist and permanently inactivates the receptor
  • ✓It is a competitive antagonist, so the remaining opioid can reoccupy the receptor as naloxone's level falls
  • CIt is a partial agonist whose diminished response wears off
  • DIt induces the P-450 enzymes that metabolize the opioid
Rationale

A competitive antagonist competes with the agonist for binding to the receptor, and if it binds there is no response. Because the competition is for the same site, the balance shifts back as the antagonist is cleared. Naloxone and naltrexone are competitive antagonists with strong affinity for the opioid receptors.

Watch for

(A) describes the noncompetitive pattern, in which the drug combines with a different part of the receptor and inactivates it, so more agonist does not help. If naloxone worked that way, redosing would be unnecessary — which is exactly the clinical error this distinction prevents.

pharmacodynamicsaffinity and mechanism of action
38. A drug is described as having very high affinity for its receptor. What does this tell the nurse?
  • AThe drug will produce a strong therapeutic response
  • ✓The drug attaches to and binds the receptor strongly; among agonists that predicts a greater response, but an antagonist with high affinity produces no response at all
  • CThe drug must be an agonist, since only agonists bind receptors
  • DThe drug has a high therapeutic index
Rationale

Affinity is the degree to which a drug attaches to and binds with a receptor, and the drug with the best fit and strongest affinity elicits the greatest response. Affinity describes the strength of attachment; whether a response follows is a separate question, which is why naloxone can be a strong-affinity antagonist.

Watch for

(A) conflates affinity with intrinsic activity, and the lock-and-key figure invites it by pairing "the better the fit" with "the better the response." Binding strongly and producing an effect are two different claims.

pharmacodynamicsaffinity and mechanism of action
39. An antibiotic kills bacteria by causing formation of a defective cell wall that ruptures the cell. Which mechanism of action is this?
  • AReceptor interaction
  • BEnzyme interaction
  • ✓Nonselective interaction with cellular processes
  • DCompetitive antagonism
Rationale

Drugs with nonspecific mechanisms of action do not interact with receptors or enzymes; their main targets are cell membranes and cellular processes such as metabolic activities. By incorporating into a normal metabolic process they cause a defect in the final product — an improperly formed cell wall that results in cell death through cell lysis, or the lack of a necessary energy substrate leading to cell starvation. Some cancer drugs and antibiotics work this way.

Watch for

(B) is the chapter's other non-receptor mechanism and the closer distractor: in a selective enzyme interaction the drug inhibits, more commonly, or enhances, less commonly, a specific enzyme. The clue is the target — a defective structural product rather than a catalyzed reaction.

types of drug therapymaintenance, supplemental, and supportive
40. A patient takes a beta blocker daily to keep blood pressure within a target range and prevent end-organ damage. Which type of therapy is this?
  • APalliative
  • ✓Maintenance
  • CSupportive
  • DSupplemental
Rationale

Maintenance therapy does not eradicate preexisting problems but prevents progression of a disease or condition, and it is used for chronic illnesses. Hypertension is the chapter's own example: maintenance therapy keeps blood pressure within given limits, which prevents certain end-organ damage.

Watch for

(D) is the closest miss, because a daily drug feels like something the body is being supplied with. Supplemental, or replacement, therapy supplies a substance the body cannot make or does not make in sufficient quantity — insulin, or iron in iron-deficiency anemia. A beta blocker replaces nothing.

types of drug therapymaintenance, supplemental, and supportive
41. A patient who is vomiting and has diarrhea receives intravenous fluids and electrolytes to prevent dehydration while the illness resolves. Which type of therapy is this?
  • ✓Supportive
  • BSupplemental
  • CMaintenance
  • DProphylactic
Rationale

Supportive therapy maintains the integrity of body functions while the patient is recovering from illness or trauma. The chapter's examples are fluids and electrolytes for a vomiting patient with diarrhea, and fluids, volume expanders, or blood products for a patient who has lost blood during surgery.

Watch for

(B) is the reflex answer because fluid is being supplied, and supplemental therapy also supplies something. Supplemental therapy replaces a substance the body cannot produce in adequate quantity as an ongoing deficit; supportive therapy holds body functions steady through a temporary insult.

42. A patient in the final stages of cancer receives high-dose opioid analgesics. The family asks whether starting these means treatment has been abandoned. Which response reflects the chapter?
  • APalliative therapy is begun only after all curative treatment has been stopped
  • ✓Palliative therapy focuses on relief from symptoms, pain, and stress, and can be provided along with curative treatment
  • CPalliative therapy is a form of maintenance therapy that prevents disease progression
  • DPalliative therapy is limited to the last 48 hours of life
Rationale

Palliative therapy makes the patient as comfortable as possible and focuses on providing relief from the symptoms, pain, and stress of a serious illness, with the goal of improving quality of life for both patient and family. It is typically used in the end stages of an illness when attempts at curative therapy have failed, but it can be provided along with curative treatment.

(A) is the widespread assumption the chapter explicitly contradicts in the same paragraph where it names the typical timing. "Typically used at the end stages" is not the same claim as "only after curative therapy stops."

  • AEmpiric
  • ✓Prophylactic
  • CSupportive
  • DMaintenance
Rationale

Prophylactic therapy is provided to prevent illness or another undesirable outcome during a planned event. (A) is the reflex answer, because both types start before any organism has been identified. The deciding fact is that this patient has no infection — prophylaxis prevents, while empiric therapy treats something already present.

44. A patient arrives with signs of pneumonia. Cultures are drawn, and an antibiotic active against the organism most commonly associated with that infection is started before results return. Which type of therapy is this?
  • AProphylactic
  • ✓Empiric
  • CPalliative
  • DSupplemental
Rationale

Empiric therapy is based on clinical probabilities and involves drug administration when a pathologic condition has a high likelihood of occurrence based on the patient's initial presenting symptoms — the chapter's example is an antibiotic active against the most commonly associated organism, started before culture and sensitivity reports are available.

(A) is the mirror error of the previous item, and the culture is what gives it away. You do not draw cultures on a patient who has no infection; the pending culture means illness is present and the choice is a probability judgment, not prevention.

therapeutic index, tolerance, and dependencetherapeutic index
45. A patient is started on warfarin, and the nurse notes that it has a low therapeutic index. What does this mean for the plan of care?
  • AThe drug is unusually safe and rarely associated with overdose
  • ✓The difference between a therapeutically active dose and a toxic dose is small, so closer monitoring is required
  • CThe drug requires a loading dose to reach steady state
  • DThe drug has low bioavailability by the oral route
Rationale

The therapeutic index is the ratio of a drug's toxic level to the level that provides therapeutic benefits, and it determines the safety of the therapy. With a low therapeutic index the difference between an active dose and a toxic dose is small, which brings a greater likelihood of an adverse reaction and requires closer monitoring. Warfarin and digoxin are the chapter's examples.

Watch for

(A) inverts the scale, and the inversion is built into the wording — "low" sounds like low risk. The index is a ratio, so a small ratio means the therapeutic and toxic doses sit close together.

therapeutic index, tolerance, and dependencetherapeutic index
46. A different drug is described as having a high therapeutic index. What does this indicate?
  • AThe drug is more dangerous and needs frequent serum levels
  • ✓The drug has a wide margin between therapeutic and toxic levels and is rarely associated with overdose events
  • CThe drug reaches toxic levels after a single dose
  • DThe drug must be given intravenously to be safe
Rationale

Drugs with a high therapeutic index are rarely associated with overdose events, and the chapter's example is amoxicillin.

(A) is the mirror error. Consistent monitoring belongs to the low-index drugs — warfarin, digoxin, and the aminoglycosides and vancomycin taken up in Chapter 39 — precisely because their margin is narrow.

therapeutic index, tolerance, and dependencetolerance versus dependence
47. A patient has needed steadily larger doses of an analgesic over several months to obtain the same relief, and reports no craving and no withdrawal when a dose is late. Which term describes this?
  • APsychological dependence
  • BPhysical dependence
  • ✓Tolerance
  • DIdiosyncratic reaction
Rationale

Tolerance is a decreasing response to repeated drug doses, or reduced response to a drug after prolonged use. Dependence, by contrast, is a physiologic or psychological need for a drug.

Watch for

(B) is the standing confusion in this course, and it is not the student's fault: Chapter 10's key terms call tolerance the same thing as physical dependence, while this chapter keeps them separate. When a stem turns on the distinction, this chapter's version is the more precise one — tolerance is a decreasing response, dependence is a need.

48. A patient with an opioid use disorder develops tachycardia and other physical symptoms when the drug is withheld, and describes an obsessive desire for its euphoric effects. Which terms apply? Select all that apply.

(C) attaches the definition of physical dependence to the word tolerance, which is the substitution this chapter exists to prevent. Tolerance is a decreasing response to repeated doses, not a need to avoid withdrawal.

drug interaction principlesadditive, synergistic, and antagonistic
49. Two antibiotics with similar actions are given together, and the combined effect is greater than the sum of their individual effects. Which term names this?
  • AAdditive effects
  • ✓Synergistic effects
  • CAntagonistic effects
  • DIncompatibility
Rationale

Synergistic effects are drug interactions in which the effect of a combination of two or more drugs with similar actions is greater than the sum of the individual effects — the chapter's arithmetic is 1 + 1 is greater than 2. Sulfamethoxazole-trimethoprim and an aminoglycoside plus a beta-lactam are the course's worked examples.

Watch for

(A) is the reflex answer for any beneficial combination. Additive means the combination equals the sum, 1 + 1 = 2. The word "greater" in the stem is doing all the work.

drug interaction principlesadditive, synergistic, and antagonistic
50. A tetracycline is given together with a bactericidal antibiotic, and the combined effect is less than the sum of the individual effects. Which term names this?
  • AAdditive effects
  • BSynergistic effects
  • ✓Antagonistic effects
  • DIncompatibility
Rationale

Antagonistic effects are interactions in which the effect of a combination is less than the sum of the individual effects, 1 + 1 equals less than 2, usually caused by an antagonizing, blocking, or reducing effect of one drug on another.

Watch for

(D) is chosen because both terms sound like drugs working against each other. Incompatibility is a physical or chemical event between two parenteral drugs mixed together — a precipitate, haziness, or color change — not a diminished pharmacologic effect in the patient.

54. A patient took a sleep medication and reports feeling restless and being unable to sleep at all that night. There is no rash, swelling, or respiratory involvement. How should this reaction be classified?
  • AAllergic reaction
  • ✓Idiosyncratic reaction
  • CPharmacologic reaction
  • DSynergistic effect
Rationale

An idiosyncratic reaction is an abnormal and unexpected response to a medication, other than an allergic reaction, that is peculiar to an individual patient. A response opposite to the drug's intended effect in one individual fits that definition.

(A) is the default label students apply to any unexpected reaction. 55. A nurse omits a scheduled dose of a patient's antihypertensive, and the patient's blood pressure rises. Which term best describes what has occurred?
  • AAn adverse drug reaction, because the outcome occurred at a therapeutic dose
  • ✓An adverse drug event, because it is an undesirable occurrence related to failing to administer a prescribed medication
  • CAn idiosyncratic reaction, because the response was peculiar to this patient
  • DNeither, because no drug was given
Rationale

An adverse drug event is any undesirable occurrence related to administering or failing to administer a prescribed medication, and it is the broadest of the chapter's four overlapping terms. A medication error is any preventable adverse drug event, and it may or may not cause patient harm.

(D) is the intuitive answer, since nothing entered the patient. The definition of an adverse drug event explicitly includes failing to administer, which is what separates it from an adverse drug reaction — the narrower term, restricted to responses at therapeutic dosages.

57. A patient of Mediterranean descent with G6PD deficiency is prescribed a sulfonamide antibiotic. Which statements are accurate? Select all that apply.
