1. A patient arrives with pain that began 40 minutes ago, is sharp and well localized over the right flank, and is accompanied by tachycardia, sweating, pallor, and an elevated blood pressure. Kidney stones are suspected. How does the nurse classify this pain, and what accounts for the vital sign changes?
  • AChronic pain, because the vital sign changes show the body has adapted to a long-standing stimulus
  • ✓Acute pain, because the onset is sudden and the sympathetic nervous system is responding
  • CCentral pain, because an autonomic response localizes the problem to the central nervous system
  • DReferred pain, because flank pain always originates outside the flank
Rationale

Table 10.2 gives acute pain as sudden in onset (minutes to hours), usually sharp and localized, with a physiologic sympathetic response of tachycardia, sweating, pallor, and increased blood pressure. Kidney stones are one of the table's own acute examples, alongside myocardial infarction, appendicitis, dental procedures, and surgical procedures.

Watch for

(A) inverts the table. Chronic pain is slow in onset (days to months), dull and persistent, and carries no sympathetic surge, which is exactly why a chronic pain patient with normal vital signs is so often disbelieved.

2. A patient with peripheral neuropathy reports a dull aching pain that has been present for the past 8 months. Vital signs are normal and the patient is calmly watching television. What is the correct interpretation?
  • ✓The pain is chronic, and normal vital signs do not argue against it
  • BThe pain is acute, because it is still present
  • CThe normal vital signs indicate the reported pain level is exaggerated
  • DThe absence of a sympathetic response means the pain has resolved
Rationale

Table 10.2 lists peripheral neuropathy among its chronic examples, alongside arthritis, cancer, and lower back pain, and gives chronic pain as persistent or recurring and endless in duration. The chapter defines chronic pain as lasting 3 to 6 months or longer. The physiologic sympathetic response belongs to the acute column only.

Watch for

(C) is the mirror error of the previous item and is the clinically dangerous one. Having just learned that acute pain comes with visible vital sign changes, a learner reads calm vital signs as evidence against pain.

pain classification by onset, source, and causesomatic versus visceral versus superficial pain
3. Two patients report severe pain. The first has pain arising from skeletal muscles, ligaments, and joints; the second has pain arising from an obstructed hollow organ. On the chapter's own pairing of source to drug class, which statement is correct?
  • ✓The first responds best to nonopioid analgesics such as NSAIDs, and the second usually requires opioids
  • BBoth are visceral in origin and both require opioids
  • CThe first usually requires opioids, and the second responds best to NSAIDs
  • DThe source of pain does not predict which analgesic class is effective
Rationale

Somatic pain originates from skeletal muscles, ligaments, and joints and responds best to nonopioid analgesics such as NSAIDs, and the chapter includes bone pain in that group. Visceral pain originates from organs and smooth muscles and usually requires opioids for relief.

Watch for

(C) is the exact reversal, and it is the reflex answer because severe pain pulls for the strong drug regardless of source. Cue: soma is the body wall, so skeletal muscle, ligaments, joints, and bone go to nonopioids.

pain classification by onset, source, and causesomatic versus visceral versus superficial pain
4. A patient has severe pain from extensive breakdown of the mucous membranes of the mouth. Which analgesic class does the chapter pair with pain of this source?
  • ANonopioids, because superficial pain is grouped with somatic pain for treatment
  • ✓Opioids, because superficial pain, like visceral pain, usually requires opioids for relief
  • CNo analgesic, because superficial pain is by definition mild
  • DAdjuvant anticonvulsants, because all mucous membrane pain is neuropathic
Rationale

Superficial pain originates from the skin and mucous membranes and, like visceral pain, usually requires opioids for relief. Deep pain occurs in tissues below skin level and is defined only as the opposite of superficial pain.

Watch for

(A) exploits an asymmetry the chapter builds in. Superficial and deep are the anatomically opposed pair, so a learner expects superficial to be treated as the opposite of visceral. For treatment it groups with visceral instead.

5. Three patients describe pain felt somewhere other than where the nurse would expect it: one has cholecystitis with pain in the back and scapular area, one has burning and tingling in a leg amputated 6 months ago, and one has pain that began after a stroke. Which set of classifications is correct?
  • ✓Referred pain, phantom pain, central pain
  • BPhantom pain, referred pain, central pain
  • CReferred pain, central pain, phantom pain
  • DCentral pain, phantom pain, referred pain
Rationale

Referred pain occurs when visceral nerve fibers synapse at a level in the spinal cord close to fibers supplying specific subcutaneous tissues, and the chapter's own example is cholecystitis pain referred to the back and scapular areas. Phantom pain occurs in the area of a body part removed surgically or traumatically and is often described as burning, itching, tingling, or stabbing. Central pain results from a disorder causing central nervous system damage, and the chapter names stroke among its causes.

Watch for

(C) swaps the last two, which is the common error because an amputation and a stroke both involve damaged neural tissue. The discriminator is whether the body part is gone (phantom) or the central nervous system itself is damaged in an intact body (central). Note the chapter's own edge case: pain in a paralyzed limb after spinal cord injury is filed under phantom.

pain threshold, tolerance, and dependence termsthreshold versus tolerance
7. Two patients receive the same stimulus during a dressing change. Both report that the stimulus registers as painful at the same intensity, but one continues the activity while the other stops. Which pair of terms describes what is the same and what differs?
  • ✓Pain threshold is the same, and pain tolerance differs
  • BPain tolerance is the same, and pain threshold differs
  • CBoth threshold and tolerance are identical, and the difference is drug tolerance
  • DThe two terms describe the same phenomenon and cannot differ within one patient
Rationale

Pain threshold is the level of a stimulus that results in the sensation of pain, and the chapter describes it as similar for most individuals. Pain tolerance is the amount of pain a patient can endure without it interfering with normal function, and it can vary from patient to patient.

Watch for

(B) is the reflex inversion, because both terms sound like "how much pain a person can take." Cue: threshold is physiologic and is where pain first registers, while tolerance is psychologic and is how long you keep going.

pain threshold, tolerance, and dependence termsthreshold versus tolerance
8. A nurse reads that a patient's cultural background, attitude, and environment have changed how much pain the patient will endure before stopping an activity. Which term is being described?
  • APain threshold, which varies widely with culture and attitude
  • ✓Pain tolerance, which can vary from patient to patient
  • COpioid tolerance, which develops after a week of opioid use
  • DAnalgesic ceiling effect, which limits how much relief a drug can give
Rationale

Pain tolerance is the amount of pain a patient can endure without it interfering with normal function and can vary from patient to patient, unlike pain threshold, which the chapter describes as similar for most individuals. If a stem varies by culture, attitude, or environment, it is describing tolerance.

Watch for

(A) is the mirror error of the previous item. (C) is the second-order trap the key terms box sets: pain tolerance and drug tolerance share a word but are unrelated concepts, and only the drug one is defined as needing larger doses for the same effect.

pain threshold, tolerance, and dependence termstolerance, physical dependence, and addiction
9. A patient on long-term opioid therapy for chronic back pain reports that the same dose no longer controls the pain, and states plainly that the only reason for wanting more is relief. There is no craving and no use for other effects. Which term applies?
  • APsychologic dependence
  • BAddiction
  • ✓Tolerance
  • DAnalgesic ceiling effect
Rationale

Tolerance is the state in which repetitive exposure to a given drug over time induces changes in drug receptors that reduce the drug's effects, so larger doses are required to maintain the same level of analgesia. The key terms box equates opioid tolerance with physical dependence and calls it a normal physiologic condition.

Watch for

(A) and (B) are the same thing in this chapter's own equation, and they pull because needing more sounds like addiction. The discriminator the chapter gives is why the patient wants the drug: relief is not addiction, and use for effects other than pain relief is.

pain threshold, tolerance, and dependence termstolerance, physical dependence, and addiction
10. A patient who has been receiving opioids has doses stopped abruptly and develops unpleasant physical withdrawal symptoms, but has never craved the drug or used it for anything but pain. What does this demonstrate?
  • APsychologic dependence, which the chapter calls addiction
  • ✓Physical dependence, the physical adaptation of the body to the presence of an opioid
  • CThat the patient has been diverting the medication
  • DAn analgesic ceiling effect
Rationale

Physical dependence is the condition in which unpleasant physical withdrawal symptoms occur if a drug taken over a period of time is stopped abruptly or smaller doses are given, and the chapter calls it the physical adaptation of the body to the presence of an opioid. Psychologic dependence, which the chapter equates with addiction, is a pattern of compulsive use with continuous craving and use for effects other than pain relief.

Watch for

(A) is the mirror error of the previous item, and it is the one that causes real harm. Withdrawal on abrupt discontinuation is expected physiology, not evidence of addiction, and the chapter says outright that psychologic dependence in patients on escalating opioids for cancer pain is unusual.

  • ✓Opioid tolerant, and the patient will generally require higher dosages

Rationale

Opioid tolerant is defined as the opposite of opioid naive and describes patients who have been receiving opioid analgesics, legally or otherwise, for a period of 1 week or longer. nociception and gate theorythe four processes of nociception
12. A patient's injured tissue releases chemicals that act on peripheral nerve fiber receptors and convert the noxious stimulus into a pain impulse. Which of the four processes of nociception is this, and which process runs in the opposite direction?
  • ✓Transduction, and modulation is the process that runs downward from the brain stem
  • BTransmission, and perception runs downward from the brain
  • CModulation, and transduction runs downward from the brain
  • DPerception, and transmission runs downward from the spinal cord
Rationale

Fig. 10.1 numbers the four processes as transduction (noxious stimuli propagate the pain message at the injury), transmission (the pain impulse moves from the spinal cord to the brain), perception of pain at the brain, and modulation, in which neurons from the brain stem release neurotransmitters that block the pain impulse. Only modulation runs downward. The chemicals released by injured cells are bradykinin, histamine, potassium, prostaglandins, and serotonin.

Watch for

(B) is the reflex pick, because transduction and transmission differ by three letters and both describe early events. Transduction happens at the injury; transmission carries the impulse from cord to brain.

nociception and gate theorythe gate and the nerve fibers that control it
13. A nurse rubs a patient's shoulder and the patient's pain eases. Using gate theory, what has happened at the dorsal horn of the spinal cord?
  • ✓Large myelinated A fibers were recruited, closing the gate so that impulses are not transmitted to the higher centers of the brain
  • BThe gate was opened, which is what prevents pain from being sensed
  • CSmall unmyelinated C fibers were recruited, which closes the gate
  • DThe threshold of the gate cells was lowered, allowing more impulses through
Rationale

Closing of the gate is affected by activation of A fibers, which inhibits impulse transmission to the brain and avoids pain sensation. The chapter gives massage and liniment as its own example: large sensory A nerve fibers from peripheral receptors carry pain-modulating impulses to the spinal cord, and the A fibers tend to close the gate. If impulses are stopped by a gate at this junction, no impulses are transmitted to the higher centers of the brain.

Watch for

(B) inverts the gate's logic, which is counterintuitive by exactly one step: a closed gate means no pain, so effective analgesia is a closed gate. (C) has the two fiber types crossed.

14. A patient's dull, poorly localized pain worsens. Which nerve fiber is carrying it, and what is that fiber doing to the gate?
  • AA fibers, which are unmyelinated and slow, opening the gate
  • ✓C fibers, which are small, unmyelinated, and slow, opening the gate so that pain is sensed
  • CC fibers, which are large and fast, closing the gate
  • DA fibers, which are large and fast, closing the gate
Rationale

Table 10.3 gives C fibers as having no myelin sheath, small fiber size, and slow conduction, producing dull and nonlocalized pain. Opening of the gate is affected by the stimulation of C fibers, which allows impulses to be transmitted to the brain and pain to be sensed.

Watch for

(D) is the mirror error of the previous item, and every attribute it lists is genuinely true of A fibers, which is what makes it attractive. A fibers carry sharp, well-localized pain and close the gate. (A) attaches one fiber's name to the other fiber's attributes.

nociception and gate theoryendogenous opioids and conditions that alter the threshold
15. A runner reports euphoria and reduced pain after prolonged exertion. Which explanation matches the chapter?
  • ✓Endogenous substances bond with opioid receptors and close the spinal cord gates, inhibiting the transmission of pain impulses
  • BEndogenous substances open the spinal cord gates so that pain impulses bypass the brain
  • CExertion depletes prostaglandins, which are the body's only pain mediator
  • DEnkephalins are synthetic drugs given to produce the runner's high
Rationale

Enkephalins and endorphins are produced within the body to fight pain and are considered the body's painkillers. Both are capable of bonding with opioid receptors and inhibiting the transmission of pain impulses by closing the spinal cord gates. Endorphin is a condensed version of endogenous morphine, and these substances are released whenever the body experiences pain or prolonged exertion, which is responsible for the phenomenon of runner's high.

Watch for

(B) is the gate inversion again in a new setting. (D) forgets that enkephalins are endogenous; Table 10.5 lists enkephalins as the prototypical agonist at the delta receptor, and that prototype is a body substance rather than a marketed drug.

special pain situations and pca safetypatient-controlled analgesia
17. A nurse finds a patient's spouse pressing the PCA button while the patient sleeps, explaining that the patient was hurting earlier and should not wake up in pain. What is the correct nursing response and why?
  • ADocument the spouse as an approved second operator so that the dosing is recorded
  • ✓Stop the practice, because this is PCA by proxy, which the Institute for Safe Medication Practices advises against and which has caused numerous deaths
  • CAllow it as long as the spouse presses no more often than the lockout permits
  • DAllow it only during sleep, when the patient cannot press the button
Rationale

PCA is designed so that patients self-medicate by pressing a button on the infusion pump. The chapter names the hazard directly: well-meaning family members pressing the dosing button rather than letting able patients do so on their own, called PCA by proxy, and it states that numerous deaths have occurred when family members administered too much of the opioid drug. The Institute for Safe Medication Practices advises against PCA by proxy.

Watch for

(C) is the reflex compromise, because the lockout feels like a hardware guarantee of safety. The lockout limits the dose, not the patient's level of sedation, and the whole safety logic of PCA is that an oversedated patient cannot press the button.

18. A postoperative patient is too confused to operate a PCA pump reliably. What does the chapter direct?
  • AHave the nurse press the button on the patient's behalf at scheduled intervals
  • BHave a family member press the button whenever the patient appears uncomfortable
  • ✓Use a different method of pain control
  • DContinue the PCA and accept that the patient will receive less drug
Rationale

The chapter states that for patients who are unable to self-medicate using the PCA pump, a different method of pain control must be used. Morphine and hydromorphone are the drugs commonly given by PCA, and the method has been shown to be very effective and to reduce the total opioid dose used.

Watch for

(A) is the same proxy error wearing a uniform, and it pulls harder than (B) because a nurse pressing the button feels professional rather than well-meaning. The chapter's alternative is not a different operator but a different method.

special pain situations and pca safetyopioid-tolerant patients and breakthrough pain
19. A patient who takes long-acting opioids daily for chronic back pain is admitted with a new fracture. How should the acute pain be managed?
  • AHold the chronic regimen and start standard opioid-naive doses for the fracture
  • ✓Continue an equivalent amount of the chronic pain medication and add an extra 20% to 40% more opioid to treat the acute pain
  • CSubstitute an agonist-antagonist to reduce the risk for oversedation
  • DTreat with nonopioids only, because the patient is already opioid tolerant
Rationale

The chapter states that effective management of acute-on-chronic pain requires patients to receive equivalent amounts of their chronic pain medication in addition to an extra 20% to 40% more opioids to treat the acute pain. Longer-acting opioids such as extended-release oxycodone are usually better choices for these patients, because shorter-acting drugs are more likely to produce a psychologic high that reinforces addictive tendencies.

Watch for

(A) is the undertreatment error the chapter spends a paragraph condemning; a tolerant patient started at naive doses gets neither baseline nor acute coverage. (C) is worse than it looks, because an agonist-antagonist given to an opioid-tolerant patient can both reduce analgesic effects and cause withdrawal symptoms.

special pain situations and pca safetyopioid-tolerant patients and breakthrough pain
20. A patient on scheduled extended-release oxycodone reports pain returning between doses. Which regimen matches the chapter?
  • ✓Give immediate-release oxycodone as needed between the scheduled extended-release doses
  • BGive extended-release oxycodone as needed between scheduled immediate-release doses
  • CCrush the extended-release tablet so that it acts faster during the painful interval
  • DDiscontinue the extended-release product and use as-needed dosing only
Rationale

Breakthrough pain is pain that occurs between doses of pain medication, and the chapter's regimen is as-needed doses of immediate-release dosage forms given between scheduled doses of extended-release dosage forms. If the patient requires larger doses for breakthrough pain, the interval of the scheduled extended-release opioid may need to be shortened or a more potent drug started.

Watch for

(B) reverses which form is scheduled and which is as needed, producing a regimen that undertreats baseline pain and oversedates at the same time. (C) is the fatal one: chewing or crushing any extended-release opioid can cause oversedation, respiratory depression, and even death from rapid drug absorption.

who analgesic ladder and adjuvant analgesicsthe three-step ladder
22. A patient's newly assessed pain is being treated with a nonopioid alone, with an adjuvant added. Which step of the WHO analgesic ladder is this, and what does the presence of the adjuvant tell you?
  • ✓Step 1, and the adjuvant does not change the step, because adjuvants are optional at every step
  • BStep 2, because adding any second drug advances the patient to step 2
  • CStep 2, because adjuvants are what define step 2
  • DStep 3, because adjuvants are reserved for the highest step
Rationale

Step 1 of the WHO ladder is the use of nonopioids, with or without adjuvant medications, once the pain has been identified and assessed. Steps 2 and 3 are both described as with or without adjuvants, so adjuvants are optional at every step and it is the opioid that advances the step.

Watch for

(C) and (D) both try to make adjuvants the discriminator, which is the single most common way this ladder is misread. What moves a patient from step 1 to step 2 is the addition of an opioid, and from step 2 to step 3 an opioid indicated for moderate to severe pain.

who analgesic ladder and adjuvant analgesicsthe three-step ladder
23. A patient on a nonopioid plus an adjuvant reports pain that persists and is increasing. According to the ladder, what comes next?
  • ✓Add an opioid, with or without nonopioids and with or without adjuvants
  • BRemove the adjuvant so that the nonopioid can be titrated upward
  • CMove directly to opioids indicated for moderate to severe pain
  • DRepeat step 1 with a different nonopioid before advancing
Rationale

Step 2 of the ladder states that if pain persists or increases, treatment is the use of opioids with or without nonopioids and with or without adjuvants. Step 3, opioids indicated for moderate to severe pain, follows only if pain persists or increases again.

Watch for

(C) is the mirror error of skipping a rung, and it is tempting because increasing pain feels like a step 3 problem. Note the chapter's own caveat as well: not all patients will be treated effectively using the ladder method, and some may need to seek an experienced pain management physician.

24. A patient describes burning, tingling, electrical sensations in both feet and says that the bed sheets hurt. Opioid doses have been increased twice without benefit. What does the chapter indicate?
  • AIncrease the opioid again, because the dose is simply not high enough yet
  • ✓Add an adjuvant such as gabapentin, pregabalin, or amitriptyline, because opioids are not completely effective in neuropathic pain

Adjuvant drugs are commonly used in the treatment of neuropathic pain, in which opioids are not completely effective, and the chapter names the antidepressant amitriptyline and the anticonvulsants gabapentin and pregabalin. (A) is the reflex escalation and it is the cleanest right-drug-class, wrong-pain-type error the chapter sets up. Burning, tingling, electrical sensations, and allodynia are the cue to change class, not to raise the dose.

72. A patient with metastatic bone pain has pain present for most of the day. How should analgesia be scheduled?

  • BOn schedule during waking hours only
  • ✓Around the clock, with additional doses as needed for breakthrough pain
  • DOnly when the reported pain score exceeds 7 of 10
Rationale

When pain is present for more than 12 hours a day, analgesic doses are individualized and are best administered around the clock rather than on an as-needed basis, because around-the-clock or scheduled dosing maintains steady-state levels of the medication and prevents drug troughs and pain escalation. It is best to treat the patient's pain before it becomes severe, which is the rationale for considering pain the fifth vital sign.

(B) is the option built to look responsible, and it reintroduces a nightly trough the patient wakes up in. (D) waits for severe pain, which is exactly what scheduled dosing exists to prevent.
