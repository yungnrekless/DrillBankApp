fungal infections and mycosis typesyeasts versus molds
1. A microbiology report on a wound describes the organism as a single-celled fungus that reproduces by budding, with a daughter cell pouching out of and breaking off from a mother cell. Which term names this organism?
  • ✓A yeast
  • BA mold
  • CA dermatophyte
  • DA mycelium
Rationale

Yeasts are single-celled fungi that reproduce by budding, a process in which "a daughter cell forms by pouching out of and breaking off from a mother cell." Fungi as a group are a very large, diverse group of eukaryotic microorganisms consisting of yeasts and molds.

Watch for

(B) pulls because the two words are learned as a pair and both name fungi. Molds are multicellular, built of long branching filaments called hyphae that entwine into a mycelium — the structural opposite of a single cell that buds.

2. A second report describes a fungus made of long, branching filaments that entwine to form a complex branched structure. What is that structure called, and what type of fungus is this?
  • ✓A mycelium, and the fungus is a mold
  • BA mycelium, and the fungus is a yeast
  • CA hypha, and the fungus is a yeast
  • DA sterol, and the fungus is a mold
Rationale

Molds are multicellular fungi characterized by long, branching filaments called hyphae, which entwine to form a complex branched structure known as a mycelium. The key points restate the yeast and mold contrast verbatim and add that yeasts may be harmful, causing infections, or helpful, aiding in baking or brewing beer.

Watch for

(B) is the mirror error of the previous item, keeping the right structure but attaching it to the single-celled fungus. (C) swaps the individual filament for the entwined mass; both hypha and mycelium are defined inside the Molds key-term entry rather than as their own terms, which is why they get skimmed past.

3. A patient with athlete's foot asks the nurse what the word "mycosis" on his chart means. Which explanation is accurate?
  • ✓It is the general term for any fungal infection
  • BIt is the name of the fungus that infects skin, nails, or hair
  • CIt refers only to fungal infections that have become systemic
  • DIt is the main sterol in fungal cell membranes
Rationale

Mycosis is "the general term for any fungal infection," and the body text states the same thing as "an infection caused by a fungus." Athlete's foot is the chapter's own example of the mild end of a range that runs from annoying symptoms to life-threatening systemic mycoses.

Watch for

(B) names the dermatophyte, which is the organism rather than the infection, and the reflex is to fuse the two. The chapter states the chain outright: fungi that cause integumentary infections are dermatophytes, and the infections they cause are dermatomycoses. (D) is the definition of ergosterol.

fungal infections and mycosis typesmycosis, dermatophyte, and the four types
4. A nurse is reviewing the four general types of mycotic infection. Which type is NOT an infection of a layer of the integumentary system?
  • ✓Systemic
  • BCutaneous
  • CSubcutaneous
  • DSuperficial
Rationale

There are four general types of mycotic infection — systemic, cutaneous, subcutaneous, and superficial — and "the latter three are infections of various layers of the integumentary system (skin, hair, or nails)." The treatment split follows the same line: systemic mycoses and some cutaneous or subcutaneous mycoses get oral or parenteral drugs, while superficial infections are treated topically.

Watch for

(D) pulls because "superficial" sounds like the least skin-specific of the four names, so students reach for it as the odd one out. It is squarely one of the three integumentary types; systemic is the single name that is not.

  • ✓A patient who is immunocompromised as a result of cancer chemotherapy

The most severe systemic fungal infections generally affect people whose host immune defenses are compromised — organ transplant recipients taking immunosuppressive drug therapy, cancer patients immunocompromised by chemotherapy, and patients with AIDS. 8. A blood culture grows Candida glabrata, and the chart documents involvement of the blood and lungs. Candidiasis is the one mycosis appearing in both halves of Table 42.1. What separates the systemic row from the superficial row?
  • AThe reservoir, since the systemic form is soil-borne and the superficial form is human
  • BThe transmission route, since only the systemic form is acquired by inhalation
  • ✓The tissue affected, since the systemic row lists blood and lungs while the superficial row lists mucous membrane and skin
  • DThe mode of acquisition, since only the superficial form results from antibiotic overgrowth
Rationale

Table 42.1 lists candidiasis twice, and the axis separating the rows is where the organism lands. The systemic row gives blood and lungs and widens the organism list to five Candida species; the superficial row gives mucous membrane and skin for C. albicans alone, with its own hedge that the infection may be disseminated.

(A) and (B) both fail on the same fact: the two rows carry an identical reservoir, humans, and an identical transmission clause, direct contact plus overgrowth in response to treatment with an antibiotic to which the organism is nonsusceptible. 11. A nursing student lists fluconazole as an imidazole antifungal. Which correction should the instructor make?
  • ✓Fluconazole is a triazole; ketoconazole is the imidazole the chapter lists
  • BFluconazole is an echinocandin and not an azole at all
  • CFluconazole is not classified according to chemical structure
  • DBoth imidazole and triazole are wrong, since fluconazole is a polyene
Rationale

The chapter's four chemical classes are polyenes (amphotericin B and nystatin), imidazoles (ketoconazole), triazoles (fluconazole, itraconazole, voriconazole, posaconazole, and isavuconazonium), and echinocandins (caspofungin, micafungin, and anidulafungin).

Watch for

(D) is chosen by students who overcorrect once told that "imidazole" is wrong and assume the whole azole label must be wrong too. The error is one level down: fluconazole is an azole, just the triazole kind.

16. A nurse explains why caspofungin does not damage human cells. Which fungal structure does the echinocandin class attack?
  • AErgosterol in the fungal cell membrane
  • ✓Glucans in the fungal cell wall
  • CFungal DNA synthesis
  • DMicrotubules within the fungal cell
Rationale

The echinocandins act by "preventing the synthesis of glucans, essential components of fungal cell walls that are not present in human cells." That absence from human cells is the chapter's stated basis for selective toxicity.

Watch for

(A) is the reflex pick because four of the six profiled drugs work on the membrane through ergosterol, which makes the cell-wall answer look like the outlier when it is the correct one. Caspofungin is this chapter's single cell-wall drug.

17. A nurse reviews amphotericin B's mechanism before the first dose. Which statement describes how the polyenes kill fungal cells?
  • ✓They bind sterols in the fungal cell membrane, forming a channel that lets potassium and magnesium leak out
  • BThey inhibit fungal cytochrome P-450 enzymes so that ergosterol is never produced
  • CThey prevent glucan synthesis in the fungal cell wall
  • DThey are deaminated to 5-fluorouracil, which blocks fungal DNA synthesis
Rationale

The polyenes, amphotericin B and nystatin, bind to sterols in the cell membranes of fungi; once the drug binds ergosterol, a channel forms that allows potassium and magnesium ions to leak out, altering fungal cellular metabolism and killing the cell.

Watch for

(B) is the azole mechanism and is the mirror error of this pair. The chapter flags the resemblance itself, noting that inhibiting ergosterol production produces "a defect similar to that caused by the polyene antifungals." The polyene binds the finished sterol and punches a channel; the azole shuts down production so the sterol is never made.

18. A patient on an azole antifungal takes several other drugs metabolized by the liver. Which feature of the azole mechanism explains why this class carries such a large human interaction list?
  • AThe azoles bind ergosterol directly, and humans produce ergosterol as well
  • ✓The azoles work by inhibiting cytochrome P-450 enzymes, and human drug metabolism runs through the same enzyme system
  • CThe azoles block glucan synthesis, and glucans are shared with human cells
  • DThe azoles are converted to 5-fluorouracil in the liver
Rationale

The imidazoles and triazoles "work by inhibiting fungal cell cytochrome P-450 enzymes that are needed to produce ergosterol." Only the azole mechanism runs through cytochrome P-450, which is why the interaction section names that enzyme system as "a common underlying source of the problem."

Watch for

(C) inverts the chapter's own selective-toxicity statement. Glucans are specifically "not present in human cells," which is exactly why the echinocandins do not carry this interaction burden.

antifungal mechanisms of actionthe two unclassified mechanisms
19. Two older antifungals sit outside the chemical classes and are easily swapped. Which pairing of drug to mechanism is correct?
  • AFlucytosine binds microtubules and blocks mitosis; griseofulvin becomes 5-FU and blocks DNA synthesis
  • ✓Flucytosine is deaminated to 5-fluorouracil and interferes with fungal DNA synthesis; griseofulvin binds microtubules and inhibits fungal mitosis
  • CBoth act as antimetabolites against fungal DNA
  • DBoth inhibit ergosterol production through fungal cytochrome P-450
Rationale

Flucytosine is an antimetabolite; inside a susceptible fungal cell the enzyme cytosine deaminase converts it to 5-FU, which interferes with fungal DNA synthesis, and human cells are unharmed because they lack that enzyme. Griseofulvin enters the fungal cell through an energy-dependent transport system and inhibits fungal mitosis by binding microtubules.

Watch for

(A) is the direct swap, and it is tempting because the two drugs share every surface feature: both old, both outside the chemical classes, both largely replaced by newer drugs. One extra discriminator is that flucytosine is the drug the chapter says acts "in much the same way as the antiviral drugs"; griseofulvin is compared to nothing.

23. A patient with severe systemic candidiasis is to receive a polyene antifungal intravenously. Which order should the nurse question?
  • AAmphotericin B intravenously
  • ✓Nystatin intravenously

Nystatin "is not available in a parenteral form" but does come in several oral and topical formulations. 24. Amphotericin B is ordered together with flucytosine for a patient with a cryptococcal infection. What is the rationale for the combination?
  • AFlucytosine reduces amphotericin B's infusion reactions
  • ✓The two drugs are synergistic against Candida and cryptococcal infections
  • CFlucytosine protects the kidneys from amphotericin B
  • DThe combination shortens amphotericin B's long half-life
Rationale

Amphotericin B "is sometimes given with flucytosine in the treatment of Candida and cryptococcal infections because of the synergy of the two drugs." From flucytosine's side the indication is narrower: endocarditis and cryptococcal meningoencephalitis, in conjunction with amphotericin B.

(C) borrows the drug's best-known toxicity and invents a protective role for the partner drug. antifungal drug interactions and cytochrome p-450enzyme competition as the common cause
31. A patient taking several medications is started on an azole antifungal. Which patient factor accounts for most antifungal drug interactions?
  • AThe patient's cardiac history
  • BA history of gallbladder surgery
  • CThe patient's ethnic background
  • ✓The patient's cytochrome P-450 enzyme system
Rationale

"A common underlying source of the problem is that many of the antifungal drugs, as well as other drugs, are metabolized by the cytochrome P-450 enzyme system." Two such drugs "compete for the limited amount of enzymes, and one of the drugs ends up accumulating."

Watch for

(A) pulls because the most alarming consequence named in Table 42.3 is cardiac — quinidine plus an azole prolonging the QT interval on the electrocardiogram. That is a result of an interaction, not its cause. The cause is enzyme competition, and the answer is a metabolic system rather than an organ history or a demographic.

  • ✓Azole antifungal levels will fall, so the antifungal may fail to work
  • CBoth the azole and the anticonvulsant levels will rise
  • DThe azole will prolong the QT interval on the electrocardiogram
Rationale

Table 42.3's final azole row lists phenytoin, rifampin, phenobarbital, and carbamazepine with the effect "decreased levels of azole antifungals." Those four are enzyme inducers, which speed metabolism and lower the level of the affected drug — here the antifungal itself.

Watch for

(A) is the mirror error of the previous item, applying the inhibitor direction to inducers. The four named drugs do not become toxic; they make the antifungal stop working, which is a treatment failure rather than a toxicity, and that is a much quieter problem to detect.

35. A patient is to begin an intravenous amphotericin B infusion. Which measure reduces the likelihood of an infusion-related reaction?

  • ✓Infuse the dose over a longer-than-average time of 2 to 6 hours
  • CStop the infusion for 2 hours partway through and then resume
  • DForce fluids throughout the infusion
Rationale

The likelihood of infusion-related reactions "can be reduced by using longer-than-average drug infusion times (i.e., 2 to 6 hours)." Premedications — antiemetics, antihistamines, antipyretics, and corticosteroids — are often given for the same purpose.

Watch for

(A) is the reflex inversion, chosen on the intuition that briefer exposure means a smaller reaction. When an intravenous antiinfective in this book produces infusion reactions, the answer is to slow it down, which is the same logic used for vancomycin and red man syndrome.

(D) inverts the reason the lipid forms exist at all — fewer adverse effects is precisely their advantage. The gate on them is cost, not safety, and reaching for a lipid form first skips a gate the chapter states twice.

38. Thirty minutes into an amphotericin B infusion, a patient's vital signs decline and he develops difficulty breathing along with worsening chills and nausea. What is the nurse's priority action?

  • ✓Discontinue the infusion while continuing to monitor the patient closely, then contact the prescriber immediately
  • CGive the ordered premedications now and continue the infusion

40. A patient with oral candidiasis is prescribed nystatin lozenges. Which instruction is correct?
  • AChew the lozenge thoroughly before swallowing it
  • BSwallow the lozenge whole with a full glass of water
  • ✓Allow the lozenge to dissolve slowly and completely in the mouth
  • DDissolve the lozenge in warm water and drink the solution
Rationale

Nystatin may be given orally as lozenges or troches: "These dosage forms must be slowly and completely dissolved in the mouth for optimal effects. They are not to be chewed or swallowed whole." Both oral nystatin forms work by local contact with the oral mucosa. Lozenges are generally not used in children younger than 5 years of age.

Watch for

(A) and (B) are the two actions the chapter explicitly forbids, and they pull because nearly every other oral medication the patient has taken is either chewed or swallowed. Swallowing nystatin immediately does not poison the patient; it means the drug never treated the mouth at all.
