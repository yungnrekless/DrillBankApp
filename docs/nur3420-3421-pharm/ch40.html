antiviral terminology and viral genome vocabularygeneral versus specific drug terms
1. A nurse is charting for a patient whose medication list includes acyclovir for recurrent genital herpes and zidovudine for HIV infection. Which single term correctly describes both drugs?
  • AAntiretroviral drugs, because both suppress viral replication
  • ✓Antiviral drugs, the general term for drugs that destroy viruses either directly or indirectly by suppressing their replication
  • CAntibiotics, because both act against an infectious organism
  • DImmunoglobulins, because both attack a foreign antigen
Rationale

The key-terms box makes the relationship a general-to-specific one: antiviral drugs are "a general term for drugs that destroy viruses, either directly or indirectly by suppressing their replication," while antiretroviral drugs are "a specific term for antiviral drugs that work against retroviruses such as HIV." Every antiretroviral is an antiviral, so only the general term covers both drugs on this list.

Watch for

(A) is the reflex pick the moment HIV appears anywhere in a stem, but acyclovir treats herpesviruses, not retroviruses, so it is not an antiretroviral. The chapter's own structure and its key points split the two halves the same way.

antiviral terminology and viral genome vocabularygeneral versus specific drug terms
2. The same nurse must identify which of those two drugs belongs to the narrower category the chapter reserves for HIV therapy. Which statement is accurate?
  • ✓Only zidovudine is an antiretroviral, because antiretroviral names drugs that work against retroviruses such as HIV
  • BOnly acyclovir is an antiretroviral, because herpesviruses replicate by reverse transcription
  • CBoth are antiretrovirals, because the two terms are interchangeable
  • DNeither is an antiretroviral, because that term refers only to vaccines
Rationale

Retroviruses are the family Retroviridae, they carry RNA as their genome, and they replicate using reverse transcriptase; HIV is named as the currently most clinically significant retrovirus. Acyclovir's targets, HSV-1, HSV-2, and varicella-zoster virus, are herpesviruses and replicate without reverse transcriptase.

Watch for

(C) is the mirror error of the previous item, collapsing the general term into the specific one so that everything antiviral gets called antiretroviral. (B) hands reverse transcription to the herpesviruses, which is the same confusion running the other way.

antiviral terminology and viral genome vocabularynucleic acid vocabulary
3. A patient's antiretroviral regimen lists tenofovir alafenamide as a nucleotide reverse transcriptase inhibitor and zidovudine as a nucleoside reverse transcriptase inhibitor. What structural difference does that labeling describe?
  • ✓A nucleotide is a nucleoside with an attached phosphate unit
  • BA nucleoside is a nucleotide with an attached phosphate unit
  • CA nucleotide is built from RNA and a nucleoside from DNA
  • DA nucleoside is found only in host cells and a nucleotide only in viruses
Rationale

The chapter states it twice: "A nucleoside is a single unit consisting of a base and its attached sugar... A nucleotide is a nucleoside plus an attached phosphate." The key-terms box adds that the phosphate unit is what makes up the side chain backbone of a DNA or RNA molecule. The labeling is not cosmetic, since tenofovir alafenamide is the nucleotide agent while zidovudine, lamivudine, abacavir, emtricitabine, stavudine, and didanosine are nucleosides.

Watch for

(B) is the inversion, and it is the common one because the two words differ by three letters and both appear in the same drug class. The cue is that nucleoTide equals phosphaTe.

antiviral terminology and viral genome vocabularynucleic acid vocabulary
4. A patient newly diagnosed with HIV asks why the virus is called a retrovirus. Which explanation is accurate?
  • AIt carries both DNA and RNA within the same virion
  • ✓It carries RNA as its genome and uses reverse transcriptase to build DNA from that RNA, the reverse of the usual direction
  • CIt carries DNA as its genome and copies that DNA into RNA inside the nucleus
  • DIt replicates outside host cells and then enters them as mature virions
Rationale

Retroviruses "contain RNA (as opposed to DNA) as their genome and replicate using the enzyme reverse transcriptase," and reverse transcriptase is defined as "an RNA-directed DNA polymerase" that promotes "the synthesis of a DNA molecule from an RNA molecule, which is the reverse of the usual process."

Watch for

(A) is chosen by students who remember that a viral genome may be either nucleic acid and conclude it can be both; the chapter states a virion's genome is DNA or RNA "but not both." (D) contradicts the definition of a virus as the smallest known class of microorganisms, which "can only replicate inside host cells."

influenza antiviral selectionspectrum by drug
5. A patient with laboratory-confirmed influenza B has a new order for amantadine. What should the nurse recognize about this order?
  • ✓Amantadine is only effective against influenza A, so the drug does not match the diagnosis
  • BAmantadine covers influenza A and B, so the order is appropriate
  • CAmantadine is a neuraminidase inhibitor and is preferred for influenza B
  • DAmantadine is appropriate here only if the patient is younger than 12 months
Rationale

Amantadine is "only effective against influenza A," and Table 40.1 sorts it and rimantadine under influenza A while listing zanamivir and oseltamivir under influenza A and B. A further problem sits behind the spectrum issue: CDC guidelines "do not recommend the use of amantadine or rimantadine to prevent or treat the flu."

Watch for

(D) inverts the age rule, since being younger than 12 months is a printed contraindication for amantadine, not an indication. (C) borrows the neuraminidase inhibitor label that belongs to oseltamivir and zanamivir.

influenza antiviral selectionspectrum by drug
6. The prescriber asks which antiviral would actually cover this patient's influenza B. Which pair of drugs is effective against both influenza A and influenza B?
  • AAmantadine and rimantadine
  • ✓Oseltamivir and zanamivir
  • CAcyclovir and valacyclovir
  • DEntecavir and lamivudine
Rationale

Table 40.1 gives zanamivir and oseltamivir for influenza A and B. Both are neuraminidase inhibitors, and the chapter explains that this enzyme "enables budding virions to escape from infected cells," so blocking it traps new virions in the cell they were made in.

Watch for

(A) is the mirror error of the previous item, since the two adamantanes are the influenza A only pair. (D) names two hepatitis B drugs, and (C) names two herpesvirus drugs, both of which read as plausible only if the drug is not sorted by the virus it treats.

influenza antiviral selectionspectrum by drug
7. A patient whose household member has active influenza asks for something to keep from getting sick. Which neuraminidase inhibitor fits, and why?
  • AZanamivir, because the inhaled route reaches the respiratory tract before infection takes hold
  • ✓Oseltamivir, because it is oral and is indicated for both prophylaxis and treatment
  • CEither drug, because both are approved for prophylaxis and for treatment
  • DNeither drug, because neuraminidase inhibitors are approved only for hospitalized patients
Rationale

Oseltamivir is oral and is used for both prophylaxis and treatment, while zanamivir is inhaled by Diskhaler and is indicated for treatment of active illness only. Their adverse effects split the same way: oseltamivir causes nausea and vomiting, zanamivir causes diarrhea, nausea, and sinusitis.

Watch for

(A) is the reflex pick because an inhaled drug sounds preventive at the site of viral entry, and the route is the very thing that makes the two drugs feel interchangeable. The indication, not the route, is the discriminator.

herpesvirus antiviral selection and look-alike namesmatching the drug to the herpesvirus
8. A patient who received a kidney transplant 3 months ago develops cytomegalovirus retinitis. Which antiviral does the chapter identify for this infection?
  • AAcyclovir
  • ✓Ganciclovir
  • COseltamivir
  • DRibavirin
Rationale

Ganciclovir is the nucleoside analogue for CMV, which is HHV-5, and it is also given prophylactically "in high-risk patients, such as those receiving organ transplants." Its ophthalmic form must be surgically inserted.

Watch for

(A) is the reflex pick because acyclovir is the antiviral students know best and the two names differ by four letters, but acyclovir's spectrum is HSV-1, HSV-2, and varicella-zoster virus, not CMV. Review question 5 works this exact swap on a post-transplant patient.

herpesvirus antiviral selection and look-alike namesmatching the drug to the herpesvirus
9. A patient presents with a recurrent episode of genital herpes caused by HSV-2. Which drug does the chapter name as the drug of choice?
  • AGanciclovir, because it covers all of the herpesviruses
  • ✓Acyclovir, which is the drug of choice for both initial and recurrent episodes
  • CFoscarnet, because it is the only injectable option
  • DTrifluridine, which is used for herpes simplex infections at any site
Rationale

Acyclovir is a nucleoside analogue for HSV-1, HSV-2, and varicella-zoster virus, and the chapter calls it "the drug of choice" for both initial and recurrent episodes. Trifluridine's indication in Table 40.1 is herpes simplex keratitis specifically, not herpes simplex at any site.

Watch for

(A) is the mirror error of the previous item, generalizing ganciclovir from CMV to the whole herpesvirus family. Note also that the chapter cautions "there can be anatomic overlap between the two types," so an option claiming HSV-1 can never cause genital lesions would be a distractor rather than a fact.

10. A patient with severe disseminated herpes infection needs intravenous antiviral therapy, and a separate order calls for a topical antiviral ointment. Which drug can supply both of these forms?
  • AValacyclovir
  • BFamciclovir
  • ✓Acyclovir
  • DEntecavir
Rationale

Acyclovir is the one drug in this group available in oral, topical, and injectable forms; valacyclovir and famciclovir are oral only. Valacyclovir is "a prodrug that is metabolized to acyclovir," dosed "three times daily versus five times daily."

Watch for

(A) is the reflex pick because valacyclovir is the form most outpatients actually take, but it is oral only. The chapter warns twice that "acyclovir may be mistaken for valacyclovir and famciclovir" and to "note the slight inconsistencies in the spelling," so the working cue is that any stem naming an infusion, a finger cot, or an ointment is acyclovir, while the prefix val- signals a prodrug formulated for oral use, as in valacyclovir to acyclovir and valganciclovir to ganciclovir.

11. A prescription written by trade name reads Zostrix for a patient being treated for genital herpes lesions. What should the nurse do?
  • AAdminister it, because Zostrix is the topical acyclovir product
  • ✓Clarify the order, because Zostrix is capsaicin for arthritic pain while Zovirax 5% ointment is the topical acyclovir product
  • CAdminister it, but only once the lesions have crusted over
  • DClarify the order, because Zostrix is an oral drug and this patient needs a topical one
Rationale

The chapter's safety box, written up from a real reported error, contrasts Zostrix, capsaicin "derived from hot chili peppers" and used for arthritic pain, with Zovirax 5% ointment, topical acyclovir used for genital herpes. The fix the box names is using generic names.

Watch for

(A) is the reflex pick because the two trade names differ by three letters and both begin with the same syllable, and nothing in the trade name signals which drug is inside. (D) accepts the wrong drug and then argues about its route, which is the error that lets a look-alike order through.

12. A patient telephones on the third day of a painful dermatomal rash diagnosed as herpes zoster and asks whether antiviral therapy is still worth starting. Within what window does the chapter say acyclovir should be started for herpes zoster?
  • AWithin 2 days of onset, the same window used for the influenza antivirals
  • ✓Within 72 hours of onset, although the effect on recovery is usually not dramatic
  • CWithin 7 days of onset
  • DAt any point during the outbreak, because the window applies only to influenza
Rationale

Acyclovir in herpes zoster is started within 72 hours of onset and "may speed recovery, but this effect is usually not dramatic." The influenza figure is a different number: oseltamivir and zanamivir must be started within 2 days of symptom onset.

Watch for

(A) is the reflex pick because the 2-day influenza figure is repeated in both the assessment and the implementation sections and is the number that sticks. The cue is 2 days for influenza, 72 hours for zoster. A third figure, 24 to 48 hours, appears only in patient teaching and is keyed to the patient noticing a recurrence, not to a clinician's clock.

hepatitis b and c antiviral therapysorting the drug to the hepatitis type
13. A patient with chronic hepatitis B is started on entecavir. Which instruction and expectation match the chapter's description of this drug?
  • ATake it with a full meal, and expect a 4-week course
  • ✓Take it on an empty stomach, and expect a course of at least 12 months
  • CTake it with ribavirin, and expect a 12-week course
  • DTake it on an empty stomach, and expect a single dose
Rationale

Entecavir is a nucleoside reverse transcriptase inhibitor for hepatitis B, given for a minimum of 12 months and on an empty stomach. Its black box warning covers acute exacerbations of hepatitis B, lactic acidosis, hepatomegaly, and resistance developing in unrecognized HIV infection, which is why HIV status matters before it is started.

Watch for

(A) is the reflex pick because the chapter's general rule really is to take oral antivirals with meals; entecavir and indinavir are the two printed empty-stomach exceptions. Ribavirin in (C) belongs to the hepatitis C regimens, not to hepatitis B, whose drugs are entecavir, lamivudine, and tenofovir.

hepatitis b and c antiviral therapysorting the drug to the hepatitis type
14. A patient with chronic hepatitis C is prescribed sofosbuvir. Which statement about this drug is accurate?
  • ✓It is the first-in-class RNA polymerase inhibitor and is not to be used as monotherapy
  • BIt is a nucleoside reverse transcriptase inhibitor used for hepatitis B
  • CIt is given as monotherapy, because it was the first regimen that does not require interferon
  • DIt is a neuraminidase inhibitor active against RNA viruses generally
Rationale

Sofosbuvir is "the first-in-class RNA polymerase inhibitor" for chronic hepatitis C and the first treatment "that can be given without interferon," but it is used with ribavirin and is "not to be used as monotherapy." Harvoni, sofosbuvir with ledipasvir, was the separate milestone: "the first treatment for hepatitis C that does not require the combined use of ribavirin," one pill once a day.

Watch for

(C) is the reflex pick because "without interferon" and "as monotherapy" sound like the same claim, and they are two different claims about two different drugs. (B) is the mirror error of the previous item, sorting a hepatitis C drug into the hepatitis B group; the cue is that hepatitis B gets entecavir, lamivudine, and tenofovir while hepatitis C gets the -buvir, -asvir, and -previr combinations headed by sofosbuvir.

15. An infant is hospitalized with severe respiratory syncytial virus infection and ribavirin is ordered. Which route matches this indication, and what does that route change for staff?
  • AOral, and no special staff precaution applies
  • ✓Inhaled aerosol, and pregnant health care providers must stay out of the room during administration
  • CIntravenous, and the drug must be infused over 1 hour
  • DInhaled aerosol, and staff need only wear gloves
Rationale

Inhaled ribavirin by small-particle aerosol generator is for hospitalized infants with severe RSV, while the oral form is used for hepatitis C, so the route names both the disease and the population. Ribavirin carries a black box warning for significant teratogenic and embryocidal effects, and the chapter states that "pregnant health care providers, or those wishing to become pregnant, must stay out of the patient rooms when the aerosol form is being administered."

Watch for

(D) is the reflex pick because personal protective equipment is the usual answer to an exposure hazard; here the rule removes the at-risk staff member from the room instead. The same exposure logic extends to the patient's household, since ribavirin is contraindicated in pregnant women and their male sexual partners, may be present in the body for up to 6 months after exposure, and requires at least two reliable forms of contraception during treatment and for 6 months after.

antiretroviral classes and mechanismsthe three viral enzymes and the class suffixes
16. A patient's regimen includes raltegravir. Which viral enzyme does this drug inhibit, and what does that enzyme normally do?
  • AProtease, which chemically separates new viral RNA from viral protein molecules
  • ✓Integrase, which splices the newly made viral DNA into the host cell DNA to form a provirus
  • CReverse transcriptase, which builds DNA from the viral RNA genome
  • DNeuraminidase, which lets budding virions escape the infected cell
Rationale

Integrase strand transfer inhibitors work by "inhibiting the catalytic activity of the enzyme integrase," and integrase "promotes the integration of this viral DNA into the host cell DNA. This hybrid DNA complex is known as a provirus." The chapter says the naming is deliberate: "the name of each class... provides a reminder of its role," and the suffix -tegravir marks this class, as in raltegravir, dolutegravir, and cabotegravir.

Watch for

(C) is the reflex pick because reverse transcriptase is the HIV enzyme students meet first and it acts immediately before integrase in the replication sequence, so the wrong step feels like the right one. (D) names an influenza enzyme rather than an HIV one.

antiretroviral classes and mechanismsthe three viral enzymes and the class suffixes
17. A different patient is taking indinavir. Which enzyme does this drug inhibit?
  • AIntegrase, and the suffix -navir marks that class
  • ✓Protease, which chemically separates the new viral RNA from viral protein molecules
  • CReverse transcriptase, an RNA-directed DNA polymerase
  • DProtease, which splices the viral DNA into the host chromosome
Rationale

Protease inhibitors work by "inhibiting the protease retroviral enzyme," and protease "serves to chemically separate the new viral RNA from viral protein molecules"; the key-terms box adds that a protease cleaves the peptide bonds linking amino acids. The suffix -navir marks this class, as -tegravir marks the integrase inhibitors.

Watch for

(A) is the mirror error of the previous item, attaching the -navir suffix to the integrase class. (D) is the half-right pick that catches more students: it names the correct class but gives integrase's job, so the enzyme name alone is not enough to answer on.

antiretroviral classes and mechanismsreverse transcriptase inhibitors and the entry blockers
18. A regimen contains both zidovudine and nevirapine, and the patient asks why two drugs that hit the same enzyme are both needed. What distinguishes an NRTI from an NNRTI?
  • ANRTIs block reverse transcriptase and NNRTIs block integrase
  • ✓Both block reverse transcriptase; the NRTIs are nucleoside or nucleotide analogues and the NNRTIs are not
  • CBoth block protease; the NRTIs are analogues and the NNRTIs are not
  • DNRTIs are used only in pregnancy and NNRTIs only in treatment-experienced patients
Rationale

Both classes block reverse transcriptase, so membership is the reliable discriminator: zidovudine, abacavir, didanosine, emtricitabine, lamivudine, stavudine, and the tenofovirs are the NRTIs, while nevirapine, delavirdine, doravirine, efavirenz, etravirine, and rilpivirine are the NNRTIs. The chapter also notes a clinical use for the distinction, that switching a patient from a protease inhibitor to an NNRTI may help reduce lipodystrophy.

Watch for

(A) is the reflex pick because the extra N looks as though it must signal a different target; it signals a different chemical structure instead, since the two classes are one letter apart, hit the same enzyme, and appear in the same HAART recipes.

antiretroviral classes and mechanismsreverse transcriptase inhibitors and the entry blockers
19. A treatment-experienced patient with HIV is being taught to give an antiretroviral by subcutaneous injection. Which drug and class is this?
  • ✓Enfuvirtide, the only fusion inhibitor and the only injected antiretroviral named in this chapter
  • BMaraviroc, the only entry inhibitor, given subcutaneously
  • CRaltegravir, an integrase inhibitor available only by injection
  • DTenofovir, a nucleotide analogue given subcutaneously
Rationale

Enfuvirtide works by "inhibiting viral fusion" and is the only drug in its class as well as the only subcutaneously injected antiretroviral the chapter names. Fusion is defined as "the process by which viruses attach themselves to, or fuse with, the cell membranes of host cells."

Watch for

(B) is the reflex pick because enfuvirtide and maraviroc are twinned in every other respect: both block HIV before it gets inside, both are the sole member of their class, and both are indicated for treatment-experienced patients. Maraviroc is oral, so route is what separates them.

20. A patient is starting maraviroc and the pharmacy sends an FDA-approved medication guide along with it. Which description of this drug is accurate?
  • AIt is a fusion inhibitor that binds the viral envelope before attachment
  • ✓It is an entry inhibitor and CCR5 antagonist that binds the type 5 chemokine coreceptors on the CD4 cells, and it carries a risk of hepatotoxicity with allergic-type features
  • CIt is an integrase inhibitor, and it does not interact with CYP3A4 inducers or inhibitors
  • DIt is a protease inhibitor associated with nephrolithiasis
Rationale

Maraviroc works by "selectively and reversibly binding to the type 5 chemokine coreceptors located on the CD4 cells," is the only drug in its class, requires an FDA-approved medication guide before dispensing, and carries "hepatotoxicity with allergic-type features."

Watch for

(A) is the mirror error of the previous item, since fusion inhibition is enfuvirtide's mechanism and maraviroc binds the host cell's coreceptor rather than the virus. (C) states raltegravir's CYP3A4 exemption and (D) states indinavir's signature toxicity, both real facts moved onto the wrong drug.

hiv monitoring and prophylaxis regimensthe two numbers
21. A patient with HIV has a CD4 count that has fallen to 180 cells/mm3. What does this number most directly signal?
  • AThe viral load is now undetectable
  • ✓This is the level at which prophylaxis for opportunistic infections is most frequently given
  • CThe patient has reached the primary goal of antiretroviral therapy
  • DThe patient no longer requires antiretroviral therapy
Rationale

A CD4 count below 200 cells/mm3 is the point at which prophylaxis for opportunistic infections "is most frequently given." Opportunistic infections are defined by the host rather than the organism: infections caused by "any type of microorganism that occur in an immunocompromised host but normally would not occur in an immunocompetent host."

Watch for

(C) is the reflex pick because CD4 count and viral load are both numbers a patient tracks and the two merge in memory. The cue is that CD4 200 is a floor you fall below and act on, while viral load below 50 copies/mL is a target you drive down to.

22. The same patient's viral load report reads fewer than 50 copies/mL. How should the nurse interpret this result?
  • ✓It represents an undetectable viral load and a primary goal of antiretroviral therapy
  • BIt is the threshold for beginning prophylaxis against opportunistic infections
  • CIt is evidence of drug resistance
  • DIt means the virus has been eliminated from the body
Rationale

A viral load below 50 copies/mL is "an undetectable" viral load and "a primary goal of antiretroviral therapy." Evidence of drug resistance is a different pattern entirely, a falling CD4 count and a rising viral load moving in opposite directions at the same time.

Watch for

(B) is the mirror error of the previous item, swapping the two numbers so that the therapy target becomes the prophylaxis trigger. (D) is the eradication distractor the chapter plants throughout, since these drugs suppress but do not cure the infection and undetectable is not the same as eliminated.

23. A nurse sustains a needlestick from a device used on a patient with HIV. Which regimen does the chapter give for occupational exposure prophylaxis?

  • ✓Raltegravir plus emtricitabine and tenofovir, started after the exposure
  • CAcyclovir, started within 72 hours
  • DDidanosine alone

24. The HIV-negative partner of a patient with HIV asks about a daily pill that would keep them from becoming infected. What should the nurse describe?
  • ARaltegravir plus Truvada, taken daily for life
  • ✓Preexposure prophylaxis with tenofovir and emtricitabine, up to 90% effective if it is used as prescribed
  • CAn HIV vaccine, now available to high-risk partners
  • DZidovudine monotherapy, begun after any exposure
Rationale

PrEP is tenofovir and emtricitabine taken daily before exposure by people at "very high risk," and it is "up to 90%" effective "if it is used as prescribed."

Watch for

(A) is the mirror error of the previous item, applying the postexposure regimen to a preexposure situation because Truvada sits inside both. (C) is wrong for a mechanistic reason worth holding onto: reverse transcriptase "has a high rate of errors," which allows frequent genetic mutations and hampers the development of an effective vaccine.

25. A patient asks why HIV is treated with a combination of at least three drugs rather than with one. Which statements support the use of combination therapy? Select all that apply.
Select all that apply · scored all or nothing

  • ✓The combination reduces viral load, which is a primary goal of therapy
  • CThe combination carries fewer toxicities than single-drug therapy
  • DThe combination reduces T-cell counts
  • EThe combination increases the incidence of opportunistic infections

HAART uses "combinations of antiretroviral drugs" that work by different mechanisms, "reducing the likelihood of viral resistance," and driving the viral load below 50 copies/mL is a primary goal of therapy. 26. A heart transplant recipient taking cyclosporine develops a CMV infection. Why does the chapter prefer ganciclovir over foscarnet for this patient?
  • ✓Cyclosporine is nephrotoxic, and the dose-limiting toxicity of foscarnet and cidofovir is renal toxicity
  • BCyclosporine suppresses the bone marrow, and ganciclovir spares the marrow
  • CFoscarnet has no activity against CMV
  • DGanciclovir is the only one of the two available in an oral form
Rationale

The chapter reasons it out as a decision: cyclosporine "is nephrotoxic. Therefore using foscarnet in this patient may be more dangerous than using ganciclovir." Ganciclovir's dose-limiting toxicity is bone marrow suppression, while "that of foscarnet and cidofovir is renal toxicity."

Watch for

(B) inverts the toxicity assignment, since marrow suppression is ganciclovir's own dose-limiting problem and cannot be the reason to choose it. The cue that survives the exam is to match the drug to the organ the patient has not already compromised.

27. A patient immunocompromised after a bone marrow transplant develops a CMV infection. Which drug does the chapter suggest may be the better choice here, and why?
  • AGanciclovir, because its dose-limiting toxicity is renal
  • ✓Foscarnet, because ganciclovir's dose-limiting toxicity is bone marrow suppression
  • CAcyclovir, because it spares both the marrow and the kidney
  • DZidovudine, because it treats CMV in immunocompromised hosts
Rationale

The chapter states that "a patient... immunocompromised because of a bone marrow transplant might be better treated using foscarnet," because ganciclovir's dose-limiting toxicity is bone marrow suppression and this patient's marrow is already the compromised organ.

(A) is the mirror error of the previous item, swapping the two organ toxicities so the same drug is chosen for the opposite reason. (D) is wrong twice over: zidovudine is an HIV drug, and its major dose-limiting adverse effect is also bone marrow suppression, which is the reason a patient is often switched off it.

29. A patient starting acyclovir for recurrent genital herpes says, "So this will get rid of the virus for good." Which response is accurate?
  • A"Yes, a full course will eradicate the virus."
  • ✓"No. These drugs suppress the infection but do not cure it. What you should see is that lesions crust over and recurrences become less frequent."
  • C"Yes, as long as your partner is treated at the same time."
  • D"No, but taking it every day will keep you from passing the virus to a partner."
Rationale

The patient teaching section states that these drugs "suppress but do not cure the viral infection," and what the patient can realistically expect is that "herpetic lesions will crust over, and the frequency of recurrence will decrease."

(A) is the eradication distractor the chapter plants in more than one review question, once worded as "these drugs will eventually eliminate the virus from my body," and it pulls because a completed antibiotic course really does clear a bacterial infection. 30. A patient taking nevirapine calls to report a rash that now includes blistering, oral lesions, fever, and myalgias. What is the priority nursing response?
  • AReassure the patient that rash is a listed adverse effect of nevirapine and continue the drug
  • ✓Tell the patient to contact the prescriber immediately or seek prompt emergency medical attention
  • CAdvise an antihistamine and reassess at the next scheduled visit
  • DHold the next dose and call back in 24 to 48 hours
Rationale

Rash is a listed adverse effect of nevirapine along with fever, nausea, headache, and abnormal liver function test results, but the chapter directs that blistering, fever, malaise, myalgias, oral lesions, swelling or edema, or conjunctivitis calls for contacting the prescriber immediately or seeking prompt emergency medical attention.

Watch for

(A) is the reflex pick because rash genuinely is on nevirapine's adverse effect list, so the drug name appears to settle the question; it is the accompanying findings that set the priority. The cue is that an isolated rash is monitored, while a rash plus mucous membrane or systemic signs is an emergency. (D) borrows the 24-to-48-hour figure from the teaching about recurrent herpes episodes, where it has nothing to do with an adverse reaction.
