sedative and hypnotic terminologydose decides the label
1. A patient receives a small dose of a CNS depressant before a painful dressing change and becomes calm and less irritable but stays awake and conversational. That evening the same drug is given at a much larger dose and the patient sleeps through the night. Which statement best explains what the nurse observed?
  • ✓The drug acted as a sedative at the lower dose and as a hypnotic at the higher dose
  • BThe drug is a hypnotic, and the smaller dose simply failed to reach the sleep-inducing threshold of its class
  • CThe patient developed tolerance between the two doses
  • DThe evening dose produced a paradoxical reaction
Rationale

Sedatives have an inhibitory effect on the CNS to the degree that they reduce nervousness, excitability, and irritability without causing sleep, whereas hypnotics cause sleep. The Overview states the rule directly: "a sedative can become a hypnotic if it is given in large enough doses," which is why a sedative-hypnotic is defined as a drug that can act in the body either as a sedative or as a hypnotic (p. 184).

Watch for

(B) treats the label as a fixed property of the molecule. The chapter says a drug is one or the other depending on the degree to which it inhibits the transmission of nerve impulses to the CNS, not because of what the package insert calls it.

sedative and hypnotic terminologydose decides the label
2. A nursing student charts that a newly ordered drug "is a sedative and therefore cannot cause sleep at any dose." Which correction should the nurse make?
  • AThe statement is correct; a sedative never induces sleep
  • ✓Sedative and hypnotic name effects at different points on a dose continuum, and a sedative given in large enough doses becomes a hypnotic
  • COnly barbiturates cross from sedation into sleep induction; benzodiazepines do not
  • DThe distinction depends on the route of administration, not the dose
Rationale

The chapter states that "any of these drugs can function along a continuum as a sedative and/or hypnotic and/or anxiolytic, depending on the dosage and patient sensitivity," and that hypnotics cause sleep and have "a much more potent effect" on the CNS (p. 184).

Watch for

(A) is the mirror error of the previous item — it freezes the label to the molecule instead of to the dose. (C) invents a class restriction; the continuum statement is made about the sedative-hypnotics generally, benzodiazepines included.

sedative and hypnotic terminologytherapeutic index and GABA
3. A patient's family asks why the prescriber chose a benzodiazepine rather than a barbiturate for short-term sedation. Which fact about the barbiturates most directly supports that choice?
  • ✓Barbiturates have a low therapeutic index, so the difference between the therapeutic and the toxic concentration is small
  • BBarbiturates have a high therapeutic index, so much larger doses are required to achieve an effect
  • CBarbiturates have no effect on GABA and are therefore less predictable
  • DBarbiturates are not controlled substances and are therefore more easily diverted
Rationale

Therapeutic index is the ratio between the toxic and therapeutic concentrations of a drug; if the index is low, the difference between the two is small and use of the drug is more hazardous (p. 183). The chapter applies this to the barbiturates by name — they "are physiologically habit forming and have a low therapeutic index" (p. 188) — and says they fell out of use because of "the favorable safety profile and proven efficacy of the benzodiazepines."

Watch for

(B) inverts the index, and the inversion is intuitive because "high" sounds like high risk. A high index means a wide, safe gap. (D) is false twice over: the barbiturates are scheduled C-II, C-III, or C-IV depending on the drug.

4. A drug ordered for a patient is described as potentiating the action of gamma-aminobutyric acid in the brain. Which effect should the nurse anticipate?
  • AIncreased CNS stimulation, because gamma-aminobutyric acid is excitatory
  • ✓Increased CNS depression, because gamma-aminobutyric acid is the primary inhibitory neurotransmitter in the brain
  • CNo CNS effect, because gamma-aminobutyric acid acts only at the neuromuscular junction
  • DAnalgesia without any sedation
Rationale

GABA is the primary inhibitory neurotransmitter found in the brain and is the key compound affected by sedative, anxiolytic, psychotropic, and muscle-relaxing medications (p. 183). Barbiturates potentiate the action of GABA (p. 189), and benzodiazepines are thought to bind GABA receptors or adjacent receptors and, like GABA, inhibit stimulation of the brain (p. 185).

Watch for

(A) inverts the neurotransmitter's role, and that single inversion unravels every mechanism statement in the chapter. GABA is inhibitory, so potentiating it deepens depression rather than producing stimulation.

sleep architecture and rem disruptionrem interference versus rem rebound
5. A 68-year-old woman has taken alprazolam almost every night for a week to help her fall asleep. She tells the nurse, "I am sleeping very well, but I'm so tired during the day. I don't understand how I can get such good sleep and still feel tired." Which explanation should the nurse give?
  • ✓The drug is reducing her cumulative REM sleep time, and REM sleep supplies part of the restfulness of sleep
  • BShe is experiencing REM rebound from the alprazolam
  • COne week is too short for the drug to have taken effect
  • DThe daytime fatigue indicates that the alprazolam dose is too low
Rationale

"Prolonged sedative-hypnotic use may reduce the cumulative amount of REM sleep; this is known as REM interference. This can result in daytime fatigue because REM sleep provides a certain component of the 'restfulness' of sleep" (p. 184). Sleep quantity is preserved while sleep quality is degraded, which is exactly what she describes.

Watch for

(B) is the reflex pick because rebound is the other REM word in the key-term box. Rebound happens after the drug is stopped; she is still taking it nightly, so the phenomenon in play is the one that happens while on the drug.

6. A patient who has taken a sedative-hypnotic nightly for a month stops it abruptly and then reports several nights of unusually frequent, vivid dreams and nightmares. Which phenomenon is this?
  • AREM interference
  • ✓REM rebound
  • CParadoxical excitement
  • DRebound insomnia
Rationale

REM rebound is excessive REM sleep following discontinuation of a sleep-altering drug (p. 183). With the barbiturates the chapter is explicit that during rebound "the proportion of REM sleep is increased and nightmares often ensue" (p. 189).

Watch for

(A) is the mirror error of the previous item. Interference happens while the patient is on the drug and yields less REM with daytime fatigue; rebound happens after stopping and yields more REM with vivid dreams. (D) is the neighboring withdrawal phenomenon, but it names too much wakefulness rather than too much dreaming.

7. A patient stops a benzodiazepine abruptly and reports that she now lies awake far longer than she ever did before she started the drug. Which phenomenon should the nurse document, and what prevents it?
  • AREM rebound, prevented by taking the drug earlier in the evening
  • ✓Rebound insomnia, prevented by a gradual weaning-off period
  • CParadoxical excitement, prevented by increasing the dose
  • DA hangover effect, prevented by reducing the evening dose
Rationale

Rebound insomnia is greater insomnia than pretreatment and occurs with abrupt discontinuation (p. 186). "Gradual weaning-off periods are recommended for benzodiazepines and all CNS depressants" (p. 194), and patient teaching instructs patients not to abruptly discontinue these drugs in order to avoid rebound insomnia.

Watch for

(A) confuses the two rebound phenomena. Both follow withdrawal, which is why the words trade places so easily, but REM rebound is too much dreaming and rebound insomnia is too much wakefulness.

benzodiazepine pharmacology and adverse effectssite of action and contraindications
10. A patient asks how her newly prescribed benzodiazepine works in the brain. Which description matches the chapter?
  • AIt depresses the brainstem reticular formation, reducing nerve impulses traveling to the cerebral cortex
  • ✓It depresses activity in the hypothalamic, thalamic, and limbic systems of the brain
  • CIt acts directly on the excitation-contraction coupling of muscle fibers
  • DIt agonizes melatonin receptors and regulates circadian rhythm
Rationale

Benzodiazepines act on the hypothalamic, thalamic, and limbic systems of the brain; their receptor sites are thought to be either GABA receptors or other adjacent receptors, and like GABA they inhibit stimulation of the brain (p. 185).

Watch for

(A) is the barbiturate mechanism — the brainstem reticular formation, with reduced nerve impulses traveling to the cerebral cortex (p. 189). Both classes are CNS depressants that involve GABA, so the anatomic sites are the only thing separating them and they trade places readily.

12. A 79-year-old is started on a benzodiazepine for sleep. Which dosing principle does the chapter attach specifically to benzodiazepines in older adults?
  • AReduce the usual adult dose by half whenever possible
  • ✓Use the lowest effective dose, because of the significant fall hazard
  • CGive the full adult dose but restrict it to alternate nights
  • DKeep the dose unchanged and double the interval between doses
Rationale

Benzodiazepines pose "a significant fall hazard in older adults, and the lowest effective dose must be used" (p. 185).

(A) is the barbiturate rule, where "the usual dose is reduced by half whenever possible" (p. 189). Two classes, two different instructions, and the half-dose figure belongs to the barbiturates only.

13. An older adult is given a benzodiazepine at bedtime to help her sleep and instead stays awake most of the night, watching television and reading in bed. How should the nurse describe this response?
  • AAn allergic reaction
  • BA teratogenic effect
  • ✓A paradoxical reaction
  • DAn idiosyncratic response to the trade formulation
Rationale

The chapter lists "paradoxical excitement or nervousness" among the commonly reported benzodiazepine adverse effects (p. 185), and repeats the pattern as "paradoxical restlessness or excitement" for barbiturates (p. 189) and "paradoxical excitement in children" (p. 193). The drug produced the opposite of its intended effect.

Watch for

(A) is the reflex pick because any unexpected drug response reads as allergy. Nothing here describes urticaria, angioedema, rash, or the other hypersensitivity findings the chapter names.

benzodiazepine pharmacology and adverse effectsadverse effects in the older adult
14. A patient started on a benzodiazepine for insomnia complains of drowsiness the morning after each dose. What is this effect, and whom does the chapter say it affects most?
  • ✓A hangover effect, more problematic in older adults and in patients with altered renal and hepatic function
  • BREM rebound, which resolves once the drug reaches steady state
  • CAn early sign of overdose requiring flumazenil
  • DRebound insomnia, which requires an increased dose
Rationale

The chapter names a "hangover" effect and gives daytime sleepiness as its example (p. 186); patient teaching adds that hangover effects are "more problematic in older adults or in patients with altered renal and hepatic function" (p. 196). It also occurs less frequently with benzodiazepines and nonbenzodiazepines than with barbiturates.

Watch for

(C) escalates a listed adverse effect into an emergency. Flumazenil is for oral overdose or excessive intravenous sedation, not for morning grogginess in a patient taking a prescribed dose.

benzodiazepine overdose and flumazenil reversalmatching the antidote to the class
15. A patient is brought to the emergency department after taking an overdose of an oral benzodiazepine. He is drowsy but responsive, and his vital signs are stable. Which intervention should the nurse anticipate?
  • AHemodialysis
  • ✓Administration of flumazenil
  • CAdministration of naloxone
  • DImmediate intubation and mechanical ventilation
Rationale

Flumazenil is the benzodiazepine antidote and can be used to acutely reverse the sedative effects of benzodiazepines; it antagonizes their action on the CNS by directly competing with the benzodiazepine for binding at the receptors, and it is used in cases of oral overdose or excessive intravenous sedation (p. 186).

Watch for

(D) tempts because respiratory failure is the feared outcome of any CNS depressant, but the chapter states that benzodiazepine overdose alone rarely results in hypotension and respiratory depression, and this patient is still responsive.

benzodiazepine overdose and flumazenil reversalmatching the antidote to the class
16. The patient in the next bed took an overdose of an opioid analgesic and is unresponsive with a respiratory rate of 6 breaths/min. Which reversal drug applies to him?
  • AFlumazenil
  • ✓Naloxone
  • CActivated charcoal
  • DProcaine
Rationale

The chapter pairs the two antidotes deliberately: flumazenil reverses the benzodiazepines by competing for benzodiazepine receptor binding, while naloxone is the opioid antagonist carried in Chapter 10. Flumazenil has no action at opioid receptors.

Watch for

(A) is the mirror error of the previous item, and it is the most common error in this chapter because both drugs are competitive antagonists given for CNS depressant overdose. Match the antidote to the drug class, not to the symptom.

benzodiazepine overdose and flumazenil reversalthe limits of reversal
17. Flumazenil is given to a patient who overdosed on a long-acting benzodiazepine, and she wakes up and answers questions appropriately. What should the nurse do next?
  • ADiscontinue monitoring, since the antagonist has reversed the drug
  • ✓Continue close monitoring, because flumazenil may wear off before the benzodiazepine does and the patient may become sedated again
  • CGive naloxone as well, to cover the possibility of a co-ingestion
  • DWithhold any further flumazenil permanently, since the maximum dose has been reached
Rationale

The note to Table 12.3 states that flumazenil has a relatively short half-life and a duration of effect of 1 to 4 hours, so if it is used to reverse a long-acting benzodiazepine, "the dose of the reversal drug may wear off and the patient may become sedated again, requiring more flumazenil."

Watch for

(A) treats a successful reversal as the end of the intervention, which is exactly what the table's own note defeats. The antidote is shorter acting than the drug it is reversing.

benzodiazepine overdose and flumazenil reversalthe limits of reversal
18. Flumazenil is ordered for two purposes on the same unit — reversal of moderate sedation in one patient and suspected benzodiazepine overdose in another. Which statement about the maximum cumulative doses in Table 12.3 is correct?
  • ABoth regimens stop at a total of 1 mg
  • ✓Sedation reversal stops at 1 mg, and suspected overdose may go up to 3 mg
  • CSedation reversal stops at 3 mg, and suspected overdose stops at 1 mg
  • DNeither regimen has a stated ceiling
Rationale

Table 12.3 gives 0.2 mg intravenously over 15 seconds for reversal of moderate sedation or general anesthesia, repeated at 60-second intervals up to four additional times, to a maximum total dose of 1 mg. For suspected benzodiazepine overdose the regimen starts at 0.2 mg over 30 seconds and escalates through 0.3 mg and then 0.5 mg doses at 1-minute intervals to a cumulative dose of 3 mg.

Watch for

(C) swaps the two ceilings, and the swap is easy because both regimens open with the same 0.2 mg dose. The difference between the 1 mg and the 3 mg ceiling is the cleanest discriminator between the two indications.

sedative-hypnotic interactions and black box warningsenzyme inhibition versus enzyme induction
19. A patient taking a benzodiazepine mentions that she drinks a large glass of grapefruit juice every morning. What should the nurse anticipate?
  • ✓Decreased benzodiazepine metabolism, with prolonged effect and possible toxicity
  • BIncreased benzodiazepine metabolism, with loss of therapeutic effect
  • CNo interaction, since grapefruit affects only the oral anticoagulants
  • DAn additive CNS depressant effect contributed by the juice itself
Rationale

Grapefruit and grapefruit juice alter drug metabolism by inhibition of the cytochrome P-450 system and can result in prolonged effect, increased effect, and toxicity (p. 186). Table 12.4 groups grapefruit juice with the azole antifungals, verapamil, diltiazem, protease inhibitors, and macrolide antibiotics under decreased benzodiazepine metabolism and prolonged benzodiazepine action.

Watch for

(B) runs the interaction backwards. Inhibition slows metabolism, so more drug stays in the body longer; only induction produces the loss of effect described there.

(A) is the mirror error of the previous item — it applies the grapefruit and azole direction to an inducer. Inducers speed metabolism and cause treatment failure; inhibitors slow it and cause toxicity.

sedative-hypnotic interactions and black box warningsthe three black box warnings
22. A prescriber orders an opioid analgesic and a benzodiazepine together for a postoperative patient. Which statement reflects the 2016 FDA black box warning?
  • AThe combination is absolutely prohibited in every patient
  • ✓The combination should be used only if no other alternatives are available, because of extreme sleepiness, respiratory depression, coma, and death
  • CThe warning applies only when the opioid is given intravenously
  • DThe warning concerns complex sleep behaviors such as sleepwalking and sleep-driving
Rationale

The 2016 warning covers all opioids and all benzodiazepines regarding the risk of combined use, and states that they should be used "only if no other alternatives are available," with risks including extreme sleepiness, respiratory depression, coma, and death (p. 186). The assessment section operationalizes it by watching for severe decreases in blood pressure, respiratory rate, reflexes, and level of consciousness.

Watch for

(D) is the 2019 warning, which belongs to the nonbenzodiazepines. It is the most memorable of the three, so it gets attached to the wrong box; three warnings, three years, three different scopes.

23. A patient asks which drugs carry the FDA warning about complex sleep behaviors, including sleepwalking, sleep-driving, and engaging in other activities while not fully awake. Which answer is correct?
  • AThe entire benzodiazepine class
  • ✓The nonbenzodiazepines zolpidem, eszopiclone, and zaleplon
  • CAll barbiturates
  • DThe orexin receptor antagonists
Rationale

The 2019 warning names complex sleep behaviors for the nonbenzodiazepines zolpidem, eszopiclone, and zaleplon (p. 184). The late-2020 warning is the one covering the entire benzodiazepine class, and it concerns the risk for abuse, misuse, addiction, physical dependence, and withdrawal.

(A) crosses the years. It is the correct scope for the 2020 dependence warning and the wrong scope for the 2019 sleep-behavior warning, and the two collapse together because they were issued about a year apart.

  • ✓Zolpidem and eszopiclone are classified as miscellaneous hypnotic drugs that share many characteristics with the benzodiazepines but are chemically distinct from them

25. A woman is discharged after a hysterectomy with a new prescription for zolpidem 10 mg at bedtime. What is the nurse's most appropriate action?
  • AReview the adverse effects with her and reinforce the bedtime timing
  • BSuggest a glass of wine at bedtime if the drug alone is not effective
  • ✓Contact the prescriber to question the dose
  • DHelp her locate a pharmacy that stocks the drug

The FDA now recommends doses of 5 mg as a maximum dose for women, or 5 to 10 mg for men, because of the risk for next-morning impairment (p. 188). A 10 mg dose for a female patient is twice the recommended maximum.

(A) is the safe-looking choice, and it is wrong precisely because teaching about a dose the FDA capped at half leaves the hazard in place. (B) fails on a separate rule: alcohol is an additive CNS depressant, and Box 12.1 says it fragments sleep rather than producing it.

26. A patient with a history of alcohol and sedative misuse needs help falling asleep. Which drug in this chapter is described as an option for patients likely to abuse CNS depressants, and why?
  • ATemazepam, because it is intermediate acting and therefore less reinforcing
  • ✓Ramelteon, because it is not a controlled substance, is technically not a CNS depressant, and does not lead to withdrawal symptoms
  • CSuvorexant, because the orexin receptor antagonists are not scheduled
  • DPhenobarbital, because its long half-life smooths withdrawal
Rationale

Ramelteon is structurally similar to melatonin and works as an agonist at melatonin receptors in the CNS; it is "technically not a CNS depressant" and is "not classified as a controlled substance because of its lack of observed dependency risk" (p. 188). The nursing sections add that it does not carry the potential for abuse or dependence and does not lead to withdrawal symptoms, so it may be an option for patients likely to abuse CNS depressants.

Watch for

(C) is the closest wrong answer, because suvorexant is also a newer non-benzodiazepine hypnotic with an unfamiliar mechanism. Suvorexant is a schedule IV drug; ramelteon is the only unscheduled hypnotic in this chapter.

nonbenzodiazepine and novel-mechanism hypnoticsmelatonin agonists and orexin antagonists
27. Suvorexant is ordered for a patient with insomnia. Which explanation of its mechanism is accurate?
  • ✓It blocks orexin receptors, opposing hypothalamic neuropeptides that promote wakefulness
  • BIt agonizes melatonin receptors and resets the circadian rhythm
  • CIt potentiates GABA at the brainstem reticular formation
  • DIt decreases calcium release from the sarcoplasmic reticulum of muscle fibers
Rationale

Suvorexant is the first drug in a new class called the orexin receptor antagonists. Orexin A and orexin B are hypothalamic neuropeptides that play a key role in promoting wakefulness and regulating the sleep-wake cycle, and orexin antagonists compete with the physiologic effects of orexin (p. 188).

Watch for

(B) is the ramelteon mechanism, and the two are the chapter's easiest swap because both are newer hypnotics that do not work through GABA. Suvorexant blocks a wake-promoting peptide; ramelteon agonizes a sleep-promoting receptor.

29. A patient asks how phenobarbital differs from a benzodiazepine in where it acts. Which description fits the barbiturates?
  • ✓They act primarily on the brainstem in an area called the reticular formation, reducing nerve impulses traveling to the cerebral cortex
  • BThey act on the hypothalamic, thalamic, and limbic systems
  • CThey act at the neuromuscular junction

Barbiturates are CNS depressants that act primarily on the brainstem in an area called the reticular formation, and they work by reducing the nerve impulses traveling to the cerebral cortex; they also potentiate the action of the inhibitory neurotransmitter GABA and raise the seizure threshold (p. 189).

(B) is the benzodiazepine site. Both classes are CNS depressants that involve GABA, so the anatomic site is the only thing separating them and it is the detail most often traded between them.

30. A patient is scheduled for a short surgical procedure requiring anesthesia induction, and the neurosurgical team also wants intracranial pressure reduced. Which barbiturate duration group does Table 12.5 assign these purposes to?
  • ✓Ultrashort acting, such as methohexital and thiopental
  • BShort acting, such as pentobarbital and secobarbital
  • CIntermediate acting, such as butabarbital
  • DLong acting, such as phenobarbital and mephobarbital
Rationale

The ultrashort-acting barbiturates methohexital and thiopental are used for anesthesia for short surgical procedures, anesthesia induction, control of convulsions, and reduction of intracranial pressure in neurosurgical patients (p. 189). The long-acting group is used for epileptic seizure prophylaxis, and the short- and intermediate-acting groups carry the identical printed indication of sedation and control of convulsive conditions.

Watch for

(D) picks the group whose drug names are most familiar, since phenobarbital is the prototype. Because the short and intermediate groups share word-for-word identical indications, any item separating the four tiers must be answered on the drug names rather than on the stated purpose.

32. A woman taking oral contraceptives is started on phenobarbital. What should the nurse teach her?
  • AThe contraceptive will be more effective, so its dose may be reduced
  • ✓Phenobarbital accelerates the metabolism of the contraceptive, so she should use an additional method of contraception as a backup
  • CPhenobarbital has no meaningful effect on oral contraceptives
  • DShe should stop the contraceptive entirely, since the combination causes clot formation
Rationale

Barbiturates as a class are notorious enzyme inducers: they increase the activity of hepatic microsomal or cytochrome P-450 enzymes, causing many drugs to be metabolized more quickly, which usually shortens their duration of action (pp. 189 and 190). With oral contraceptives the printed result is accelerated metabolism of the contraceptive drug and possible unintended pregnancy, and the chapter advises an additional method of contraception as a backup.

Watch for

(D) borrows the clot-formation outcome, which belongs to the anticoagulant row, and attaches it to the wrong drug. Induction lowers the other drug's level, so the victim drug fails at its own job: the anticoagulant stops preventing clots and the contraceptive stops preventing pregnancy.
