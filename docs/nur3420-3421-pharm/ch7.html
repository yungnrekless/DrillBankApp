1. A patient empties a bag onto the bedside table during a medication history: acetaminophen (Tylenol), warfarin (Coumadin), ginkgo biloba, morphine sulfate, and diphenhydramine (Benadryl). Which of these are legend drugs? Select all that apply.
Select all that apply · scored all or nothing
  • Aacetaminophen (Tylenol)
  • ✓warfarin (Coumadin)
  • Cginkgo biloba
  • ✓morphine sulfate
  • Ediphenhydramine (Benadryl)
Rationale

Legend drugs are "medications that are not legally available without a prescription from a prescriber; also called prescription drugs." Acetaminophen and diphenhydramine both appear in Table 7.1 as OTC products, and ginkgo biloba is a dietary supplement — a third legal category that is not a drug at all.

Watch for

(C) pulls because an herb is the least ordinary item in the bag and feels like it needs special classification, but "legend" means prescription-only and has nothing to do with folklore or herbal tradition; ginkgo is sold freely under DSHEA. (A) and (E) pull because both are real drugs with real toxicity, and students slide from how dangerous a product is to how it is legally obtained.

2. A patient tells the nurse, "The pharmacist keeps my pseudoephedrine (Sudafed) behind the counter now, so it must have become a prescription drug." Which response is accurate?
  • ACorrect — it was reclassified as a legend drug because of its abuse potential
  • ✓It is still an OTC drug; it is kept behind the counter because it can be used to manufacture methamphetamine
  • CIt is now classified as a dietary supplement, so DSHEA governs where it is shelved
  • DIt became prescription-only in 2009, when the FDA began requiring special labeling
Rationale

Table 7.1 still lists pseudoephedrine (Sudafed) among the OTC antihistamines and decongestants. The chapter explains that pseudoephedrine is "used to manufacture the widely abused street drug methamphetamine" and that "because of the potential for abuse, products containing pseudoephedrine must be sold from behind the pharmacy counter."

Watch for

(A) confuses where a drug is stored with what it legally is; counter placement is an abuse-diversion control, not reclassification to legend status. (D) borrows a real 2009 event — the FDA labeling requirement for acetaminophen, aspirin, and NSAIDs — and attaches it to the wrong drug.

3. A patient receiving scheduled chemotherapy also attends weekly acupuncture and massage sessions and intends to complete the full chemotherapy course. Which term names what this patient is doing?
  • AAlternative medicine
  • ✓Complementary medicine
  • CConventional medicine
  • DIatrogenic therapy
Rationale

Complementary medicine is "alternative medicine when used simultaneously with, rather than instead of, standard Western medicine," and the chapter adds that this practice "is also referred to as integrative medicine."

Watch for

(A) is the reflex pick because acupuncture and massage are themselves alternative therapies, and the option matches the therapy rather than the pattern. The axis this term names is not what the therapy is but what it is used with, and this patient is still receiving chemotherapy.

4. A second patient declines the recommended chemotherapy entirely and treats the diagnosis with herbal preparations alone. Which term names what this patient is doing?
  • AComplementary medicine
  • BIntegrative medicine
  • ✓Alternative medicine
  • DConventional medicine
Rationale

Alternative medicine is "herbal medicine, chiropractic, acupuncture, massage, reflexology, and any other therapies traditionally not emphasized in Western medical schools." It becomes complementary only when used simultaneously with, rather than instead of, standard Western medicine.

Watch for

(A) is the mirror error of the previous item — this patient stopped the conventional therapy, so nothing is being complemented. (B) fails a second way: integrative is simply the chapter's other name for complementary, so whenever both appear as options the discriminator lies elsewhere in the stem.

5. A patient holding a ginkgo capsule asks the nurse what part of it actually produces an effect in the body. Which answer is accurate?
  • AThe herbs — the bark, root, leaf, seed, flower, and fruit components of the plant
  • ✓The phytochemicals — the pharmacologically active ingredients in the remedy
  • CThe herbal medicine — the practice of using herbs to heal
  • DThe standardized legend ingredients the FDA verified before marketing
Rationale

Phytochemicals are "the pharmacologically active ingredients in herbal remedies," and the chapter describes herbal products as containing "a wide variety of active phytochemicals (plant compounds)." Herbs are the plant parts themselves, and herbal medicine is the practice of using them.

Watch for

(A) names the level immediately below the answer and pulls hard because the capsule literally contains plant material; the stem asks what produces the effect, not what the capsule is made of. (C) swaps a practice for a substance, which is the same nesting error one step further out.

6. A nursing student asks why interest in "natural health" surged in the United States beginning in the 1960s. Which answer reflects the chapter?
  • AClinical trials had begun to show that herbs outperform conventional drugs
  • ✓Concern over the iatrogenic effects of conventional medicine, together with a desire for more self-reliance
  • CThe FDA had begun approving herbal products as drugs, which reassured consumers
  • DHerbal products had become substantially cheaper than prescription drugs
Rationale

The chapter states that "in the 1960s, concerns were expressed over the iatrogenic effects of conventional medicine. These concerns, along with a desire for more self-reliance, led to a renewed interest in 'natural health.'" Iatrogenic effects are "unintentional adverse effects that are caused by the actions of a prescriber or other health care professional, or by a specific treatment."

Watch for

(A) pulls because evidence of benefit feels like the natural driver of a health trend, but the chapter attributes the shift to distrust of conventional care — and it says elsewhere that "there are few published scientific data regarding the safety of dietary supplements." (C) inverts the regulatory picture entirely.

7. A patient asks what the FDA actually requires of the company that makes her supplement. Which requirements currently apply to dietary supplement manufacturers? Select all that apply.
Select all that apply · scored all or nothing
  • AComply with quality-control standards set by the DSHEA of 1994
  • BProve the product's efficacy before it is sold
  • ✓Identify the active ingredients on the label
  • DObtain FDA approval before the product is marketed
  • ✓Demonstrate that the product is free from contaminants such as microbes, pesticides, and heavy metals
Rationale

"The DSHEA requires no proof of efficacy and sets no standards for quality control for supplements," and "dietary supplements do not need approval from the FDA before they are marketed." Since June 2007, however, manufacturers "must provide data that demonstrate product identity, composition, quality, purity, and strength of active ingredients" and "must also demonstrate that products are free from contaminants such as microbes, pesticides, and heavy metals."

Watch for

(A), (B), and (D) are each a genuine FDA requirement moved to the wrong category — all three apply to legend drugs, for which "the FDA has specific and stringent requirements." (D) is the strongest pull, because a product sitting on a pharmacy shelf looks approved.

8. A patient states flatly that the FDA has no authority whatsoever over herbal products. Which response is accurate?
  • AThat is correct; when DSHEA passed in 1994 the FDA lost all jurisdiction over supplements
  • ✓The FDA banned ephedra, posts warnings on its website, and has set manufacturing standards since 2007, but it does not approve supplements before they are marketed
  • CThe FDA approves each supplement for safety, though not for effectiveness
  • DThe FDA reviews every supplement label for accuracy before the product reaches the shelf
Rationale

The chapter's operative rule is that these products "are not regulated by the FDA unless there is sufficient data to support a recall of the product. Companies are not required to provide evidence of safety and/or effectiveness." The FDA nonetheless banned ephedra in April 2004, sent a consumer-warning letter about kava in 2002, "posts warnings on herbal products on its website," and imposed good manufacturing practice requirements in June 2007.

Watch for

(A) overshoots in one direction while (C) and (D) undershoot in the other, and the chapter's position is the modest middle one. (C) pulls hardest because "approved for safety but not effectiveness" sounds like a sensible regulatory compromise and describes no real category here.

9. A patient asks how dietary supplements came to have a law of their own. Which sequence is accurate?
  • ✓The FDA threatened to remove supplements from the market in 1993, the public mounted a letter-writing campaign, and the 103rd Congress passed the DSHEA in 1994
  • BCongress passed the DSHEA in 1972 as part of the FDA's OTC Drug Review
  • CThe German Commission E monographs of 1978 were adopted into United States law as the DSHEA
  • DThe US Pharmacopeia drafted the DSHEA in 2002 as part of its verification program
Rationale

"In 1993, the FDA threatened to remove dietary supplements from the market. The American public reacted with a massive letter-writing campaign to Congress, and the 103rd Congress responded by passing the Dietary Supplement and Health Education Act (DSHEA) of 1994." Before that, "neither federal legislation nor the FDA provided any safeguards," and "manufacturers were responsible only for ensuring product safety."

Watch for

(B) moves the 1972 date, which belongs to the FDA's OTC Drug Review, onto the supplement law. (C) and (D) move the act to the wrong actor entirely: the 1978 Commission E monographs are German, and the 2002 US Pharmacopeia program is independent certification, not legislation.

10. A patient wants some assurance that a supplement bottle actually contains what its label claims. Which resource does the chapter offer?
  • AThe FDA preapproval seal, required on every supplement since 1994
  • ✓The US Pharmacopeia's Dietary Supplement Verification Program, which certifies products it has independently tested
  • CThe Commission E monographs, which certify United States supplement manufacturers
  • DThe DSHEA registry of verified supplement ingredients
Rationale

In 2002 the US Pharmacopeia, "an independent organization that is the government's official standard-setting authority for dietary supplements, began certifying products that it had independently tested as part of its Dietary Supplement Verification Program."

Watch for

(C) pulls because Commission E is the one herbal-quality body most students can name, but it is the German equivalent of the FDA, and its 1978 monographs "focus on herbs whose effectiveness for specific indications is supported by the research literature" rather than certifying United States products. (A) describes a seal that does not exist.

11. A supplement label reads "supports joint comfort," and the patient asks whether the manufacturer is allowed to print that. Which statement is accurate?
  • ✓Manufacturers may claim an effect but cannot promise a specific cure on the product label
  • BManufacturers may make any claim, including a cure, because DSHEA removed all labeling limits
  • CManufacturers may print only the ingredient list; any statement of effect is prohibited
  • DManufacturers may promise a cure if the ingredient appears in the Commission E monographs
Rationale

Dietary supplement manufacturers "may currently claim an effect but cannot promise a specific cure on the product label." The chapter adds that "herbal products are not FDA-approved drugs, and therefore their labeling cannot be relied on to provide consumers and patients with adequate instructions for use or even information about warnings."

Watch for

(B) and (C) are the two intuitive extremes — total freedom or total silence — and the real rule sits between them. (B) pulls hardest for a student who has learned "supplements are unregulated" and generalizes that into a permission the law does not actually grant.

Box 7.1 groups its criteria under three headings — indication for use, safety profile, and practicality for over-the-counter use. 13. A drug under review for OTC status has a wide gap between the dose that produces toxicity and the dose that produces benefit. How does that bear on its candidacy?
  • ✓It favors reclassification, because Box 7.1 requires a high therapeutic index
  • BIt argues against reclassification, because a wide gap makes the dose hard to titrate
  • CIt is irrelevant, because therapeutic index applies only to intravenous drugs
  • DIt favors reclassification only if the drug also requires blood level monitoring
Rationale

Box 7.1 lists "high therapeutic index" under safety profile, and the box's own footnote defines the index as the "ratio of toxic to therapeutic dosage." A high ratio means the toxic dose sits far above the therapeutic dose, which is the wide safety margin a self-treating consumer needs.

Watch for

(B) inverts the direction, and it is the common inversion because "narrow" sounds precise and precision sounds safe. (D) pulls because monitoring sounds like extra protection, but a drug needing serum levels drawn fails the box's "easy to monitor" practicality criterion and stays prescription-only.

14. A manufacturer proposes OTC status for a drug whose toxic concentrations lie just above therapeutic ones, so that periodic serum levels are drawn. Which response is accurate?
  • AIt qualifies, because the monitoring requirement proves the drug has been well studied
  • BIt qualifies, because a narrow therapeutic index makes a drug safer for self-treatment
  • ✓It does not qualify, because Box 7.1 requires a high therapeutic index and a drug that is easy to monitor
  • DIt does not qualify, because Box 7.1 bars reclassification of any drug that ever required a prescription
Rationale

Box 7.1's safety profile requires a "high therapeutic index," the ratio of toxic to therapeutic dosage, and its practicality heading requires drugs that are "easy to use" and "easy to monitor." A narrow margin requiring serum levels fails both headings at once.

Watch for

(B) is the mirror error of the previous item, reading a narrow margin as tight control rather than as no room for error. (D) overshoots in the opposite direction: "more than 40 medications that were previously available by prescription only have been reclassified to OTC status," so prior prescription status is the starting point of the process rather than a disqualifier.

15. A nurse is asked what evidence the FDA relies on when deciding whether to reclassify a drug to OTC status. Which answer is accurate?
  • ✓Clinical trials and postmarketing safety surveillance data submitted by the manufacturer
  • BConsumer satisfaction surveys collected by retail pharmacies
  • CThe Commission E monographs
  • DThe volume of the drug sold during the preceding year
Rationale

Reclassification data come "from clinical trials and postmarketing safety surveillance data, which are submitted to the FDA by the manufacturer." The procedure has been "criticized as overly time-consuming," but it is structured to ensure that reclassified products are "safe and effective when used by the average consumer."

Watch for

(B) pulls because every Box 7.1 criterion is written from the consumer's side of the counter, which makes a consumer survey sound on-topic. The box asks whether the average consumer can diagnose, monitor, and safely use the drug — not whether consumers were pleased with it.

17. A patient with chronic back pain reports taking two 325-mg acetaminophen tablets eight times a day and adds, "It's only Tylenol." What is the nurse's concern?
  • ✓The daily total is 5.2 g, above the 3 to 4 g/day ceiling, and excessive doses cause liver toxicity
  • BThe daily total is 2.6 g, which is within limits, so no concern exists
  • CThe daily total exceeds the ceiling, and the resulting risk is gastrointestinal ulceration
  • DThe dose is safe, because a drug sold OTC cannot reach a toxic amount
Rationale

Two 325-mg tablets is 650 mg per dose, and eight doses is 5200 mg, or 5.2 g/day. The chapter states that acetaminophen doses are "not to exceed a total of 3 to 4 g/day" and that "liver toxicity is associated with excessive doses ... and is a leading cause of liver failure."

Watch for

(C) gets the arithmetic right and the organ wrong; gastrointestinal ulceration belongs to the NSAIDs, and the chapter never assigns it to acetaminophen. (D) is the chapter's central fallacy written as a distractor: "patients often assume that if a drug is sold OTC, it is completely safe ... This is not true."

  • ✓Gastrointestinal ulceration, kidney dysfunction, myocardial infarction, and stroke
  • CReye syndrome
  • DRebound congestion and dependency
Rationale

The chapter associates NSAIDs with "gastrointestinal ulceration, kidney dysfunction, myocardial infarction, and stroke." In 2009 the FDA began requiring specific labeling for acetaminophen, aspirin, and NSAIDs, which is the regulatory response to this cluster of risks.

(A) is the mirror error of the previous item — liver failure is the acetaminophen profile, and the chapter never assigns it to NSAIDs. (C) is a third, separate profile: aspirin alone is tied to Reye syndrome, and only in children.
