1. A nurse reviews a swab result from a patient's pressure injury, which has grown Staphylococcus aureus. The patient is afebrile, the wound has no redness, drainage, or pain, and the white blood cell count is normal. What should the nurse anticipate?
  • ✓No antibiotic will be started, because bacteria growing without overt signs is colonization
  • BA broad-spectrum antibiotic will be started immediately because the culture is positive
  • CA narrow-spectrum antibiotic will be started because the organism has been identified
  • DThe wound will be recultured because the first result must be a contaminant
Rationale

Colonization is the establishment and growth of microorganisms on the skin, open wounds, or mucous membranes or in secretions without causing an infection, and in colonization "these patients do not have any overt signs of infection." The chapter states plainly that colonization does not require antibiotic treatment, and that treating it anyway "may be one way in which drug-resistant organisms emerge."

Watch for

(C) is the reflex pick because a named organism looks like an invitation to switch to definitive therapy, and (B) pulls for the same reason with less discipline. Definitive therapy still needs an infection to treat; a positive culture by itself proves only that the organism is present.

  • AColonization, which still does not require antibiotic treatment
  • ✓Infection, because microorganisms are now invading and multiplying in body tissue
  • CA superinfection arising from the wound flora
  • DAn expected inflammatory response to daily dressing changes
Rationale

Infections are invasions and multiplications of microorganisms in body tissues. The chapter's classic signs and symptoms are "fever, chills, sweats, redness, pain and swelling, fatigue, weight loss, increased white blood cell (WBC) count, and the formation of pus," and this patient now has most of them.

(A) is the mirror error of the previous item. The same organism in the same wound crossed from colonization into infection when the overt signs appeared, so the presence of bacteria is never the discriminator — the signs are.

3. A culture report describes an organism that stained red, has two cell membranes, and has a smaller outer capsule and peptidoglycan layer. What should the nurse expect about treating it?
  • AIt is gram positive, and drug molecules generally penetrate it easily
  • ✓It is gram negative, and infections with it are generally more difficult to treat
  • CIt is gram positive, so a narrow-spectrum drug will be sufficient
  • DIt is gram negative, so any cell wall inhibitor will be effective
Rationale

The chapter's Clinical Pearl is "Gram stain purple = Gram positive. Gram stain red = Gram negative." Gram-negative organisms have more complex cell walls with two cell membranes, which makes "gram-negative bacterial infections more difficult to treat, because the drug molecules have a harder time penetrating the more complex cell walls."

Watch for

(A) inverts the color rule, which is the most swapped fact in this section. It also attaches the right consequence to the wrong organism: it is the gram-positive cell, with its very thick peptidoglycan wall, that drugs penetrate more easily.

4. A patient admitted for an elective hip replacement develops pneumonia on hospital day 4. Nothing at admission suggested a respiratory infection. How should this infection be classified?
  • ACommunity-associated, because the patient came from home
  • ✓Health care-associated, because it was not present or incubating at admission and began more than 48 hours after admission
  • CCommunity-associated, because the patient had not been hospitalized within the past year
  • DHealth care-associated, because postoperative pneumonia is always the more severe form
Rationale

A health care-associated infection is acquired during the course of receiving treatment for another condition in a health care institution; it is "not present or incubating at the time of admission but occurs more than 48 hours after admission." These infections matter because the organisms "have been exposed to strong antibiotics in the past and are the most drug resistant and the most virulent."

Watch for

(C) is the dangerous distractor because it quotes the correct community-associated criterion — no hospitalization within the past year — and applies it to the wrong question. That criterion describes where the patient was before admission, not where this pneumonia came from. (D) makes severity the discriminator, which it never is; timing plus setting are.

6. A patient is scheduled for colorectal surgery. Temperature and white blood cell count are normal and there is no sign of infection, but an antibiotic is ordered to be given on call, about 30 minutes before the incision. Which type of therapy is this?
  • AEmpiric therapy
  • ✓Prophylactic therapy
  • CDefinitive therapy
  • DTreatment of a superinfection
Rationale

Prophylactic antibiotic therapy is given before anticipated exposure to an infectious organism in an effort to prevent the development of infection, and the chapter times the surgical dose "generally 30 minutes before the incision to ensure adequate tissue penetration." Nothing in this patient's findings shows an infection to treat.

Watch for

(A) pulls because prophylactic and empiric therapy both happen before any organism has been named, so timing alone will not separate them. The discriminator is whether an infection exists yet: prophylaxis has none, while empiric therapy has one whose identity is not yet known.

7. A patient arrives with severe headache, fever, and nuchal rigidity, and acute meningitis is suspected. Cultures are drawn and an antibiotic is started within the hour, before any results are back. Which type of therapy is this?
  • AProphylactic therapy
  • ✓Empiric therapy
  • CDefinitive therapy
  • DSubtherapeutic therapy
Rationale

Empiric therapy is administration based on the practitioner's judgment of the pathogens most likely to be causing an apparent infection, used "to avoid treatment delay before specific culture information has been obtained." The chapter names suspected acute meningitis as its own example of an infection whose risk of severe complications is too high to wait on.

Watch for

(A) is the mirror error of the previous item. This patient already has an apparent infection, so nothing is being prevented; the drug is treating something real whose name is still unknown.

8. Culture and sensitivity results return and identify the organism, and the prescriber narrows the regimen. Which statement best explains this change?
  • ✓Definitive therapy uses the most narrow-spectrum, least toxic drug based on sensitivity results
  • BDefinitive therapy uses the broadest-spectrum drug available to prevent relapse
  • CEmpiric therapy resumes once the organism is known
  • DNarrowing therapy is done mainly to reduce the cost of the admission
Rationale

Definitive therapy is administration based on known results of culture and sensitivity testing, which are usually available in 48 to 72 hours, "using the most narrow-spectrum, least toxic drug based on sensitivity results." Overuse of broad-spectrum antibiotics contributes to resistance.

Watch for

(B) inverts the spectrum rule, and it is the intuitive inversion because broader coverage feels safer in an already sick patient. Broad coverage is what empiric therapy buys while waiting; definitive therapy gives it up on purpose.

9. A wound culture and an antibiotic are both ordered for a patient with a draining surgical wound. Which action should the nurse take first?
  • AGive the first antibiotic dose, then collect the culture at the next dressing change
  • ✓Collect the culture specimen, then give the first antibiotic dose
  • CGive the antibiotic and hold the culture until the patient is afebrile
  • DCollect the culture only if the first dose fails to lower the fever
Rationale

"Culture specimens must be obtained before drug therapy is initiated whenever possible." Otherwise the presence of antibiotics in the tissues may result in misleading culture results, and the nursing section restates the consequence directly: "Taking antibiotics before a culture would lead to false negative results."

Watch for

(A) is the reflex pick because giving the drug feels like the urgent intervention and the swab feels like paperwork. Collecting first delays treatment by minutes; giving first can cost the team its only accurate organism identification.

11. A nurse explains that an ordered penicillin causes defective bacterial cell walls that are unstable and rupture. How should the nurse describe this drug's action?
  • ✓Bactericidal, because it kills the bacteria
  • BBacteriostatic, because it inhibits growth without killing
  • CAntimetabolite, because it blocks folic acid synthesis
  • DBacteriostatic, because lysis only slows reproduction
Rationale

Penicillins inhibit synthesis of the bacterial peptidoglycan cell wall by binding penicillin-binding proteins, which "causes the formation of defective cell walls that are unstable and easily broken down," and "bacterial death usually results from lysis (rupture) of the bacterial cells." Bactericidal antibiotics are those that kill bacteria.

Watch for

(D) is built on the word lysis. A student who does not know the term reads "rupture" as damage short of death and downgrades a cidal drug to a static one. Cue: cidal kills; static holds the population stationary.

12. The same patient's second drug is doxycycline, which binds the bacterial ribosome so the organism cannot make protein and cannot multiply while host defenses clear it. How should the nurse describe this action?
  • ABactericidal, because loss of protein synthesis is always fatal to bacteria
  • ✓Bacteriostatic, because it inhibits growth rather than killing the bacteria
  • CAntimetabolite, because it competes with para-aminobenzoic acid
  • DCell wall inhibitor, because ribosomes are part of the cell wall
Rationale

Tetracyclines are bacteriostatic drugs that inhibit bacterial protein synthesis by binding to the 30S bacterial ribosome. Bacteriostatic antibiotics "do not actually kill bacteria but rather inhibit their growth."

(A) is the mirror error of the previous item, upgrading a static drug to a cidal one. (D) pulls on a separate confusion: the ribosome is inside the cell, and no protein synthesis inhibitor is a cell wall drug.

13. A patient asks how a sulfonamide can poison bacteria without poisoning human cells. Which explanation is accurate?
  • ✓Sulfonamides compete with para-aminobenzoic acid to block bacterial folic acid synthesis, and human cells use folic acid taken in from outside
  • BSulfonamides destroy the bacterial cell wall, and human cells have no cell wall
  • CSulfonamides bind the 50S ribosome, and human ribosomes are 30S
  • DSulfonamides are activated only by bacterial beta-lactamase
Rationale

In competitive inhibition, sulfonamides compete with para-aminobenzoic acid for the bacterial enzyme tetrahydropteroic acid synthetase, preventing bacterial synthesis of folic acid. "Microorganisms that require exogenous folic acid (not synthesized by the bacterium itself) are not affected by sulfonamide antibiotics. Therefore these drugs do not affect folic acid metabolism in human cells."

Watch for

(B) is the reflex pick because the chapter's own key point says most antibiotics work by inhibiting bacterial cell wall synthesis in some way. That generalization does not reach the sulfonamides, macrolides, or tetracyclines in this same chapter, so it must not be carried onto a named-class stem. A stem mentioning folic acid is never a beta-lactam item.

14. A patient receiving azithromycin asks how it stops the bacteria. Which response is accurate?

  • BIt binds to the 30S ribosomal subunit and inhibits protein synthesis

  • DIt prevents cross-linking of the bacterial cell wall

Macrolides are bacteriostatic drugs that inhibit protein synthesis by binding reversibly to the 50S ribosomal subunits of susceptible microorganisms, although "in high enough concentrations they may be bactericidal to some susceptible bacteria."

16. A patient tells the nurse, "I'm allergic to erythromycin — it upsets my stomach." What is the nurse's best action?
  • ADocument a drug allergy to erythromycin and request an alternative from the prescriber
  • ✓Ask open-ended questions about the reaction and document nausea as an adverse effect rather than an allergy
  • CDocument a sensitivity to all macrolides
  • DPlan to give the drug with an antihistamine
Rationale

"Many patients will say that they are 'allergic' to a medication when in fact what they experienced was a common mild adverse effect such as stomach upset or nausea," and patients who report drug allergies "need to be asked open-ended questions to elicit descriptions of prior allergic reactions so that the actual severity of the reaction can be assessed."

Watch for

(A) is the safe-feeling pick, and its cost is invisible at the bedside: mislabeling an intolerance as an allergy removes an effective drug from the patient's chart permanently. (C) compounds the same error by generalizing it to a whole class.

21. A patient with glucose-6-phosphate dehydrogenase deficiency is prescribed a sulfonamide. What is the nurse's primary concern?

  • ✓The red blood cells will be destroyed, producing hemolytic anemia
  • CThe drug will be eliminated too quickly, producing a subtherapeutic level
  • DThe patient will develop kernicterus
Rationale

In G6PD deficiency, administration of antibiotics such as sulfonamides, nitrofurantoin, and dapsone "may result in the hemolysis, or destruction, of red blood cells." 22. A patient with an uncomplicated urinary tract infection is prescribed sulfamethoxazole/trimethoprim. Which explanation of why this drug was chosen is accurate?
  • ✓Sulfonamides achieve very high concentrations in the kidneys, through which they are eliminated
  • BSulfonamides are excreted unchanged in the stool and sterilize the bowel reservoir
  • CSulfonamides are bactericidal against all gram-negative organisms
  • DSulfonamides are the only antibiotic class safe in pregnancy at term
Rationale

"Sulfonamides achieve very high concentrations in the kidneys, through which they are eliminated. Therefore SMX-TMP is often used in the treatment of UTIs." The fixed combination pairs a sulfonamide with trimethoprim, a nonsulfonamide antibiotic, in a 5:1 ratio, and the pairing allows a synergistic antibacterial effect.

(C) upgrades a bacteriostatic antimetabolite to a bactericidal drug, the most common class error with this group. (D) inverts a contraindication outright: sulfonamides are contraindicated in pregnant women at term and in infants younger than 2 months of age.

  • BTo prevent the photosensitivity reaction

beta-lactam ring and enzyme inhibitorsbeta-lactamase versus its inhibitors
27. A culture report notes that the organism produces beta-lactamase. What does this mean for the patient's therapy?
  • ✓The bacteria make an enzyme that opens the beta-lactam ring, so all beta-lactam drugs lose their antibacterial efficacy
  • BThe patient produces an enzyme that inactivates the drug in the liver
  • CA medication has already been added to the antibiotic to protect it
  • DThe organism is resistant to penicillins only, not to the other beta-lactams
Rationale

Beta-lactamases are "any of a group of enzymes produced by bacteria that catalyze the chemical opening of the crucial beta-lactam ring structures in beta-lactam antibiotics." The enzyme breaks the bond between the carbon and nitrogen atoms of the ring, and "when this happens, all beta-lactam drugs lose their antibacterial efficacy." Penicillinase is the subset that specifically inactivates penicillins.

Watch for

(B) relocates the enzyme into the patient, which is where most drug-metabolizing enzymes live; this one is bacterial. Cue: if the bacteria make it, it is the enzyme; if a pharmacist dispenses it, it is the inhibitor. (C) describes the countermeasure rather than the problem.

30. Nafcillin is ordered for a staphylococcal infection. How does nafcillin withstand the staphylococcal enzyme that destroys natural penicillins?
  • AIt is packaged with a beta-lactamase inhibitor
  • ✓Its own structure carries a large, bulky side chain near the beta-lactam ring that acts as a barrier to the penicillinase enzyme
  • CIt has no beta-lactam ring for the enzyme to open
  • DIt is given only intravenously, so the enzyme never reaches it
Rationale

The penicillinase-resistant drugs, also called antistaphylococcal penicillins, are "stable against hydrolysis by most staphylococcal penicillinases." Their chemical structure "features a large, bulky side chain near the beta-lactam ring. This side chain serves as a barrier to the penicillinase enzyme."

Watch for

(A) is the reflex pick because it is the other solution to the same problem, and the two get fused. The combination products resist by carrying a separate inhibitor drug; the penicillinase-resistant penicillins resist by their own shape, with nothing added. Note that penicillinase-resistant does not mean proof against every beta-lactamase — certain strains of S. aureus are resistant to these drugs and require alternative regimens.
