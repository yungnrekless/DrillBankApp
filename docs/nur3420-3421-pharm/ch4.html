1. Two patients receive the same drug at the same dose and respond differently. The nurse attributes the difference to their age, sex, size, and body composition. Which term does the chapter's key-term box use for that variation?
  • ABias
  • BPharmacogenomics
  • ✓Drug polymorphism
  • DEthnopharmacology
Rationale

The key term defines drug polymorphism as "variation in response to a drug because of a patient's age, sex, size, and/or body composition." That list notably does not name genetics. The body text on p. 48 broadens it to environmental factors such as diet and nutritional status, cultural factors, and genetic factors, and the chapter is inconsistent between the two.

Watch for

(B) pulls because all three terms in this box explain why patients respond differently, and the box defines them within a few lines of each other. Pharmacogenomics is the narrow one: "the study of genetics in drug response."

2. A researcher is studying how one inherited gene variant changes a patient's response to a drug. Which term names that study?
  • ✓Pharmacogenomics
  • BDrug polymorphism
  • CAdherence
  • DBias
Rationale

Pharmacogenomics is "the study of genetics in drug response." The chapter cross-references Chapter 8 for it. Ethnopharmacology is the broader field, defined as the effect of ethnicity on absorption, metabolism, distribution, and excretion as well as genetic variation.

Watch for

(B) is the mirror error of the previous item. The key-term definition of polymorphism lists age, sex, size, and body composition, not genes, so a stem naming a specific gene variant is not asking about polymorphism.

3. A patient who is a rapid acetylator is started on a drug that undergoes acetylation. What should the nurse anticipate?
  • ADrug accumulation that may require lower dosages
  • BDelayed absorption requiring a change of route
  • CNo effect on blood levels, because acetylation does not alter drug concentration
  • ✓Subtherapeutic drug concentrations that may require higher dosages
Rationale

The chapter states that rapid acetylators "metabolize drugs more quickly, which predisposes the patient to subtherapeutic drug concentrations and may require higher drug dosages." Its clinical pearl on the same page reads, "Ethnicity can have an impact on drug metabolism."

Watch for

(A) is the reflex pick for anyone who reads "rapid metabolism" as "more drug in the body." Faster metabolism clears the drug faster, so levels fall rather than rise, and the dose goes up rather than down.

4. A slow acetylator is prescribed that same drug at a standard dose. Which risk is the nurse monitoring for?
  • ✓Toxicity from drug accumulation
  • BSubtherapeutic levels from rapid clearance
  • CFailure of the drug to be absorbed at all
  • DAn allergic reaction to the acetyl group
Rationale

Slow acetylation is decreased metabolism, so the drug accumulates and toxicity is the risk. Chapter 2 lists the same pair in its Table 2.6, fast acetylator with increased metabolism and slow acetylator with decreased, and Chapter 38 names slow acetylation as one of its two genetic host factors alongside G6PD deficiency.

Watch for

(B) is the mirror error of the previous item. Slow acetylation is the accumulation side of this pair; rapid clearance and subtherapeutic levels belong to the fast acetylator.

5. A Black patient with newly diagnosed hypertension is started on drug therapy. According to the chapter's cultural implications box, which statement reflects the expected response?
  • AResponse is best to beta blockers and poorest to calcium channel blockers
  • ✓Response is better to diuretics than to beta blockers and ACE inhibitors, and best to calcium channel blockers, especially diltiazem
  • CResponse to single-drug therapy is better than to combination therapy
  • DResponse to thiazide diuretics is poorer than in White patients
Rationale

The box states that Black patients respond "better to diuretics than to beta blockers and angiotensin-converting enzyme inhibitors," "less effectively to beta blockers," "best to calcium channel blockers, especially diltiazem," and "less effectively to single-drug therapy." The body text reinforces the diuretic half separately: thiazide diuretics "appear to be more effective in Blacks than in Whites."

(A) and (D) each invert exactly one claim in a four-claim row, which is all a distractor has to do here. (C) reverses the single-drug statement. Hold the row as four separate claims rather than one impression.

7. A nurse is preparing to give a first dose to a patient who recently immigrated. Which action does the chapter direct before that medication is administered?
  • AApply the health beliefs listed for the patient's cultural group in the chapter's table of common practices
  • BWithhold the drug until a family member confirms what the patient believes
  • ✓Complete a cultural assessment covering language needs, health beliefs, past medicine use, herbal and home remedies, over-the-counter drugs, religious practices, community support, and dietary habits
  • DDocument that a cultural assessment is not indicated for a first dose
Rationale

The chapter's checklist directs a thorough cultural assessment before any medication is administered, covering languages spoken and the need for an interpreter, health beliefs and practices, past uses of medicine, herbal and folk and home remedies or supplements, over-the-counter drug use, usual responses to illness, responsiveness to medical treatment, religious practices and beliefs, support from the patient's cultural community, and dietary habits.

Watch for

(A) is the assumption the chapter explicitly forbids in the most important sentence of the section: "consult with the patient rather than assume that the patient holds certain beliefs because he or she belongs to a certain ethnic group." The table exists to describe variation, not to predict an individual.

8. An older adult Asian patient is admitted for drug therapy. Which cultural issue does the chapter tie most directly to that therapy?
  • AHospital diets will be unacceptable to this patient
  • ✓The use of herbal products may be an important practice for this patient
  • CX-ray studies will be refused by this patient
  • DHospitalization will be valued mainly as an opportunity for socialization
Rationale

The assessment checklist names "use of herbal treatments, folk remedies, home remedies, or supplements" as a required question, and the body text warns that alternative therapies such as herbal and homeopathic remedies "can inhibit or accelerate drug metabolism" and therefore alter a drug's response. The chapter also notes that a diet high in fat "has been documented to increase the absorption of some drugs."

Watch for

(A), (C), and (D) are group-level assumptions rather than assessment findings, and none has a pharmacologic mechanism attached to it. The herbal option is the only one the chapter connects to drug metabolism.

11. Over 100 people died in the United States after ingesting a sulfanilamide preparation made with diethylene glycol, a component of automobile antifreeze, which had never been tested for toxicity. Which legislation did this produce, and what did it require?
  • AThe Kefauver-Harris Amendments of 1962, requiring proof of therapeutic efficacy
  • ✓The Federal Food, Drug, and Cosmetic Act of 1938, requiring data proving drug safety and establishing the investigational new drug application process
  • CThe Durham-Humphrey Amendment of 1951, establishing legend drugs
  • DThe Controlled Substance Act of 1970, establishing the schedules
Rationale

Table 4.1 credits the Federal Food, Drug, and Cosmetic Act of 1938 with requiring manufacturers to "provide data proving drug safety" with FDA review and with establishing the investigational new drug application process, and names the sulfanilamide elixir tragedy as its prompt.

Watch for

(A) is the reflex pick for anyone who remembers only that a tragedy produced a law. The pairing is chronological and it is the mnemonic: the earlier disaster produced the earlier, weaker requirement, which was safety alone.

12. Pregnant women in Europe who took thalidomide, a sedative-hypnotic, gave birth to seriously deformed infants. Which US legislation followed, and what did it add?
  • AThe Sherley Amendment of 1912, prohibiting fraudulent claims
  • BThe Orphan Drug Act of 1983, promoting research on drugs for rare diseases
  • CThe Federal Food, Drug, and Cosmetic Act of 1938, establishing the investigational new drug application process
  • ✓The Kefauver-Harris Amendments of 1962, requiring manufacturers to demonstrate both therapeutic efficacy and safety
Rationale

Table 4.1 pairs the Kefauver-Harris Amendments with the thalidomide tragedy and states they required manufacturers to "demonstrate both therapeutic efficacy and safety" of new drugs.

13. A drug label carries the statement "Caution—Federal law prohibits dispensing without a prescription." Which act required that legend, and what does the term legend drug mean?
  • ✓The Durham-Humphrey Amendment of 1951, and legend drug means prescription drug
  • BThe Durham-Humphrey Amendment of 1951, and legend drug means nonprescription drug
  • CThe Federal Food and Drugs Act of 1906, and legend drug means a drug with addicting potential
  • DThe Harrison Narcotic Act of 1914, and legend drug means a narcotic
Rationale

Table 4.1 states that the Durham-Humphrey Amendment "established legend drugs or prescription drugs" and required the quoted legend on the label. The key term gives legend drugs as "another name for prescription drugs," while over-the-counter drugs are the ones available without a prescription.

(B) keeps the correct act and flips the definition, which is the error the word itself invites. "Legend" here means the printed caution required on the label, not "legendary" or "listed."

14. A caller identifying himself as a hospitalized patient's grandson telephones the unit and asks for her diagnosis and prognosis. Which understanding guides the nurse's response?
  • AThe privacy rule applies only to written records, so verbal information may be shared with family
  • BDiagnosis may be released but payment information may not
  • ✓HIPAA requires health care providers, insurers, public health authorities, employers, and schools to protect individually identifying health information, and severe fines may follow a breach
  • DHIPAA binds only clinicians, so information a school or employer holds may be released freely
Rationale

The chapter states HIPAA "requires all health care providers, health and life insurance companies, public health authorities, employers, and schools to maintain patient privacy regarding protected health information," which "includes any individually identifying information such as patients' health conditions, account numbers, prescription numbers, medications, and payment information." The key term adds that if confidentiality is breached, "severe fines may be imposed."

(D) narrows the covered entities down to clinicians, which is the usual misreading of HIPAA. The chapter's own list names employers and schools right alongside providers, which is why this act reaches so much further than the chart.

us drug legislation timelinelabeling, privacy, and supplements
15. A patient brings in an herbal product whose label reads "For depression." What does the Dietary Supplement Health and Education Act require of that product's manufacturer?
  • ATo prove both safety and efficacy before marketing, as for any prescription drug
  • ✓At least to ensure the product's safety, though not necessarily its efficacy, and to make no unsubstantiated claims such as "Known to cure depression"
  • CTo prove efficacy only, since supplements are presumed safe
  • DNothing, because dietary supplements are entirely unregulated
Rationale

The chapter states the 1994 act requires manufacturers "at least to ensure their safety (although not necessarily their efficacy)" and prohibits "any unsubstantiated claims" in product labeling, and gives this exact worked example: a label "may read 'For depression' but cannot read 'Known to cure depression.'" Chapter 7 develops this framework further.

Watch for

(A) applies the drug standard to a supplement, which is the reflex assumption. The FDA has less regulatory authority over vitamin, herbal, and homeopathic preparations precisely because they are designated dietary supplements rather than drugs.

clinical drug trial phaseswho is enrolled in each phase
16. A pharmaceutical company begins the first human testing of a new antihypertensive. Who receives the drug, and what is being determined?
  • ASmall numbers of volunteers who have hypertension, to determine effectiveness
  • BLarge numbers of patients with hypertension, to detect rare adverse effects
  • CThe general population after marketing, to compare the drug against an existing agent
  • ✓Small numbers of healthy subjects, to determine the optimal dosage range and the pharmacokinetics of the drug
Rationale

Phase I uses "small numbers of healthy subjects rather than those who have the disease" and is designed "to determine the optimal dosage range and the pharmacokinetics of the drug and to ascertain if further testing is needed." Blood tests, urinalyses, vital signs, and specific monitoring tests are performed. All of this follows preclinical in vitro and animal studies, which rule many compounds out before any human is dosed.

Watch for

(A) is the reflex pick, and it is the single most counterintuitive fact in the sequence: the first humans to receive a new drug do not have the disease it treats. Volunteers who have the disease enter at phase II.

clinical drug trial phaseswho is enrolled in each phase
17. The same drug moves on to a study enrolling a small number of volunteers who actually have hypertension. What is this phase determining?
  • AThe optimal dosage range and pharmacokinetics in healthy volunteers
  • ✓The drug's effectiveness and any adverse effects, with therapeutic dosage ranges refined
  • CInfrequent or rare adverse effects in large numbers of patients
  • DPostmarketing comparison against another drug in the same therapeutic category
Rationale

Phase II uses "small numbers of volunteers who have the disease that the drug is designed to diagnose or treat," to "determine the drug's effectiveness and identify any adverse effects," and "therapeutic dosage ranges are refined during this phase."

Watch for

(A) is the mirror error of the previous item. The switch from healthy subjects to subjects who have the disease is the boundary between phases I and II, and the change in purpose follows from it.

clinical drug trial phasesreaching the market and staying watched
18. A drug is being studied in large numbers of patients followed by medical research centers, with objectives of establishing clinical effectiveness, safety, and dosage range. What happens when this phase is complete?
  • AThe manufacturer submits an investigational new drug application so that human testing may begin
  • BThe drug automatically enters a further trial phase before any marketing can occur
  • ✓The manufacturer submits a new drug application, and the drug goes on the market if the FDA approves it
  • DThe drug is marketed immediately with no further FDA review
Rationale

Phase III uses large numbers of patients and provides information about "infrequent or rare adverse effects," with the three objectives of establishing "clinical effectiveness, safety, and dosage range." After phase III the FDA receives a report and the company submits a new drug application, and the drug is put on the market once that application is approved.

Watch for

(A) swaps the two applications, which is the most common error here because both are applications to the same agency. The investigational new drug application comes first and permits study in humans; the new drug application comes after phase III and permits marketing.

clinical drug trial phasesreaching the market and staying watched
19. Six months after a drug reaches the market, reports of severe adverse effects never seen during the trials begin arriving. Which phase does this belong to, and why did the effects appear only now?
  • APhase III, because rare adverse effects are its stated purpose
  • BPhase II, because adverse effects are identified there
  • CPhase I, because pharmacokinetics were established there
  • ✓Phase IV, because these are postmarketing studies and severe effects may appear for the first time once a drug is used in the larger general population
Rationale

Phase IV consists of "postmarketing studies that are voluntarily conducted by pharmaceutical companies to obtain further proof of the therapeutic and adverse effects," with data gathered "for at least 2 years after the drug's release," often comparing the new drug with another in the same therapeutic category. The chapter notes that some drugs pass every trial phase without problems and then produce severe adverse effects in the general population.

Watch for

(A) pulls because phase III is the phase whose stated purpose is infrequent or rare adverse effects. Timing settles it: anything happening after the drug is on sale is by definition after phase III.

investigational study design and informed consentblinding and bias
20. In a drug study, the subjects do not know whether they are receiving the investigational drug or the placebo, but the investigator does know. What is this design called, and whose bias does it minimize?
  • AA double-blind investigational drug study, minimizing bias on the part of both investigator and subjects
  • BAn open-label study, which minimizes no bias at all
  • ✓A blinded investigational drug study, minimizing bias on the part of research subjects
  • DA postmarketing study, minimizing investigator bias
Rationale

The key term defines a blinded study as one in which "the subjects are purposely unaware" of whether they receive the drug or a placebo, and states that this "serves to minimize bias on the part of research subjects." The body text confirms that in a blinded study the subject does not know but "the investigator does know."

Watch for

(A) is the reflex pick because both terms contain the word blind. Count who is unaware: one party is blinded, two parties is double-blind.

investigational study design and informed consentblinding and bias
21. In a second study, neither the research staff nor the subjects know which subjects receive the real drug. What is this design called, and what does it add?
  • ✓A double-blind investigational drug study, minimizing bias on the part of both the investigator and the subject
  • BA blinded investigational drug study, minimizing bias on the part of the subject only
  • CA placebo-free design, since blinding removes the need for a placebo
  • DA preclinical design, since the researchers are not yet involved
Rationale

The key term defines a double-blind study as one in which "both the investigator(s) and the subjects" are purposely unaware, minimizing bias "on the part of both." Dosage forms often look identical except for a secret code, which is revealed at the end of the study and can be broken earlier by the principal investigator in a clinical emergency.

Watch for

(B) is the mirror error of the previous item. Both definitions turn on the word bias, which is why the chapter gives bias its own key term, defined as "any systematic error in a measurement process."

22. A nurse is asked to obtain informed consent for a drug study. Which set of requirements matches the chapter?
  • ✓Explain purpose, procedures, benefits, and risks; the document is written in a language the patient understands and is dated and signed by the patient and at least one witness
  • BExplain purpose and benefits only; the document is signed by the patient alone
  • CExplain risks only; the document is signed by the prescriber and a witness
  • DExplain purpose, procedures, benefits, and risks; the document is signed once therapy has begun
Rationale

The chapter requires that the "purpose of the study, the procedures to be used, the possible benefits, and the risks involved" be explained, and that the document be "written in a language understood by the patient" and "dated and signed by the patient and at least one witness." Obtaining consent may be carried out by a nurse or other health care professional depending on how the study is designed.

(D) keeps all four content elements intact and breaks only the timing, which is the half people stop reading. Consent "must be obtained more than a given number of days or hours before certain procedures are performed and must always be obtained when the patient is fully mentally competent."

24. A patient with a life-threatening illness asks why some drugs reach patients faster than others. Which explanation matches the chapter?
  • AExpedited approval skips the preclinical phase so human testing can start sooner

drug safety warnings and recallsblack box warnings
25. A newly hired nurse reads that a drug on the unit carries a black box warning and asks whether the drug has been taken off the market. What is the accurate answer?
  • AThe drug has been withdrawn and may no longer be dispensed
  • BThe warning applies only to investigational drugs still in trials
  • ✓The drug can still be prescribed; the black box warning is the strictest FDA warning, indicating that serious adverse effects have been reported, so the prescriber must be aware of the risk and the patient must be warned
  • DThe drug may now be given only under an approved protocol
Rationale

A black box warning is "the strictest warning from the FDA," indicating "that serious adverse effects have been reported" with the drug. The chapter states plainly, "The drug can still be prescribed," though the prescriber must be aware of the potential risk and the patient must be warned. The warning text is bordered in solid black, which is where the name comes from.

Watch for

(A) confuses the warning with the withdrawal mechanism. Removal from the market is a recall; a black box warning is a warning, not a withdrawal. (D) borrows the dispensing restriction that belongs to schedule I controlled substances.

drug safety warnings and recallsrecall classes
26. A drug product is recalled because its use carries a reasonable probability of serious adverse health effects or death. Which class is this, and where does it sit on the scale?
  • AClass III, the most serious type of recall
  • BClass II, because the effects may be temporary or medically reversible
  • CClass I, the least serious type of recall
  • ✓Class I, the most serious type of recall
Rationale

A class I recall is "the most serious type of recall," in which use "carries a reasonable probability of serious adverse health effects or death." Class II is less severe, with temporary or medically reversible effects and a low probability of lasting major harm, and class III is least severe.

Watch for

(A) inverts the direction of the scale, which is worth guarding against because plenty of clinical severity scales run the other way. Recall classes run from I as the worst down to III, the same direction as the controlled substance schedules.

  • ✓The dispensing rules cannot distinguish them, because both allow a written or oral prescription that expires in 6 months with no more than five refills; only abuse and dependency potential differ
  • CSchedule IV expires in 6 months while schedule III expires in 12 months
  • DSchedule III requires a written prescription only, the same as schedule II
Rationale

Table 4.3 gives schedule III and schedule IV identical dispensing restrictions: "written or oral prescription that expires in 6 months; no more than five refills in a 6-month period; container must have warning label." (A) manufactures a distinction where the tables give none, which is what a test taker does when a question seems to demand that two adjacent categories differ on the axis being asked about. Here the honest answer is that this axis does not separate them.

controlled substance schedulesdispensing restrictions in practice
32. One patient is discharged on a codeine and acetaminophen combination product and another on codeine alone. How are the two scheduled?
  • AThe combination is schedule II and codeine alone is schedule III
  • BBoth are schedule II, because both contain codeine
  • ✓The combination is schedule III and codeine alone is schedule II
  • DBoth are schedule V, because combination opioid products contain limited quantities
Rationale

Table 4.3 lists codeine among the schedule II examples and "codeine with selected other medications (e.g., acetaminophen)" among the schedule III examples. Chapter 10 states the same from the drug side: the combination "is classified as a Schedule III controlled substance," while codeine not combined with other drugs "is classified as a Schedule II."

(A) is the reflex inversion, because adding a second drug feels as though it ought to raise the level of control. The combination product sits at the lower schedule, not the higher one.

35. A nurse fails to give the care a reasonably prudent nurse would render under similar circumstances, and a patient is harmed. Which term names this, and why?
  • ANegligence only, because malpractice applies solely to physicians
  • BFraud, because a professional standard was breached
  • ✓Malpractice, because it is a special type of negligence committed by a professional with specialized education and training
  • DNeither, because a licensed nurse is not held to a legal standard of care
Rationale

Negligence is "the failure to act in a reasonable and prudent manner" or failure of the nurse to give the care that a "reasonably prudent (cautious) nurse would render or use under similar circumstances." Malpractice is "a special type of negligence" or the failure of a professional with specialized education and training to act in a reasonable and prudent way. Every malpractice case is negligence; not every negligence case is malpractice.

Watch for

(A) restricts malpractice to medicine, which is the common lay understanding of the word. The definition turns on specialized education and training, not on which profession holds it.

37. A nurse fails to notice an infiltrated IV of a caustic drug, and a second nurse catches it before any tissue injury occurs. Which element of malpractice liability is missing?
  • ADuty, because the second nurse took over responsibility
  • BBreach of duty, because the error was corrected in time
  • CCausation, because monitoring an IV site is not the nurse's duty
  • ✓Damage, because no harm resulted
Rationale

The four elements are duty, breach of duty, causation, and damage, and all must be present. The chapter's own worked example runs duty as accurate assessment and monitoring of the IV site with timely reporting of changes, breach as not noticing that the site is swollen, red, painful, and warm, causation as failing to note extravasation that results in the need for skin grafting, and damage as extensive skin and nerve damage with limited use of the arm.

Watch for

(B) treats an error that was caught as no breach at all. The breach happened; what is absent is the harm at the end of the chain, and without damage the set is incomplete.
