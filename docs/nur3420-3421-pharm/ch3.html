1. A pregnant patient asks the nurse how the medication she swallows could possibly reach her baby. Which explanation correctly describes the principal mechanism?
  • ✓Substances move passively across the placenta from an area of higher concentration to an area of lower concentration
  • BSubstances are pumped across the placenta by energy-requiring carriers in the cell membrane
  • CThe placenta filters out all drug molecules and admits only nutrients
  • DSubstances cross the placenta only after the membranes rupture in labor
Rationale

The chapter states that transfer of both drugs and nutrients to the fetus occurs primarily by diffusion across the placenta, although not all drugs cross the placenta. Diffusion is defined in the key terms box as the passive movement of a substance between different tissues from areas of higher concentration to areas of lower concentration.

Watch for

(B) describes active transport, which the key terms box defines directly against diffusion — the tell that the two will be swapped on an exam. Diffusion is passive and needs no energy; active transport requires energy and uses a pump.

2. A drug is moved across a cell membrane against its concentration gradient by a carrier protein that consumes cellular energy to do it. Which key term names this process?
  • ADiffusion, because the substance is moving between two different tissues
  • ✓Active transport, because the movement requires energy and uses a pumping mechanism within the cell membrane
  • CFirst-pass elimination, because a carrier protein is involved
  • DProtein binding, because the drug is attached to a protein
Rationale

The key terms box defines active transport as the active, energy-requiring movement of a substance between different tissues via pumping mechanisms contained within cell membranes. The energy requirement and the pump are what separate it from diffusion.

Watch for

(A) is the mirror error of the previous item. Diffusion also moves a substance between tissues, so the phrase in that option is true but does not discriminate; the discriminator is energy, and diffusion needs none.

3. A patient at 8 weeks' gestation tells the nurse she has been taking an over-the-counter remedy she bought herself. Why is the nurse most concerned about the timing of this exposure?
  • ✓The first trimester is the period of greatest danger of drug-induced developmental defects
  • BThe first trimester is when the greatest percentage of maternally absorbed drug reaches the fetus
  • CThe third trimester is when developmental defects are most likely, so the timing is reassuring
  • DDrug exposure is equally hazardous at every point in pregnancy
Rationale

The chapter identifies the first trimester as the period of greatest danger of drug-induced developmental defects, because during this period the fetus undergoes rapid cell proliferation and the skeleton, muscles, limbs, and visceral organs are developing at their most rapid rate. Self-treatment is strongly discouraged at any time during pregnancy but especially during the first trimester.

Watch for

(B) fuses the chapter's two superlatives into one trimester. Greatest danger and greatest transfer belong to opposite ends of the pregnancy, which is exactly why the chapter states both in a single sentence.

4. A patient at 34 weeks' gestation receives a drug that has been safely used earlier in her pregnancy, and the nurse notes that fetal exposure is now higher than it was before. What accounts for the increase?
  • AThe first trimester is when drug transfer to the fetus peaks, and residual drug is still circulating
  • ✓Enhanced blood flow to the fetus, increased fetal surface area, and increased free drug in the maternal circulation
  • CThe placenta has begun to actively pump drug toward the fetus
  • DThe fetal liver now metabolizes drug faster than the mother's
Rationale

During the last trimester the greatest percentage of maternally absorbed drug gets to the fetus, and the chapter attributes this to enhanced blood flow to the fetus, increased fetal surface area, and an increased amount of free drug in the mother's circulation.

Watch for

(A) is the mirror error of the previous item. The first trimester is when exposure is most damaging, not when the most drug arrives — early damage is structural because organs are being built, while late transfer is quantitative because blood flow and surface area are maximal.

5. A newly pregnant patient with epilepsy tells the nurse she plans to stop all of her medications until after delivery so the baby is not exposed to anything. Which response reflects the chapter's position?
  • AStopping all medications is the safest course for any pregnancy
  • ✓Without drug therapy, maternal conditions such as hypertension, epilepsy, diabetes, and infection could seriously endanger both her and the fetus
  • CShe should halve every dose rather than stop, since dose is the only variable that matters
  • DThe decision depends only on the drug's molecular weight
Rationale

The chapter states that certain situations require the use of drugs during pregnancy, because without drug therapy maternal conditions such as hypertension, epilepsy, diabetes, and infection could seriously endanger both the mother and the fetus, and the potential for harm far outweighs the risks of appropriate drug therapy.

Watch for

(A) is the reflex pick because the whole section is about fetal hazard, and stopping everything looks like the maximally cautious answer. The chapter's caution is aimed at self-treatment, not at treatment of a maternal condition that itself threatens the pregnancy.

9. A pregnant patient with a serious infection is prescribed a drug for which possible fetal risk in humans has been reported. The patient asks whether this means the drug is forbidden in pregnancy. Which response is correct?
  • AIt is forbidden, since human fetal risk has been reported
  • ✓In selected cases, consideration of the potential benefit versus risk may warrant use of the drug in a pregnant patient
  • CIt may be used freely, since only possible risk was reported
  • DThe rating applies only after delivery
Rationale

Table 3.1 states that for category D drugs, possible fetal risk in humans has been reported; however, in selected cases consideration of the potential benefit versus risk may warrant use of these drugs in pregnant women. Only category X carries the flat prohibition, where fetal abnormalities have been reported and positive evidence of fetal risk in humans is available, and those drugs are not to be used in pregnant women.

Watch for

(A) collapses category D into category X, which is the reflex response to any mention of human fetal risk. The discriminator between the last two categories is not whether harm is documented but whether benefit can ever justify the drug anyway. Several drugs in this course sit at C or D, so a letter alone does not mean do not give.

10. A nurse reviews a newer drug label that carries no letter category and instead has three narrative subsections. Which subsection contains information about contraception, pregnancy testing, and infertility?
  • APregnancy
  • BLactation
  • ✓Females and Males of Reproductive Potential
  • DWarnings and Precautions
Rationale

Under the FDA rule of June 2015, prescribing information uses three subsections. The Females and Males of Reproductive Potential subsection carries information about contraception, pregnancy testing, and infertility, while Pregnancy carries dosing and potential risks to the developing fetus, and Lactation carries the amount of drug in breast milk and the potential effect on the child.

Watch for

(A) pulls because contraception and pregnancy testing both contain the word pregnancy, but that subsection is about dosing and fetal risk once a pregnancy exists. The textbook continues to use the letter categories because drugs are still being phased in and the transition is not complete, so the student will likely encounter both systems.

12. A breastfeeding mother taking a prescribed medication asks whether nursing more often will help clear the drug out of her own system faster. Which response is correct?
  • AYes, breastfeeding is a significant route of maternal drug excretion
  • ✓No, breast milk is not the primary route for maternal drug excretion, and drug levels in milk are usually lower than those in the maternal circulation
  • CYes, but only for fat-soluble drugs
  • DNo, because drugs do not enter breast milk at all
Rationale

The chapter states that breast milk is not the primary route for maternal drug excretion and that drug levels in breast milk are usually lower than those in the maternal circulation. The amount of infant exposure depends largely on the volume of milk consumed.

Watch for

(A) has the physiology running backwards. The concern in breastfeeding runs one direction only, toward the infant; nothing about nursing meaningfully clears drug from the mother. (D) overcorrects in the opposite direction, and the chapter is explicit that a wide variety of drugs easily cross into breast milk.

18. A drug is ordered at 15 mg/kg/day for a child whose chart records a weight of 22 lb. What is the nurse's first step?
  • AMultiply 22 by 15, since the order is already weight-based
  • ✓Convert the weight to kilograms by dividing the pounds by 2.2, giving 10 kg
  • CConvert the weight by multiplying the pounds by 2.2, giving 48.4 kg
  • DAsk the pharmacy to convert the order to a milligram-per-pound basis
Rationale

The chapter's Clinical Pearl states that pediatric doses use kilograms, not pounds, and that the nurse takes the weight in pounds and divides by 2.2 to get kilograms. The chapter's worked example is a 10-lb baby weighing 4.5 kg.

Watch for

(A) is the named error. The chapter states outright that a common source of medication error and potential toxicity is confusing pounds with kilograms, and using 22 as though it were kilograms would deliver more than twice the intended dose. (C) inverts the conversion and makes the child heavier than an adult, which is the arithmetic tell.

19. A drug reference lists a pediatric dose as 30 mg/kg/day divided every 8 hours. A nurse gives 30 mg/kg at each of the three scheduled times. What error has occurred?
  • ANone, because mg/kg/day and mg/kg/dose mean the same thing
  • ✓The nurse administered three times the intended daily dose by treating a per-day figure as a per-dose figure
  • CThe nurse administered one-third of the intended daily dose
  • DThe nurse should have divided the daily dose by the child's weight
Rationale

The chapter gives this its own Clinical Pearl, noting that most pediatric doses are in mg/kg/day but some are in mg/kg/dose. A per-day figure is divided among the day's doses; reading it as a per-dose figure multiplies the total by the number of doses given.

Watch for

(A) is the reflex pick because the two labels differ by one short word and are easy to read past. The size of the error is however many doses per day the schedule contains, which is a factor of three or four in most regimens.

20. A nurse is given a drug reference that lists a usual dosage range per 24 hours in milligrams per kilogram. How does the nurse determine the safe range for a particular child?

  • DBy using the adult dose and reducing it to one-half to two-thirds
Rationale

The chapter's procedure directs the nurse to determine the dose parameters by multiplying the weight by the minimum and maximum daily doses of the drug, which produces the safe range, and then to compare the prescribed dosage with that calculated safe range.

(D) borrows the older adult rule of one-half to two-thirds of the standard adult dose and applies it to a child. Both ends of the lifespan need reduced doses, but children are dosed by mg/kg against a calculated safe range, not as a fraction of the adult dose.

25. A 7-year-old requires a milligram-per-kilogram dose of a drug that is proportionally larger than the adult dose. Which statement explains this?

28. A nurse notes that an older adult absorbs an oral drug more slowly and less completely than a younger patient. Which changes account for this?
  • AIncreased gastric acid production and accelerated gastric emptying
  • ✓Less acidic gastric pH from reduced hydrochloric acid production, slowed gastric emptying, reduced blood flow to the gastrointestinal tract, and a decreased absorptive surface area from blunted and flattened villi
  • CAn increase in absorptive surface area as villi lengthen with age
  • DIncreased cardiac output driving more drug into the portal circulation
Rationale

The older adult pharmacokinetics box lists all four findings: gastric pH is less acidic because of a gradual reduction in the production of hydrochloric acid, gastric emptying is slowed because of a decline in smooth muscle tone and motor activity, blood flow to the GI tract is reduced by 40% to 50%, and the absorptive surface area is decreased because the aging process blunts and flattens villi.

Watch for

(A) reverses two findings at once, and it pulls because heartburn is common in older adults and suggests too much acid rather than too little. Table 3.3 records the gastrointestinal change as increased pH from alkaline gastric secretions, which is the same finding stated on the pH scale instead of the acid scale.

  • APolypharmacy
  • ✓The prescribing cascade
  • CNonadherence
  • DThe Beers Criteria
Rationale

The chapter defines the prescribing cascade as drugs given specifically to counteract the adverse effects of other drugs, and its own example is a potassium supplement to counteract the potassium loss caused by certain diuretic medications.

(A) is the reflex pick because adding a second drug does increase the count, and the cascade is one route into polypharmacy. The discriminator is causality: polypharmacy is a count of concurrent drugs, while a cascade is a chain in which one drug is added to treat another drug's adverse effect.

  • CA therapeutic duplication
  • DAn adverse drug withdrawal event

The key terms box defines polypharmacy as the use of many different drugs concurrently in treating a patient who often has several health problems, and the chapter attributes it to the more frequent occurrence of chronic diseases together with the multiple drug options available. The chapter states that appropriate drug doses for older adults may sometimes be one-half to two-thirds of the standard adult dose, and that dosing should follow the admonition to start low and go slow, meaning to start with the lowest possible dose and increase the dose slowly based on patient response.

(A) is the reflex pick because starting at the standard dose and titrating downward feels like the same destination reached from the other side, but the adverse effect is the harm the rule exists to prevent. (D) applies the pediatric method, which belongs to the other end of the lifespan.

The chapter gives this its own Clinical Pearl, stating that a low serum creatinine does not mean good renal function in the elderly, that creatinine is a byproduct of muscle mass, and that a patient with a creatinine of 0.3 mg/dL may have a creatinine clearance indicative of renal dysfunction.
