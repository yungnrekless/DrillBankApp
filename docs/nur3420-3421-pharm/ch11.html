anesthesia terminology and scopegeneral versus local anesthesia
1. A patient scheduled for an abdominal hysterectomy under general anesthesia asks why she will need a breathing tube and a ventilator when her neighbor had a hand laceration repaired without one. Which explanation is accurate?
  • ✓General anesthesia alters CNS nerve impulses throughout the entire body and depresses the respiratory drive, so ventilatory support is required
  • BGeneral anesthesia blocks only the nerves that innervate the surgical site, but the tube keeps secretions out of the airway
  • CThe breathing tube is required because all general anesthetics are gases and there is no other way to deliver them
  • DGeneral anesthesia preserves the respiratory drive, but the ventilator is used to speed emergence at the end of the case
Rationale

General anesthesia is a drug-induced state in which the CNS nerve impulses are altered to reduce pain and other sensations throughout the entire body, involving complete loss of consciousness and depression of respiratory drive. The overview states the consequence flatly: loss of normal respiratory function requires mechanical or manual ventilatory support to avoid brain damage and suffocation.

Watch for

(B) borrows the local anesthesia definition, which limits the effect to the tissues innervated by the anesthetized nerves. The two key-term definitions are built on the same sentence frame and differ in exactly two slots, which is what makes them swappable.

anesthesia terminology and scopegeneral versus local anesthesia
2. The neighbor is having a forearm laceration sutured after infiltration with lidocaine and asks whether she too could stop breathing. Which response is accurate?
  • ✓Local anesthesia does not involve paralysis of respiratory function; it eliminates pain sensation only in the tissues innervated by the anesthetized nerves
  • BLocal anesthesia depresses the CNS enough that a ventilator must be at the bedside for every procedure
  • CLocal anesthesia produces complete loss of consciousness but spares the respiratory drive
  • DLocal anesthesia alters CNS nerve impulses throughout the entire body, but at a dose too small to matter
Rationale

Local anesthesia is a drug-induced state in which peripheral or spinal nerve impulses are altered to reduce or eliminate pain and other sensations in tissues innervated by those nerves. The chapter contrasts it directly with general anesthesia: local anesthesia does not involve paralysis of respiratory function but only elimination of pain sensation in the tissues innervated by anesthetized nerves.

Watch for

(C) is the mirror error of the previous item and also smuggles in the moderate sedation definition, which is the one that pairs altered consciousness with a preserved respiratory drive. Local anesthesia leaves consciousness intact as well.

3. A nurse preparing a patient for a cesarean birth under spinal anesthesia is asked by a student how spinal anesthesia is classified. Which response is correct?
  • AIt is a form of general anesthesia, because the drug is placed near the spinal cord and reaches the CNS
  • ✓It is a form of local anesthesia, because it does not depress the CNS at a level that causes loss of consciousness
  • CIt is a form of moderate sedation, because consciousness is only partly reduced
  • DIt is monitored anesthesia care, because sedation and analgesia are combined
Rationale

The key term for spinal anesthesia opens with the words "Local anesthesia induced by injection of an anesthetic drug near the spinal cord," and the key points repeat it: spinal anesthesia, or regional anesthesia, is a form of local anesthesia. The body text explains that because spinal anesthesia does not depress the CNS at a level that causes loss of consciousness, it can be thought of as a large-scale type of local rather than general anesthesia.

Watch for

(A) is the reflex pick because a needle near the spinal cord feels central, and the word "spinal" pulls toward the CNS. The cue is that a needle near the spinal cord still means local, and the block runs distal to the injection site.

4. A patient scheduled for a colonoscopy with moderate sedation asks whether that counts as being "put under." How should the nurse classify moderate sedation?
  • AAs a form of local anesthesia, because the patient stays awake and breathes on his own
  • ✓As a milder form of general anesthesia that causes partial or complete loss of consciousness but does not generally reduce the normal respiratory drive
  • CAs monitored anesthesia care, because a provider stays at the bedside
  • DAs a form of spinal anesthesia delivered intravenously
Rationale

Both the key term and the key points file moderate sedation under the general heading: it is a milder form of general anesthesia resulting in partial or complete loss of consciousness but without reducing normal respiratory drive. Moderate sedation, conscious sedation, and procedural sedation are synonymous terms.

Watch for

(A) pulls because everything about moderate sedation's clinical use reads local — outpatient, awake, self-ventilating. Pair it against the previous item to hold both: spinal anesthesia feels central and is classified local; moderate sedation feels local and is classified general.

5. A procedure is documented as monitored anesthesia care. Which description matches what the patient will receive?
  • ✓Local anesthesia along with sedation and analgesia, with the patient able to answer questions appropriately and protect the airway
  • BGeneral anesthesia with an inhaled agent, titrated to keep the respiratory drive intact
  • CContinuous monitoring of a mechanically ventilated patient receiving a neuromuscular blocking drug
  • DA spinal injection with the patient monitored until sensation returns
Rationale

Monitored anesthesia care is a planned procedure in which the patient undergoes local anesthesia along with sedation and analgesia. Its three fundamental elements are safe sedation, control of anxiety, and pain control; patients are able to answer questions appropriately and protect their airways, and can be discharged soon after the procedure.

Watch for

(B) confuses monitored anesthesia care with moderate sedation, and the two are separated by a different axis than depth. Monitored anesthesia care is defined by what is combined; moderate sedation is defined by how deep.

6. An anesthesia provider plans to combine several drug classes rather than use a single agent. Select all the accurate statements about adjunct anesthetics and balanced anesthesia.

An adjunct is any drug that enhances clinical therapy when used simultaneously with another drug, and adjuncts can be thought of as "helper drugs." general anesthetic classes and adjunct drugsthe Overton-Meyer theory
9. An instructor asks why a highly lipid-soluble anesthetic is more potent than a water-soluble one. Which response reflects the Overton-Meyer theory?
  • ✓Potency varies directly with lipid solubility, because nerve cell membranes, the brain, the spinal cord, and the blood-brain barrier all have high lipid content
  • BPotency varies directly with water solubility, because the drug must dissolve in plasma before it can reach the brain
  • CPotency varies directly with molecular weight, because larger molecules occupy more receptors
  • DPotency varies inversely with lipid solubility, because fat stores the drug away from the brain
Rationale

The Overton-Meyer theory describes the relationship between the lipid solubility of anesthetic drugs and their potency. Nerve cell membranes have high lipid content, as do the brain, spinal cord, and blood-brain barrier, so lipid-soluble anesthetics can easily cross the blood-brain barrier to concentrate in nerve cell membranes.

Watch for

(D) is chosen by students who remember from Chapter 2 that fat sequesters drugs, then apply that to potency. Overton-Meyer runs the other way: fat-soluble drugs are stronger anesthetics than water-soluble drugs.

general anesthetic classes and adjunct drugsthe Overton-Meyer theory
10. Two anesthetics are compared and the more water-soluble one turns out to be substantially less potent. How should the nurse interpret that finding?
  • ✓It is expected, because potency varies directly with lipid solubility, so the more water-soluble drug is the weaker anesthetic
  • BIt is unexpected, because water solubility speeds delivery to the brain and should raise potency
  • CIt confirms that the Overton-Meyer theory explains every property of the anesthetic drugs
  • DIt reflects protein binding rather than solubility, which is what actually determines anesthetic potency
Rationale

Because potency varies directly with lipid solubility, the water-soluble member of a pair is predicted to be the weaker anesthetic. It is the lipid-soluble drug that crosses the blood-brain barrier readily to concentrate in the high-lipid nerve cell membranes.

Watch for

(B) is the mirror error of the previous item, inverting the same axis from the other end. (C) overreaches in a way the chapter itself refuses: many theories have been proposed, the drugs vary widely in chemical structure, and Overton-Meyer has been used to explain only some of the properties of anesthetic drugs.

12. A patient's blood pressure falls steadily during a lengthy procedure under general anesthesia. How should the nurse interpret this?
  • ✓It is the one major complication of general anesthesia the chapter names, and it threatens perfusion of the heart, peripheral circulation, liver, kidneys, and respiratory tract
  • BIt indicates an allergic reaction and the anesthetic should be presumed contraindicated
  • CIt is an unexpected finding, since general anesthetics reliably raise blood pressure
  • DIt is the earliest and most specific sign of malignant hyperthermia
Rationale

Adverse effects of general anesthetics are dose dependent and vary with the individual drug; the sites primarily affected are the heart, peripheral circulation, liver, kidneys, and respiratory tract. One major complication of general anesthesia is hypotension, affecting perfusion of those organs.

(D) is tempting because malignant hyperthermia is the chapter's headline emergency, but the blood pressure sign it lists is *unstable* blood pressure rather than a predictable fall, and its defining sign is a rapid rise in body temperature.

16. A 79-year-old with declining liver and kidney function is scheduled for general anesthesia. Which explanation of the increased risk matches the chapter's lifespan box?
  • ✓Declining liver function decreases drug metabolism and declining renal function decreases drug excretion, either of which can lead to drug toxicity, unsafe levels, or overdose
  • BDeclining liver function increases absorption while declining renal function increases distribution into tissue
  • COlder adults are less sensitive to CNS drug effects, which offsets the slower clearance
  • DThe risk is unchanged as long as the patient is taking fewer than five medications
Rationale

The older adult patient is affected more adversely by anesthesia than the young or middle-aged adult. A decline in liver function results in decreased metabolism of drugs and a decline in renal function leads to decreased drug excretion; if both organs are impaired the risk is even greater. Older adults are also more sensitive to the effects of drugs affecting the central nervous system.

Watch for

(B) names two pharmacokinetic steps the box never mentions. The two steps it does name are metabolism, which is hepatic, and excretion, which is renal — the same phases Chapter 2 defines. (D) contradicts the box's polypharmacy warning that the more drugs a patient takes, the higher the risk for adverse reactions and drug-drug interactions.

17. A preoperative patient reports that a cousin nearly died of a very high fever during surgery. Which two agents are classically associated with malignant hyperthermia?
  • ✓Volatile inhalational anesthetics and the depolarizing neuromuscular blocker succinylcholine
  • BNitrous oxide and the nondepolarizing neuromuscular blocker rocuronium
  • CPropofol and neostigmine
  • DLidocaine and epinephrine
Rationale

Malignant hyperthermia is an uncommon but potentially fatal, genetically linked adverse metabolic reaction to general anesthesia, and it is classically associated with the use of volatile inhalational anesthetics as well as the depolarizing NMBD succinylcholine. 19. A patient arrived on the orthopedic unit from the PACU 2 hours after a hip replacement with a temperature of 98.9 degrees F. It is now 104.8 degrees F. Another nurse comments that the patient must be developing an infection from the hip replacement. What is the nurse's priority action?
  • ✓Treat this as possible malignant hyperthermia, notify the anesthesia provider and surgeon immediately, and anticipate dantrolene
  • BObtain wound and blood cultures and wait for the results before notifying anyone
  • CAdminister an antipyretic and recheck the temperature in one hour
  • DDocument the reading as an expected postoperative inflammatory response
Rationale

The chapter's exemplar temperature for malignant hyperthermia is higher than 104 degrees F, it describes the reaction as a rapid progression of hyperthermia that may be fatal if not promptly recognized and aggressively treated, and it extends monitoring past the surgical suite into regular postoperative care. Dantrolene is stocked by law at every institution that provides general anesthesia.

Watch for

(B) is the reflex pick because a colleague has already named infection, and cultures feel like the diligent answer. A surgical-site infection does not raise a temperature nearly six degrees in 2 hours, and waiting on cultures spends the time the reaction is fatal without.

neuromuscular blocker mechanisms and reversaldepolarizing versus nondepolarizing
23. Succinylcholine is given and the nurse observes brief twitching across the patient's hands and face before paralysis sets in. Which mechanism explains it?
  • ✓Succinylcholine is a competitive agonist that binds in place of acetylcholine and depolarizes the membrane, so during the depolarizing phase the muscles fasciculate before the desensitizing phase produces paralysis
  • BSuccinylcholine is a competitive antagonist that blocks acetylcholine, and the twitching is the last stored acetylcholine being consumed
  • CThe twitching is a histamine release reaction rather than a receptor effect
  • DThe twitching is an early sign that reversal has begun
Rationale

Depolarizing NMBDs work much like the neurotransmitter acetylcholine and are competitive agonists that bind in place of it at the motor endplates. During phase I, the depolarizing phase, the muscles fasciculate or twitch; after continued depolarization the muscles are no longer responsive to the acetylcholine released, and the muscle becomes paralyzed in phase II, the desensitizing phase.

Watch for

(B) swaps the one word the whole class distinction turns on. Depolarizing agents are competitive agonists that mimic acetylcholine; nondepolarizing agents are competitive antagonists that block it. Only the agonist mechanism produces fasciculations, which are most pronounced in the muscle groups of the hands, feet, and face.

neuromuscular blocker mechanisms and reversaldepolarizing versus nondepolarizing
24. A patient receives rocuronium for intubation and no fasciculations occur. What accounts for this?
  • ✓Rocuronium is a nondepolarizing blocker, a competitive antagonist of acetylcholine, so the nerve cell membrane is not depolarized and skeletal muscle contraction does not occur
  • BRocuronium is a depolarizing blocker given slowly enough to avoid the twitching phase
  • CThe fasciculations were suppressed by the anticholinesterase drug given at induction
  • DRocuronium mimics acetylcholine, but at a dose too low to depolarize the membrane
Rationale

Nondepolarizing NMBDs also bind to acetylcholine receptors at the neuromuscular junction, but instead of mimicking acetylcholine they block its actions. The nerve cell membrane is not depolarized, the muscle fibers are not stimulated, and skeletal muscle contraction does not occur. Cisatracurium, rocuronium, vecuronium, and pancuronium are all nondepolarizing.

Watch for

(D) is the mirror error of the previous item, keeping the agonist language for the antagonist drug. Both classes bind the same receptor, which is what makes the pair confusable, and succinylcholine is the only depolarizing NMBD in the chapter.

neuromuscular blocker mechanisms and reversalreversal agents
25. At the end of a procedure the anesthesia provider gives neostigmine to reverse vecuronium. How does that work?
  • ✓It prevents the enzyme cholinesterase from breaking down acetylcholine, so acetylcholine builds up at the motor endplate and displaces the nondepolarizing blocker molecule
  • BIt binds the blocker molecule directly and carries it out of the circulation
  • CIt depolarizes the membrane so the muscle can contract through the block
  • DIt relaxes skeletal muscle, which restores the normal resting tone
Rationale

Anticholinesterase drugs such as neostigmine and pyridostigmine are antidotes for nondepolarizing NMBDs such as vecuronium, rocuronium, and cisatracurium. They work by preventing cholinesterase from breaking down acetylcholine, which causes acetylcholine to build up at the motor endplate, where it eventually displaces the nondepolarizing molecule and returns the nerve to its original state.

Watch for

(B) describes sugammadex, a selective relaxant binding agent used for the reversal of rocuronium or vecuronium — a different mechanism for the same two drugs. Anticholinesterase reversal works only because raised acetylcholine can out-compete a blocker that is a competitor to begin with.

26. A nurse asks why no anticholinesterase was given to reverse the succinylcholine at the end of a short case. Which response is correct?

The chapter states the exception directly: succinylcholine is not reversed with acetylcholinesterase inhibitors because of its short duration of action and natural breakdown. (C) is the mirror error of the previous item — sugammadex is the selective relaxant binding agent for rocuronium or vecuronium, both nondepolarizing. Giving an anticholinesterase for succinylcholine applies the right drug to the wrong mechanism, since succinylcholine is an agonist already producing the block rather than a competitor waiting to be displaced.

27. A patient with end-stage renal disease requires neuromuscular blockade for a long procedure. Which drug does the chapter name as the drug of choice, and on what grounds?

Most drugs are biotransformed in the liver and eliminated by the kidneys. Cisatracurium is broken down by Hoffman elimination, a process dependent on pH and temperature, which makes it the drug of choice for patients with end-stage renal disease.

neuromuscular blocker safety and adverse effectsthe ventilation rule and what these drugs do not do
28. A nurse notices that vecuronium and vancomycin are stored beside each other, and is asked to give the antibiotic to a patient who is breathing on his own. Which principle governs this situation?
  • ✓Never give a neuromuscular blocker to someone who is not on mechanical ventilation; the nurse must ensure the patient is ventilated before giving one and double-check that a blocker is not inadvertently given
  • BA neuromuscular blocker may be given to a spontaneously breathing patient as long as oxygen is applied by mask
  • CThe risk applies only to continuous infusions, not to single doses
  • DAn accidental dose would be tolerated, because these drugs cause sedation and the patient would simply sleep
Rationale

Neuromuscular blocking drugs paralyze the skeletal muscles required for breathing — the intercostal muscles and the diaphragm — leaving the patient unable to breathe independently, so mechanical ventilation is required to prevent brain damage or death from suffocation. The Clinical Pearl states it as a rule: never give a neuromuscular blocker to someone who is not on mechanical ventilation.

Watch for

(D) is the belief that makes this error feel survivable, and it is precisely what the chapter denies. In such an error the patient would experience a horrendous death, because the mind is alert but the patient cannot speak or move. Vancomycin and vecuronium are the chapter's own named sound-alike pair, and these are high-alert drugs for that reason.

  • DHe is unconscious, because the drug blocks acetylcholine in the brain as well as at the muscle
Rationale

The chapter is explicit that NMBDs, when used alone, do not cause sedation or relieve pain or anxiety, and therefore the patient must also receive appropriate medications to manage pain and/or anxiety. In the ICU these drugs are used to prevent the patient from fighting against the ventilator.

Watch for

(B) is the reflex reassurance and is exactly why this error is dangerous: paralysis is not unconsciousness. These drugs act at the neuromuscular junction rather than the cortex, so a paralyzed patient may be fully awake and aware.

The typical time course begins with muscle weakness, followed by total flaccid paralysis. (B) inverts the sequence, and it pulls because respiratory arrest is the outcome students associate most strongly with these drugs, so the diaphragm feels like the headline. The diaphragm is last, which is why respiratory arrest marks a completed block rather than an early one.

37. Why are true allergic reactions to local anesthetics generally limited to the ester type?
  • ✓Esters are metabolized by cholinesterase in the plasma and liver and are converted into a para-aminobenzoic acid compound, and that compound is responsible for the allergic reactions

Different enzymes are responsible for the breakdown of the two groups. 40. Two hours after an epidural, a postpartum patient can move both legs and asks to walk to the bathroom. Her blood pressure is 88/54. What should the nurse understand?
  • ✓Recovery from local anesthesia occurs in reverse order — motor activity returns first, then sensory function, and autonomic function last — so autonomic blockade and hypotension can persist after movement has returned

  • CAutonomic function returns first, so hypotension at this point is unexpected and suggests hemorrhage
  • DSensory function returns first, then motor function, then autonomic function
Rationale

Autonomic activity is affected first, then pain and other sensory functions are lost, and motor activity is the last to be lost. When the effects wear off, recovery occurs in reverse order: motor activity returns first, then sensory functions, and finally autonomic activity. Local anesthetics also produce sympathetic blockade, blocking the actions of norepinephrine and epinephrine.

Watch for

(B) is the reflex conclusion because movement is the most visible sign of recovery. A patient who can move a limb has not necessarily regained sensation, and one reporting returning sensation still has autonomic blockade — which is why hypotension can persist after an epidural appears to be wearing off.
