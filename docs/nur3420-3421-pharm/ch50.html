1. A nursing student is reviewing which stomach cells the drugs in this chapter target. Which statements about the cells of the gastric glands are accurate? Select all that apply.
Select all that apply · scored all or nothing
  • ✓Parietal cells produce and secrete hydrochloric acid
  • ✓Chief cells secrete pepsinogen, a precursor to pepsin
  • ✓Mucous cells secrete mucus that serves as a protective coat and are also called surface epithelial cells
  • ✓Endocrine and enterochromaffin cells are named among the cell types of the gastric glands
  • EChief cells are the primary site of action for many of the drugs used to treat acid-related disorders
Rationale

The key terms box defines parietal cells as the cells that produce and secrete HCl and names them the primary site of action for many of the drugs used to treat acid-related disorders; chief cells secrete the gastric enzyme pepsinogen, a precursor to pepsin; mucous cells secrete mucus that serves as a protective mucous coat against the digestive properties of HCl and are also called surface epithelial cells; and the gastric glands definition lists parietal, chief, mucous, endocrine, and enterochromaffin cells.

Watch for

(E) moves the drug target from the parietal cell to the chief cell and is the only false option here. (D) reads as unfamiliar and gets dropped by students who read the three-cell paragraph but not the gland definition, since the chapter never returns to endocrine and enterochromaffin cells.

gastric cells and secretionspepsinogen versus pepsin
2. A patient asks why a drug that suppresses stomach acid would have any effect on the digestion of protein. Which explanation is correct?
  • AChief cells release pepsin directly, and acid suppression shuts down the chief cells
  • ✓Chief cells release pepsinogen, an inactive proenzyme that becomes pepsin only after exposure to acid
  • CPepsinogen breaks down protein on its own, and acid only speeds the process up
  • DParietal cells release both acid and pepsin, so blocking one blocks the other
Rationale

Pepsinogen is defined as a proenzyme, or enzyme precursor, that becomes pepsin when activated by exposure to acid, and pepsin is the enzyme in the stomach that breaks down proteins. Removing the acid removes the activation step, which is why suppressing acid also suppresses proteolysis.

Watch for

(A) collapses two stages into one by having the chief cell release the active enzyme. The order is the whole point: proenzyme from the chief cell first, acid second, active pepsin third.

3. A patient with a duodenal ulcer is told the ulcer involves digestion of the lining of the GI tract. Which substance is doing that digesting, and where did it come from?
  • APepsinogen, secreted in active form by the parietal cells
  • ✓Pepsin, activated from chief cell pepsinogen by gastric acid
  • CHydrochloric acid alone, secreted by the chief cells
  • DIntrinsic factor, secreted by the mucous cells
Rationale

Peptic ulcer disease is defined as a general term for gastric or duodenal ulcers that involve digestion of the GI mucosa by the enzyme pepsin, and the chapter states that ulceration is driven by the proteolytic actions of pepsin together with the caustic effects of hydrochloric acid.

Watch for

(A) is the mirror error of the previous item, naming the inactive precursor as the active digester and relocating it to the wrong cell. (C) drops pepsin entirely, but the chapter calls these acid-peptic disorders precisely because both the enzyme and the acid are involved.

parietal cell receptors and sites of drug actionthe three receptors and their second messengers
4. A patient started on an H2 receptor antagonist asks how blocking a histamine receptor could possibly lower stomach acid. Which mechanism explains it?
  • ✓Histamine occupation results in production of adenylate cyclase, which converts ATP to cyclic AMP, which provides the energy for the proton pump
  • BHistamine occupation releases calcium ions that directly neutralize gastric acid in the lumen
  • CHistamine occupation opens the proton pump itself, so blocking histamine inhibits the pump irreversibly
  • DHistamine occupation stimulates the chief cells to release pepsinogen
Rationale

Occupation of the histamine receptor results in the production of adenylate cyclase, which converts ATP to cyclic adenosine monophosphate, which provides energy for the proton pump. This is also why prostaglandins are described as working by preventing histamine binding: without adenylate cyclase, no cAMP can be formed and no second messenger is available to activate the proton pump.

Watch for

(B) borrows the second messenger belonging to the other two receptors, and it adds a lumen action that belongs to antacids. Calcium ions drive the acetylcholine and gastrin arms, never the histamine arm.

5. The same patient asks what happens at the acetylcholine and gastrin receptors of the parietal cell. Which statement is accurate?
  • ABoth use cyclic AMP as their second messenger, exactly as histamine does
  • ✓Both use calcium ions rather than cyclic AMP as the second messenger
  • CAcetylcholine uses cyclic AMP and gastrin uses calcium ions
  • DNeither has a second messenger, because both act directly on the pump
Rationale

The chapter states the contrast explicitly: for acetylcholine and for gastrin, the second messenger that drives the proton pump is not cAMP but is instead calcium ions. Only the histamine arm runs through adenylate cyclase and cAMP.

Watch for

(A) is the mirror error of the previous item, generalizing the histamine pathway to all three receptors. (C) splits the pair, which is what students pick when they remember that something differs but not which arm.

6. A nurse is asked which of the parietal cell's three stimulating receptors can be blocked by a currently marketed drug. Which answer reflects the chapter?
  • AAll three, by H2 antagonists, anticholinergics, and gastrin antagonists respectively
  • ✓Histamine is blocked by drugs in current use; acetylcholine can be blocked by anticholinergics that have been superseded for this purpose; there is currently no drug that blocks gastrin
  • COnly gastrin, because it is a hormone rather than a locally released mediator
  • DNone of them, which is why proton pump inhibitors had to be developed
Rationale

H2 receptor antagonists block the histamine receptor and remain in use; anticholinergics such as atropine block the ACh receptor, but the chapter states these drugs are no longer used for this purpose and have been superseded by other drug classes discussed in this chapter; and there is currently no drug to block the binding of the hormone gastrin to its corresponding receptor.

Watch for

(A) invents a gastrin receptor antagonist, a drug class the chapter says does not exist, and simultaneously revives anticholinergics the chapter has explicitly retired for hyperacidity.

7. Over one admission a patient receives an antacid, an H2 receptor antagonist, and a proton pump inhibitor. Which statement correctly places the three along the acid-secretory pathway?
  • ✓The H2 antagonist acts at a receptor, the proton pump inhibitor at the pump, and the antacid in the lumen on acid that has already been secreted
  • BThe antacid acts at a receptor, the H2 antagonist at the pump, and the proton pump inhibitor in the lumen
  • CAll three act on the parietal cell membrane before any acid is secreted
  • DThe proton pump inhibitor acts at the histamine receptor and the H2 antagonist acts at the pump
Rationale

H2 receptor antagonists competitively block the H2 receptor upstream; the hydrogen-potassium-ATPase pump is the final step in the acid-secretory process of the parietal cell and is where proton pump inhibitors act, which is why blocking it blocks all three receptor arms at once; and antacids work by neutralizing gastric acidity, since they do not prevent the overproduction of acid.

Watch for

(C) is attractive because all three are filed mentally as acid drugs, but only the antacid works on acid that already exists. That single distinction is what makes antacids symptom relief rather than acid suppression.

8. A nurse is asked which acid-related conditions the chapter names as the most harmful and which as the most common. Which pairing is correct?

  • BMost harmful, mild to moderate hyperacidity; most common, peptic ulcer disease

Rationale

The chapter states that the most harmful of these conditions involve hypersecretion of acid and include peptic ulcer disease and esophageal cancer, but that the most common condition is mild to moderate hyperacidity. 9. A patient with long-standing gastroesophageal reflux disease asks what could happen if it goes untreated for years. Which chapter-supported answer is correct?
  • ✓It can progress to erosive esophagitis and to Barrett esophagus, a precancerous condition
  • BIt resolves on its own once acid production declines with age
  • CIt progresses to Zollinger-Ellison syndrome
  • DIt causes hypersecretion of intrinsic factor and vitamin B12 overload
Rationale

GERD is described as the tendency of excessive and acidic stomach contents to back up, or reflux, into the lower and even upper esophagus, and over time this condition can lead to more serious disorders such as erosive esophagitis and Barrett esophagus, a precancerous condition.

(C) borrows a real condition from the H2 antagonist indications, where Zollinger-Ellison syndrome appears as a pathologic gastric hypersecretory condition and a form of hyperchlorhydria. It is not a sequela of reflux.

10. A patient with a duodenal ulcer tests positive for Helicobacter pylori and asks whether the organism fully explains the ulcer. Which response reflects the chapter?
  • AYes, H. pylori is the sole cause of peptic ulcer disease
  • ✓The organism is found in about 90% of patients with duodenal ulcers, but it is also found in many patients without ulcer disease, and more than one factor is involved in ulceration
  • CThe organism is found in about 10% of patients with duodenal ulcers, so it is rarely the explanation
  • DThe organism causes gastric ulcers only, never duodenal ulcers
Rationale

H. pylori is found in 90% of patients with duodenal ulcers and 70% of those with gastric ulcers, but it is also found in many patients who do not have peptic ulcer disease, its presence is not associated with acute perforating ulcers, and the chapter concludes that more than one factor is involved in ulceration.

Watch for

(A) states the claim more strongly than the chapter does, and it is the version most students carry into the item. (C) recycles the 10% figure, which belongs to prevalence in patients younger than 30 rather than to duodenal ulcers.

11. A patient with an H. pylori-positive gastric ulcer is started on treatment. Which regimen matches the chapter's description of first-line therapy?
  • ✓A 10- to 14-day course of a proton pump inhibitor plus clarithromycin and either amoxicillin or metronidazole
  • BA 3-day course of an H2 receptor antagonist plus a single antibiotic
  • CA 6-week course of an antacid alone
  • DA 10- to 14-day course of sucralfate plus misoprostol
Rationale

First-line therapy as printed is a 10- to 14-day course of a proton pump inhibitor and the antibiotics clarithromycin and either amoxicillin or metronidazole, or a combination of a proton pump inhibitor, bismuth subsalicylate, and the antibiotics tetracycline and metronidazole. Many different combinations are used, but all incorporate those key drugs.

Watch for

(B) is picked by students who retain "antibiotic plus acid drug" without the duration or the number of antibiotics. Patient teaching stresses taking each drug, including the antibiotics, exactly as prescribed and without fail, because the condition may recur if the protocol is not followed.

13. A patient who received proton pump inhibitor prophylaxis throughout an intensive care unit stay is transferred to a medical-surgical unit and remains on the drug. Which action does the chapter support?
  • AContinue the drug indefinitely, since no bleeding occurred in the intensive care unit
  • ✓Question continuing the prophylaxis, because data do not support its continued use once the patient is moved out of the intensive care unit
  • CChange to a scheduled antacid, the stepdown drug for prophylaxis
  • DIncrease the dose, because risk peaks after transfer
Rationale

The chapter states that data do not support the continued use of stress ulcer prophylaxis once the patient is moved out of the ICU. Prophylaxis is defined by the setting, and guidelines suggest only that all critically ill patients receive either a histamine receptor-blocking drug or a proton pump inhibitor while they are in that setting.

antacid mechanism and safe self-usewhat antacids actually do
14. A patient says antacids are the best choice because they coat the stomach and protect the ulcer. Which correction is accurate?
  • ✓Antacids neutralize acid that has already been secreted; forming a coating over an ulcer is sucralfate's mechanism
  • BAntacids do coat the stomach lining, and that is their primary mechanism
  • CAntacids act on the parietal cells to prevent the overproduction of acid
  • DAntacids destroy pepsinogen before acid can activate it
Rationale

Antacids work primarily by neutralizing gastric acidity; they do not prevent the overproduction of acid but instead help neutralize acid secretions. Sucralfate is the drug that binds directly to the surface of an ulcer to form a protective barrier the chapter calls a liquid bandage. Antacids do promote mucosal defense, but indirectly and especially at lower dosages, by stimulating the stomach's own secretion of mucus, prostaglandins, and bicarbonate.

Watch for

(C) is the other half of the same misconception and is the half the chapter states as an explicit negation. Antacids reduce symptoms such as pain and reflux; they do not reduce acid production.

15. A 50-year-old who has self-treated heartburn with large amounts of antacids for years reports that they no longer help. Which nursing action is the priority?
  • ARecommend a higher-potency antacid
  • ✓Facilitate evaluation by a health care provider, because long-term self-medication with antacids may mask symptoms of serious underlying disease
  • CAdd a second antacid at bedtime
  • DReassure the patient that reduced response to antacids over time is expected and harmless
Rationale

Long-term self-medication with antacids may mask symptoms of serious underlying disease such as bleeding ulcer or malignancy, and patient teaching directs that a health care provider be contacted after 2 weeks of self-prescribed antacid use. The chapter's own case study follows exactly this patient to endoscopy, which finds GERD and gastritis.

Watch for

(A) is the reflex pick, because escalating a drug that used to work reads as the obvious fix. Years of escalating self-treatment is a diagnostic red flag, not a dosing problem.

16. A patient with a known small bowel obstruction requests an antacid. Which consideration applies?
  • AAny antacid is acceptable, because the only usual contraindication is known allergy
  • ✓Magnesium-containing antacids may be contraindicated in small bowel obstruction because of their laxative effect
  • CAluminum-containing antacids are contraindicated because they bind phosphate
  • DAntacids are contraindicated only during pregnancy
Rationale

The only usual contraindication to antacids is known allergy, but other contraindications may include severe renal failure or electrolyte disturbances and GI obstruction; the chapter's own example is that magnesium antacids may be contraindicated for patients with small bowel obstructions because of the laxative effect.

(A) quotes the first clause of the contraindication sentence and stops before the exceptions, which is exactly how that sentence is built. (D) inverts the pregnancy statement, since antacids are considered safe in pregnancy if prolonged administration and high dosages are avoided.

18. A patient taking a magnesium-only antacid several times a day reports loose stools. Which explanation and action are correct?
  • ✓Magnesium antacids commonly have a laxative effect and can cause diarrhea, so a combination aluminum and magnesium product may be preferred
  • BMagnesium antacids commonly constipate, so the diarrhea is unrelated to the drug
  • CThe patient should increase the dose of the magnesium-only product
  • DDiarrhea from an antacid is the earliest sign of milk-alkali syndrome
Rationale

Magnesium-containing antacids, especially milk of magnesia, can cause diarrhea, and frequent administration of these antacids alone often cannot be tolerated. If constipation or diarrhea occurs with single-component drugs, a combination aluminum- and magnesium-based antacid may be preferred, and the planning outcome names avoidance of antacids containing only magnesium.

Watch for

(B) reverses the flagship confusable pair of this chapter. Magnesium is the laxative salt; aluminum and calcium are the constipating ones.

19. A patient taking an aluminum-only antacid reports no bowel movement in 3 days. Which explanation is correct?
  • AAluminum antacids cause diarrhea, so something other than the antacid is responsible
  • ✓Aluminum and calcium antacids cause constipation, and magnesium is added to combination products specifically to counteract it
  • CAluminum causes constipation only in patients with renal failure
  • DConstipation from an antacid always indicates a bowel obstruction
Rationale

Aluminum-containing and calcium-containing antacids cause constipation, and many aluminum- and calcium-based formulations include magnesium, which not only contributes to acid-neutralizing capacity but also counteracts the constipating effects of aluminum and calcium. The chapter describes the net effect of the combination as a balancing out of both adverse effects.

Watch for

(A) is the mirror error of the previous item. The chapter's human need statements map constipation to aluminum-containing antacids and diarrhea to magnesium-containing antacids, one to one.

20. A patient with chronic kidney disease asks which type of antacid is safest. Which choice does the chapter support, and why?
  • ✓An aluminum-based antacid, because aluminum- and sodium-based antacids are more easily excreted
  • BA magnesium-based antacid, because magnesium is cleared by the kidneys
  • CA calcium-based antacid, because it doubles as a calcium supplement
  • DNo antacid can be used safely in kidney disease
Rationale

Aluminum- and sodium-based antacids are often recommended for patients with renal compromise because they are more easily excreted, whereas both calcium- and magnesium-based antacids are more likely to accumulate to toxic levels in patients with renal disease, and antacids containing magnesium must be avoided in patients with renal failure.

Watch for

(B) is chosen by students who reason that a renally cleared salt suits a kidney patient, which is the opposite of what the chapter says. (C) picks up a true statement, that calcium antacids can serve as a source of calcium, and applies it to the one population where calcium accumulates.

21. A patient abruptly stops a high-potency antacid after weeks of heavy use and reports heartburn worse than before starting it. Which complication does this describe?
  • AMilk-alkali syndrome
  • ✓Acid rebound
  • CMetabolic alkalosis
  • DHypophosphatemia
Rationale

Acid rebound is rebound hyperacidity in which the patient experiences hyperacidity when antacid use is discontinued. It occurs with the discontinuation of antacids that have high acid-neutralizing capacity and with overuse or misuse, because if acid neutralization is sudden and high, the immediate elevation in pH to alkalinity is followed by just as rapid a decline in pH to a more acidic state.

Watch for

(A) and (C) are the reflex picks because all three complications sound like too much base. Discontinuation is what pins acid rebound, hypercalcemia pins milk-alkali syndrome, and sodium bicarbonate abuse pins metabolic alkalosis.

22. A patient using large doses of a calcium carbonate antacid presents with headache, nausea, alkalosis, and hypercalcemia. Which complication does this describe?
  • AAcid rebound
  • ✓Milk-alkali syndrome
  • CSystemic alkalosis from sodium bicarbonate

Chronic high-dose use of calcium-containing antacids, or their use in renal failure, can cause milk-alkali syndrome, characterized by headache, nausea, alkalosis, and hypercalcemia. Calcium antacids can also cause kidney stones, and acid rebound is more common with the calcium-containing products.

antacid drug interactions and dose spacingthe four interaction mechanisms
24. A nurse is reviewing why antacids interfere with so many other oral drugs. Which mechanisms does the chapter name? Select all that apply.
Select all that apply · scored all or nothing
  • ✓Adsorption of other drugs to the antacid, which reduces the ability of the other drug to be absorbed
  • ✓Chelation, the chemical inactivation of other drugs, producing insoluble complexes
  • ✓Increased stomach pH, which changes the absorption of acidic and basic drugs
  • ✓Increased urinary pH, which changes the excretion of acidic and basic drugs
  • EInduction of the hepatic cytochrome P-450 enzyme system
Rationale

The chapter enumerates exactly four mechanisms: adsorption of other drugs to antacids, which reduces the ability of the other drug to be absorbed; chelation, which is the chemical inactivation of other drugs that produces insoluble complexes; increased stomach pH, which increases the absorption of basic drugs and decreases the absorption of acidic drugs; and increased urinary pH, which increases the excretion of acidic drugs and decreases the excretion of basic drugs. Most drugs are either weak acids or weak bases, so pH in both the GI and urinary tracts matters.

Watch for

(E) is imported from the H2 antagonist section, where cimetidine binds enzymes of the hepatic cytochrome P-450 microsomal oxidase system. Antacids in this chapter act physically and by pH, never on hepatic enzymes.

25. An antacid raises gastric pH. What is the effect on other oral drugs still in the stomach?
  • ✓Absorption of basic drugs increases and absorption of acidic drugs decreases
  • BAbsorption of acidic drugs increases and absorption of basic drugs decreases
  • CAbsorption of all drugs increases

Increased stomach pH increases the absorption of basic drugs and decreases the absorption of acidic drugs. Because most drugs are either weak acids or weak bases, pH conditions in the GI tract affect the extent to which drug molecules are absorbed.

antacid drug interactions and dose spacingspacing antacids from other drugs
27. A patient is due for oral ciprofloxacin and asks for an antacid at the same time. Which action is correct?
  • AGive both together, because the interaction is minor
  • ✓Give the antacid either 2 hours before or 2 hours after the quinolone dose
  • CHold the ciprofloxacin until the antacid has been discontinued
  • DGive the antacid 15 minutes after the quinolone
Rationale

Significant patient harm may ensue when the quinolone antibiotics ciprofloxacin, levofloxacin, and moxifloxacin are given with antacids, because antacids can reduce their absorption by more than 50%. Antacids must therefore be given either 2 hours before or 2 hours after the dose of a quinolone antibiotic, or serious infections may go unsuccessfully treated because of altered absorption.

Watch for

(C) is the over-correction distractor. Nothing in this chapter withholds a drug because of an antacid interaction; implementation states that this dosing schedule can be safely implemented without interrupting the safe dosing of other medications.

h2 receptor antagonistsclass mechanism and roster
29. A patient started on famotidine for GERD asks how it works. Which explanation is accurate?
  • ✓It competitively blocks the H2 receptor, which also makes the parietal cell less responsive to acetylcholine and gastrin, reducing but not completely abolishing acid secretion
  • BIt irreversibly inhibits the hydrogen-potassium-ATPase pump, abolishing all gastric acid secretion
  • CIt neutralizes the acid already present in the stomach
  • DIt binds to the ulcer surface and forms a protective barrier
Rationale

H2 receptor antagonists competitively block the H2 receptor of acid-producing parietal cells, which makes the parietal cell less responsive not only to histamine but also to the stimulation of ACh and gastrin. Up to 90% inhibition of vagal- and gastrin-stimulated acid secretion occurs when histamine is blocked, but complete inhibition has not been shown, and these drugs reduce but do not completely abolish acid secretion.

Watch for

(B) describes the proton pump inhibitors, and one word separates the classes: H2 antagonists block competitively and reversibly, proton pump inhibitors bind irreversibly. A surmountable receptor block cannot produce achlorhydria.

30. Which statement about the currently available H2 receptor antagonists is accurate?
  • ✓Cimetidine, famotidine, and nizatidine are available, and ranitidine was pulled from the market in 2020

Sucralfate is a mucosal protectant that acts locally, not systemically, binding directly to the surface of an ulcer; once in contact with stomach acid it dissociates into aluminum hydroxide and sulfate anions, and the sulfated sucrose molecules bind to positively charged tissue proteins at the bases of ulcers and erosions to form a protective barrier the chapter compares to a liquid bandage, which also limits the access of pepsin.
