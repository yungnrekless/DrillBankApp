incidence and prevalencebasic concepts — risk, proportion, rate
1. Why do epidemiologists prefer rates and proportions over raw case counts when describing disease frequency?
  • ARates and proportions are easier to calculate from surveillance data
  • ✓They account for the size of the population at risk, so 50 cases means something different in a population of 250 versus 250,000
  • CRaw counts are not permitted in peer-reviewed publications
  • DProportions eliminate the need to track time
Rationale

Rates and proportions take the size of the population at risk into account. Fifty cases of influenza might be a serious epidemic in a population of 250 but a low rate in a population of 250,000. A count alone tells you nothing without the denominator and the time period.

2. What distinguishes a rate from a proportion?
  • AA rate's denominator includes the numerator; a proportion's does not
  • BA proportion's denominator depends on both population size and time; a rate's does not
  • ✓A rate's denominator is a function of both population size and time, unlike a proportion
  • DThere is no meaningful difference; the terms are interchangeable
Rationale

A proportion is a ratio in which the denominator includes the numerator, ranging from 0 to 1. A rate is a ratio that is not a proportion, because its denominator depends on both population size and time.

Watch for

The proportion definition (numerator inside the denominator) is often flipped with the rate definition (denominator = population size x time) — the two definitions get swapped in distractors.

  • ✓Incidence

5. According to the formula relating incidence and prevalence, when prevalence is small (P < 0.1), which expression is correct?
  • AI = P x D
  • ✓P = I x D
  • CD = P x I
  • DP = I / D
Rationale

When prevalence is small, P = I x D — prevalence equals the incidence rate multiplied by the average duration of disease.

6. Breast cancer and lung cancer in women have similar incidence, but breast cancer has substantially higher prevalence. What explains this?
  • ABreast cancer is diagnosed earlier, inflating incidence
  • BLung cancer has a much longer average duration of illness
  • ✓Breast cancer has a much longer average duration because of higher survival, so more existing cases accumulate at any given time
  • DPrevalence is unrelated to survival duration
Rationale

With five-year survival around 85% for breast cancer versus roughly 15% for lung cancer in women, breast cancer patients live with the disease far longer. Since prevalence = incidence x duration, the longer duration produces a higher prevalence even at similar incidence.

Watch for

A stem that attributes the prevalence difference to incidence rather than duration is testing whether you remember that duration (survival/recovery), not new-case risk, is what separates the two cancers here. ---

8. What distinguishes the case fatality rate (CFR) from the cause-specific mortality rate?
  • AThey are identical measures with different names
  • ✓The CFR's denominator is the number of persons diagnosed with the disease; the cause-specific rate's denominator is the total population at risk
  • CThe CFR's denominator is total deaths, while the cause-specific rate's denominator is the diagnosed population
  • DThe CFR is calculated per 100,000 and the cause-specific rate is calculated per 1,000
Rationale

CFR = deaths from a specific disease / number of persons diagnosed with that disease. Cause-specific mortality rate = deaths from a specific cause / total population at risk. The denominators are what separate them.

9. Why is the proportionate mortality ratio (PMR) NOT considered a true rate?
  • AIts numerator counts only deaths under age 65
  • ✓Its denominator is total deaths in the period, not the population at risk, so it does not estimate the risk of death
  • CIt can only be calculated retrospectively
  • DIt excludes infectious disease deaths
Rationale

PMR's denominator is total deaths, not the population at risk. Because of this, it does not estimate risk and its size can shift purely because deaths from other causes rise or fall, even when the cause of interest stays constant.

This is the classic "not a rate" trap. Any answer option calling the PMR a risk estimate or a true rate is the intended distractor.

10. A researcher computes a CFR of 86% for a disease. What is the survival rate?
  • A86%
  • ✓14%
  • CCannot be determined without additional data
  • D100%
Rationale

Survival rate = 1 minus CFR. An 86% CFR yields a 14% survival rate.

The fact bank notes the textbook itself is internally inconsistent (one place uses 87%/13%, another 86%/14%). Don't memorize a specific number as "the" figure for any disease — just apply the rule survival = 1 - CFR to whatever CFR the stem gives you.

12. Why is comparing crude mortality rates across two populations potentially misleading?
  • ACrude rates cannot be expressed per 100,000
  • ✓Crude rates are affected by the population's age distribution, since older populations have higher mortality; this is corrected by age adjustment
  • CCrude rates only apply to infectious diseases
  • DCrude rates require CFR data that is often unavailable
Rationale

The crude mortality rate is affected by the population's age distribution — an older population will show more deaths regardless of underlying health. Rate/age adjustment corrects for this by adjusting to a standard population so comparisons are fair. ---

13. The epidemiologic triangle consists of which three elements?
  • APerson, place, time
  • ✓Agent, host, environment
  • CSensitivity, specificity, prevalence
  • DPrimary, secondary, tertiary prevention
Rationale

The epidemiologic triangle is the agent-host-environment model: disease results from complex relationships among causal agents, susceptible persons, and environmental factors, and a change in any one element changes risk.

causal modelstriangle, web, ecological model
14. A nurse is investigating a single-source outbreak of a specific bacterial pathogen. Which causal model best fits this scenario, and why?
  • AThe web of causality, because bacterial disease is always multifactorial
  • ✓The epidemiologic triangle, because it fits a simple single-agent infectious model
  • CThe ecological model, because it addresses policy-level determinants
  • DNone of these models apply to infectious disease
Rationale

The epidemiologic triangle (agent-host-environment) fits a simple single-agent infectious disease model. The web of causality is the better fit for multifactorial chronic disease where many variables interact.

causal modelstriangle, web, ecological model
15. A nurse is studying the multiple interacting contributors to type 2 diabetes (diet, genetics, socioeconomic status, access to care, activity level). Which model best fits this situation?
  • AThe epidemiologic triangle, because diabetes has a single identifiable agent
  • ✓The web of causality, which recognizes complex interrelationships among many factors increasing or decreasing risk
  • CPoint epidemic analysis
  • DThe case-control design
Rationale

The web of causality recognizes the complex interrelationships of many factors interacting, sometimes subtly, to raise or lower disease risk — a better fit than the triangle when many variables interact, as in multifactorial chronic disease.

causal modelstriangle, web, ecological model
16. How does the ecological model extend beyond the epidemiologic triangle and web of causality?
  • AIt applies only to infectious disease outbreaks
  • ✓It treats multiple determinants of health as interrelated and synergistic across biologic, behavioral, social, and environmental levels, including policy, culture, and economics, with a life-span perspective
  • CIt replaces the need for agent, host, and environment entirely
  • DIt is used only for calculating incidence rates
Rationale

The ecological model (IOM, 2002) treats the multiple determinants of health as interrelated and acting synergistically or antagonistically rather than as discrete factors, spanning biologic, mental, behavioral, social, and environmental levels including policy, culture, and economics — captured by the phrase "healthy people in healthy communities." ---

17. A patient has no discernible pathological process but is susceptible to disease. An intervention aimed at this patient to promote health and prevent disease onset is an example of which level of prevention?
  • ✓Primary
  • BSecondary

Primary prevention promotes health and prevents the occurrence of disease, injury, or disability in people who are susceptible but have no discernible pathological process (prepathogenesis). Examples include immunizations, seat-belt teaching, folic acid at preconception, and water fluoridation.

27. In series (sequential) testing, a person is classified as positive only if all tests are positive. What is the effect on sensitivity and specificity?
  • AIncreases sensitivity, decreases specificity
  • ✓Increases specificity, decreases sensitivity, producing fewer false positives
  • CIncreases both sensitivity and specificity equally
  • DHas no effect on either measure
Rationale

Series testing requires all tests to be positive to call the result positive, which enhances specificity and reduces false positives, at the cost of lower sensitivity.

28. In parallel (simultaneous) testing, a person is classified as positive if any one test is positive. What is the effect, and what is the classic example?
  • AIncreases specificity; example is confirmatory Western blot alone
  • ✓Increases sensitivity and reduces false negatives, at the cost of lower specificity; example is a blood bank considering a sample positive on either ELISA or Western blot
  • CHas no effect on sensitivity or specificity
  • DIncreases both sensitivity and specificity
Rationale

Parallel testing calls a result positive if any test is positive, which enhances sensitivity (fewer false negatives) but lowers specificity. The blood bank example treats a sample as positive if it is positive on either ELISA or Western blot.

screening, validity, and reliabilityseries vs. parallel testing
29. In a series testing protocol for HIV using ELISA followed by Western blot, which test should be performed first, and why?
  • AThe more specific test first, to rule out disease quickly
  • ✓The more sensitive test first, to catch all possible cases, followed by a more specific test to eliminate false positives
  • CIt does not matter which test is performed first
  • DBoth tests must be run simultaneously, not sequentially
Rationale

In series testing, the sequence matters: a very sensitive test is used first to catch all cases, and then a very specific test is applied to eliminate false positives. A person is called positive only if both are positive.

Watch for

Reversing the order (specific test first) is the classic distractor. The sensitive test always goes first in a series protocol.

30. A blood pressure cuff consistently reads 10 mm Hg higher than the true value on every patient. How should this be characterized?
  • AReliable and valid
  • BUnreliable and invalid
  • ✓Reliable but not valid — consistent, but wrong
  • DValid but not reliable
Rationale

Reliability is the consistency or repeatability of a measure; validity is whether the measure is truly measuring what it claims to measure. A cuff that reads consistently 10 mm Hg high is reliable (consistent) but not valid (inaccurate) — a measure can be consistent without being valid. ---

31. A researcher wants to study a rare disease efficiently by comparing exposure histories of people who have the disease against those who do not. Which design is most appropriate?
  • AProspective cohort study
  • ✓Case-control study

32. Which measure of association is produced by a case-control study, and why can it not directly calculate incidence or prevalence?
  • ARelative risk; because subjects are chosen by exposure status
  • ✓Odds ratio; because subjects are chosen by outcome (disease) status, not by exposure or by sampling the whole population
  • CAttack rate; because it follows an outbreak population
  • DCase fatality rate; because it tracks disease progression

In a case-control study, subjects are enrolled because they are known to have the outcome (cases) or not (controls), so neither incidence nor prevalence can be calculated directly. The design uses the odds ratio instead.

33. Which study design provides the best estimate of incidence and the clearest temporal sequence between exposure and outcome?
  • ACross-sectional study
  • BCase-control study
  • ✓Prospective cohort study
  • DEcological study
Rationale

A prospective cohort study follows a group sharing a characteristic of interest forward over time to observe new outcomes developing, giving the best estimate of incidence and risk and the clearest temporal sequence — though it is expensive, slow, and poorly suited to rare diseases.

34. A cohort study finds that smokers develop lung cancer at a rate of 20 per 1,000 person-years, compared to 2 per 1,000 person-years among nonsmokers. What measure of association does this represent, and how is it calculated?

  • ✓Relative risk; the ratio of cumulative incidence rates in the exposed versus unexposed groups

Rationale

A cohort study allows calculation of incidence, and relative risk (risk ratio) is the ratio of cumulative incidence rates in exposed versus unexposed groups. Only a cohort design, which follows people forward, can directly estimate incidence and therefore relative risk.

A cross-sectional study collects exposure and outcome data simultaneously ("correlational"). It gives prevalence, not incidence, allows no calculation of risk, and the temporal sequence between exposure and outcome is unclear.

43. Which three general categories of bias can produce a statistical association that is not truly causal?
  • ✓Selection bias, misclassification/information bias, and confounding
  • BRecall bias, publication bias, and the ecological fallacy
  • CSensitivity, specificity, and predictive value
  • DSampling bias, response bias, and attrition bias
Rationale

A statistical association can arise from chance, bias, or confounding. The three general categories of bias named are selection bias (how subjects enter the study), misclassification/information bias (how data are collected or classified), and confounding (a third factor tied to both exposure and outcome).
